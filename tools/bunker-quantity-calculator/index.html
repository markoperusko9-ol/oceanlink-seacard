Bunker quantity calculator

Turn a sounded volume into tonnes the way a bunker surveyor does: volume correction factor from Table 54B, standard volume at 15°C, then weight in air.

How it works

Oil expands as it warms, so a tank volume means nothing until it is brought back to 15°C. The supplier gives the density at 15°C on the bunker delivery note. Three steps turn your sounding into tonnes:

Standard volume = observed volume × VCF
Weight in air (t) = standard volume × (density at 15°C − 0.0011)

The volume correction factor comes from Petroleum Measurement Table 54B (ASTM D1250-80, API MPMS 11.1, IP 200), the table for fuel oils and other products. The tool works the table's own formula and rounds the factor to four decimals, as the printed table does:

VCF = exp[ −α × ΔT × (1 + 0.8 × α × ΔT) ], ΔT = temperature − 15
α = 186.9696 ÷ ρ² + 0.4862 ÷ ρ for fuel oils (ρ = density at 15°C in kg/m³, 839 and above)

Lighter products use the table's other constants: jet fuels and kerosenes from 788 to 838.5 kg/m³, gasolines up to 770 kg/m³ and a transition zone between them. Subtracting 0.0011 from the density is the short form of Table 56. It changes mass in vacuum to weight in air, which is what bunkers are bought in.

Worked example

Sounding gives 161.68 m³ at 34°C. Density at 15°C is 0.9903. Table 54B gives a VCF of 0.9870, so the standard volume is 161.68 × 0.9870 = 159.578 m³. The weight factor is 0.9903 − 0.0011 = 0.9892, so the tank holds 159.578 × 0.9892 = 157.85 t.

How this tool was checked

The volume correction factor reproduces all 44 values tested from three tables published by Measurement Canada under API Standard 2540 (1980): jet fuel at 800 kg/m³, gasoline at 730 kg/m³ and diesel at 840 kg/m³, from −30°C to 40°C. The whole calculation returns the 157.85 t of a published bunker survey example.

Limits

  • The volume must already be corrected for trim and list from your sounding tables, and free water must be taken off before you start.
  • This is the 1980 edition of Table 54B (ASTM D1250-80). If your contract names the 2004 edition, the factor can differ in the fourth decimal.
  • The density must be the density at 15°C in vacuum from the BDN or a laboratory. A density measured at tank temperature has to go through Table 53B first.
  • The quick rule of 0.00064 per degree skips the weight-in-air step. In the example it gives 158.2 t, which is 0.3 t more.

FAQ

How do you calculate bunker quantity in tonnes?

Multiply the observed volume by the volume correction factor from ASTM Table 54B to get the standard volume at 15 degrees Celsius. Then multiply by the density at 15 degrees minus 0.0011 to get the weight in air in tonnes.

Which ASTM table is used for bunkers?

Table 54B gives the volume correction factor for fuel oils from the density at 15 degrees Celsius and the oil temperature. Table 56 gives the weight factor, which in short form is the density minus 0.0011.

What is the difference between weight in air and mass in vacuum?

Weight in air allows for the buoyancy of air and is about 0.11 percent less than mass in vacuum. Bunkers are normally traded on weight in air, so the density in vacuum is reduced by 0.0011.
