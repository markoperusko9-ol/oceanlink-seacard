How it works

Great circle. The main figures are the shortest path on the WGS84 ellipsoid, the same model an ECDIS uses, so they should match your route check to a tenth of a mile. The spherical figures further down are what the textbook formula gives, with one minute of arc taken as one nautical mile:

cos D = sin Lat₁ × sin Lat₂ + cos Lat₁ × cos Lat₂ × cos d.long

The two differ by up to about half a percent because the Earth is not a perfect sphere. Use the spherical line to check your exam working, and the WGS84 line for the passage plan.

Rhumb line. Mercator sailing on WGS84: tan Course = d.long ÷ DMP, and the distance is the length of the meridian arc between the two latitudes divided by the cosine of the course. On an exact east–west course the tool switches to parallel sailing.

Vertex. The point of the great circle nearest the pole, from cos Lat(v) = cos Lat₁ × sin Course. If it is marked outside the track, the highest latitude you actually reach is at your departure or arrival point.

Worked example

From 35°00'N 140°00'E to 37°00'N 123°00'W. The spherical method gives 4,476 nm, initial course 055.3° and a vertex near 47°40'N 169°38'W. On WGS84 the great circle is 4,489.4 nm and the rhumb line is 4,723.4 nm on 088.5°, so the great circle saves about 234 nm, at the price of going up to nearly 48°N.

How this tool was checked

The WGS84 distances and courses were compared with the PROJ geodesic library for 460 pairs of positions, including pairs almost exactly opposite each other on the globe. They agreed to better than 0.001 nm. The rhumb line was compared with direct numerical integration for 157 pairs and agreed to better than 0.001 nm. The spherical method reproduces the worked example in the Ocean Link guide.

Limits

  • The tool knows nothing about land, ice, load line zones or weather. A great circle often has to be limited by a maximum latitude (composite sailing).
  • The vertex is computed on the sphere, so it can differ from an ECDIS by a few miles.

FAQ

Why is the great circle distance different from my textbook answer?

Textbook working treats the Earth as a sphere and one minute of arc as one nautical mile. An ECDIS and this tool use the WGS84 ellipsoid for the main figure. The two can differ by up to about half a percent. The spherical answer is shown as well so you can check your exam working.

When is a great circle worth it over a rhumb line?

On long passages with a large change of longitude in middle or high latitudes. Near the equator, or on courses close to north–south, the saving is small.
