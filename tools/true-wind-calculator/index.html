How it works

The relative (apparent) wind is the true wind combined with the head wind made by the ship's own movement. Take the ship's movement back out and the true wind is left:

True wind = Relative wind − Ship's head wind  (as vectors)

The tool first turns the relative angle into a true bearing (heading plus the angle, to starboard or port), then subtracts the ship's course and speed vector. The true wind is always further aft than the relative wind, unless you are stopped.

Wind direction is where the wind comes from. The Beaufort force is read from the WMO table using the true wind speed in whole knots.

Worked example

Heading 000°T at 10 kn. The anemometer shows 10 kn on the starboard beam (090° relative). The ship makes 10 kn of head wind from the north. Remove it and the true wind is from 135°T at 14.1 kn, Force 4.

How this tool was checked

The calculation was run on 2,000 random cases built the other way round: a known true wind and ship movement were combined into a relative wind, and the tool had to return the original true wind. It also passes head wind, following wind and calm cases worked by hand, and every boundary of the Beaufort table.

Limits

  • Heading and speed through the water give the wind over the water, which is what the sea state follows. For wind over the ground, enter course and speed over the ground.
  • The anemometer reads at its own height and is disturbed by the ship's structure. Check the result against the look of the sea.

FAQ

What is the difference between true wind and relative wind?

Relative, or apparent, wind is what the anemometer measures on a moving ship. True wind is the wind over the sea with the ship's own movement taken out. Weather reports and the logbook use true wind.

Why is the true wind always further aft than the relative wind?

The ship's movement adds a head wind from right ahead. That pulls the relative wind towards the bow. Taking it out moves the direction aft again.
