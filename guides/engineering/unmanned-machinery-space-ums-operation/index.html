Unmanned Machinery Space (UMS) Operation: A Practical Guide for Engineers

Quick answer

UMS operation means the engine room is left unattended for periods, usually at night. The ship relies on alarms, automatic safety systems, bridge control and an on-call duty engineer. SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part E sets the minimum equipment, class rules and the company SMS set the detail, and the Chief Engineer decides each day whether the plant is fit to go UMS.

What UMS actually means and why ships use it

UMS stands for unmanned (or periodically unattended) machinery space. The engine room is not abandoned. It is left without a watchkeeper because the ship has been built, surveyed and equipped to look after itself between rounds.

Three things replace the watchkeeper:

  • Monitoring and alarms. The machinery alarm system watches pressures, temperatures, levels and running states. It raises alarms in the engine control room, on the bridge and at the duty engineer's cabin.
  • Automatic protection. Safety systems slow down or shut down machinery. Standby pumps start on their own and the power management system restores power after a blackout.
  • A duty engineer on call. The duty engineer is immediately available to go below when an alarm calls.

The practical benefits are rest and fatigue control. Engineers work a day-work pattern and take turns on call instead of keeping three watches. This only works if the ship is genuinely fit for it.

A ship needs both the regulatory equipment and a class notation for unattended operation. Class notations differ between societies. Lloyd's Register uses UMS, DNV uses E0 and ABS uses ACCU, for example. Your ship's class certificate shows which one applies.

The regulatory framework in plain terms

The core international requirements sit in SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part E (Regulations 46 to 54). They are additional requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces. In summary:

Regulation What it covers
46 General: safety in all sailing conditions, including manoeuvring, must be equivalent to a manned engine room
47 Fire precautions, including fire detection and protection of high-risk areas
48 Protection against flooding, with bilge level alarms
49 Control of propulsion from the navigation bridge
50 Communication between the bridge, control room and engineers' accommodation
51 The alarm system, including continuous power with automatic changeover to a standby supply, an alarm on loss of normal supply, fail-safe design as far as practicable, and where alarms are shown
52 Safety systems that act automatically on serious malfunctions
53 Special requirements for machinery, boiler and electrical installations, such as standby arrangements
54 Special consideration for passenger ships

Regulation 38 of the same chapter requires an engineers' alarm, operable from the engine control position and audible in the engineers' accommodation.

For watchkeeping, STCW Code Section A-VIII/2 covers engineering watches. It expects that, in periodically unattended machinery spaces, the designated duty engineer is immediately available and on call to attend.

The detail comes from class rules, flag requirements and your company SMS under the ISM Code. That detail includes which alarms escalate where, time delays, dead-man settings and the conditions that forbid UMS. Do not assume one ship's arrangement applies to the next.

Going UMS: the pre-UMS round, step by step

Most companies require a checklist to be completed and signed before the space is left unattended. The usual sequence is:

  1. Get the Chief Engineer's agreement. Follow the CE's standing orders, which say who may put the plant on UMS and under what conditions.
  2. Check the bridge's situation. UMS is normally not used when manoeuvring, in restricted or congested waters, in heavy weather, or when the bridge expects engine movements. The detail depends on the SMS.
  3. Fuel. Top up the service and settling tanks so they will last until the next manned period. Drain the water from the settling tanks. Confirm the purifiers are running steadily, and that sludge tank levels are acceptable and their alarms are healthy.
  4. Bilges and drains. Pump or empty bilges and bilge wells in line with MARPOL and the Oil Record Book requirements. Check the bilge level alarms. Make sure sounding pipe cocks are closed and no drain is left open.
  5. Standby machinery. Set each standby pump to auto. Confirm the standby generator is selected for auto-start and the power management system is in automatic mode. Check that the air receivers are charged and drained and the compressors are in auto.
  6. Walk the space. Look for leaks, especially fuel pipes near hot surfaces. Check for loose items, open doors, oily rags, and any hot work or maintenance left unfinished. Confirm no isolation or lock-out will stop a standby unit starting.
  7. Test the alarms. Run a sample of alarm tests as the checklist requires. Confirm the fire detection system is active with no zones isolated, unless that has been formally agreed and recorded.
  8. Hand over. Select the duty engineer on the alarm extension panel and inform the bridge, including the officer of the watch's means of calling the engineer. Make the log entry with the time.

The point of the round is simple. Nothing should need a human before the next manned period. If something will, you are not ready to go UMS.

Answering an alarm at night: worked scenario

Scenario: It is 0230 and the cabin alarm panel sounds with bilge well high level, aft.

  1. Acknowledge at the cabin panel and read the alarm. Unacknowledged alarms normally escalate after a set delay. Depending on design, they spread to all engineers' cabins, the public spaces and the bridge.
  2. Call the bridge. Tell the OOW you are going below and what the alarm is.
  3. Activate the personnel or dead-man alarm as you enter the space, if the ship has one. Many ships fit one so that a lone engineer who stops resetting it raises a general alarm. Use is set by class and the SMS.
  4. Assess from the control room first. Check other active alarms, generator load and main engine status. Then go to the bilge well.
  5. Find the cause, not just the level. Possible causes include a gland leak, a leaking cooler drain, a burst pipe, a purifier overflow or a stern tube leak. A level that keeps rising after you pump is a flooding situation. Inform the bridge and call the Chief Engineer immediately.
  6. Rectify, record and report. Handle and record bilge water according to the Oil Record Book requirements. Log the alarm, the cause and the action taken. Report to the CE.
  7. Leave the space safely. Reset the dead-man alarm or the cabin selection. Inform the bridge you are back on UMS.

If you cannot fix the problem with confidence, ask the CE whether the space should stay manned. Calling for help or keeping a watch is never wrong.

Typical faults, symptoms and causes

Most call-outs fall into a few familiar groups:

  • Fuel supply. A service tank low-level alarm means a tank was not topped up, a purifier has tripped or sludged, or the transfer pump is not in auto. A purifier alarm often traces back to unsteady feed temperature or a dirty bowl.
  • Bilge high level. Usually leaks and drains. Occasionally it is a sensor fault, which should still be proved and not assumed.
  • Blackout or generator trip. The power management system should start the standby generator, connect it and restart essential consumers in sequence. On attendance, check what tripped, whether preferential trips acted, and whether every essential pump restarted.
  • Main engine slowdown or shutdown. Possible triggers are low lube oil pressure, high cooling water temperature, oil mist detector or high scavenge temperature. Never reset a shutdown and restart without finding the cause and informing the bridge and CE.
  • Fire detection alarm. Treat it as real until proven otherwise. Raise the alarm and follow the fire plan and muster list. Do not investigate alone in a smoke-logged space.
  • Nuisance alarms. A faulty sensor, loose wiring or a failing alarm card can cause repeated false alarms. Repeated false alarms make people ignore real ones. Record them and repair them through the planned maintenance system.

A failure of the alarm system itself undermines the basis for UMS. Examples are a power supply fault, a self-monitoring alarm or a dead cabin panel. Your SMS and CE's standing orders normally require the engine room to be manned until it is repaired.

Checks, testing and maintenance of UMS equipment

UMS depends on sensors and safety devices that nobody notices until they fail. Keep them in the PMS and test them as the manufacturer, class and SMS require. This normally covers:

  • Alarm and shutdown simulation. Prove that sensors, slowdowns and shutdowns act. For pressure and temperature sensors, simulate the actual condition where practicable, not just the wiring.
  • Fire detection. Test the detector heads by zone, check the panel faults and confirm the extension panels work.
  • Bilge level switches. Test them physically, not only with the test button where that is possible.
  • Standby changeover. Test standby pump changeovers and generator auto-start, and carry out blackout restoration trials when permitted.
  • Alarm power supply changeover. Check the changeover from normal to standby supply and its failure alarm.
  • Engineers' alarm, dead-man alarm and bridge-to-engineer calls.
  • Bridge control of propulsion. Check bridge control, its emergency stop, and the transfer of control between stations.

Record every test. Class surveyors and PSC inspectors often ask for alarm test records and may ask for a live demonstration. A bypassed or isolated sensor should be logged, risk-assessed and reported. Do not leave one quietly forgotten.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners like

Expect these, especially at the operational and management level.

  • "What does SOLAS require for a ship to operate UMS?" Name Chapter II-1 Part E. Run through fire detection, flooding alarms, bridge control, communications, the alarm system, safety systems and standby arrangements. Then say that class and the SMS add detail.
  • "Talk me through your pre-UMS round." Give a logical order: fuel, bilges, standby machinery, walkround, alarms, bridge handover, log entry.
  • "When would you not go UMS?" Examples:
    • manoeuvring or restricted waters
    • heavy weather
    • an alarm or fire detection defect
    • major machinery out of service
    • hot work or open systems
    • whenever the CE or master orders
  • "You get a blackout at night on UMS. What do you do?" Inform the bridge and go below. Confirm the standby generator has taken load. Restore steering gear, lube oil and cooling to the main engine. Find out why the generator tripped, then report and log.
  • "Why is a dead-man alarm fitted?" It protects the lone engineer. If they are injured or overcome, the failure to reset raises an alarm.
  • "What happens if the duty engineer doesn't acknowledge?" The alarm escalates according to the system design, normally to other engineers and the bridge.

Examiners want a safe, ordered answer that always includes informing the bridge. They are not looking for one fixed set of numbers.

Key points
  • UMS replaces the watchkeeper with alarms, automatic safety systems, bridge control and an on-call duty engineer.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part E (Regulations 46–54) sets the minimum requirements; class notation, flag and the company SMS add the detail.
  • Before going UMS, nothing should need human attention before the next manned period: tanks topped up, bilges dry, standbys in auto, alarms healthy.
  • Always inform the bridge before entering the engine room on a call-out, and use the dead-man alarm where fitted.
  • Do not reset a main engine shutdown without finding the cause and informing the bridge and Chief Engineer.
  • A defective alarm or fire detection system normally means the SMS requires the engine room to be manned until it is repaired.
  • Test and record alarms, safety devices and standby changeovers through the PMS; inspectors check these records.

Frequently asked questions

Can any ship operate with an unmanned engine room?

No. The ship must meet SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part E and normally hold a class notation for unattended machinery operation. The equipment must also be working on the day. A ship without the notation, or with key alarms defective, should keep a manned watch.

Who decides when the engine room goes UMS?

The Chief Engineer, under the company SMS and in agreement with the master on navigational conditions. Standing orders usually say who may switch to UMS and the conditions that forbid it, such as manoeuvring or heavy weather.

Should I go into the engine room alone on a UMS call-out?

Many companies allow a single duty engineer to attend, but only after informing the bridge and activating the dead-man or personnel alarm where fitted. For fire, flooding or anything beyond a routine fault, call the Chief Engineer and further help. Your SMS is the rule here.

What is the dead-man alarm in the engine room?

It is a timer that the lone engineer must reset at intervals while in the machinery space. If it is not reset, it gives a pre-warning and then a general alarm so someone comes to check. Whether it is fitted, and its timing, depend on class rules and ship design.

Is a UMS checklist mandatory?

The ISM Code requires procedures for key shipboard operations, and most SMSs include a pre-UMS checklist. The exact content varies by company and ship. Follow your own ship's checklist and keep it signed and filed.

Test yourself

Where are the main SOLAS requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces found?

Additional requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces are in SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part E (Regulations 46–54). Chapter II-2 covers fire protection generally.

During a night call-out on UMS, what should the duty engineer do before entering the engine room?

Good practice and most SMSs require informing the OOW and using the personnel alarm, so a lone engineer is never unaccounted for. Resetting alarms unseen or disabling escalation defeats the system.

The main engine shut down on low lube oil pressure while on UMS. What is the correct action?

Shutdowns under SOLAS II-1 Regulation 52 protect the engine from serious damage. Restarting without finding the cause risks bearing failure, so the bridge and CE must be involved.

The machinery alarm system shows a power supply failure alarm. What does this mean for UMS?

SOLAS II-1 Regulation 51 requires the alarm system to be continuously powered, with automatic changeover to a standby supply and an alarm on loss of the normal supply. With a supply fault, the system's reliability is compromised, so the space should be manned until it is repaired, as company SMSs normally require.

Which of these conditions would normally rule out going UMS?

Company SMSs and CE standing orders typically require a manned engine room when manoeuvring or in restricted waters. The other options are conditions for safe UMS.
