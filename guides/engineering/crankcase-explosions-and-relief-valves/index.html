Crankcase Explosions and Crankcase Relief Valves

Quick answer

A crankcase explosion starts at a hot spot. The hot spot vaporises lube oil, the vapour condenses into a fine flammable mist, and the hot spot then ignites that mist. Relief valves vent the pressure of the first (primary) explosion and then close again. This stops air rushing in and causing a much worse secondary explosion. Oil mist detection and bearing temperature monitoring are there to catch the problem before it gets that far. Never open the crankcase until the engine has cooled for the period the maker specifies.

How a crankcase explosion develops

A diesel crankcase is always full of lubricating oil, but normal splash oil is not the danger. Splash droplets are too large to ignite easily, and the atmosphere stays too lean or too coarse to burn.

The dangerous sequence is:

  1. A hot spot forms. Common causes are an overheating bearing, a rubbing guide or crosshead, a failing chain drive or gear, or piston blow-by on a trunk engine.
  2. Oil vaporises. Lube oil hitting the hot surface turns to vapour.
  3. Vapour condenses into mist. The vapour drifts to cooler parts of the crankcase and condenses into very fine droplets. This is the white oil mist that is highly flammable once its concentration rises into the flammable range.
  4. Ignition. The hot spot that made the mist, or a flame from blow-by, ignites it.

Examiners want to hear the distinction: the hot spot both creates the explosive mist and supplies the ignition. Remove the hot spot early and there is no explosion.

Typical root causes behind hot spots:

  • Loss of lube oil pressure or flow, including blocked filters, a pump failure or a choked bearing oil hole
  • Contaminated oil, for example with water ingress or fuel dilution
  • Incorrect bearing clearances after overhaul, or a wiped or fatigued bearing
  • Misalignment or crankshaft deflection problems
  • Piston ring failure on trunk engines, so that hot gas blows past into the crankcase
  • On crosshead engines, a defective piston rod stuffing box, which can let scavenge fire products or hot material reach the crankcase

Primary and secondary explosions

Primary explosion. The mist ignites and pressure rises sharply. It may be fairly mild, and it may lift the relief valves and blow out a short flame or puff of smoke. Crankcase doors can be distorted or blown off if the relief arrangement is defective.

After the primary. The burnt gases cool quickly and contract, leaving a partial vacuum in the crankcase.

Secondary explosion. If the relief valves fail to reseat, or a door has been blown off, fresh air is drawn in. That air mixes with the remaining unburnt mist, still in contact with the hot spot, and a second explosion follows. Because it has a full supply of oxygen, it is usually far more violent than the primary. Secondary explosions are the ones that wreck engine rooms and kill people.

This is why relief valves must be self-closing non-return valves. It is also why nobody should open a crankcase door on a recently stopped engine that has had a mist alarm: you would be supplying the air.

Crankcase explosion relief valves: requirement and construction

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 27 requires crankcase explosion relief valves on internal combustion engines above a threshold. The threshold is a cylinder bore of 200 mm or more, or a crankcase volume of 0.6 m³ or more. The valves must be of a suitable type with enough relief area. Their discharge must be directed so as to minimise the risk of injury to personnel. Class rules, based on IACS Unified Requirements M9 and M10, set the detail: number and position of valves, total free area and opening pressure. Type testing of the relief valves is covered by IACS UR M66. Check your engine maker's manual and class documentation for the figures for your engine.

Typical construction (spring-loaded type):

Component Purpose
Light alloy valve disc Low inertia, so it lifts fast
Spring Holds the valve shut. Lets it lift at a small overpressure and closes it quickly afterwards
Sealing ring (often an elastomer O-ring) Keeps the valve gas-tight when closed, so air is not drawn in
Flame arrester (oil-wetted steel gauze) Cools escaping gas and quenches flame so fire does not emerge into the engine room
Deflector/cover Directs the discharge away from where people stand, usually downward or sideways

The valve has three jobs:

  • Relieve the pressure of the primary explosion so the crankcase does not rupture.
  • Arrest flame so burning gas does not ignite the engine room.
  • Reclose immediately so air cannot enter and feed a secondary explosion.

Relief valves do not prevent explosions. They limit the damage. Prevention comes from good lubrication, good maintenance and early detection.

Detection: oil mist detectors and temperature monitoring

Class rules (IACS UR M10 and the society's own rules) require larger engines to have one of the following:

  • crankcase oil mist detection,
  • bearing temperature monitoring, or
  • an equivalent approved arrangement.

The applicable size limits are set in IACS UR M10 and class rules. SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 47 adds a requirement for periodically unattended machinery spaces. Check class documentation for your engine. Many modern engines have both.

Oil mist detector (OMD). Mist density is measured optically, as light obscuration or scatter. Older designs draw samples from each crankcase compartment through pipes to a central unit with a rotating valve or comparison cell. Newer designs fit a sensor head on each compartment. The OMD typically gives two kinds of alarm:

  • High mist alarm: the absolute mist density is above the alarm level.
  • Deviation alarm: one compartment differs from the average. This points to a developing local hot spot even when overall density is still low.

On many main engines a high mist alarm also triggers an automatic slowdown or shutdown request.

Other indicators:

  • Main, crankpin and crosshead bearing temperature sensors
  • Splash oil temperature sensors
  • Lube oil outlet temperature and pressure trends
  • Smell of hot oil, white mist from crankcase vents, hot crankcase doors
  • Unusual knocking, or a change in running sound

Keep trend records. A slow rise in one unit's bearing or splash oil temperature is often the first warning.

Worked scenario: OMD alarm on the main engine at sea

You are the duty engineer and the main engine OMD sounds a high mist alarm on unit 4. Follow your company SMS and the engine maker's instructions. The broad sequence below is what examiners expect:

  1. Inform the bridge immediately. Request a slowdown, or confirm that the automatic slowdown has taken effect. Navigational safety decides when the engine can be stopped.
  2. Call the Chief Engineer. Raise the alarm in the engine room.
  3. Keep everyone away from the crankcase doors and relief valves. Clear the area alongside the engine.
  4. Stop the engine when permitted by the bridge. Do not stop it in a way that hazards the ship.
  5. Keep the lube oil pump running and maintain cooling, unless the maker says otherwise. This helps carry heat away from the hot spot. Follow the maker's guidance on turning gear.
  6. Do not open the crankcase until the cooling period specified by the maker has passed. Opening early can admit air and cause a secondary explosion.
  7. Prepare firefighting equipment and stop unnecessary ventilation near the engine if your procedures call for it.
  8. After the cooling period, open the crankcase with no naked lights and with firefighting cover. Inspect for the hot spot: discoloured or wiped bearings, white metal in the sump, scuffed guides, blue-tinted surfaces.
  9. Find and correct the root cause. This may mean checking the lube oil (consider analysis), bearing clearances, oil supply and filters.
  10. Test the OMD to confirm it is working, and do not restart until the cause is cleared. Record everything in the engine log.

A false alarm is not proven until you have checked. Repeated nuisance alarms usually point to a maintenance problem with the detector, such as dirty optics, drained sampling pipes or a faulty sensor. Fix the detector; never mute it and carry on.

Checks and maintenance

Carry out maintenance at the intervals in your Planned Maintenance System and the engine maker's manual.

Relief valves:

  • Check that the valve is free to move and that the spring is intact. Some designs allow manual lifting for this check; follow the maker's method.
  • Inspect the sealing ring for hardening, cracking or damage, and renew it with genuine spares.
  • Check that the flame arrester gauze is clean, undamaged and not painted over or clogged with sludge.
  • Make sure the deflector is fitted and the discharge path is clear. Do not store gear, rags or tools in front of a relief valve.
  • Never wire, blank, tie down or wedge a relief valve, even temporarily.
  • After any lift, inspect the valve before relying on it again.

Crankcase and doors:

  • Door joints must be tight. Air leaks undermine the closed-crankcase principle.
  • Crankcase vent and breather pipes must be clear. Class rules restrict their size and their interconnection with other engines' vents.

OMD and monitoring:

  • Carry out functional tests with the maker's test method, for example test smoke or a test function.
  • Clean the optics or lenses and check filters and sampling pipe drains.
  • Calibrate and zero the detector as instructed.
  • Prove the slowdown or shutdown function where fitted, and record the test.
  • Check bearing and splash oil temperature sensors against trend data.

Lube oil:

  • Carry out regular sampling and analysis. Watch for water, fuel dilution and wear metals.
  • Keep purifier performance and filter differential pressure under control.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Why does a crankcase explosion need a hot spot? The hot spot vaporises oil, the vapour condenses into flammable mist, and the hot spot then ignites it. Normal splash oil droplets are too large to be the main hazard.

Q: Why is the secondary explosion worse? Cooling after the primary explosion creates a partial vacuum. If air gets in, it mixes with the remaining mist near the still-hot spot. The resulting explosion is more violent because it has plenty of oxygen.

Q: What are the functions of a crankcase relief valve? It relieves the pressure of the primary explosion, quenches flame through the arrester, directs the discharge away from personnel, and recloses quickly to stop air ingress.

Q: What do you do on an OMD alarm? Inform the bridge and slow down, call the Chief Engineer, and keep people clear. Stop the engine when the bridge allows, keep lube oil circulating, and do not open the crankcase until the maker's cooling period has passed. Then locate and correct the cause.

Q: What is the advantage of a deviation alarm on an OMD? It compares each compartment with the average. That picks up a local hot spot early, before the overall mist density reaches the high alarm level.

Q: Which regulation requires crankcase relief valves? SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 27, with details in class rules based on IACS Unified Requirements M9 and M10.

Key points
  • A hot spot both creates the flammable oil mist (by vaporisation and condensation) and ignites it.
  • The secondary explosion, fed by air drawn into the post-explosion partial vacuum, is the most dangerous stage.
  • Relief valves relieve pressure, arrest flame and reclose. They limit damage but do not prevent explosions.
  • SOLAS II-1 Reg 27 requires relief valves on engines with a bore of 200 mm or more, or a crankcase volume of 0.6 m³ or more. Class rules set the details.
  • On an OMD alarm: inform the bridge, slow down, keep clear, stop when permitted, and keep the crankcase closed for the maker's cooling period.
  • Never paint over, blank or tie down a relief valve. Keep the flame arrester clean and the seals in good condition.
  • OMD testing, bearing temperature trends and lube oil analysis are your early warning system.

Frequently asked questions

What causes a crankcase explosion on a ship's engine?

A hot spot inside the crankcase, usually an overheating bearing, a rubbing surface or piston blow-by, vaporises lube oil. The vapour condenses into a fine mist, and once that mist reaches a flammable concentration the hot spot ignites it. Underlying causes include poor lubrication, contaminated oil, wrong clearances and misalignment.

Why should you not open the crankcase immediately after an oil mist alarm?

The hot spot may still be hot and the crankcase still full of mist. Opening a door lets in fresh air, which can trigger an explosion with people standing right in front of it. Wait for the cooling period stated by the engine maker and your company procedures.

What is the flame arrester in a crankcase relief valve for?

It is usually an oil-wetted steel gauze that cools the escaping gases and quenches the flame. This stops burning gas emerging into the engine room and starting a fire or injuring personnel. It must be kept clean and never painted over.

Which engines must have crankcase explosion relief valves?

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 27 requires them on internal combustion engines with a cylinder bore of 200 mm or more, or a crankcase volume of 0.6 m³ or more. The number, position and relief area are set by class rules based on IACS Unified Requirements M9 and M10. Check your engine's class documentation for specifics.

What is the difference between a high mist alarm and a deviation alarm?

A high mist alarm triggers when absolute mist density exceeds the set level. A deviation alarm triggers when one compartment differs from the average of the others. The deviation alarm can reveal a local hot spot earlier, before total mist density gets high.

Test yourself

Which oil condition in a crankcase presents the greatest explosion risk?

Condensed oil mist from vaporisation at a hot spot is fine enough to form a flammable atmosphere. Splash droplets and sump oil are not the main hazard. See the engine maker's manual and standard marine engineering texts.

Why must crankcase relief valves reclose quickly after lifting?

Cooling after the primary explosion creates a partial vacuum. Reclosing stops air ingress that would feed a more violent secondary explosion. See SOLAS II-1 Reg 27 and IACS UR M9.

The main engine OMD gives a high mist alarm at sea. What is the correct first action?

The bridge must be informed and speed reduced. Lube oil is normally kept circulating, and the crankcase stays closed for the maker's cooling period. Follow the company SMS and the maker's instructions.

During maintenance you find a relief valve flame arrester gauze painted over. What is the correct response?

The arrester must be clear to vent pressure and quench flame. Removing or blanking it defeats its purpose. See the engine maker's manual and your PMS instructions.

Under SOLAS II-1 Reg 27, crankcase relief valves are required on engines meeting which criterion?

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 27 applies by bore or crankcase volume, not by propulsion role or UMS status. Auxiliary engines meeting the size threshold are included.
