Positive Displacement Pumps: How They Work, Faults and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

A positive displacement pump moves a fixed volume of liquid per stroke or revolution. Its flow depends mainly on speed and stays nearly constant as discharge pressure changes (apart from a small slip loss), so it must never run against a closed discharge without a working relief valve. On board you will find them as gear, screw, vane, mono and reciprocating pumps on fuel, lube oil, sludge, bilge, OWS and hydraulic duties.

What a positive displacement pump is and why we use it

A positive displacement (PD) pump traps a definite volume of liquid in a chamber and physically pushes it into the discharge. Each stroke or revolution moves roughly the same volume, whatever the back pressure, apart from a small loss called slip. Slip is liquid leaking back from discharge to suction through internal clearances.

A centrifugal pump works differently. It gives energy to the liquid by velocity, so its flow falls off as discharge head rises. If you close a centrifugal pump's discharge, it churns and heats up but does not build unlimited pressure. If you close a PD pump's discharge, pressure keeps rising until something gives: the relief valve lifts, the motor trips, a joint blows or the casing cracks.

We choose PD pumps where we need:

  • Viscous liquids, such as heavy fuel, lube oil and sludge, which centrifugal pumps handle poorly.
  • Self-priming or good suction lift, such as bilge, stripping and sludge duties.
  • Steady flow at varying pressure, such as fuel supply and booster circuits, or main and auxiliary lube oil on many installations.
  • Low shear, so the oil and water are not emulsified before an oily water separator.
  • High pressure at modest flow, such as hydraulic steering gear, deck machinery and hatch cover hydraulics.

Main types you will meet on board

Type How it displaces Typical shipboard duties
Reciprocating (piston/plunger) Piston moves in a cylinder with suction and delivery valves Bilge, stripping, older boiler feed, emergency duties
External gear Two meshing gears carry liquid round the casing Fuel transfer, purifier feed, lube oil transfer
Internal gear Rotor and idler gear with a crescent Viscous oils, sludge transfer
Screw (twin or triple) Intermeshing screws form sealed cavities that travel axially Fuel supply/booster, lube oil, some cargo and stripping duties
Vane Sliding vanes in an eccentric rotor Hydraulic systems, light oil transfer
Progressive cavity (mono) Helical metal rotor turning in an elastomer stator Sludge, sewage, OWS feed, bilge
Axial or radial piston, variable stroke Swashplate or eccentric varies the stroke Hydraulic steering gear, deck machinery

A few points examiners like to hear:

  • Triple-screw pumps have one driven power rotor. The two idler rotors are turned by the liquid pressure, not by metal contact under load, so they depend on a lubricating liquid.
  • Twin-screw pumps with external timing gears keep the screws out of contact. This suits liquids with poor lubricity.
  • Mono pumps have a rubber stator. Running dry for even a short time can burn it out.
  • Variable displacement pumps change flow by altering the stroke while the motor runs at constant speed. This is the principle behind many electro-hydraulic steering gears.

How it works, step by step

Single-acting reciprocating pump

  1. Suction stroke. The piston withdraws and the cylinder volume increases, so pressure falls. Pressure in the suction line now exceeds cylinder pressure. The suction valve lifts and liquid flows in. The delivery valve is held shut by discharge pressure.
  2. Delivery stroke. The piston advances and cylinder pressure rises. The suction valve closes and the delivery valve opens. The trapped volume is pushed into the discharge.
  3. Pulsation. Flow is intermittent. An air chamber on the discharge, and sometimes on the suction, acts as a cushion and smooths the flow. A double-acting pump delivers on both strokes, which also reduces pulsation.

External gear pump

  1. As the teeth come out of mesh on the suction side, the space between them opens and draws liquid in.
  2. The liquid is carried round the outside, between the teeth and the casing, not through the middle.
  3. On the discharge side the teeth re-mesh and squeeze the liquid out.
  4. Tight clearances at the tooth tips and side plates limit slip. Wear in these clearances is the main cause of falling capacity.

Screw pump

As the rotors turn, the screw profiles form sealed cavities. These travel steadily from suction to discharge, so flow is smooth with very little pulsation. Screw pumps run quietly and are well suited to continuous fuel and lube oil service.

Controlling the output

You cannot throttle a PD pump with the discharge valve the way you can a centrifugal pump. Flow is controlled by:

  • changing the pump speed;
  • changing the stroke, on variable displacement pumps;
  • recirculating part of the flow back to suction or tank through a bypass or pressure-regulating valve.

Normal operation, starting and routine checks

Normal operating values come from the maker's manual, the system drawings and the plate on the pump. Every installation differs, so learn your own ship's figures during familiarisation rather than relying on someone else's.

Typical starting sequence (follow your company procedure and the maker's instructions):

  1. Check the system line-up. The suction tank should have liquid, and the destination tank should have room.
  2. Open the suction and discharge valves fully. Check that any filter or strainer changeover cock is set to a clean element.
  3. For pumps that must not run dry (gear, screw and mono), make sure the casing is flooded. Vent if a vent is fitted.
  4. For viscous oils, confirm the oil is at a suitable temperature. Cold heavy oil raises suction losses and motor load.
  5. On the first start after electrical work, or after the motor has been disconnected, check the direction of rotation. A gear or screw pump running backwards will try to pump from discharge to suction.
  6. Start the pump. Watch the suction and discharge pressure gauges and the motor current. Listen for noise.
  7. Confirm flow actually reaches its destination by checking tank soundings or level gauges, not just by watching pressure.

Routine running checks:

  • suction and discharge pressure compared with normal values;
  • motor current;
  • bearing and casing temperature;
  • seal or gland leakage;
  • unusual noise or vibration;
  • strainer differential pressure;
  • relief valve not continuously lifting;
  • coupling guard in place.

Maintenance through the PMS:

  • cleaning strainers and filters;
  • checking clearances (gear tip and side, screw, and valve lift and seating on reciprocating pumps);
  • renewing mechanical seals or repacking glands;
  • checking bearings and coupling alignment;
  • checking the air chamber is not waterlogged;
  • testing and resetting the relief valve to the maker's set pressure, with records kept as your SMS requires.

The relief valve: the most important fitting on the pump

A relief valve between the discharge and the first isolating valve protects the pump, the motor and the pipework from overpressure. It is either built into the pump casing or fitted externally on the discharge line.

  • An internal relief valve usually returns liquid straight back to the pump suction. If it lifts continuously, the same liquid circulates round and round. It heats up quickly, and in some cases it can damage the pump. An internal relief valve is a safety device, not a flow controller.
  • An external relief valve returning to a tank gets rid of the heat and is better where recirculation may last longer.
  • The set pressure must sit above the normal working pressure but below the design pressure of the pump and system. The exact figure is on the valve, in the maker's manual or on the system drawing. Never adjust it by guesswork.
  • A relief valve that leaks or is set low causes an apparent loss of capacity, because the pump is working but part of the flow goes straight back.
  • Gagging a relief valve, or screwing it down hard to get more pressure, is a classic cause of burst filters, split pipes and fires on fuel systems.

Faults, symptoms and how to trace them

Symptom Likely causes
No delivery Suction valve shut, tank empty, strainer choked, air leak in suction line, pump not primed, wrong rotation, relief valve stuck open, broken coupling or key
Reduced capacity Worn internal clearances causing slip, partly choked strainer, relief valve leaking or set low, liquid too thin or too hot, air drawn in at gland or joint, worn valves on reciprocating pump
Noisy running or knocking Cavitation from a restricted suction or liquid too viscous, entrained air, worn bearings, misalignment, waterlogged air chamber on reciprocating pump
High motor current or overload trip Discharge restricted or valve shut, cold or very viscous liquid, choked discharge filter, seized or rubbing rotors, bearing failure
Overheating Running dry, continuous recirculation through relief valve, tight clearances after overhaul
Excessive seal leakage Worn or damaged mechanical seal, running dry, shaft run-out, wrong gland packing or overtightening

Worked scenario: fuel transfer pump runs but the settling tank level does not rise

  1. Check the pressure gauges first. If suction shows a high vacuum and discharge pressure is low, the problem is on the suction side. Look for a choked strainer, a closed or partly closed suction valve, or cold oil.
  2. If suction is normal and discharge pressure is at the relief setting, something downstream is shut or blocked. The relief valve is lifting. Stop the pump and check the line-up before anything else.
  3. If both pressures look low and the pump sounds rough, suspect air ingress. Check the strainer cover joint and the suction pipe flanges, and make sure the suction tank has not been pumped down to the suction bellmouth.
  4. If pressures look normal but the transfer rate has dropped slowly over months, suspect internal wear. Check clearances at the next opportunity.
  5. Do not forget the simple answer: the oil may be going to the wrong tank because a valve is mis-set. Confirm by sounding the tanks.

Safety points

  • Never start or run a PD pump against a closed discharge unless the relief arrangement is proven and the procedure allows it.
  • Isolate before opening up. Apply lock-out tag-out on the electrical supply. Shut and tag the suction and discharge valves. Relieve trapped pressure through vents or drains before slackening any cover. Liquid trapped in a PD pump or its pipework can stay under pressure.
  • Hot oil. Fuel and lube oil pumps may hold heated oil. Allow it to cool, and wear suitable PPE.
  • Fire risk. Fuel pump leaks onto hot surfaces are a well-known engine room fire cause. Report and repair leaks from seals, filter covers and fittings promptly. Keep spray shields and lagging in place.
  • Reciprocating pumps have exposed moving parts on some designs. Keep guards fitted.
  • Hydraulic systems can hold stored energy in accumulators. Follow the maker's procedure for depressurising before any work.

Oral exam: questions examiners commonly ask

Why must a PD pump have a relief valve? Because flow is fixed by speed. If the discharge is blocked, pressure keeps rising until something fails. The relief valve limits this pressure.

Why is a PD pump preferred for bilge or OWS feed? It primes itself and gives good suction lift. A low-shear type, such as a mono or piston pump, does not emulsify oil and water, which helps the separator work properly.

What is slip and what increases it? Slip is internal leakage from discharge back to suction. It increases with wear, a higher pressure difference across the pump and lower liquid viscosity.

What is the purpose of the air chamber? It smooths the pulsating delivery of a reciprocating pump and reduces shock in the pipework. If it fills with liquid, it stops working.

How do you control the output of a PD pump? By changing speed, by changing stroke on variable displacement pumps, or by bypassing flow back to suction or tank. You do not throttle the discharge.

Why should gear and screw pumps not run dry? The pumped liquid lubricates and cools the close-clearance parts. Running dry causes rapid wear, seizure and seal damage.

How does a variable displacement pump work in a steering gear? The motor runs at constant speed. The control mechanism tilts a swashplate or shifts an eccentric to vary the stroke and reverse the flow direction, which moves the rudder actuator.

Key points
  • A positive displacement pump delivers a fixed volume per stroke or revolution, so its flow depends mainly on speed and stays nearly constant as discharge pressure changes, apart from a small slip loss.
  • Never run a PD pump against a closed discharge; the relief valve protects the pump, motor and pipework.
  • An internal relief valve returning to suction is a safety device, not a flow control, because continuous lifting heats the liquid.
  • Control flow by speed, stroke or bypass, never by throttling the discharge.
  • Gear, screw and mono pumps rely on the pumped liquid for lubrication and must not run dry.
  • Loss of capacity usually means suction restriction, air ingress, a leaking relief valve or internal wear causing slip.
  • Take normal pressures and relief settings from the maker's manual and your own ship's records, not from memory of another vessel.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between a positive displacement pump and a centrifugal pump?

A PD pump traps and pushes a fixed volume, so its flow stays nearly constant whatever the discharge pressure. A centrifugal pump gives energy to the liquid by velocity, so its flow falls as discharge head rises. This is why a PD pump needs a relief valve and a centrifugal pump can be throttled at the discharge.

Why are screw pumps used for fuel and lube oil service?

They give smooth, nearly pulse-free flow and handle viscous oils well. They run quietly and suit continuous duty. Some ships use other pump types for these services, so check your own installation.

Can a positive displacement pump run dry?

Most types should not. Gear and screw pumps depend on the liquid to lubricate tight clearances, and a mono pump's rubber stator can be damaged very quickly if it runs dry. Always check the maker's instructions for the pump you are operating.

Why does a gear pump lose capacity over time?

Wear at the gear tips, side plates and bearings opens up internal clearances. More liquid then slips back from discharge to suction. A relief valve that leaks or has drifted below its set pressure can produce the same symptom, so check it as well.

How is the relief valve set on a ship's pump?

It is set to the pressure stated by the maker or shown on the system drawing, above working pressure but below the design pressure of the system. Testing and resetting are done according to the planned maintenance system and company procedures, and the results are recorded. Never adjust it by trial and error to get more output.

Test yourself

A gear pump's discharge valve is closed while the pump is running. What happens?

A PD pump delivers a fixed volume per revolution, so blocking the discharge drives pressure up until the relief valve lifts or a component fails. This principle applies to all positive displacement pumps.

Which is the correct way to reduce the output of a fixed-displacement screw pump?

PD pump output is controlled by speed, stroke or bypass. Throttling the discharge only raises pressure, and throttling the suction causes cavitation.

Which change increases slip in a gear pump?

Slip is internal leakage back to suction. It increases with wear, with a higher pressure difference and with thinner liquid.

What is the purpose of the air chamber on a reciprocating bilge pump?

The trapped air cushions the intermittent flow from a reciprocating pump. If the chamber becomes waterlogged it stops working.

Why is a mono (progressive cavity) pump often chosen as an oily water separator feed pump?

Low-shear pumping keeps oil droplets large, so the separator can remove them. High-shear pumps emulsify the mixture and make separation harder.
