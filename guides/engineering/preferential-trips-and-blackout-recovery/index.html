Preferential Trips and Blackout Recovery: A Practical Guide for Marine Engineers

Quick answer

A preferential trip is a staged load-shedding arrangement. When a generator becomes overloaded, it disconnects non-essential consumers so the generator breaker stays closed and propulsion, steering and safety services keep their power. If a blackout still happens, recovery means getting power back safely and in the right order: confirm emergency power, establish why the breaker opened before reclosing, put a main generator on the dead bus, let essential auxiliaries restart one after another, then restore loads gradually and investigate the root cause.

Why preferential trips exist

A ship's generators are protected by overcurrent, short-circuit, reverse-power and under-voltage protection. If the only generator on the bus (or the last one remaining) is overloaded and its overcurrent protection trips, every consumer on that bus loses power. That includes the steering gear, main engine lube oil pumps, cooling water pumps and fuel supply. Losing all of that to save the galley ranges and accommodation air conditioning makes no sense.

The preferential trip sits in front of the generator's own overcurrent trip. It senses the overload first and disconnects consumers the ship can live without. This lets the generator ride through the overload and stay on the board.

SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires this outcome, not a particular design. Where generators run in parallel, the loss of one set must not overload the survivors to the point where propulsion, steering and ship safety are lost. Where a single generator normally supplies the load, the Part E requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces call for load-shedding to protect those same services. The exact relay settings, number of stages and allocation of loads are set by the shipbuilder and classification society. Your ship's electrical drawings and the switchboard maker's manual are the authority, not a textbook figure.

How it works, step by step

The details vary by switchboard maker and age of ship, but the logic is broadly the same:

  1. Sensing. On older switchboards, a preferential trip relay is fed from the generator current transformers. On modern ships, the power management system (PMS) does the same job using kW or current measurement. Some systems also act on falling frequency.
  2. Pick-up below the generator trip. The setting is deliberately lower than the generator overcurrent trip setting. Load shedding therefore always happens before the generator breaker would open.
  3. Time delay. A short delay stops the relay reacting to brief starting currents.
  4. Staged shedding. If the overload persists, the first group of non-essential loads is tripped. If it still persists after a further delay, the second group goes, and so on. Each stage removes progressively more important (but still non-essential) consumers.
  5. Lockout. Shed consumers usually stay off until someone manually resets them. This stops the system cycling loads on and off.

Typical groupings (always check your own ship):

Stage Typical consumers
First Galley equipment, laundry, accommodation air conditioning, workshop supplies
Later Non-essential ventilation, some deck machinery, provision plant (for a limited time), reefer sockets on some ships
Never shed Steering gear, main engine essential pumps, fire and emergency services, navigation equipment

Modern PMS also uses heavy consumer start blocking. A large motor such as a bow thruster, cargo pump or ballast pump cannot start until enough spare capacity is online. The PMS may automatically start and synchronise another generator before releasing the start request.

Worked scenario: two generators in parallel, one loses fuel

Two diesel generators are sharing load during river passage. No. 1 suffers fuel starvation because its filter is choking.

  • No. 1's output falls. No. 2 picks up the load, and No. 1 begins to be driven as a motor by No. 2.
  • No. 1's reverse power protection trips its breaker. This is correct, because a motoring generator is a load, not a source.
  • No. 2 is now carrying the entire ship's load and goes into overload.
  • The preferential trip sheds the first group of non-essential loads, then the next if needed. No. 2 stays below its own overcurrent trip setting.
  • The PMS, or the watchkeeper, starts the standby generator, synchronises it and shares load. The engineer then finds and fixes the cause on No. 1.

Now imagine the preferential trip relay has drifted or its timer has failed. No. 2 trips on overcurrent and the ship blacks out in a river. Testing this relay matters for exactly that reason.

Blackout recovery: the sequence

If the main switchboard goes dead, the emergency generator should start automatically and supply the emergency switchboard. SOLAS sets a maximum of 45 seconds for it to start and connect. On ships designed for unattended machinery spaces, a standby main generator should also start automatically and connect to the dead bus.

The order below is the general practice. Your ship's blackout procedure in the SMS takes precedence.

  1. Communicate. Inform the bridge immediately; they will already have alarms. Call the chief engineer and any extra hands. The bridge needs to know whether to expect propulsion back in a few minutes or longer.
  2. Confirm emergency power. Check that the emergency generator is running, the emergency switchboard is live and emergency lighting is on. On many ships a steering pump is fed from the emergency switchboard, but confirm your own ship's arrangement.
  3. Check the standby generator. If it auto-started and closed, the main bus is live. If it did not, find out why before forcing it on.
  4. Find why the breaker opened. Read the protection indications: overcurrent, short circuit, reverse power, under-voltage, or prime mover shutdown (low lube oil pressure, high cooling water temperature, overspeed). Do not repeatedly close onto a short circuit.
  5. Start a generator if needed. Starting air is precious because the compressors are dead. Use it carefully, and know where the emergency compressor or emergency-fed compressor is.
  6. Close onto the dead bus. The generator breaker is normally allowed to close onto a dead bus without synchronising.
  7. Let the sequential restart work. Confirm that steering gear, main engine lube oil, cooling water, fuel supply and booster pumps, and auxiliary blowers come back in order. Manually start anything that did not.
  8. Restore the emergency switchboard feed. Once the main bus feeds the emergency switchboard again, the emergency generator comes off load. On most ships it is interlocked so that it is not paralleled with the main generators in normal service. Let it run off-load to cool down, stop it, and return it to automatic.
  9. Bring a second generator online. Synchronise and share load before reconnecting heavy consumers.
  10. Restore non-essential loads gradually. Reset the preferential trip groups one at a time.
  11. Main engine. Restart only when lube oil, cooling water and fuel conditions are normal. On heavy fuel oil, check viscosity and temperature, because the fuel will have cooled. Then hand control back with the bridge's agreement.
  12. Record and investigate. Make the log book entries and report through the SMS. Investigate the root cause. Local or port rules may require loss of propulsion to be reported; check with the master.

Checks, testing and maintenance

Under the ISM Code, maintenance and testing of critical equipment are part of the SMS. Blackout protection is about as critical as it gets.

  • Preferential trip test. Follow the PMS task and the maker's method. This might be secondary injection to the relay, a test facility in the switchboard, or a PMS simulation. Confirm that each stage trips the correct consumers after the correct delay, and that reset works.
  • Standby generator auto-start. Test it to confirm it starts and closes onto a dead bus or on the PMS start command. This test is usually done in port and under strict control.
  • Emergency generator. Test it at the interval set by your SMS, including automatic starting. SOLAS requires that the complete emergency system can be periodically tested. Check its starting batteries or alternative starting means, fuel level, and that the selector is in auto after the test. Leaving it in manual is a classic audit and port state control finding.
  • Starting air. Check bottle pressures, the condition of the drains, and the emergency compressor.
  • Breakers. Check spring charging, under-voltage release, trip indication reset and racking position. Ordinary maintenance should not leave a breaker in the test position.
  • Sequential restart. Confirm that the timers and auto-restart selections on essential motor starters are set as designed.
  • Fuel system. Many blackouts start with fuel: changeover errors, choked filters, water in fuel, or purifier upsets. Keep a careful watch on these, especially during ECA changeovers.

Common faults and symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Overload trips generator without shedding Relay setting drift, failed timer, CT or wiring fault, preferential trip disabled after maintenance
Nuisance shedding at normal loads Setting too low, faulty CT, load sharing imbalance putting one set into overload
Standby generator does not start Low starting air, mode not in auto, shutdown not reset, start interlock active, fuel valve closed
Generator starts but breaker won't close Spring not charged, under-voltage coil fault, trip not reset, breaker not fully racked in
Breaker closes then trips again Original fault still present (short circuit, a large motor restarting together with others), or generator under-voltage
Emergency generator fails Flat batteries, left in manual, fuel shut, low coolant or heater fault in cold climates
Repeated blackouts during manoeuvring Single generator running with thruster use, heavy consumer blocking bypassed

The simplest prevention is often operational. Run enough generators for manoeuvring, pilotage, narrow channels and heavy weather, as your SMS requires.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

  • What is a preferential trip and why is it fitted? It sheds non-essential loads in stages on generator overload, so the generator is not tripped and essential services are kept.
  • How is it set relative to the generator overcurrent trip? Lower in current, with a short time delay, so shedding always happens first.
  • Name loads that are shed and loads that are never shed. Shed: galley, laundry, accommodation air conditioning. Never shed: steering gear, main engine essential pumps, fire and emergency services.
  • Why is reverse power protection needed, and how does it link to preferential tripping? A motoring generator loads the healthy one. Once it trips out, the survivor may be overloaded, and the preferential trip protects it.
  • Describe your actions on blackout. Give the sequence in order: bridge, emergency power, cause, generator onto dead bus, sequential restart, emergency generator back to auto, restore loads, main engine, records.
  • What is sequential restart? Timed restarting of essential motors after power returns, so their starting currents do not all hit the generator together.
  • What is the SOLAS time for the emergency generator to take load? Not more than 45 seconds.
  • How do you test the preferential trip? Explain the method in your ship's PMS. Examiners want to hear about the test and confirming that the correct loads trip, not a memorised number.

Examiners expect you to talk through your actual ship. Know where the relay is, what each stage sheds and where the emergency compressor is.

Key points
  • A preferential trip sheds non-essential loads in stages so an overloaded generator stays on the board.
  • It is set below the generator overcurrent trip, with time delays; exact settings come from ship drawings and maker data.
  • Reverse power trips the faulty generator, and the preferential trip then protects the remaining one from overload.
  • SOLAS requires the emergency generator to start and connect within 45 seconds.
  • On blackout, find why the breaker opened before reclosing, and conserve starting air.
  • Sequential restart brings essential motors back one after another to avoid overloading the generator.
  • After recovery, return the emergency generator to auto, reset shed loads gradually, record the event and investigate the root cause.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a preferential trip and an overcurrent trip?

The overcurrent trip protects the generator by opening its breaker, which removes power from everything on that bus. The preferential trip acts first, at a lower setting. It disconnects only non-essential consumers so the generator breaker never needs to open.

Which loads are on the preferential trip?

Typically galley equipment, laundry, accommodation air conditioning and workshop supplies come off first, with other non-essential services in later stages. Essential services such as steering gear and main engine pumps are never shed. The exact allocation is shown in your ship's electrical drawings.

What is the first thing to do in a blackout?

Inform the bridge and call for assistance. Then confirm that the emergency generator has taken the emergency switchboard. After that, check why power was lost before restarting or reclosing a main generator, and follow your ship's blackout procedure.

Why shouldn't the emergency generator be left in manual after testing?

In manual it will not start automatically on a blackout. The emergency switchboard, emergency lighting and any steering or fire services it feeds would then stay dead. This is a frequent audit and port state control finding.

How often is the preferential trip tested?

The interval is set by your company's planned maintenance system and class requirements, not by a single universal figure. Testing should confirm that each stage trips the correct loads after the correct delay and that reset works.

Test yourself

What is the main purpose of a preferential trip?

Preferential tripping sheds non-essential consumers to keep power to propulsion, steering and safety services, the outcome SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires.

What is the maximum time SOLAS allows for the emergency generator to start automatically and connect?

SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires the emergency generating set to start automatically and supply the emergency switchboard within 45 seconds.

Which consumer would normally be shed by the preferential trip?

Hotel loads are non-essential. Steering, main engine lube oil and fire detection are essential services that load-shedding must protect under SOLAS Chapter II-1.

Two generators are in parallel and one suffers fuel starvation. Which protection should open its breaker?

A generator losing its prime mover is driven as a motor by the healthy set, and reverse power protection disconnects it. Class rules require this protection for generators running in parallel.

Why are essential motors restarted sequentially after a blackout?

Starting currents are several times running current, so restarting motors together could trip the restored generator. Sequential restart is standard design practice and, for ships with periodically unattended machinery spaces, is addressed in SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part E.
