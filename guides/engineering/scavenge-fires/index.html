Scavenge Fires: Causes, Detection, Action and Prevention

Quick answer

A scavenge fire is a fire in the scavenge air space of a two-stroke crosshead engine. It happens when oil residue that has built up there is ignited by hot gas or sparks blowing past the piston rings. Small fires are handled by reducing load, cutting fuel to the unit and increasing cylinder lubrication. Large fires mean stopping the engine and using the fixed extinguishing system, following the engine maker's instructions.

What a scavenge fire is and why it matters

On a slow-speed two-stroke crosshead engine, the space under the piston is separated from the crankcase by the diaphragm and the piston rod stuffing box. Scavenge air from the receiver passes through this space and enters the cylinder through the liner ports when the piston uncovers them.

This space slowly collects cylinder oil, unburnt fuel, carbon and sludge. If enough builds up and a source of ignition arrives, you get a scavenge fire. The fire triangle is simple:

  • Fuel: oily residue on the scavenge box floor, piston underside, piston rod and liner ports
  • Oxygen: a constant supply of pressurised scavenge air
  • Ignition: hot combustion gas and burning particles blowing past leaking piston rings

The direct damage can be serious enough. Heat can distort the liner, piston rod, stuffing box and the diaphragm. The bigger danger is escalation. A fire that heats the stuffing box or the diaphragm can create a hot spot on the crankcase side, and a hot spot is a classic trigger for a crankcase explosion.

Trunk-piston four-stroke engines do not have a separate scavenge space, so they do not get scavenge fires in this sense. Blow-by on those engines goes straight into the crankcase.

Root causes: how oil gets there and what lights it

Examiners want both halves of the cause: where the combustible material comes from and what ignites it.

Sources of combustible residue - Excessive cylinder oil feed rate, or a feed rate that doesn't suit the fuel's sulphur content and the oil's BN, so surplus oil drains down - Poor combustion from faulty or dribbling injectors, wrong fuel temperature or viscosity, poor fuel quality, or overload. This leaves unburnt fuel and carbon on the liner - Worn liners, or worn, stuck, broken or collapsed rings that let oil and combustion products past - Leaking piston rod stuffing box, letting system oil up from the crankcase side (worn scraper rings, broken springs) - Blocked scavenge drains, so residue accumulates instead of being removed

Sources of ignition - Blow-by of hot gas past the piston rings. This is the main one - Rings that are stuck in their grooves or broken, or a liner with ovality or scuffing - Afterburning or late combustion, which puts hot gas near the ports as they open - Low scavenge air pressure from a fouled turbocharger, dirty air cooler or failing auxiliary blower. This lowers the pressure margin and worsens blow-by

In practice, most fires come down to blow-by plus neglected drains.

Detection: symptoms you will see on watch

Modern engines have temperature sensors in each unit's scavenge space, linked to an alarm and often to a slowdown. Do not rely on the alarm alone. Know the other signs:

  • Scavenge space temperature high alarm on one unit
  • Rising exhaust gas temperature on the affected cylinder. A fire in the scavenge space heats the air going into that cylinder
  • Drop in engine speed or power, and irregular running
  • Turbocharger surging on some engines, from disturbed air flow
  • Sparks, smoke or burning oil from the scavenge drain cocks of that unit
  • Paint blistering or discolouration on the scavenge doors or receiver near the unit
  • Heavier smoke at the funnel

If you can't tell which unit is affected, compare the exhaust and scavenge temperatures, and check the scavenge doors for paint discolouration or heat from a safe position, using an infrared thermometer if one is available. Do not touch them by hand. Do not open any door or cover to look inside. That admits air and can expose you to flame.

Immediate actions: small fire vs large fire

Your first reference is always the engine maker's instruction manual and your company SMS/emergency procedures. The sequence below is the generally accepted practice tested at orals.

Small fire (contained, engine running steadily) 1. Inform the bridge and the Chief Engineer, then call extra hands as per the SMS. 2. Reduce engine load and speed as the maker recommends, usually to slow or the minimum safe revolutions agreed with the bridge. 3. Cut fuel to the affected unit. On older engines you lift or cut out that fuel pump. On electronically controlled engines you cancel injection on the unit through the control system. 4. Increase cylinder lubrication to the affected unit. This helps the rings seal and protects the liner while it is hot. 5. Keep the scavenge drain cocks on that unit closed, so sparks and burning oil are not blown into the engine room. 6. Monitor scavenge and exhaust temperatures. A small fire usually burns itself out once its fuel is used up. Restore load gradually only when temperatures are back to normal and the maker's conditions are met.

Large fire (spreading, temperatures still rising, or more than one unit) 1. Inform the bridge. Stop the engine, with the Master's agreement where the situation allows. 2. Shut off fuel. Stop the auxiliary blowers if the maker's procedure says so, to reduce air supply. 3. Engage the turning gear and keep turning the engine as the maker directs. This helps prevent seizure and distortion while the engine cools. 4. Release the fixed scavenge fire-extinguishing medium into the affected space. Depending on the design this may be steam, CO2, water mist or another medium. Use only the medium and method your engine is designed for. Do not improvise. 5. Boundary cool the area around the scavenge box if required, and keep the fire party ready. 6. Watch the oil mist detector and crankcase temperatures closely, because of the risk of a crankcase explosion. 7. Do not open scavenge doors or crankcase doors until the engine has cooled for the period the maker specifies. Fresh air on hot residue can cause re-ignition. Opening a hot crankcase can trigger an explosion.

During any scavenge fire, keep people clear of the scavenge doors and the scavenge space relief valves. These valves can lift and throw out flame.

Worked scenario: alarm at 0300

You are 4th Engineer on a UMS ship. The scavenge temperature high alarm sounds for unit No. 4, and the exhaust temperature on No. 4 is climbing while the other units are steady. Speed has dropped slightly.

  • You go to the ECR and call the bridge: suspected scavenge fire on No. 4, request reduction to slow. You call the Chief Engineer as per the standing orders.
  • With the bridge's agreement, speed comes down. You cancel fuel to No. 4 and increase its cylinder lubrication.
  • The drain cocks on No. 4 stay shut. You check the scavenge doors from a safe position and see blistered paint, but no flame outside.
  • After some time, the No. 4 scavenge temperature starts falling. The Chief keeps the engine at reduced load and plans an inspection at the next opportunity.
  • If the temperature had kept rising or spread to the neighbouring units, the next step would be to stop the engine, engage the turning gear and use the fixed system.
  • Log the timeline, the alarms and the actions taken. Report as required by the SMS.

The point the examiner is looking for: you informed the bridge before changing speed. You also isolated the unit and did not open anything.

After the fire: inspection before restart

Once the engine is cool and the space is safe to open, carry out a thorough inspection. Follow the maker's checklist and the Chief Engineer's instructions:

  • Piston rings and piston crown: look for broken, stuck or collapsed rings and for burning
  • Liner: look for scuffing, cracks and distortion, and check that the ports are clear
  • Piston rod: check for discolouration from heat, surface damage and straightness concerns
  • Stuffing box: check the springs, which can lose tension when overheated, and the sealing and scraper rings
  • Diaphragm and scavenge box: look for heat damage and check the door gaskets
  • Scavenge space relief valves: check their condition and freedom of movement
  • Fire detection sensors and the extinguishing system: restore them to service and recharge or reinstate the medium
  • Tie rods: some makers call for a tension check after a severe fire, because heat can relax them. Follow your engine's instruction manual.
  • Clean out all residue. Prove the drains are clear.
  • Find and fix the root cause, such as an injector, the rings or the lube feed. If you restart without fixing it, you will have another fire.

Class and flag notification requirements after a significant engine casualty depend on the flag state, the classification society and the company SMS. Check them, don't assume.

Prevention: routine checks and maintenance

Most scavenge fires can be prevented with routine attention.

  • Drain the scavenge spaces regularly, as set by the SMS or the Chief Engineer's standing orders, often once per watch. Note how much comes out and what it looks like. More oil than usual, or black and carbon-laden drainings, point to trouble on that unit. A drain that stays dry when it shouldn't may be blocked.
  • Inspect through the scavenge ports whenever the opportunity arises in port. Check the ring condition, ring movement, liner surface, deposits and how clean the space is.
  • Clean the scavenge spaces and the underside of the pistons as per the PMS.
  • Set the cylinder oil feed rate to suit the engine maker's guidance, the fuel sulphur content and the oil's BN. Use scrape-down analysis where your company does it.
  • Maintain the injectors and fuel system: atomisation, timing, and correct fuel temperature and viscosity.
  • Keep the scavenge air system healthy: clean the turbocharger, keep the air coolers clean, and make sure the auxiliary blowers work.
  • Avoid prolonged overload and running with known bad combustion on a unit.
  • Test the scavenge temperature alarms and keep the fixed extinguishing system ready for use.

  • List five causes, and separate the sources of fuel from the sources of ignition.

  • Why must you not open the scavenge or crankcase doors soon after a fire?

Key points
  • A scavenge fire is oil residue in the scavenge space of a two-stroke crosshead engine, ignited by blow-by past the piston rings.
  • The main causes are leaking or stuck rings, excess cylinder oil, poor combustion, a leaking stuffing box and blocked drains.
  • Early signs are a scavenge temperature alarm, a rising exhaust temperature on one unit, power loss and sparks at the drains.
  • For a small fire: inform the bridge, reduce speed, cut fuel to the unit, increase its cylinder lube and keep its drains closed.
  • For a large fire: stop the engine, engage the turning gear and use the fixed system as the engine maker specifies.
  • Never open the scavenge or crankcase doors until the engine has cooled. There is a risk of re-ignition and crankcase explosion.
  • Regular draining, port inspections and good combustion prevent most scavenge fires.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main cause of a scavenge fire?

The usual cause is hot gas blowing past leaking, stuck or broken piston rings and igniting oil residue in the scavenge space. The residue builds up from excess cylinder oil, poor combustion or a leaking stuffing box. Blocked scavenge drains make it much worse.

Why do we increase cylinder lubrication during a scavenge fire?

Extra cylinder oil helps the rings seal against the liner, which reduces further blow-by. It also protects the hot liner and rings from scuffing and seizure. You do this together with cutting fuel to the affected unit, not instead of it.

Can a scavenge fire cause a crankcase explosion?

Yes. If the fire heats the piston rod, the stuffing box or the diaphragm, it can create a hot spot on the crankcase side. A hot spot can produce oil mist and then ignite it. This is why you watch the oil mist detector closely and do not open the crankcase doors while the engine is hot.

Which extinguishing medium is used for scavenge fires?

It depends on the engine design and the installation. Steam, CO2, water mist and other media are all used on different ships. Always use the fixed system and method in the engine maker's manual and your ship's procedures. Do not improvise with other media.

How often should scavenge drains be checked?

The interval is set by the company SMS, the PMS or the Chief Engineer's standing orders. Many ships check them each watch. Note both how much comes out and what it looks like, because a change on one unit is an early warning of ring or combustion trouble.

Test yourself

During a small scavenge fire, what should be done with the scavenge drain cocks of the affected unit?

Keeping the drains closed stops sparks and burning oil being blown into the engine room. This is standard practice in engine makers' instructions.

What is the most common source of ignition in a scavenge fire?

Hot gas and burning particles blowing past leaking or stuck rings ignite the residue. This is why ring condition is checked through the scavenge ports.

Why should the scavenge doors not be opened soon after a fire is extinguished?

Admitting air too early risks re-ignition and, on the crankcase side, explosion. Wait for the cooling time given in the engine maker's manual.

Which of these is a typical symptom of a scavenge fire in one unit?

Heated air entering the affected cylinder raises its exhaust temperature. Speed and power usually drop at the same time.

Why are scavenge fires not a feature of four-stroke trunk-piston engines?

Trunk-piston engines have no diaphragm or separate under-piston air space, so blow-by goes into the crankcase. A scavenge space is a feature of crosshead two-stroke engines.
