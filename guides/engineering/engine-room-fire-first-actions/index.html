Engine Room Fire: First Actions

Quick answer

In an engine room fire, raise the alarm, inform the bridge, and attack the fire with portable equipment only if it is small and you have a safe escape. If you cannot control it quickly, get out, isolate fuel and air from outside the space, boundary cool, and account for everyone. The Master decides on releasing the fixed system, and only after a confirmed headcount.

Why engine room fires need a drilled response

Most engine room fires start the same way. Fuel oil, lube oil or hydraulic oil escapes under pressure and finds a hot surface. Typical ignition points are the exhaust manifold, turbocharger casing, indicator cocks or an exposed boiler surface. The fire grows fast because there is plenty of fuel, plenty of forced ventilation and a large open volume.

SOLAS Chapter II-2 is built around stopping this sequence:

  • Reducing ignition risk: jacketed high-pressure fuel lines and insulation of hot surfaces (Reg. 4).
  • Controlling spread: remote stops and closing appliances (Reg. 5).
  • Detection: fire detection systems (Reg. 7).
  • Suppression: fire-fighting systems (Reg. 10).
  • Escape: means of escape and EEBDs (Reg. 13).

The watchkeeper's job in the first minutes is to use this equipment in the right order. Exact sequences, responsibilities and muster points are set by your ship's muster list, fire control plan and company SMS. Learn your own ship's versions, not a generic list.

The first minutes: step by step

The order below reflects common practice and what examiners expect. Your ship's procedures take precedence.

  1. Raise the alarm. Use the nearest manual call point or the general fire alarm. Inform the bridge immediately, stating the location, what is burning (if known) and whether anyone is injured or missing.
  2. Assess in seconds, not minutes. Ask yourself:
    • Is the fire small and localised?
    • Do you have a clear escape route behind you?
    • Is the right extinguisher at hand?
  3. Attack only if safe. A small oil fire can often be knocked down with a portable or semi-portable foam or dry powder extinguisher. If a local application (water-mist) system covers the area, it may be released. Keep low, keep the exit behind you, and never fight alone for long.
  4. Isolate the fuel if it is safe to do so. Stopping the leaking pump or closing the local valve removes the source. If you cannot reach it, the remote quick-closing valves and pump stops outside the space do the job.
  5. If it is not out quickly, get out. Close doors behind you. Use the nearest safe exit or the emergency escape trunk, and use an EEBD if smoke is present.
  6. Report to the muster point and to the officer in charge so the headcount can start.
  7. The fire team takes over. It works under the direction of the person designated on the muster list and fire control plan, usually the Chief Engineer for engine room fires, with the Master in overall command on the bridge.

The instinct to stay and fight is what kills engineers. Smoke from an oil fire reduces visibility and makes the atmosphere unbreathable within moments. A fire that is not controlled in the first attempt rarely will be with portables.

Cutting fuel and air: the isolation equipment

Once the space is evacuated, the aim is to starve the fire. The controls involved are usually grouped at a fire control station or another position outside the machinery space.

Action Equipment Purpose
Shut fuel at source Remote quick-closing valves on fuel and lube oil tanks (pneumatic or wire-operated) Stops gravity or head feed from tanks into the fire
Stop pumps Remote stops for fuel transfer, booster, purifier and lube oil pumps Stops pressurised oil supply
Stop air Remote stops for engine room supply and exhaust fans Removes forced oxygen supply
Close openings Fire dampers, skylights, funnel flaps, doors, any other openings Seals the space so a fixed system can work
Contain Boundary cooling of adjacent decks, bulkheads and accommodation fronts Prevents spread by conduction to adjacent spaces

Practical points:

  • Know which valve feeds what. Shutting every QCV will stop the generators that draw from those tanks. The emergency generator should then start and power the emergency switchboard. Be ready for the blackout.
  • The emergency fire pump is located outside the main machinery space so it remains available when the main fire pumps are lost. Know how to start it from cold.
  • The bridge must handle the ship. Loss of propulsion and steering power affects navigation. The bridge team considers sea room, anchoring and displaying not-under-command signals.

Releasing the fixed system: the Master's decision

Fixed CO2 is the most common total-flooding system in category A machinery spaces. High-expansion foam, water-based systems and other approved systems are also fitted, depending on the ship. Whatever the medium, the logic before release is the same.

Before release:

  • Headcount complete. Everyone is accounted for. CO2 at extinguishing concentration is lethal.
  • Space sealed. Ventilation is stopped, dampers and openings are closed, and fuel is isolated. If the space is not sealed, the gas escapes and the fire continues.
  • Release authorised. Release is on the Master's order, or that of the person designated in the SMS.
  • Released as per the instructions posted at the release station. CO2 systems give an audible and visual pre-discharge warning in the protected space. Opening the release cabinet typically triggers the alarm and may stop fans automatically. Release generally involves separate controls for the main valve to the space and for the cylinders.

After release:

  • Keep boundary cooling going and monitor boundary temperatures.
  • Do not open up early. Hot surfaces can reignite fuel vapour as soon as oxygen returns.
  • The decision to re-enter is a command decision. It is normally taken only after a period of cooling, decided by the Master with the Chief Engineer, taking account of company guidance and boundary readings.
  • Re-entry is made by a BA-equipped team with charged hoses, by the route least likely to admit large volumes of air.
  • The space is ventilated and the atmosphere tested before anyone enters without breathing apparatus.

Local application water-mist systems are different. They protect specific high-risk areas such as purifiers, boiler fronts and generator engines. SOLAS II-2 Reg. 10.5.6 requires them in category A machinery spaces above 500 m³ on passenger ships of 500 GT and above and cargo ships of 2,000 GT and above. Because they are water-based, they can be activated without evacuating the whole machinery space, although personnel should clear the immediate protected area. They are a first-line tool, not a last resort.

Worked scenario: purifier room fire during UMS

Situation: At night the ship is on UMS. The duty engineer is called by a fire alarm from the purifier room's flame detector. On arrival, smoke is visible through the door glass and flames are near the fuel oil purifier.

  1. Before or on reaching the engine room, the duty engineer confirms the bridge knows. They report: fire in the purifier room, alone, no injuries yet.
  2. The engineer does not open the door wide into a well-developed fire. They check whether the local water-mist section for the purifier area has activated. If it has not, they release it manually.
  3. The engineer stops the purifiers and their fuel supply using the remote stops and quick-closing valve controls outside the purifier room.
  4. Fire knocked down? Keep watch for reignition and report. Keep the space closed and have the fire party ready before anyone opens up to inspect.
  5. Fire still growing? Leave the space and report to the muster point. The Chief Engineer and fire team take over. If the fire spreads beyond the purifier room, the full sequence follows:
    • QCVs and remote stops operated.
    • Ventilation stopped and the space sealed.
    • Headcount taken.
    • Fixed system considered on the Master's order.

Examiner's follow-up: "What probably caused it?" Good answers include:

  • A leaking sludge or feed line.
  • A failed purifier seal causing overflow.
  • Oil-soaked lagging.
  • A heater surface hot enough to ignite the fuel.

Checks, maintenance and typical faults

Fire drills are required by SOLAS III/19. Testing and maintenance follow the ship's PMS and IMO maintenance guidelines. Frequencies vary by flag and company, so follow your ship's schedule.

Items engineers own:

  • Quick-closing valves: tested from the remote position, then reset and confirmed open. Pneumatic QCV air reservoirs must be charged, and wire-operated QCVs need free-running wires.
  • Remote stops: for fans and oil pumps, tested to show the machinery actually stops.
  • Fire dampers and closing appliances: must move freely and seal, with correct position indicators.
  • Detectors: tested with the correct test equipment, and any isolated or faulty heads recorded.
  • Water-mist local application: pump, section valves and nozzles checked.
  • Fixed CO2: the routine checks the ship's crew can do, with periodic inspection by a competent person or approved service provider as required by the flag.
  • Emergency fire pump and emergency generator: started and run regularly, including on load where required.

Faults that turn a small fire into a big one:

  • A QCV seized open or its reset left half-closed.
  • A fire damper rusted open.
  • Fans that restart automatically after a blackout.
  • Missing spray shields or hot-surface insulation, and oil-soaked lagging.
  • Fire doors held open with wedges.
  • An emergency fire pump that will not prime.
  • Detector heads isolated during maintenance and never restored.
  • A crew that has never located the release station in the dark.

Symptoms that should prompt immediate action:

  • Smell of hot oil.
  • Oil mist haze.
  • Fuel pressure dropping without explanation.
  • Fuel dripping near the exhaust.
  • Wet or stained lagging.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: You discover a fire in the engine room. What do you do first? Raise the alarm and inform the bridge. Then assess whether it is small enough to tackle safely with the exit behind you. If not, evacuate, close doors and report for the headcount.

Q: Why must ventilation be stopped before releasing CO2? The fans would bring in oxygen and drive the gas out, so the extinguishing concentration would never be reached. The space must be sealed for total flooding to work.

Q: Who authorises CO2 release, and what must be confirmed first? The Master, or the person designated in the SMS. A complete headcount confirming nobody is in the space must come first, along with closure of openings and isolation of fuel.

Q: Where are QCVs operated from, and why? From a position outside the space. Reaching them must not depend on entering the fire area.

Q: Why not ventilate soon after the fire appears to be out? Hot steel and residual fuel vapour can reignite when oxygen returns. Cooling time and controlled re-entry by a BA team are needed.

Q: What is the difference between local application and total flooding? Local application, typically water mist, protects specific high-risk equipment and can operate without evacuating the whole space. Total flooding fills the whole space and needs it evacuated and sealed.

Examiners want the correct order, the reason behind each step, and familiarity with your own ship's equipment and fire control plan.

Key points
  • Alarm and bridge first; fight only a small fire with a safe escape behind you.
  • Most engine room fires are oil spraying or leaking onto hot surfaces such as exhausts and turbochargers.
  • Starve the fire from outside: quick-closing valves, pump stops, fan stops, dampers, openings.
  • Fixed system release only on the order of the Master (or the person designated in the SMS), after a full headcount and with the space sealed.
  • Do not reopen early; reignition from hot surfaces is a real risk, and re-entry is done with BA and charged hoses.
  • Expect a blackout and emergency generator start when fuel is isolated; know the emergency fire pump.
  • QCVs, remote stops and dampers only work if tested, reset and maintained under the PMS.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first thing to do if you find a fire in the engine room?

Raise the alarm and inform the bridge, giving the location and nature of the fire. Then decide quickly whether it is small enough to tackle with portable equipment while keeping a safe exit behind you. If not, evacuate and close the doors.

Who can order the release of CO2 into the engine room?

Normally the Master, or the person designated in the company SMS and muster list. Release follows confirmation that everyone is accounted for, ventilation is stopped and openings are closed. Follow the instructions posted at the release station.

What are quick-closing valves and when are they used?

They are valves on fuel and lube oil tank outlets that can be closed remotely from outside the space, pneumatically or by wire. They are used in a fire to stop oil from tanks feeding it. They must be tested and correctly reset under the planned maintenance system.

Can a water-mist system be used with people in the engine room?

Local application water-mist systems protecting equipment like purifiers, boiler fronts and generator engines can be activated without evacuating the whole machinery space, although personnel should clear the immediate protected area. Total-flooding CO2 is a different matter and requires full evacuation. Check your own system's operating instructions.

How long should you wait before re-entering after a CO2 release?

There is no single universal figure. The decision is taken by the Master with the Chief Engineer, based on boundary temperatures, cooling time and company guidance. Re-entry is by a BA team with charged hoses, and the atmosphere must be tested before anyone enters without BA.

Test yourself

Why must engine room ventilation fans be stopped before releasing a fixed CO2 system?

Total flooding only works in a sealed space; running fans supply oxygen and expel the gas (SOLAS II-2 Reg. 5 control of ventilation).

What must be confirmed before a total-flooding CO2 release into the engine room?

CO2 at extinguishing concentration is lethal, so all personnel must be accounted for first (FSS Code Ch. 5 pre-discharge alarm requirements; ship's muster list and SMS).

From where are fuel tank quick-closing valves designed to be operated during a fire?

Remote closure from outside the space means isolation does not depend on entering the fire area (SOLAS II-2 Reg. 4).

What is the most common cause of engine room fires?

Fuel, lube or hydraulic oil contacting exhaust and turbocharger surfaces is the classic cause, hence jacketed fuel lines and hot-surface insulation (SOLAS II-2 Reg. 4).

Why should the engine room not be opened up soon after the fire seems extinguished?

Premature ventilation risks reignition, so cooling time, boundary monitoring and controlled BA re-entry are needed (company SMS and fire-fighting procedures).
