Earth faults on ship's electrical systems

Quick answer

An earth fault is an unintended conducting path between a live conductor and the hull. Most LV ship systems are insulated (unearthed), so a single earth fault does not trip anything but leaves the plant one fault away from a short circuit. You find it with the earth lamps or insulation monitor, then by careful sectional isolation and megger testing under proper isolation.

Why earth faults matter: insulated versus earthed systems

An earth fault is any unintended conducting path between a live conductor and earth. On a steel ship, earth means the hull.

The effect of an earth fault depends on how the system neutral is arranged:

  • Insulated (unearthed) system. No point of the distribution system is deliberately connected to the hull. This is the usual arrangement for the main LV power system (typically 440 V or 380 V three-phase) and for the lighting system fed through transformers. A single earth fault gives very little fault current, so nothing trips and the supply to essential services carries on.
  • Earthed neutral system. The neutral is connected to the hull, either solidly or through an impedance. A phase-to-earth fault is effectively a short circuit, so protection trips the faulty circuit. Fault location is immediate, but the consumer is lost.
  • HV systems. Many HV installations (for example 6.6 kV) earth the generator neutral through a resistor. This limits earth fault current to a controlled value so damage is reduced and protection can detect the fault. Whether an earth fault on HV gives alarm only or trips depends on the design and class approval.

Ships favour the insulated LV system because continuity of supply to steering gear, lubricating oil pumps, cooling pumps and navigation equipment matters more than instant fault clearance.

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 45 requires a device that continuously monitors insulation level to earth on any distribution system with no connection to earth. It must give an audible or visual indication when insulation values fall abnormally low. The same regulation also restricts hull-return systems and, on tankers, earthed distribution systems, with limited exceptions. Detailed requirements come from class rules and the flag administration, so check your own ship's documentation.

How earth faults are detected: earth lamps and insulation monitors

Earth lamps (three-phase AC). Three lamps are connected in star across the three phases, with the star point connected to the hull. Each lamp must be rated for line voltage.

Condition What you see
Healthy system All three lamps glow equally, at reduced brightness
Partial earth on one phase That lamp is dimmer and the other two are brighter
Dead (solid) earth on one phase That lamp goes out and the other two glow at full brightness

The faulty phase is the dim or dark lamp, because that phase has been pulled towards hull potential.

Earth lamps (DC). Two lamps are connected in series across the supply, with the midpoint earthed. If one pole goes to earth, the lamp on that side goes dim or out and the other brightens.

Insulation monitoring device (IMD) or earth fault monitor. This is now standard on switchboards. It applies a measuring signal between the system and the hull and measures the resulting current. Depending on the design, the signal may be DC, pulsed or low-frequency AC. Pulsed or AC methods are common on systems with converters or VFDs, where plain DC injection can give false readings. From the measured current the IMD displays insulation resistance to earth and raises an alarm below a set value. The alarm set point is fixed by the maker, class or company, so learn the value on your own ship rather than relying on a figure from a textbook.

Some modern installations also have insulation fault location systems. These use current sensors on each outgoing feeder to show which circuit is leaking, which saves a lot of switching.

Each electrically separate system needs its own monitoring, because the transformer isolates them. The main 440 V board, the 230 V lighting sections, the emergency switchboard and DC systems are each monitored separately. An alarm on one tells you nothing about the others.

First fault, second fault: what actually happens

A single earth fault on an insulated system is a warning, not a breakdown. It is still serious.

  • First fault. The faulty phase drops towards hull potential. The two healthy phases rise towards full line voltage relative to the hull. Their insulation is now stressed beyond its normal working voltage to earth, which can push a weak spot elsewhere into failure.
  • Second fault on a different phase. The two faults together form a phase-to-phase short circuit through the hull. Very high current flows, and breakers trip. If those breakers feed essential consumers, or the fault is near the main bus, you can lose important services or black out.
  • Shock risk. An insulated system is not shock-proof. The cables and equipment have capacitance to earth, so a person touching a live conductor can still receive a current through their body to the hull. If one phase is already earthed, touching another phase puts you across full line voltage.
  • Fire risk. Leakage paths through carbonised insulation or wet terminal boxes can heat up, track and arc over time.

The rule on board is simple: trace and clear an earth fault as soon as it is safe to do so. Do not live with a low reading for weeks because "nothing has tripped".

Finding an earth fault step by step: a worked scenario

Scenario: It is 0200 in UMS mode at sea. The duty engineer is called by a 440 V insulation low alarm. The main switchboard IMD reading is falling, and the earth lamps show one phase slightly dim.

  1. Confirm and record. Check the IMD reading and the earth lamps. Note the phase, the time, and whether the reading is steady, falling or intermittent. Intermittent readings often follow rain, deck wash, or equipment cycling on and off.
  2. Think before switching. Ask what has recently changed. Did a pump just start? Has it started raining? Has deck lighting just come on? Was a reefer just plugged in? Often the answer points straight at the culprit.
  3. Inform. Tell the bridge before you interrupt anything that affects them. Follow your company SMS on calling the 2nd Engineer, the ETO or the Chief Engineer. Many companies require the Chief Engineer to be informed before essential feeders are switched.
  4. Sectional isolation, non-essential first. Open outgoing feeders one at a time and watch the IMD after each.
    • Start with non-essential consumers such as galley, laundry, accommodation and workshop.
    • Move to deck consumers next.
    • Leave essential services until last. Only switch these with the standby running and the bridge agreed.
    • When the reading recovers, you have found the faulty feeder.
    • Close each healthy feeder again before opening the next, unless your procedure says otherwise.
  5. Follow the feeder down. Repeat the same process at the section board or distribution board until you reach a single consumer or cable run.
  6. Isolate and prove dead. Apply lock-out tag-out and prove the circuit dead with an approved tester. Then megger the consumer and its cable separately to find out which one is faulty.
  7. Rectify. Typical repairs are drying out a motor, replacing a flooded fitting, or remaking a chafed cable. Megger again to confirm the repair before you re-energise.
  8. Log it. Record the fault in the engine log or electrical defect record and the PMS. Include the readings, cause and action taken.

If you cannot isolate an essential consumer safely at sea, report it and monitor it. Plan the repair for when the standby unit can take over, or for port.

Common causes and where to look first

Experience shows most earth faults come from a short list of places:

  • Water and moisture. These are the most common causes:
    • deck and floodlight fittings
    • cargo hold lighting
    • junction boxes on open deck after heavy weather or wash-down
    • mooring winch motors and their terminal boxes
  • Reefer containers and plugs on container ships. Damaged plugs, wet sockets and faulty units are frequent sources, especially in rain.
  • Motors idle for long periods that have absorbed moisture. Check that anti-condensation (space) heaters are working.
  • Heating elements, such as galley ranges, calorifiers and tank heaters, where sheath breakdown is common.
  • Cable damage, including:
    • chafing at penetrations and on moving equipment
    • crushing during cargo work
    • insulation degraded by oil or heat near exhausts and boilers
  • Contamination from salt, carbon dust or oil mist on terminals and inside switchgear, creating tracking paths.
  • Recent work. Check any equipment that has just been opened up, refitted or had glands disturbed.

On the lighting (230 V) system, check outdoor and wet-area fittings first, then galley and laundry equipment.

Checks, maintenance and safety

Routine checks

  • Read and log the IMD for each system at watch handover or daily rounds, as your SMS requires. A slow downward trend is an early warning.
  • Use the earth lamp test facility, and test the IMD alarm function at the intervals set in the PMS.
  • Megger motors, generators and cables at PMS intervals and keep the records. The trend in readings tells you more than any single value.
  • Inspect deck fittings, cable glands and junction box seals, especially after heavy weather.
  • Check that space heaters are working on standby motors and alternators.

Megger testing points

  • Isolate the circuit, prove it dead and apply locks and tags before you test.
  • Disconnect or protect electronics, including AVRs, drives, sensors and electronic controllers. The test voltage can damage them.
  • Use the test voltage given by the maker or your company procedure for the equipment's rated voltage.
  • Discharge the circuit after testing. Long cables and windings store charge.
  • Acceptable minimum values come from the maker, class or company. Do not quote a universal number.

Safety

  • Never assume an insulated system cannot give you a shock.
  • Work on HV equipment requires HV-trained personnel and an HV permit. The circuit must be isolated, proved dead and earthed down before anyone touches conductors.
  • Do not switch off steering gear, navigation equipment or essential engine auxiliaries without the bridge and senior engineer agreeing, and without the standby running.
  • If an earth fault is accompanied by a burning smell, heat or smoke, treat it as a possible fire first and a fault-finding job second.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners like to test understanding rather than definitions. Typical questions with outline answers:

  • Why are ship LV systems usually insulated? For continuity of supply. A single earth fault does not trip essential services.
  • What is the disadvantage? Faults can go unnoticed without monitoring. Healthy phases are stressed towards line voltage to earth, and a second fault on another phase causes a short circuit and trips.
  • Explain earth lamps. Three lamps are star-connected with the star point to the hull. The dim or dark lamp shows the faulty phase, and the other two brighten.
  • How does an insulation monitor work? It applies a measuring signal between the system and the hull. The signal is DC, pulsed or low-frequency AC depending on design. It measures the resulting current and displays insulation resistance, with an alarm.
  • How do you locate an earth fault at sea? Inform the bridge and seniors. Carry out sectional isolation, non-essential first, while watching the IMD. Follow the feeder down to the consumer. Then isolate with LOTO and megger.
  • Why earth an HV neutral through a resistor? To limit earth fault current, reduce damage and transient overvoltages, and still allow the fault to be detected.
  • Is an insulated system safe to touch? No. System capacitance to earth allows current to flow through a body.
  • What precautions apply before meggering? Isolate, prove dead, protect electronics, use the correct test voltage, and discharge afterwards.

Always relate your answer to your own ship's board. Examiners will ask where the IMD is, what its set point is and what you did the last time it alarmed.

Key points
  • An earth fault is an unintended path from a live conductor to the hull.
  • Ship LV systems are usually insulated so that a single earth fault does not trip essential services.
  • SOLAS II-1/45 requires continuous insulation monitoring with an alarm on unearthed distribution systems.
  • On earth lamps, the dim or dark lamp shows the faulty phase and the others brighten.
  • A second earth fault on another phase produces a phase-to-phase short circuit through the hull.
  • Trace faults by sectional isolation, non-essential first, after informing the bridge and seniors, then megger under LOTO.
  • An insulated system is not shock-proof, and HV work needs trained personnel and a permit.

Frequently asked questions

Why doesn't a single earth fault trip the breaker on a ship?

On an insulated LV system there is no deliberate connection between the system and the hull. A single earth fault therefore has almost no return path and draws very little current, so overcurrent protection does not operate. This is deliberate, to keep essential services running, but it means the fault must be found using the earth lamps or insulation monitor.

Which earth lamp shows the faulty phase?

The faulty phase is the lamp that goes dim or out. That phase has been pulled towards hull potential, so its lamp sees little voltage. The other two lamps glow brighter because they now see close to full line voltage.

What is the minimum acceptable insulation resistance on board?

There is no single universal figure. Acceptable values and alarm set points depend on equipment rating, the maker's instructions, class rules and company procedures. Use the values in your ship's PMS and maker's manuals, and watch the trend over time rather than a single reading.

Can I switch off the steering gear feeder to find an earth fault?

Not without proper preparation. The bridge must agree, the standby steering motor or system must be running, and your company SMS normally requires senior engineer approval. Isolate non-essential feeders first. Essential circuits are usually handled last, or the repair is deferred to a safe opportunity.

Why is the HV generator neutral earthed through a resistor?

The resistor limits the current that flows during an earth fault. This reduces damage at the fault point and helps control overvoltages. Earth fault protection can still detect the fault, and depending on the design it will either alarm or trip.

Test yourself

What is the main reason most ship LV distribution systems are insulated from the hull?

An insulated system keeps running with one earth fault, which protects continuity of essential services. It is not shock-proof, and hull return is restricted (SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 45).

On three-phase earth lamps, one lamp is dark and the other two are bright. What does this indicate?

The earthed phase is pulled to hull potential, so its lamp goes out while the healthy phases' lamps see near line voltage. A healthy system shows equal glow on all three lamps.

With one phase already earthed on an insulated system, what happens if a second phase develops an earth fault?

Two earth faults on different phases connect those phases through the hull, which is a short circuit. This is why the first fault must be found and cleared promptly.

What is the correct first approach when tracing an earth fault at sea?

Sectional isolation starting with non-essential feeders finds the faulty circuit with the least risk to the ship. Meggering must only be done on isolated equipment, as required by the company SMS and safe working practice.

Why can a person still receive a shock on an insulated (unearthed) system with no earth fault present?

Cables and equipment have distributed capacitance to the hull, which lets current flow through a person who touches a live conductor. Treat every live conductor as dangerous and follow isolation procedures.
