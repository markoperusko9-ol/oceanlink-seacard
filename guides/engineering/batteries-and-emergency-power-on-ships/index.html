Batteries and emergency power on ships

Quick answer

SOLAS requires ships within its scope to have a self-contained emergency source of electrical power, usually an emergency generator, sometimes an accumulator battery. Where the regulations require it, batteries also act as a transitional source until the generator takes the load. Engineers must know the arrangement, the routine battery and charger checks, the common failure modes and battery room safety, because examiners test all of these.

Why emergency power exists and what it must supply

When the main source of power fails (blackout, main switchboard fire, flooded engine room), certain services must keep running so the ship can be navigated, people can get out, and distress can be raised. SOLAS Chapter II-1 covers this in Regulation 42 for passenger ships and Regulation 43 for cargo ships.

The key principles:

  • Self-contained. The emergency source, its transformers if any, the transitional source, the emergency switchboard and the emergency lighting switchboard sit above the uppermost continuous deck, outside machinery casings, aft of the collision bulkhead. They must be readily accessible from the open deck.
  • Independent of the main plant. A fire or casualty that knocks out the main source must not also knock out the emergency source.
  • Duration. The emergency source must carry the emergency load for a period set by ship type: 18 hours for cargo ships and 36 hours for passenger ships, with shorter periods allowed for some passenger ships on short voyages.

Typical emergency services include:

  • emergency lighting at muster and embarkation stations, alleyways, stairways, machinery spaces and control stations
  • navigation lights
  • radio installations
  • fire detection and general alarm
  • one fire pump where it depends on the emergency generator
  • steering gear, where the rules require it

The exact list depends on ship type, size and flag. Your ship's electrical load analysis and emergency switchboard drawings show what is actually fed.

How the system works, step by step

A typical cargo ship arrangement with an emergency generator works as follows. Arrangements vary by ship and class, so trace your own drawings.

  1. Normal operation. The emergency switchboard (ESB) is fed from the main switchboard (MSB) through an interconnector (tie) breaker. The emergency generator stands by. Battery chargers keep the emergency and transitional batteries on float charge.
  2. Main power fails. The ESB senses loss of voltage. The interconnector feed to the ESB is arranged to open automatically, so the emergency generator cannot try to back-feed the whole main plant.
  3. Transitional source carries the load. Where a transitional battery is fitted, it immediately feeds essential lighting, alarms, radio and similar loads, either directly or through a UPS or changeover.
  4. Emergency generator starts. It starts automatically (mandatory on passenger ships, and on cargo ships unless a transitional source is fitted) using its starting system: electric start from its own battery, hydraulic, or compressed air. It comes up to speed and voltage, and its breaker closes onto the ESB. On passenger ships the emergency loads must transfer within a maximum of 45 seconds; on cargo ships the 45-second figure applies where the transitional source is omitted.
  5. ESB supplies the emergency loads. These include emergency lighting, the steering gear supply where applicable, the emergency fire pump and others.
  6. Main power is restored. The engineers recover the main plant. The ESB is changed back to the MSB feed, normally by a manual or interlocked transfer. The emergency generator is then stopped and the system reset to automatic.

The transitional source requirement differs by ship type:

  • Passenger ships: the emergency generator must start automatically, and a transitional source is required regardless.
  • Cargo ships: a transitional source is required unless the emergency generator can start automatically and take the load within 45 seconds.

The transitional source must last at least 30 minutes.

Batteries on board: types, charging and where they are

Batteries turn up throughout the ship, not just in the battery room:

  • the emergency source itself, on some smaller ships where batteries replace the emergency generator
  • the transitional source for emergency lighting, alarms and communications
  • emergency generator starting batteries
  • GMDSS reserve source of energy (SOLAS Chapter IV, Regulation 13)
  • UPS units for bridge equipment, the engine control room and the alarm system
  • self-contained emergency light fittings and low-location lighting on passenger ships
  • lifeboat engine starting and lighting batteries
Type Electrolyte State-of-charge check Notes
Lead-acid, vented (flooded) Dilute sulphuric acid Specific gravity with a hydrometer, plus cell voltage Needs topping up with distilled water; gasses on charge
Lead-acid, valve-regulated (VRLA/sealed) Acid held in gel or glass mat Voltage and charger readings only No topping up; sensitive to overcharge and heat
Nickel-cadmium (vented) Potassium hydroxide (alkaline) Cell voltage under charge and discharge; SG does not show charge state Robust and tolerant of deep discharge; electrolyte topped up per maker

Charging modes. Most installations use a charger or rectifier with two modes:

  • Float: holds the battery fully charged while it supplies standing loads.
  • Boost (equalising): a higher-voltage charge used after a discharge or periodically, as the maker specifies. Boost charging produces more gassing, so ventilation matters most then.

The correct float and boost voltages per cell come from the battery and charger manufacturers. Use those values, not a figure from memory. They differ between lead-acid and Ni-Cd, and between makers.

Routine checks and maintenance

Intervals come from your planned maintenance system, maker's manual and company SMS. Typical tasks:

Emergency generator

  • Start and run it on the routine set by the PMS/SMS. Check that it comes on automatically and closes onto the ESB when tested.
  • Periodically run a full blackout or simulated failure test. SOLAS requires a means of periodically testing the complete emergency system, including the automatic starting arrangements.
  • Keep the fuel tank topped up and the quick-closing valve open and reset. Check lube oil, coolant, the coolant or crankcase heater, and air or hydraulic start pressure.
  • SOLAS requires stored starting energy for at least three consecutive automatic starts. It also requires a second source for three further starts within 30 minutes, unless manual starting is shown to be effective. Check both sources are charged and available.

Batteries (vented lead-acid or Ni-Cd)

  • Check electrolyte level and top up with distilled water only, to the maker's mark.
  • Take pilot-cell readings, then all-cell readings at intervals:
    • lead-acid: SG and voltage
    • Ni-Cd: voltage only
  • Record readings so trends show up.
  • Keep tops clean and dry. Tighten terminals and coat them with petroleum jelly or the maker's recommended compound.
  • Check vent plugs, flame-arrester caps and inter-cell connectors.

Chargers and distribution

  • Check the charger mode (float or boost), output voltage and current, and the alarms.
  • Monitor the DC earth-fault indicator and investigate any fault promptly.
  • Test the charger-failure alarm and the low-voltage alarm.

Battery room or locker

  • Confirm that ventilation works and openings are not blocked.
  • Check light fittings are of a safe type, there is no stowage of junk or combustibles, the eyewash is available, and PPE is in place.
  • Keep lead-acid and Ni-Cd batteries apart, with separate tools, hydrometers and topping-up equipment.

Faults, symptoms and likely causes

Symptom Likely causes
Emergency generator cranks but won't fire Quick-closing valve tripped, empty daily tank, air in fuel, choked filter, fuel rack or solenoid stuck, cold engine with failed heater
Generator won't crank Flat or failed starting battery, charger off, loose or corroded terminal, starter motor or solenoid fault, low air or hydraulic pressure
Generator runs but breaker won't close Voltage or frequency not established, AVR fault, breaker undervoltage coil or interlock, ESB still sensing a live feed
Low SG or voltage on one cell (lead-acid) Internal short, sulphation from long undercharge, low electrolyte, contamination
All cells low Charger failed or left off, excessive standing load, battery at end of life
Excessive gassing, heat or swelling Overcharge, charger stuck on boost, high ambient temperature. In VRLA batteries this can lead to thermal runaway
White or green crust on terminals Electrolyte spillage or creep causing corrosion; raises resistance and volt drop
DC earth-fault alarm Damp or damaged cable, water in a fitting, insulation breakdown. Trace it by isolating circuits one at a time, as the maker permits

Worked scenario. During a weekly test the emergency generator starts but trips within a minute.

  1. Check the trip indication first: overspeed, low lube oil pressure, high coolant temperature, or overcurrent.
  2. If it shows low LO pressure, check the sump level and sensor before restarting.
  3. If it shows overcurrent, check what the ESB is feeding. A large motor left on the ESB, or a fault downstream, can trip the generator.
  4. Fix the cause, retest, log it, and report to the Chief Engineer. A ship with an unreliable emergency source is a serious defect, and is likely to be raised as a deficiency at port State control.

Battery room safety

Vented batteries give off hydrogen and oxygen on charge, especially on boost or overcharge. Hydrogen is lighter than air and flammable over a wide range, so ventilation is arranged to clear it from the top of the space.

  • No smoking, naked lights or sparking tools. Use insulated spanners. Remove watches, rings and metal bracelets: a short across terminals can weld a tool and cause severe burns.
  • Switch the charger off and isolate the battery circuit before disconnecting cells. Never break a live connection near the cell vents.
  • Treat any hot work near the battery room as hot work under a permit, with gas testing as your SMS requires.
  • Wear eye protection, gloves and an apron when handling electrolyte. Know where the eyewash is. Flush eyes and skin with plenty of clean water and get medical advice.
  • Neutralising agents differ:
    • sodium bicarbonate solution for acid spills
    • boric acid solution, if supplied, for alkaline spills
  • Never mix the two chemistries. Acid in a Ni-Cd cell, or alkali in a lead-acid cell, ruins it. That is why tools and hydrometers are kept separate.
  • When diluting acid, add acid to water, never water to acid.

Oral exam: what the surveyor asks

Expect these. Answer from your own ship, then back it with the regulation.

  • Where is the emergency generator, and why there? Above the uppermost continuous deck, outside machinery casings, aft of the collision bulkhead, accessible from open deck. This keeps it clear of a machinery space casualty.
  • What does the emergency generator supply on your ship, and for how long? List the loads from the ESB. Duration is 18 hours for cargo ships and 36 hours for passenger ships (a shorter period may be accepted for passenger ships on short voyages).
  • What is a transitional source and when is it needed? A battery supply that bridges the gap until the generator takes over. It must last at least 30 minutes. It is always needed on passenger ships, and on cargo ships unless the generator starts and connects automatically within 45 seconds.
  • How does the ESB know the main power has failed? It senses loss of voltage on the main feed. The interconnector opens and the emergency generator starts.
  • How many starts must the starting system give? At least three consecutive automatic starts from stored energy, plus a second source for three more within 30 minutes unless manual starting is proven effective.
  • How do you check the state of charge of lead-acid and Ni-Cd cells? Lead-acid: SG and voltage. Ni-Cd: voltage only, because the SG hardly changes with charge.
  • What is the main hazard in a battery room? Hydrogen. Control it with ventilation, safe electrical fittings and no ignition sources.
  • The emergency generator won't start in a real blackout. What do you do? Try a manual start, then the alternative start source. Check fuel, the quick-closing valve and the battery. Keep the bridge and Chief Engineer informed, and recover the main plant in parallel.
Key points
  • SOLAS II-1 Regulations 42 (passenger) and 43 (cargo) require a self-contained emergency source above the uppermost continuous deck.
  • Emergency duration is 18 hours for cargo ships and 36 hours for passenger ships, with short-voyage exceptions for some passenger ships.
  • On passenger ships the emergency generator must start automatically and take the load within 45 seconds; on cargo ships a transitional source is required unless it does so. The transitional battery source lasts at least 30 minutes.
  • The starting system must give at least three consecutive starts, plus a second source for three more within 30 minutes unless manual starting is proven.
  • Check lead-acid cells by SG and voltage; check Ni-Cd cells by voltage only.
  • Hydrogen is the battery room hazard: keep ventilation working, use safe fittings and allow no ignition sources.
  • Keep acid and alkaline batteries, tools and hydrometers strictly separate.

Frequently asked questions

How often should the emergency generator be tested on a ship?

The test interval is set by your company SMS, PMS, class and flag, not by one universal SOLAS figure. Many companies run a routine start-and-run plus a periodic full automatic start on simulated blackout. SOLAS requires that the complete emergency system, including automatic starting, can be tested periodically. Always follow your ship's schedule and log every test.

Can batteries be the only emergency source of power on a ship?

Yes. SOLAS allows an accumulator battery as the emergency source instead of a generator. It must carry the emergency load for the full required period without recharging, while keeping voltage within the specified tolerance. In practice this is more common on smaller ships, and the arrangement must be approved by the flag administration or class.

Why can't you use a hydrometer to check a Ni-Cd battery's charge?

In nickel-cadmium cells the potassium hydroxide electrolyte does not take part in the reaction the way sulphuric acid does in lead-acid cells. Its specific gravity therefore stays roughly constant whatever the state of charge. Use cell voltages and the maker's charge and discharge guidance instead.

What is the difference between float and boost charging?

Float charging holds a fully charged battery at a steady voltage while it supplies standing loads. Boost (or equalising) charging uses a higher voltage to recharge after a discharge or to equalise cells. Boost causes more gassing and heating, so ventilation must be working. Use the battery maker's voltages for each mode.

What should an engine cadet learn first about emergency power?

Learn where the emergency generator, ESB, battery rooms and chargers are, and how to start the generator manually by every available means. Learn where the quick-closing valve is and how to reset it. Then trace the ESB loads on the drawings and join the weekly tests. This is core material for your training record book and for oral exams.

Test yourself

For how long must the emergency source of power on a cargo ship supply its required services?

SOLAS II-1 Regulation 43 sets 18 hours for cargo ships. 36 hours is the passenger ship figure under Regulation 42.

Which check shows the state of charge of a vented lead-acid cell?

In lead-acid cells the sulphuric acid SG falls as the cell discharges, so SG plus voltage gives the state of charge. This does not apply to Ni-Cd cells.

What is the main explosion hazard in a battery room?

Vented cells release hydrogen, especially on boost or overcharge, so ventilation and the exclusion of ignition sources are essential.

On a cargo ship, when is a transitional source of emergency power NOT required?

Under SOLAS II-1 Regulation 43, the transitional source can be omitted on cargo ships only if the emergency generator starts and connects automatically within 45 seconds. The GMDSS reserve source is a separate requirement.

Why are lead-acid and Ni-Cd batteries kept apart, with separate tools and hydrometers?

The electrolytes are acid and alkali, and cross-contamination damages the cells. Separation is standard maker and SMS practice.
