Insulation resistance testing (megger): a practical guide for marine engineers

Quick answer

An insulation resistance test applies a DC voltage between a conductor and earth, or between conductors, and reads the leakage resistance in megohms. It shows whether the insulation is in a fit condition before the equipment is energised or returned to service. On board you test with the equipment isolated, locked out and proved dead, with electronics disconnected. Judge the result against the maker's or class minimum and, above all, against the trend from previous readings.

What insulation resistance is and why ships test it

Every winding, cable and busbar is separated from earth and from the other phases by insulation. Varnish, mica, PVC, rubber and similar materials all leak a tiny current. Insulation resistance (IR) is the resistance of that leakage path, measured in megohms (MΩ).

Ships need regular IR checks for three main reasons:

  • Salt-laden, humid air and oil mist attack insulation faster than ashore. Deck and machinery-space equipment sees condensation every time it cools down.
  • Most ship LV systems are insulated (unearthed neutral) systems. A single earth fault usually does not trip anything. The system keeps running but now has an earth on one phase. A second earth fault on another phase becomes a phase-to-phase short. IR testing and the switchboard insulation monitor are how you catch the first fault early.
  • Planned maintenance and class. IR readings on motors, generators and important cables are normally part of the PMS. Surveyors and superintendents expect to see a record and a trend.

"Megger" is a trade name that has become the everyday word for the instrument and the test. Officially it is an insulation resistance tester.

How the instrument works

Older testers had a hand-cranked DC generator. Modern ones are battery-powered electronic units. Both work the same way:

  • They apply a DC test voltage, selectable on most instruments, between the two terminals.
  • They measure the current that flows and display resistance = test voltage ÷ current.

Why DC and not AC? With AC, the capacitive charging current of a long cable or a large winding would swamp the small leakage current you actually want to measure. With DC, the capacitive current dies away within seconds and the leakage current remains.

The current drawn during a test has three parts:

Component Behaviour
Capacitive charging current High at first, decays quickly
Absorption (polarisation) current Decays slowly over minutes as the insulation molecules polarise
Leakage (conduction + surface) current Roughly steady. This is what reveals moisture, dirt and damage

This is why the reading rises during the test and why you take it at a fixed time. Common practice is one minute, so readings stay comparable.

Why not use a multimeter? A multimeter's ohms range uses a few volts. That does not stress the insulation anywhere near operating conditions. Insulation can read "open circuit" on a multimeter yet break down at working voltage.

Test voltage. Pick it to suit the equipment's rated voltage, following the maker's instructions, company procedure and class guidance. Low-voltage ship equipment is commonly tested at 500 V DC. Higher-rated and high-voltage equipment uses higher test voltages. Never apply more than the maker allows.

Many testers also have a guard terminal. It diverts surface leakage, for example across a dirty terminal board, away from the measurement. This tells you whether a low reading is surface contamination or the insulation itself.

Step by step: meggering a three-phase motor

Worked scenario: an engine-room ventilation fan motor has been idle in port and you want to check it before starting. Follow your company's procedure. The outline below is the generally accepted sequence.

  1. Permission and permit. Inform the 2nd Engineer or Chief Engineer and the ETO if carried. Raise a permit or electrical isolation certificate as your SMS requires.
  2. Isolate. Open the breaker or starter at the MSB or group panel and rack it out or lock it. Fit your personal lock and tag. Remove fuses if that is the isolation method.
  3. Prove dead. Use an approved voltage indicator. Prove it on a known live source, test the circuit, then prove the indicator again (prove–test–prove).
  4. Disconnect sensitive items. Anything electronic must be removed or isolated from the test voltage: VFD output, soft starter, space-heater circuit if fed separately, PTC/thermistor leads, filter capacitors.
  5. Check the instrument. Short the leads together, which should read zero. Separate them, which should read infinity or maximum. Check the battery.
  6. Phase to earth. Connect the earth lead to clean bare metal on the motor frame. Test U, V and W to earth in turn, or all three linked together. Hold the test for the set time, usually one minute, and note the reading.
  7. Phase to phase. At the motor terminal box, with the star or delta links removed if the design allows, test U–V, V–W and W–U.
  8. Discharge. After each test, let the instrument discharge the winding or earth it through a suitable discharge lead before touching the conductors. Large windings and long cables hold a dangerous charge.
  9. Record. Log the date, equipment, test voltage, duration, each reading, and the winding or ambient temperature. Note humidity if known and whether the motor had been running or was cold.
  10. Restore. Refit links and connections. Remove your locks and tags only when everyone is clear. Close out the permit.

If the motor reads low, it was idle, and the anti-condensation heater was off, suspect moisture. Dry it out and retest before starting it.

Reading the result: minimums, trends, temperature and PI

There is no single universal pass mark. The minimum acceptable IR depends on the equipment's rated voltage and type, the maker's instructions, class rules and company procedures. Use the figure in your manual or PMS. Do not carry a number over from another ship or another machine.

What experienced engineers actually look at:

  • Trend over time. A motor that read steadily for years and has now dropped sharply is telling you something, even if it is still above the minimum. A steady downward trend means the insulation is deteriorating.
  • Balance between phases. One phase reading much lower than the other two points to a local fault: a damaged lead, a tracked terminal, or a damaged coil.
  • Temperature. IR falls significantly as temperature rises. A hot motor tested just after stopping will read lower than the same motor cold. Record the temperature and compare like with like. Some makers give a correction method to a reference temperature.
  • Humidity and surface condition. A damp morning or salt-encrusted terminals can pull readings down without the winding itself being faulty. The guard terminal helps separate the two.

Polarisation Index (PI) is the ratio of the 10-minute reading to the 1-minute reading. Clean, dry insulation keeps absorbing, so the reading keeps rising and the ratio is clearly above one. Wet or contaminated insulation is dominated by steady leakage, so the reading flattens early and the ratio stays near one. The Dielectric Absorption Ratio works on the same idea with shorter times. PI is especially useful on generators and larger motors because it depends less on temperature than a single spot reading. Acceptable PI values come from the maker or the applicable standard.

Causes of low IR and what to do about it

Cause Typical situation Usual remedy
Moisture / condensation Idle motors, deck machinery, cargo hold lighting, reefer sockets, after washing down Anti-condensation heaters on, dry out with heater, warm air or a maker-approved method, retest
Oil and carbon dust contamination Motors near purifiers, generators, compressors Clean with an approved solvent or method, dry, retest
Salt deposits Deck equipment, open terminal boxes, poor gland sealing Clean, dry, renew seals and glands
Thermal ageing Long service at high temperature, overload history Monitor the trend. Revarnish or rewind if it does not recover
Mechanical damage Chafed cables, crushed leads, damaged junction boxes Locate, repair or renew the section
Water ingress Flooded cable trays, failed deck penetrations, submersible pumps Find the source, dry or renew, then retest

If IR is low after cleaning and drying, consult the Chief Engineer and the maker. Running equipment with poor insulation risks an earth fault, a winding burnout, or a shock hazard on the frame.

Hunting an earth fault on the switchboard. The insulation monitor or earth lamps show a low reading on a bus. The usual method is to isolate outgoing circuits one at a time until the indication clears, then megger the suspect circuit. Agree this with the Chief Engineer first. Do not switch off steering gear, navigation equipment or other essential services without the bridge's agreement and an alternative in place.

Safety points you will be asked about

  • The tester generates a hazardous voltage. Do not touch the leads or the conductors under test while testing. Warn others working nearby.
  • Stored charge. Cables, windings and capacitors stay charged after the test. Always discharge, and give large equipment extra time.
  • Electronics. The test voltage can destroy AVRs, VFDs, PLC cards, sensors and electronic ballasts. Disconnect them or test only the parts the maker permits.
  • Isolation discipline. The same rules as any electrical work: permit, lock-out tag-out, and prove dead.
  • Hazardous areas. An insulation tester can produce a spark. On tankers, gas carriers and in other hazardous zones, follow the hot work or electrical work controls in your SMS. That may include gas-free confirmation and certified-safe instruments.
  • High voltage. HV systems need HV-trained personnel, the company's HV safety rules, and earthing procedures. Higher test voltages and much larger stored charges are involved. Do not attempt HV testing without that training and authorisation.
  • Instrument condition. Use leads with intact insulation and shrouded probes. Keep the tester within its calibration and check date where your company requires it.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

Q: Why is insulation tested with DC rather than AC? With AC, the capacitive charging current would mask the leakage current. With DC, the capacitive current decays quickly and the steady leakage current remains.

Q: Why does the reading increase during the test? The capacitive charging current and the absorption current both decay with time. Total current falls, so the indicated resistance rises.

Q: What is polarisation index and why is it useful? It is the 10-minute reading divided by the 1-minute reading. It shows whether the insulation is clean and dry or wet and contaminated. It depends less on temperature than a single spot reading.

Q: The motor reads low on all three phases after a long idle period. What do you do? Suspect moisture. Check the anti-condensation heater. Dry out using a maker-approved method, then retest and compare with previous records. Report to the Chief Engineer before running it.

Q: Why is a single earth fault not immediately dangerous on an insulated system, and why fix it anyway? There is no return path through earth, so little current flows and no protection trips. A second earth fault on another phase creates a short circuit. The first fault also raises the voltage of the healthy phases relative to earth.

Q: What must you remove before meggering a motor fed by a VFD? The drive itself must be disconnected from the motor cables, along with any electronic or sensor circuits connected to the winding.

Q: What do you record? Date, equipment, test voltage, duration, readings for each test, temperature, conditions, and who carried out the test.

Key points
  • An insulation resistance tester applies DC and measures leakage resistance in megohms; a multimeter cannot do this job.
  • Always permit, isolate, lock out, prove dead and disconnect electronics before testing.
  • Discharge cables and windings after every test, because they hold a dangerous charge.
  • Acceptance limits come from the maker, class and company procedure, not from memory or another ship.
  • The trend, phase balance and temperature-corrected comparison matter more than a single reading.
  • PI (10-minute ÷ 1-minute reading) distinguishes clean, dry insulation from wet or contaminated insulation.
  • On insulated ship systems the first earth fault does not trip, so finding it early prevents a later short circuit.

Frequently asked questions

What voltage should I use to megger a ship's motor?

Choose it to suit the equipment's rated voltage, following the maker's instructions and company procedure. Low-voltage ship equipment is commonly tested at 500 V DC, and higher-rated equipment uses higher test voltages. Never exceed what the maker allows, especially where electronics are involved.

What is the minimum acceptable insulation resistance on board?

There is no single universal figure. The minimum depends on the equipment's rating and type and on the maker's, class's and company's requirements. Use the value in your manual or PMS, and treat a sharp fall from previous readings as a warning even if the reading is still above the minimum.

Why does my megger reading change with temperature?

Insulation resistance falls markedly as temperature rises, so a hot motor reads lower than the same motor cold. Record the temperature with every reading and compare at similar conditions, or use the maker's correction method if one is given.

Can I megger a circuit with a VFD or AVR connected?

No, not unless the maker specifically permits it. The DC test voltage can destroy semiconductors and control cards. Disconnect the electronics and test only the motor, cables or windings.

How do I find an earth fault shown on the switchboard earth lamps?

With the Chief Engineer's agreement, isolate outgoing circuits one at a time until the indication clears. Then megger the suspect circuit section by section to locate the fault. Do not interrupt steering, navigation or other essential services without the bridge's agreement and an alternative arrangement.
