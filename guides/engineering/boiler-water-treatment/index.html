Boiler Water Treatment: A Practical Guide for Marine Engineers

Quick answer

Boiler water treatment keeps hardness, oxygen and dissolved solids under control so the boiler does not scale up, corrode or carry water over with the steam. On board this means clean distilled make-up, chemical dosing, regular sampling and testing against the supplier's control limits, and blowdown to remove sludge and excess solids. Limits vary with boiler type, pressure and the treatment programme, so always work to the maker's and chemical supplier's figures.

Why boiler water treatment matters

A boiler is a concentrator. Steam leaves pure, so anything that came in with the feed water stays behind and builds up. Untreated, that leads to four problems:

  • Scale. Hardness salts (mainly calcium and magnesium) come out of solution on hot heating surfaces. Even a thin layer insulates the tube, the metal runs hotter than designed, and you get bulging, overheating and tube failure.
  • Corrosion. Dissolved oxygen causes pitting, often around the feed inlet and in economiser tubes. Low pH (acidic water) causes general wastage. Carbon dioxide released in the boiler forms carbonic acid in the condensate and grooves the return lines.
  • Carryover, priming and foaming. High dissolved solids, oil or suspended matter make the water foam. Water then carries over with the steam, taking salts into steam lines, heaters, turbines and valves, and water hammer becomes possible.
  • Caustic attack. Too much free caustic can attack the steel. Free caustic that concentrates under deposits can cause local wastage (caustic gouging). Where it concentrates in stressed crevices or leaking joints it can cause intergranular cracking, known as caustic embrittlement.

The aim is not just "add chemicals". It is clean feed, the right chemistry and steady control, together with blowdown.

How the treatment works, step by step

1. Keep the feed clean. The best treatment starts outside the boiler. Make-up should normally be distillate from the fresh water generator, with the salinometer and dump valve working so salty water never reaches the feed tank. Take shore water only if the company allows it and it has been tested and approved. Contaminated condensate returns should be found and stopped. Do not just treat around them.

2. Remove oxygen. Oxygen is removed in two ways: - Mechanically. Keep the hot well or cascade tank hot. Higher-pressure plants use a deaerator. - Chemically. Dose an oxygen scavenger. Common types are sodium sulphite and hydrazine, and less toxic organic scavengers have replaced hydrazine in many treatment programmes.

3. Deal with hardness. Phosphate-based treatment reacts with calcium to form a soft sludge rather than hard scale. Magnesium comes out as a non-adherent sludge when the water is kept alkaline. Polymer or tannin-type sludge conditioners keep that sludge mobile so it settles in the lower drum and does not bake onto tubes.

4. Control alkalinity and pH. An alkaline boiler water slows corrosion and supports the hardness reactions. Alkalinity is normally built with caustic soda or a blended product. It must sit inside the window your supplier gives you. Too low invites corrosion. Too high risks caustic attack and foaming.

5. Protect the condensate system. Amines are dosed for this: - Neutralising amines travel with the steam and raise condensate pH. - Filming amines lay a protective film on the metal.

6. Remove what accumulates by blowdown. - Scum (surface) blowdown removes floating impurities and lowers dissolved solids at the water surface. - Bottom blowdown removes settled sludge from the lowest point.

On motor ships the exhaust gas economiser is usually in the same water circuit as the auxiliary boiler. It is treated by the same programme, but its tubes are often where oxygen pitting and deposits show first.

Routine tests and what each one tells you

Most ships use a test kit from the chemical supplier: titrations and colour comparators, sometimes with a conductivity or pH meter. Normal ranges depend on boiler pressure, type and treatment brand, so use the control chart supplied with your programme and the boiler maker's limits. Do not rely on numbers remembered from another ship.

Test What it indicates
P-alkalinity (phenolphthalein) and total/M-alkalinity (methyl orange) Alkaline reserve. Low means corrosion risk; high means foaming and caustic risk
pH Acidity or alkalinity of the boiler water or condensate
Chloride Salt ingress (seawater or bad make-up). Chloride is not removed by treatment, only by blowdown and fixing the leak
Phosphate Reserve of hardness-control chemical
Oxygen scavenger reserve (sulphite, hydrazine or alternative) Whether enough scavenger remains to deal with oxygen
Conductivity / TDS Total dissolved solids. High levels lead to priming and carryover
Hardness (feed/condensate) Whether hardness is entering the system

Good sampling practice - Draw boiler samples through the sample cooler, never as flashing hot water into an open bottle. - Flush the sample line before filling. - Use clean bottles rinsed with the sample. - Test promptly. Scavenger results in particular change once the sample is exposed to air. - Record the results in the boiler water log, note what you dosed and blew down, and send results ashore if your programme requires it.

Watch the trend, not just today's figure. A chloride reading that creeps up every day is telling you something even while it is still "in range".

Common faults, symptoms and first actions

Symptom Likely causes What to do
Chloride rising Seawater leak in condenser or drain cooler, FWG salinity alarm or dump valve fault, contaminated make-up tank Re-test to confirm. Check FWG salinometer and make-up tank. Trace coolers. Increase blowdown as directed. Inform the Chief Engineer
Oil film in observation tank or hot well Leaking steam heating coil in a fuel or lube oil tank, leaking heater Isolate returns one at a time to find the source. Keep oil out of the boiler. Follow the company or maker procedure if oil has already entered
Low alkalinity / pH Under-dosing, acidic contamination, heavy blowdown without re-dosing Adjust dosing to the supplier chart. Look for the contamination source
High alkalinity Over-dosing Reduce dosing and blow down as required
Low scavenger reserve Air ingress, cold hot well, under-dosing Check hot well temperature and vents. Adjust dosing
Wet steam, water hammer, fluctuating gauge glass Priming or foaming from high TDS, oil, high water level or sudden steam demand Reduce load, check the level, check TDS and oil, blow down as directed
Hardness detected Poor make-up quality, seawater ingress Same investigation as rising chloride

An oil film inside a boiler is serious. It insulates the heating surface just as scale does, and it promotes foaming. Treat oil found in the observation tank as an urgent fault, not a cleaning job.

Worked scenario: steadily rising chloride

You are 4th Engineer on a motor tanker. Over four days the auxiliary boiler chloride rises each morning while the other results stay normal.

  1. Confirm the reading. Take a fresh, properly cooled sample, re-test, and check that the reagents are in date.
  2. Check the make-up. Look at the FWG salinometer reading and test the alarm and dump valve as your procedure allows. Test the distilled water tank directly.
  3. Check heat exchangers with seawater on one side. Depending on plant design this may include the atmospheric condenser, drain cooler or dump condenser. Isolate and test according to the SMS.
  4. Control the boiler water meanwhile. Use increased blowdown to bring dissolved solids back toward the limit, and re-dose to restore chemical reserves lost with the blowdown.
  5. Report and record. Tell the 2nd Engineer and Chief Engineer, log every result and action, and inform the supplier if the programme requires it.

An examiner wants to hear that you would find and stop the source, not just keep blowing down.

Safety when handling treatment chemicals and blowdown

Chemical handling - Treatment chemicals include strong alkalis and, on some ships, hydrazine, which is toxic and classified as a carcinogen (may cause cancer). - Read the Safety Data Sheet before handling. - Wear the PPE it specifies: typically goggles or a face shield, chemical gloves and an apron. - Know where the eyewash is before you start. - Never mix chemicals in the same container. - Dose through the proper dosing pot or pump with the correct valve sequence. Do not open a pressurised dosing pot.

Blowdown - Blowdown releases very hot water under pressure. - Follow the valve sequence in your procedure and the maker's manual. - Watch the gauge glass throughout and never leave the valves open unattended. - Keep the water level within limits and never blow down below the safe level. - Check the overboard or blowdown tank arrangement before opening. - Some companies restrict when bottom blowdown may be done (for example, at reduced firing). Follow your SMS.

Treatment is part of planned maintenance and the safety management system. Records matter at audits and inspections as well as for the boiler.

Oral exam questions and model answers

  • Why treat boiler water at all? To prevent scale, corrosion, carryover and caustic attack, which all lead to tube failure, poor steam quality and damage downstream.
  • Where does chloride come from and why can't you treat it? From seawater ingress or poor make-up. It is a soluble salt that chemicals do not neutralise, so you remove it by blowdown and stop the leak.
  • Difference between scum and bottom blowdown? Scum blowdown removes floating impurities and dissolved solids near the water surface. Bottom blowdown removes settled sludge from the lowest point.
  • What does phosphate do? It reacts with calcium hardness to form a soft sludge instead of hard scale, which is then removed by bottom blowdown.
  • How is oxygen removed? By keeping the feed hot (and deaerating on higher-pressure plants) and by dosing an oxygen scavenger.
  • What is priming and how do you prevent it? Water carried over with the steam. Prevent it by controlling dissolved solids, keeping oil out, maintaining a correct water level and avoiding sudden load changes.
  • What would you do if you saw oil in the observation tank? Prevent it reaching the boiler, trace the source by isolating heating returns one by one, report it, and follow the procedure for cleaning or boiling out if contamination has occurred.

Examiners often ask you to describe how you actually took and tested a sample on your last ship, so be ready to explain it from experience.

Key points
  • Treatment prevents scale, corrosion, carryover and caustic attack. Scale and oil both insulate tubes and cause overheating.
  • Good feed comes first: distilled make-up, a working salinometer and dump valve, and contamination-free condensate.
  • Phosphate controls hardness, scavengers remove oxygen, alkalinity limits corrosion, and amines protect condensate lines.
  • Chloride is only removed by blowdown, and a rising trend means you must find the leak.
  • Use the supplier's control chart and the maker's limits, not figures remembered from another ship.
  • Sample through the cooler, test promptly and log every result, dose and blowdown.
  • Read the SDS and wear the correct PPE. Blowdown is hot, pressurised work that must never be left unattended.

Frequently asked questions

What are the normal boiler water test limits on a ship?

There is no single set of figures for all ships. Limits depend on boiler type and pressure and on the treatment programme in use. Work to the control chart from your chemical supplier and the boiler maker's instructions, and ask the Chief Engineer if they conflict.

Why does chloride keep rising in the boiler?

Salt is entering the system. Common sources are a seawater leak in a condenser or drain cooler, or poor make-up water from a faulty FWG salinity control. Blowdown controls the level temporarily, but the source must be traced and fixed.

How often should boiler water be tested?

Frequency is set by the company SMS, the chemical supplier and the boiler's duty. Many ships test daily, with extra tests after contamination, heavy blowdown or a dosing change. Follow your ship's procedure and record every test.

Is hydrazine still used on ships?

Some ships still use it as an oxygen scavenger. It is toxic and classified as a carcinogen (may cause cancer), so many programmes now use sodium sulphite or less hazardous organic scavengers instead. Whatever is on board, handle it according to its Safety Data Sheet.

What happens if oil gets into the boiler?

Oil forms an insulating film on heating surfaces, causing overheating, and it promotes foaming and carryover. Stop the source, keep further oil out and report it immediately. Cleaning or boiling out is done according to the maker's and company's procedure.

Test yourself

Which method actually removes chloride from boiler water?

Chloride is a soluble salt that treatment chemicals do not neutralise, so it can only be reduced by blowdown while the leak is found. This is standard practice under the boiler maker's and treatment supplier's instructions.

What is the main purpose of phosphate treatment in a boiler?

Phosphate reacts with calcium hardness to form a non-adherent sludge, which is removed by bottom blowdown.

Which chemical is mainly used to protect condensate return lines from carbonic acid corrosion?

Neutralising amines travel with the steam and raise condensate pH, countering carbonic acid. Sulphite is an oxygen scavenger and phosphate controls hardness inside the boiler.

What should you do first on finding an oil film in the observation tank?

Oil must be kept out of the boiler and its source, usually a leaking heating coil, must be found. SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires feed systems to guard against oil contamination as far as practicable.

Excessively high dissolved solids (TDS) in boiler water most directly lead to:

High dissolved solids make the water foam and carry over with the steam, which is why TDS is controlled by blowdown. Pitting comes from oxygen, and scale comes from hardness.
