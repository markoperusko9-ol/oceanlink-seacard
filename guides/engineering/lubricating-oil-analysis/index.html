Lubricating Oil Analysis: A Practical Guide for Marine Engineers

Quick answer

Lubricating oil analysis is condition monitoring done through the oil. It tracks the oil's own health (viscosity, BN, oxidation) and what has got into it (water, fuel, insolubles, wear metals) to catch machinery problems before they become failures. The results are only useful if the sample is representative, the trend is followed over time, and the action limits are taken from the engine maker, oil supplier and your company's planned maintenance system.

What lube oil analysis is for

Lube oil analysis has three jobs:

  • Oil condition – is the oil still fit to lubricate, cool and neutralise acids? Viscosity, base number (BN) and oxidation tell you this.
  • Contamination – has anything got in that shouldn't be there? Water, fuel, cooling water treatment chemicals, catalytic fines, dust and soot.
  • Machinery wear – is something inside the engine wearing faster than it should? Wear metals in the oil point to the component.

It does not replace crankcase inspections, bearing temperature monitoring or oil mist detection. It sits alongside them in your planned maintenance system. Under the ISM Code the company must have procedures to maintain the ship and its equipment, and most companies use oil analysis as part of that. Many oil suppliers run a shore laboratory programme and send back a graded report.

Oil from the main engine system, auxiliary engines, stern tube, hydraulic systems, steering gear, compressors, gearboxes and deck machinery is commonly sampled. Which systems, and how often, is set by the maker, the oil supplier's programme and your company PMS. Do not assume one interval fits every system.

What the lab measures and what it tells you

The exact test list depends on the lab and the type of oil. The common parameters are below. Limits are deliberately not given here. Use the ones printed on your lab report or in the maker's instructions, because they differ by engine type and oil grade.

Parameter What it indicates
Viscosity (usually at 40 °C and/or 100 °C) A rise suggests oxidation, soot/insolubles, HFO contamination or cylinder oil ingress. A fall suggests distillate fuel dilution or the wrong oil being topped up
Base number (BN) Reserve alkalinity left to neutralise acids from sulphur combustion. A falling BN means the oil is being used up
Total acid number (TAN/AN) Acid build-up and oxidation. Most useful for turbine, hydraulic and gear oils
Water content Cooler leak, purifier problem, condensation, leaking jacket water
Flash point A drop is a strong sign of fuel dilution, which is also a fire and crankcase explosion concern
Insolubles Soot, oxidation products and combustion debris. These overload purifiers and filters
Wear metals (Fe, Cu, Pb, Sn, Cr, Al) Which component is wearing
Contaminant elements (Na, Mg, V, Ni, Si, B) Where the contamination is coming from
Additive elements (Ca, Zn, P, and in some oils Mg) Additive depletion, or mixing of different oils
Particle count (ISO 4406 code) Cleanliness of hydraulic and other fine-clearance systems

Rough guide to what the elements usually point to:

  • Iron – liners, crankshaft, gears, general steel wear, rust.
  • Copper – bronze bushes and bearings, or copper-alloy cooler tubes.
  • Lead and tin – white metal bearings and bearing overlays.
  • Chromium – chrome-plated piston rings.
  • Aluminium – pistons in trunk-piston engines, or aluminium-tin bearings. Aluminium together with silicon suggests catalytic fines from the fuel.
  • Sodium with magnesium – seawater. Compare against the fresh oil baseline, as some oils use magnesium-based additives.
  • Vanadium with nickel – heavy fuel oil.
  • Boron or sodium with no magnesium – often jacket water treatment chemicals. Confirm with the lab, because some oils also contain these elements as additives.
  • Silicon – dust or cat fines, but it can also be anti-foam additive. Read it against the fresh oil baseline.

Taking a representative sample: step by step

A bad sample gives you a confident report about the wrong oil. Most misleading results come from poor sampling, not from the lab.

  1. Use the designated sample point. It should be on a live, circulating line, usually after the pump and before the filters for wear trending. Follow your system diagram and the lab's guidance. Never sample from a tank drain or bottom, a dead leg, or straight after the purifier outlet, unless that is specifically what you want to measure.
  2. Sample with the machinery running, at normal temperature and load. Oil from a stopped engine lets water and debris settle out.
  3. Flush the sample cock into a waste container until the oil runs clean and at system temperature. This removes stagnant oil and dirt.
  4. Fill a clean, dry bottle supplied by the lab to the indicated level. Do not wipe the inside, and do not reuse bottles.
  5. Label it fully. Vessel, system, equipment serial, date, running hours on the oil and on the machine, oil grade, and any top-up or recent repair.
  6. Record and dispatch promptly. Note the sample in the PMS and send it at the next opportunity. A sample that sits on board for weeks loses its value.

Keep the sampling point, method and conditions the same every time. Trending only works if you compare like with like.

Onboard tests between lab samples

Lab results take time to come back. Most ships carry an onboard test kit for quick checks. These are less precise but give an early warning:

  • Viscosity comparison – a rolling-ball or flow comparator against a fresh oil reference.
  • BN test – a reagent kit that gives an approximate BN. On two-strokes it is also used on scrape-down (drain) oil.
  • Water content – a reagent cell. As a crude field check, a few drops on a hot plate crackle if water is present.
  • Salt/chloride test – tells seawater from fresh water once water has been found.
  • Blotter (spot) test – a drop on filter paper. A dense black centre or sharp ring suggests heavy insolubles or dispersancy breakdown.
  • Visual and smell – milky oil means water emulsion. A fuel smell means dilution. Sludge on the purifier bowl or filters is also a clue.

On crosshead engines, scavenge (scrape-down) drain oil analysis is a separate and important tool. Residual BN and iron content in the drain oil are used to judge whether the cylinder oil feed rate and BN suit the fuel being burned. Follow the engine maker's feed-rate guidance when making changes.

Reading the report: trends and worked scenarios

Lab reports usually grade results as normal, caution or action. A single value matters less than the rate of change compared with previous samples from the same point. A sudden step change is more worrying than a value that has been slowly creeping.

Scenario 1 – Main engine system oil: water up, sodium and magnesium up. This pattern points to seawater. Ask where seawater could reach this oil. On a central cooling plant the LO cooler is cooled by LT fresh water, so seawater has to come from elsewhere. Check for a central cooler leak salting the LT system (test the LT water chlorides), sump tank top penetrations and sounding/air pipes, and any directly seawater-cooled cooler if fitted. Oil pressure is normally higher than cooling water pressure, so a leaking cooler often loses oil while running. It may only take in water when the engine is stopped. Action: confirm with an onboard salt test, check the purifier and its water seal, trace the source, and increase sampling frequency.

Scenario 2 – Auxiliary engine: viscosity falling, flash point falling. This is classic fuel dilution, usually distillate. Look at leaking injectors, fuel pump plunger seals, and high-pressure pipe and drain arrangements. A low flash point raises the crankcase hazard, so treat it as urgent.

Scenario 3 – Main engine system oil: BN and viscosity creeping up. On a crosshead engine this often means cylinder oil and combustion residue are getting past the piston rod stuffing box. Check stuffing box condition and drain flows.

Scenario 4 – Copper rising alone. Check for bearing or bush wear, and also check cooler tube corrosion. Correlate with bearing temperatures and filter debris before opening up.

Faults, symptoms and first actions

  • Water in oil – milky oil, purifier upset, rust, loss of film strength. Act: find the source, run the purifier correctly, and avoid restarting until water is under control as per your procedures.
  • Fuel dilution – viscosity and flash point fall, possibly the oil level rises. Act: trace the fuel leak and monitor the oil mist detector.
  • HFO contamination (trunk-piston engines) – viscosity and insolubles rise, V and Ni appear, black sludge forms in the crankcase and piston undercrown. Act: check fuel pumps, injectors and seals, and intensify purification.
  • BN depletion – falling BN and corrosive wear. Act: top up or partially renew with fresh oil, and review oil consumption and the fuel sulphur level.
  • Oxidation – rising viscosity and acid number, varnish and darkening. Act: review operating temperatures and oil life, and plan a renewal.
  • Abnormal wear metals – rising specific metals, sometimes with filter debris or high bearing temperatures. Act: correlate with other monitoring, then inspect the identified component.
  • Cat fines (Al + Si) – these point to fuel treatment problems. Act: check the fuel purifier/clarifier settings and the bunker analysis.

Purifier performance shows up directly in oil analysis. Wrong gravity disc, incorrect temperature or throughput, and a lost water seal all appear as water, insolubles or sludge in the results.

Safety and records

  • Sampling lines carry hot oil under pressure. Wear gloves and eye protection, open the cock slowly, and stand clear of the jet.
  • Prolonged skin contact with used oil can cause dermatitis. Wash it off and do not leave oily rags in overalls.
  • Wipe up spills at once. Drain flushing oil to the designated waste/sludge oil tank and record oil residue handling in the Oil Record Book (MARPOL Annex I). Dispose of oily rags as garbage under MARPOL Annex V and record them in the Garbage Record Book.
  • If results suggest serious contamination or bearing failure, follow your crankcase safety procedures. Do not open a crankcase soon after stopping an engine with suspected hot spots or oil mist. Allow the cooling time your engine maker and SMS specify.
  • Keep all reports, onboard test results and actions taken in the PMS. Port state control inspectors and superintendents look for evidence that abnormal results were followed up, not just received.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: Why do you sample before the filter rather than after? A: You want to see what the machinery is putting into the oil. Sampling after the filter or purifier shows cleaner oil and can hide wear.

Q: What does a drop in flash point tell you? A: Fuel dilution. It also means a higher crankcase explosion risk.

Q: Sodium is high in the system oil. Is it seawater? A: Not necessarily. Check magnesium. Sodium with magnesium suggests seawater. Sodium with boron and no magnesium suggests treated jacket water. Then confirm with a salt test.

Q: Why does BN fall in service? A: The alkaline additives are used up neutralising acids from sulphur combustion products.

Q: What is scrape-down analysis and why is it done? A: It is analysis of cylinder drain oil from a crosshead engine, mainly residual BN and iron. It is used to judge cylinder condition and optimise cylinder oil feed rate within the maker's limits.

Q: A single result comes back in 'action'. What do you do? A: Check the sampling was correct and resample. Compare with the trend and other monitoring. Inform the chief engineer, then investigate the likely source rather than changing the oil blindly.

Key points
  • Lube oil analysis monitors oil condition, contamination and machinery wear together.
  • Trends from the same sample point matter more than any single result.
  • Sample from a live line with machinery running and warm, after flushing the cock, usually before filters.
  • Falling viscosity with falling flash point means fuel dilution, which is a crankcase safety issue.
  • Sodium plus magnesium suggests seawater; vanadium plus nickel suggests HFO; aluminium plus silicon suggests cat fines.
  • Take action limits from the maker, oil supplier and company PMS, not from memory or another ship.
  • Record every result and every follow-up action in the PMS.

Frequently asked questions

How often should main engine lube oil be sent for analysis?

The interval is set by the engine maker, the oil supplier's analysis programme and your company PMS, and it varies by system. Increase the frequency whenever a result trends towards caution or after any repair that could affect the oil.

Where is the correct sampling point for system oil?

Use the designated sample cock on a live, circulating line, normally after the pump and before the filters. Never sample from tank bottoms, drains or dead legs. Use the same point every time so the results can be trended.

What does BN mean in lube oil analysis?

Base number is the oil's reserve alkalinity, available to neutralise acids formed from sulphur in the fuel. It falls in service as additives are consumed. In a crosshead engine's system oil it can also rise if cylinder oil leaks past the stuffing box.

How can I tell if water in the oil is seawater or fresh water?

Use the onboard salt/chloride test. On the lab report, look at sodium together with magnesium, which suggests seawater, or sodium and boron without magnesium, which suggests treated cooling water. Then trace the source physically.

Can onboard test kits replace shore lab analysis?

No. Onboard kits give quick, approximate checks of viscosity, BN, water and insolubles between lab samples. Apart from iron meters used mainly on scrape-down oil, they cannot give a full wear-metal and additive-element analysis or an accurate flash point, so they complement the lab programme rather than replace it.

Test yourself

An auxiliary engine's lube oil shows falling viscosity and falling flash point. What is the most likely cause?

Distillate fuel thins the oil and lowers its flash point. Oxidation and soot raise viscosity instead. Engine maker's instructions and the oil supplier's report guidance.

Which combination of elements in a lab report most strongly suggests seawater contamination?

Seawater carries both sodium and magnesium. Vanadium and nickel point to HFO, aluminium and silicon to cat fines, and calcium and zinc are additive elements. Oil supplier lab interpretation guidance.

Why is the system oil sample normally taken before the filter rather than after it?

The aim is to see what the machinery is putting into the oil, and the filter removes part of it. Lab sampling instructions and company PMS.

On a crosshead main engine, system oil BN and viscosity are gradually rising. What should be checked first?

A worn stuffing box lets high-BN cylinder oil and combustion residue into the crankcase system oil, raising both BN and viscosity. Engine maker's instruction manual.

A lab result comes back in the 'action' band for the first time. What is the correct first response?

A single result may come from poor sampling. Confirm it, correlate it with trends and other monitoring, then investigate the source. ISM Code Section 10 and company PMS procedures.
