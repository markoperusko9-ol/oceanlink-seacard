Engine Room Ventilation: Purpose, Operation, Faults and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

Engine room ventilation supplies combustion air to the main engine, generators and boilers. It also carries away machinery heat and keeps the space safe and workable for people and equipment. Engineers must know how the fans, ducts, dampers and remote stops work, how to spot a starved or overheated engine room, and how to shut the whole system down quickly from outside before a fixed fire-extinguishing system is released.

What engine room ventilation is for

Examiners like candidates who give the purposes in order of importance, not as a random list. Engine room ventilation does four jobs:

  • Combustion air. On most ships the main engine turbochargers, the generator engines and the boiler burners draw their air from the engine room itself. If the fans cannot supply enough air, the engines are starved.
  • Heat removal. Hot surfaces, exhaust pipes, boilers, alternators and switchboards radiate a large amount of heat. The supply air carries that heat out through the funnel casing.
  • Equipment protection. Engines, electrical machinery and electronics are rated for a design ambient air temperature. If the room runs above it, output drops, insulation ages faster and electronics trip.
  • Personnel and atmosphere. Ventilation keeps the space habitable for watchkeepers. It also dilutes oil mist, fuel vapour and fumes so they do not build up.

Ship designers commonly size the system to ISO 8861, the standard for engine-room ventilation in diesel-engined ships. It works out airflow from two figures: the combustion air demand and the heat to be removed. The actual design figures for your ship are in the yard documentation and machinery manuals. Learn your own ship's figures rather than quoting generic ones.

How the system works, step by step

A typical arrangement on a motor ship works like this:

  1. Intake. Fresh air enters through weather louvres or mushroom ventilators, usually in the funnel casing or engine casing. The intakes have closing appliances (flaps or covers) and often a mesh screen or filter.
  2. Supply fans. Several fans push air down ducts into the engine room. These are usually axial fans, often two-speed and sometimes reversible. Their starters are normally at a group starter panel or the main switchboard, with remote stops outside the space.
  3. Distribution. Ducts carry the air low down and point it at the places that need it most:
    • turbocharger intakes
    • generator engines
    • the boiler front
    • the switchboard area
    • the control room air conditioning intake
    • the main working positions
  4. Rise and exhaust. The heated air rises through the engine casing and leaves through the funnel. On some ships it leaves by natural draught, on others by dedicated exhaust fans.
  5. Local extraction. Some rooms have their own exhaust fans so their fumes do not spread round the engine room. These typically include the purifier room, the workshop or welding area, the battery space and sometimes the incinerator area.
  6. Fire isolation. Ducts passing through fire boundaries are fitted with fire dampers or equivalent arrangements as required by SOLAS II-2, and main inlets and outlets have closing appliances. All fans can be stopped, and all openings closed, from a position outside the space.

Pressure balance. Many ships run the engine room at a slight positive pressure, with supply slightly above exhaust. This stops the engine room sucking in air through doors and leaky openings, which helps boiler and engine air flow. Rooms such as the purifier room are usually kept slightly negative, so oil mist stays in that room and goes out of the exhaust. The exact philosophy depends on the ship, so check your ship's design and the chief engineer's standing orders.

Normal running: what 'right' looks like

There is no single correct engine room temperature or fan setting for every ship. Signs of healthy ventilation are:

  • Temperature. Engine room air temperature is steady and within the design ambient stated by the engine and electrical makers. The difference from outside air temperature is not climbing.
  • Doors. Doors to the engine room open and close normally. They do not slam shut or need heavy force, which would show a strong pressure difference.
  • Main engine. Scavenge air temperature and exhaust temperatures are in line with the engine's performance records for that load and ambient.
  • Turbochargers. Intake filter pressure drop is within the maker's limits.
  • Atmosphere. There is no oil mist haze and no smell of fuel vapour in the purifier room.
  • Fans. Motor current is normal, with no unusual vibration or noise at the fans.

Matching fans to conditions:

Condition Usual practice
Tropics, full load All supply fans on high speed; exhaust fans as designed
Cold climate Fewer fans or low speed to protect water-filled pipes and keep air temperature up, but never so few that combustion air is short
Port, engines stopped Reduced ventilation to suit generators, boilers and people working below
Heavy maintenance, welding Local extraction running; extra portable fans if the permit requires

In cold weather, cutting fans to keep the room warm is acceptable. Starving the turbochargers is not. Always check engine parameters after changing fan configuration.

Routine checks and maintenance

Ventilation equipment is in the planned maintenance system like any other machinery. Typical tasks are below. Intervals come from your PMS and the makers' manuals.

  • Fans and motors
    • Check vibration and listen at the bearings.
    • Grease bearings where fitted.
    • On belt-driven units, check belt tension and condition.
    • Megger the motor insulation.
    • Clean impellers and casings. Salt and dirt build-up causes imbalance.
  • Intake screens and filters
    • Clean or renew them.
    • Check for salt deposits after heavy weather. Salt carried into the engine room ends up fouling turbocharger compressors.
  • Louvres, flaps and closing appliances
    • Free off and grease hinges and spindles.
    • Check seals and gaskets.
    • Confirm the open/closed markings are correct and legible.
  • Fire dampers
    • Operate them and confirm they close fully from their control position.
    • Check that position indicators read correctly.
  • Remote stops
    • Test the emergency stops outside the engine room and confirm that every fan they should stop actually stops.
    • This test is normally done as part of fire drills and the ship's maintenance plan for fire protection systems.
  • Ducts
    • Inspect for corrosion holes, disconnected sections and oil-soaked insulation near duct outlets.

LOTO warning. Never put hands into a fan casing just because the fan is switched off. Natural draught through the funnel can turn an axial fan freely, so a stopped fan can still windmill. Isolate the motor under lock-out tag-out, close the dampers, and physically secure the rotor before working inside.

Faults, symptoms and likely causes

Symptom Likely causes
Engine room temperature rising, exhaust temps and scavenge air temp up Fans tripped or on low speed; intake flaps partly closed after heavy weather; choked intake screens; high sea water temperature reducing cooler performance; hot-air recirculation from casing
Doors hard to open or slamming Too many exhaust fans or reversed fans creating negative pressure; supply fans stopped; flaps closed
Boiler flame unstable or poor combustion Low engine room pressure affecting burner air supply; funnel draught disturbed
Oil mist or fuel smell spreading Purifier room extraction fan failed; doors to separator room left open; actual oil leak (find and fix it first)
Fan motor tripping on overload Bearing failure; impeller rubbing; damper closed against running fan in some designs; supply voltage or single-phasing fault
Vibration and noise at fan Impeller fouling or damage; loose mounting; bearing wear
Condensation and water dripping from ducts Humid air on cold duct surfaces; water ingress through intakes in rain or spray
Generator or switchboard alarms for high temperature Local duct outlet blocked or redirected; fan on that section stopped

When the engine room is overheating, always ask two questions:

  1. Is it a ventilation problem? Low airflow is the usual cause.
  2. Is it a heat source problem? Look for missing lagging, a steam leak or an exhaust leak.

Both produce the same symptom, so check both.

Safety: fire, CO2 and heavy weather

Fire. SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires that machinery space ventilation can be stopped, and ventilation openings closed, from outside the space. The aim is to starve a fire of oxygen. In a machinery space fire, the usual sequence is:

  1. Raise the alarm.
  2. Muster and account for everyone.
  3. Stop the fans from the remote stop.
  4. Close the fire dampers and flaps.
  5. Close the quick-closing valves.
  6. Stop the fuel pumps.

Your ship's fire plan and muster list give the exact order and who does each step.

Fixed CO2. Gas released into a space with fans running and flaps open is blown out and wasted. CO2 also does nothing if the space is not sealed. Before release, confirm three things:

  • ventilation is stopped
  • openings are closed
  • the space is evacuated

On some ships, opening the release cabinet automatically stops the fans. On others it is a manual step. Know which applies on your vessel.

After a fire. Do not restart ventilation to clear smoke until the master and the fire team leader decide the fire is out and re-entry is planned. Fresh air can re-ignite hot material.

Heavy weather. Most weather-exposed ventilator openings must have weathertight closing appliances under the Load Line Convention (Annex I, Reg 19), although ventilators with sufficiently high coamings may be exempt. Closing them keeps water out, but it also cuts combustion air. The heavy weather checklist should say:

  • which flaps may be closed
  • which ones must stay open to keep engines and generators running

After heavy weather, check the intakes for water ingress, salt build-up and damaged louvres.

Blackout and UMS. On many ships the engine room fans are part of the sequential restart after a blackout. After recovery, always check that the fans actually came back. A recovered plant running in a hot room without air soon brings a second round of alarms.

Worked scenario: hot engine room in the tropics

You are the duty engineer on a UMS ship crossing the Red Sea in summer. In the evening you get a high exhaust temperature deviation alarm on the main engine and a high temperature alarm in the engine room.

  1. Go down and look. The engine room is noticeably hotter than usual. One supply fan is stopped. Its starter shows an overload trip.
  2. Restore airflow first. Check that the remaining fans are on high speed. If a standby fan exists, start it. Do not reset the tripped fan repeatedly without finding out why it tripped.
  3. Check the engine. Compare scavenge air temperature, exhaust temperatures and turbocharger speed with the trend for this load. Reduce load if the chief engineer or your standing orders require it. Keep the bridge informed.
  4. Investigate the trip.
    • With the motor isolated under LOTO and the dampers closed to stop draught, check by hand whether the impeller turns freely.
    • Then secure the impeller against windmilling before any work inside the casing.
    • Megger the motor.
    • Check the bearings.
  5. Look for secondary causes. Check that the intake flaps are fully open and the screens are clean. Look for a new heat source, such as a steam or exhaust leak.
  6. Record and report. Make an entry in the engine log. Call the chief engineer as per standing orders. Raise a defect report in the PMS.

The lesson: a single fan failure in hot weather becomes an engine performance problem within a watch. Treat ventilation alarms as machinery alarms, not comfort issues.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: Why does an engine lose power when the engine room is hot? Hotter air is less dense, so the turbocharger delivers less mass of air. With less air, combustion is poorer and exhaust temperatures rise. The engine reaches its thermal limits sooner.

Q: Why is the engine room usually at slight positive pressure, and the purifier room negative? Positive pressure in the engine room prevents uncontrolled air ingress through openings and helps steady combustion air supply. Negative pressure in the purifier room keeps oil mist and vapour inside that room and sends them out through the exhaust, instead of spreading round the engine room.

Q: Where are the engine room fan emergency stops on your last ship, and what do they stop? The examiner wants your actual ship, not a textbook answer. Know:

  • where the stops are
  • which fans each stop covers
  • whether the purifier room and other local fans are included

Q: What do you do with ventilation before releasing CO2? Stop all fans and close all dampers and openings. Account for all personnel before release. Follow the ship's release procedure exactly.

Q: How do you test fire dampers? Operate each one from its control position and confirm it closes fully. Check the position indicators. Record the test in the PMS. Report and repair any damper that is seized or leaks.

Q: What precautions do you take before working on a ventilation fan? - Isolate it electrically under LOTO. - Close the dampers. - Secure the impeller against windmilling. - Confirm zero energy before starting work.

Key points
  • Engine room ventilation supplies combustion air, removes heat, protects equipment and keeps the atmosphere safe.
  • Hot engine room air reduces air density, raises exhaust temperatures and cuts engine performance.
  • Engine rooms are commonly kept at slight positive pressure; purifier rooms are usually extracted to slight negative pressure.
  • SOLAS requires that machinery space fans can be stopped and openings closed from outside the space.
  • Always confirm fans stopped and openings closed before releasing a fixed CO2 system.
  • Stopped axial fans can windmill on natural draught, so lock out and secure the rotor before working inside.
  • Closing weather flaps in heavy weather must never starve the engines of combustion air.

Frequently asked questions

Why are engine room ventilation fans important for the main engine?

On most ships the main engine turbochargers draw their air from the engine room. If ventilation fails, the air gets hotter and there is less of it. Exhaust temperatures rise and the engine may have to reduce load.

Should the engine room be at positive or negative pressure?

Most designs keep the engine room at a slight positive pressure, with supply slightly above exhaust. Separator rooms and similar spaces are usually kept slightly negative so fumes stay contained. Your ship's design and the chief engineer's standing orders decide the actual practice.

Can engine room fans be stopped from outside the engine room?

Yes. SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires that machinery space ventilation can be stopped, and openings closed, from outside the space for firefighting. Locations vary by ship, so learn them during your safety familiarisation.

Is it acceptable to reduce engine room ventilation in cold weather?

Reducing fans or running at low speed is common in cold climates to protect water-filled pipes and keep the space warm. You must still supply enough combustion air for running engines and boilers. Check engine parameters after any change.

How often should fire dampers and fan remote stops be tested?

Intervals are set by the ship's maintenance plan for fire protection systems, the PMS and flag state or class requirements. Do not rely on a generic figure. Follow your company SMS and record every test.

Test yourself

What is the main effect of a significantly hotter engine room on a turbocharged diesel engine?

Hot intake air is less dense, so less mass of air is delivered and thermal loading rises. This is basic engine performance theory and appears in makers' performance guidance.

Before releasing a fixed CO2 system into the engine room, what must be done with ventilation?

Fans running or openings left open waste the gas and keep feeding oxygen to the fire. SOLAS Chapter II-2 and the FSS Code, Chapter 5, require that the space can be sealed.

Why is a purifier room commonly kept at slight negative pressure?

Dedicated extraction keeps flammable mist inside the separator space. SOLAS does not prohibit supply air, and room pressure has nothing to do with bowl operation.

An engineer must work inside an axial ventilation fan casing. Which precaution is essential besides switching the fan off?

Natural draught can spin a stopped axial fan, so energy isolation and mechanical securing are both needed. This follows the company SMS lock-out tag-out procedure.

In heavy weather, what is the correct approach to engine room ventilator closing appliances?

The Load Line Convention generally requires closing appliances for exposed ventilators, but the engines still need air. The ship's heavy weather procedure balances both needs.
