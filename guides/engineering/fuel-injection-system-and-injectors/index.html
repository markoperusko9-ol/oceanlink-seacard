Fuel Injection System and Injectors: A Practical Guide for Marine Engineers

Quick answer

The fuel injection system meters a precise quantity of fuel, raises it to very high pressure and sprays it into the cylinder at the right moment, atomised finely enough to burn completely. On ships this is done either by cam-driven jerk pumps or by electronically controlled common rail or hydraulic booster systems, with spring-loaded injectors at the cylinder. Most combustion problems you will chase as a watchkeeper, such as high exhaust temperatures, smoke, low Pmax and knocking, trace back to injection timing, quantity or atomisation.

What the injection system has to do

Every marine diesel, from a generator to a large two-stroke main engine, depends on the injection system doing four jobs correctly:

  • Metering: delivering the correct quantity of fuel per cycle for the load demanded by the governor.
  • Timing: starting and ending injection at the correct crank angle.
  • Pressurising: raising fuel pressure high enough to open the injector and atomise the fuel.
  • Atomising and distributing: breaking the fuel into fine droplets and directing the sprays so they mix with the compressed air without hitting the liner or piston crown.

Get any one of these wrong and you see it at once in exhaust temperatures, cylinder pressures, smoke and, over time, in burnt piston crowns, liner wear and fouled turbochargers.

The main components are the fuel supply circuit (booster or supply pumps, heaters, viscosity control, filters), the high-pressure pump or rail, the high-pressure pipes, and the injectors (also called fuel valves). Details of the supply side are covered in the fuel oil system guide. Here we concentrate on the high-pressure end.

How a jerk pump and injector work, step by step

The classic system, still found on many auxiliary engines and older main engines, is the cam-operated jerk pump with helix control (Bosch type). One pump serves each cylinder.

  1. Filling: with the plunger at the bottom of its stroke, fuel at supply pressure fills the barrel through the suction and spill ports.
  2. Port closure: the cam lifts the plunger. Once its top edge covers the ports, fuel is trapped and pressure rises sharply. This is effectively the start of delivery.
  3. Delivery: pressure lifts the delivery valve and travels through the HP pipe to the injector.
  4. Injector opening: in the injector, fuel pressure acts on the differential area of the needle. When the force exceeds the spring setting, the needle lifts and fuel sprays through the nozzle holes.
  5. End of delivery: the helix machined on the plunger uncovers the spill port. Pressure collapses back to the suction side and the injector spring snaps the needle shut.
  6. Delivery valve closes: its unloading collar (retraction piston) draws back a small volume from the HP pipe. This gives a clean cut-off and reduces dribbling and secondary injection, while keeping some pressure in the pipe for the next stroke.

Quantity control: the fuel rack rotates the plunger, which changes where the helix uncovers the spill port. The effective stroke changes and so does the quantity. Rack position (fuel index) is set by the governor.

Timing: on simple pumps, timing is fixed by cam position and adjusted with shims or by moving the cam. Many two-stroke engines use variable injection timing (VIT), which raises or lowers the pump barrel with load to keep firing pressure optimised. Some older designs used valve-controlled pumps with separate suction and spill valves instead of a helix.

Electronically controlled engines remove the fuel cam:

  • In hydraulically actuated systems, such as MAN ME type, control oil at high pressure drives a fuel booster for each cylinder, with an electronically controlled valve deciding timing and quantity.
  • In common rail systems, such as WinGD RT-flex/X type and many modern four-strokes, fuel is held at injection pressure in a rail. Electronically controlled valves release it to the injectors.

The principle at the injector stays the same: a spring-loaded needle opens on pressure and the nozzle atomises the fuel. Always use the maker's manual for your engine, because the layouts differ considerably.

Normal operation and what to watch

Your engine manual and shop trial / sea trial records define the normal values. Compare against those, and against the other cylinders, rather than against figures from another ship. In general terms:

  • Fuel index / rack position: where individual pump indices are recorded (or on electronically controlled engines), one cylinder needing a much higher index than the others for the same output points to a worn pump or a faulty injector. On many mechanically governed engines all racks are driven by a common regulating shaft, so differences between individual pump indices mainly reflect set-up or balancing adjustments rather than a running reading.
  • Exhaust temperatures should be balanced. Your SMS or maker will set an allowable deviation from the mean.
  • Firing pressure (Pmax) and compression pressure (Pcomp) from indicator or monitoring system readings. A low Pmax with normal Pcomp suggests an injection problem. A low Pcomp points instead to rings, exhaust valve or scavenge air.
  • Fuel viscosity at the engine inlet must be within the maker's range. Poor viscosity control gives poor atomisation, and on light fuels it can mean too little lubrication for the pump plunger.
  • HP pipe leakage alarms and leak-off drains should be dry or within normal flow.
  • On HFO engines with circulating fuel valves, check that fuel circulation and heating are maintained while stopped, so the injectors are warm and full of fuel of the correct viscosity before starting.

Checks and maintenance

Overhaul intervals come from the maker's recommendations as built into your planned maintenance system. Running hours alone are not the only trigger: abnormal readings should lead you to pull an injector early.

Injector overhaul and testing on the test rig

  • Dismantle, clean the nozzle and needle with the correct tools only. Do not use emery cloth on lapped surfaces.
  • Inspect the needle and seat for wear, scoring, erosion or blueing. Check the nozzle holes for enlargement, blocking or carbon ("trumpets").
  • Reassemble with new seals as specified and torque to the manual values.
  • On the test pump, check:
    • Opening pressure: adjust the spring setting to the maker's value.
    • Tightness: hold pressure just below the opening pressure and check for dribbling at the nozzle tip and for excessive back-leakage.
    • Spray pattern: all holes spraying, evenly atomised, no streaks or solid jets.
    • Chatter: some injector types should chatter on test, while others are not designed to. Follow the manual.
  • Use the test oil specified, at a suitable temperature if the procedure calls for it.

Fuel pump checks

  • Check timing (pump lead or VIT settings) against the engine's records after any overhaul.
  • Check rack linkages for free movement and correct zero setting, and make sure the emergency stop / shut-off action works.
  • Inspect the plunger and barrel for wear and seizure marks. Check spill ports and deflectors for cavitation erosion.
  • Inspect the delivery valve and seat.

Emissions note: injection timing, nozzles and pumps are typically NOx-critical components listed in the engine's NOx Technical File. Replacement parts and settings must match that file, or the engine's EIAPP compliance can be put in question.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms
Dribbling or leaking injector Afterburning, high exhaust temperature, smoke, carbon on nozzle tip, possible piston crown burning and fouled exhaust/turbocharger
Needle stuck open Fuel poured rather than sprayed, very high exhaust temperature, heavy smoke, possible knocking, risk of liner and piston damage
Needle stuck closed or nozzle blocked Little or no combustion in that cylinder, low exhaust temperature, low Pmax, power imbalance
Opening pressure too low Earlier start but poorer atomisation, smoke, possible carbon build-up
Opening pressure too high Slightly later start of injection, possible reduced Pmax, higher load on pump and pipes; check against maker's setting
Worn pump plunger/barrel Higher fuel index needed for the same load, late and weak injection, low Pmax, hard starting
Injection timing late Low Pmax, high exhaust temperature, poor fuel economy
Injection timing early High Pmax, possible knocking, higher thermal and mechanical load
Sticking fuel rack Cylinder not responding to load changes, unstable running, risk of overspeed if racks stick open
Pump plunger seizure Often linked to sudden temperature change or very low viscosity during HFO to distillate changeover

Fuel quality matters too. Cat fines and poor purification wear plungers, barrels and nozzles quickly, so the purifier and filters are part of injector care.

Safety around high-pressure fuel

  • Injection injuries: fuel at test-rig or engine pressure can penetrate skin. The wound may look minor but needs urgent medical attention. Never put your hand near a spraying nozzle. Use the rig's spray guard, wear eye protection and gloves, and direct spray into the collector.
  • Spray fires: a cracked HP pipe or loose union can spray fuel onto hot surfaces. SOLAS requires external HP fuel lines between pumps and injectors to be jacketed, with leakage led away and alarmed. Hot surfaces that fuel could reach must be insulated. Report any missing lagging or leaking jacket at once.
  • Before opening: stop the engine, isolate and drain the fuel, depressurise the system (including common rail and hydraulic units), engage the turning gear, and apply your company's lock-out tag-out and permit procedures.
  • Hot HFO: circulating fuel can be well above its flash point and hot enough to burn. Let it cool, or isolate the heating, before breaking joints.
  • After work: vent air from the system, check for leaks at full pressure, and watch that cylinder closely on the first start and loading.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Expect to explain with a sketch and to link symptoms to causes.

  • Explain how a helix-controlled pump varies fuel quantity. Rack rotates plunger, helix changes the point of spill, effective stroke changes. Mention whether your pump has constant beginning, constant end or variable timing.
  • What is the purpose of the delivery valve? Isolate the HP pipe from the pump, give a sharp end of injection through its unloading collar, prevent dribbling and secondary injection, and keep residual pressure in the pipe.
  • Why does the injector needle open, and why does it close sharply? Fuel pressure on the differential area overcomes the spring. When spill drops the pressure, the spring closes it.
  • One cylinder has high exhaust temperature. How do you investigate? Check the thermocouple first. Then compare the fuel index, take indicator readings (Pmax/Pcomp), consider injector condition, pump timing, scavenge and exhaust valve, and change the injector if indicated.
  • What tests do you carry out on an overhauled injector? Opening pressure, tightness or dribble, spray pattern, and chatter where applicable.
  • What happens if the timing is advanced or retarded? Covered in the table above.
  • Why are HP pipes double-walled? SOLAS fire safety: containment and detection of HP fuel leaks.
  • What precautions apply to fuel changeover regarding the injection system? Control the rate of temperature change, keep viscosity within limits, watch for plunger sticking, and follow the ship's changeover procedure.
  • How does a common rail or electronically controlled system differ from a jerk pump? Pressure generation is separated from timing. Timing and quantity are set electronically, not by a cam profile.
Key points
  • The injection system must meter, time, pressurise and atomise fuel. An error in any one shows up in exhaust temperatures and cylinder pressures.
  • In a jerk pump, rack rotation moves the helix to change the effective stroke and therefore the fuel quantity.
  • The delivery valve gives a sharp cut-off and prevents dribbling. The injector needle opens on pressure against an adjustable spring.
  • Always judge readings against your engine's own trial records and the other cylinders, not figures from another ship.
  • Injector tests cover opening pressure, tightness, spray pattern and chatter where the maker specifies it.
  • High-pressure fuel can penetrate skin and cause spray fires. Depressurise, isolate and use guards every time.
  • Injection components and timing are usually NOx-critical items under the engine's Technical File.

Frequently asked questions

What causes high exhaust temperature in one cylinder of a marine diesel?

Common injection-related causes are a dribbling or poorly atomising injector, late injection timing, or a worn fuel pump. Non-injection causes include a leaking exhaust valve, blocked scavenge ports or a faulty thermocouple. Check the sensor first, then compare fuel index and Pmax/Pcomp with the other cylinders.

How often should fuel injectors be overhauled on a ship?

There is no single universal interval. It is set by the engine maker and built into your planned maintenance system, and it can vary with fuel type and engine condition. Pull an injector early if exhaust temperature, smoke or cylinder pressure readings indicate a problem.

What is the function of the delivery valve in a fuel pump?

It separates the pump from the high-pressure pipe and opens only during delivery. Its unloading collar withdraws a small volume when it closes, giving a sharp end to injection and preventing dribbling or secondary injection. It also keeps some residual pressure in the pipe for the next cycle.

Why are high-pressure fuel pipes double-walled on ships?

SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires external high-pressure fuel lines between pumps and injectors to be jacketed so that fuel from a failed pipe is contained. The leakage is drained and alarmed rather than sprayed onto hot surfaces, which reduces the risk of engine room fires.

How is a fuel injector tested after overhaul?

On a test rig you check the opening pressure against the maker's setting, hold pressure just below opening to check for dribbling, and inspect the spray pattern for even atomisation from all holes. Some injector types are also checked for chatter. Always use the spray guard and keep hands clear.

Test yourself

In a helix-controlled jerk pump, how is the quantity of fuel delivered per stroke changed?

The rack rotates the plunger so the helix uncovers the spill port earlier or later, changing the effective stroke. See the engine maker's fuel pump description and the STCW Code, Section A-III/1 competence on operating main machinery.

An injector needle sticking in the open position will most likely cause:

Fuel is poured in rather than atomised, so it burns late and incompletely. Low temperature and low Pmax are the symptoms of a needle stuck closed or a blocked nozzle.

What is the main purpose of the unloading collar on a fuel pump delivery valve?

As the delivery valve closes, the collar withdraws a small volume from the HP line, collapsing pressure quickly so the injector closes cleanly. It does not set injector opening pressure or timing.

Why does SOLAS require jacketed high-pressure fuel lines between fuel pumps and injectors?

The jacket contains leaked fuel so it can be drained and alarmed instead of spraying onto hot surfaces. Reference: SOLAS 1974 as amended, Chapter II-2, Regulation 4.

A cylinder shows low Pmax with normal compression pressure. Which is the most likely area to investigate?

Normal Pcomp shows that the air charge and sealing are fine, so the fall in Pmax points to late, weak or poor injection. Rings, exhaust valve leakage and scavenge problems would normally lower Pcomp as well.
