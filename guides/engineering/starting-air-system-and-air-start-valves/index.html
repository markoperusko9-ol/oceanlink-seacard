Starting air system and air start valves: how they work, faults and exam questions

Quick answer

The starting air system stores compressed air in receivers and sends it, in the correct firing sequence, to the cylinders to turn the main engine until it fires on fuel. Each cylinder air start valve is held shut by a spring and opened by pilot air from the starting air distributor. A non-return valve and a flame arrester, bursting disc or relief valve on each branch protect the system against starting air line explosions.

What the starting air system does

A slow-speed two-stroke main engine is far too large to start with an electric motor. It is started by admitting compressed air into the cylinders, one after another, during their power stroke. This turns the crankshaft fast enough for fuel injection and compression ignition to take over.

The same system usually supplies other users through reducing valves:

  • control air for the engine manoeuvring system
  • safety air for the engine shutdown system
  • air for the exhaust valve air springs on some designs
  • service air and the whistle on some ships

Many four-stroke auxiliary engines also start on air. They use either direct-admission cylinder valves or an air-driven starter motor that engages the flywheel, depending on the maker.

Compressor and receiver capacity is set by classification society rules, not by SOLAS figures. Class rules commonly require the receivers to hold enough air for a fixed number of consecutive starts without recharging. More starts are required for a directly reversible engine than for a non-reversible one with a CPP. Class also sets a time within which the compressors must recharge the receivers. Check your class rules and the ship's machinery particulars for the actual numbers on your vessel.

Under SOLAS Chapter II-1, the ship must be able to bring its machinery into operation from dead ship condition without external help. That is why ships carry an emergency air compressor or another approved independent means of charging air.

Main components, from compressor to cylinder

Follow the air from the compressors to the cylinders:

Component Function
Main air compressors (normally two) Multi-stage, with intercoolers and aftercoolers, relief valves on each stage and drains to remove condensed water and oil
Emergency compressor Charges an air receiver from dead ship. It is driven independently, for example by a hand-started diesel or from the emergency switchboard, depending on the ship
Air receivers (normally two main) Store the air. Each has a relief valve, a drain, a pressure gauge, a manhole for inspection and, depending on the arrangement, a fusible plug
Main starting valve (automatic valve) A large pneumatically operated valve that opens only when a start signal is given and the interlocks are satisfied
Slow-turning valve (if fitted) A small bypass that turns the engine slowly at reduced air flow before a normal start
Non-return valve Stops a pressure surge or flame in the manifold from travelling back towards the receivers
Starting air distributor Driven from the camshaft or crankshaft. Sends pilot air to each cylinder valve at the correct crank angle, for ahead or astern
Branch pipes with flame arrester and bursting disc or relief valve Protect each cylinder branch and the manifold if a cylinder valve leaks and an explosion occurs
Cylinder air start valves Admit main starting air into the cylinder when opened by pilot air

The turning gear interlock is part of the system. While the turning gear is engaged, starting air is blocked.

How a start works, step by step

Inside the air start valve

A typical pneumatically operated cylinder air start valve works like this:

  • A spring holds the valve spindle on its seat.
  • Main starting air surrounds the valve body at manifold pressure, but this alone does not open the valve.
  • The distributor sends pilot air to the top of the valve's actuating piston. The pilot force overcomes the spring and pushes the valve open.
  • Main air then flows into the cylinder and drives the piston down.
  • When the distributor vents the pilot air, the spring closes the valve. Cylinder pressure also helps hold it shut.

The sequence of a start

  1. The bridge or engine control room gives a start order. The control system checks the interlocks: turning gear disengaged, main starting valve available and adequate air pressure.
  2. The main starting valve opens and fills the manifold up to every cylinder valve.
  3. The distributor sends pilot air to the cylinder whose piston is just past TDC on the power stroke for the chosen direction.
  4. That valve opens and the cylinder is pressurised. The valve closes before the exhaust opens, so air is not blown straight out.
  5. The next cylinder in firing order receives air. Admission periods overlap, so the engine can start from any crank position.
  6. Once the engine reaches firing speed, fuel is admitted. When the engine fires and runs on fuel, starting air is cut off, the main starting valve closes and the manifold is vented.

Reversing. For astern, the distributor timing is changed so that air reaches each cylinder in the reverse sequence. Depending on the maker, this is done by shifting or rotating the distributor cam, or by reversing valves in the pilot lines. The fuel injection timing is reversed at the same time. On some designs the exhaust valve timing is also changed, depending on the maker.

Checks and maintenance

Daily and every watch

  • Drain the receivers, compressor separators and starting air line drains.
    • Oil-water mixture: check the compressor condition.
    • Excessive water: check the coolers and the drain traps.
  • Check receiver pressure and compressor running hours. Record cut-in and cut-out behaviour.
  • After every manoeuvre, check the temperature of each cylinder starting air branch pipe. Use an infrared thermometer if possible, or touch carefully with the back of the hand. A branch noticeably hotter than the others points to a leaking air start valve.

Before departure or after a long stop

  • Carry out a blow-through or slow turning with the indicator cocks open, as your company procedure and the engine maker require.
  • Watch the indicator cocks for water, fuel or oil coming out. Liquid in a cylinder can cause hydraulic lock and serious damage.
  • Test ahead and astern starts as required by your SMS and the pre-departure checklist.

Planned maintenance, at intervals set by the maker and your PMS

  • Overhaul the cylinder air start valves:
    • Inspect and lap the valve and seat.
    • Check the spring and the piston sealing rings or O-rings.
    • Check that the spindle moves freely.
    • Pressure test before refitting.
  • Inspect and clean the distributor. Check its timing against the maker's data.
  • Inspect the non-return valve, flame arresters and bursting discs. Renew a bursting disc if it is ruptured or damaged.
  • Overhaul the compressor suction and delivery valves. Clean the coolers and test the relief valves.
  • Test the receiver relief valves. Surveyors carry out internal receiver inspections at the intervals required by class and the flag state.

Lubrication matters. Too much compressor cylinder oil carries over into the receivers and pipes, and this oil is the fuel for a starting air line explosion. Keep oil feed rates as the compressor maker specifies, and drain the receivers regularly.

Faults, symptoms and what to check

Symptom Likely causes
Engine does not turn on air Turning gear engaged or its interlock faulty; low receiver pressure; receiver or main valve shut; main starting valve not opening (control air, solenoid or sticking); control air failure
Engine turns, then stops at a certain position A cylinder air start valve sticking shut; distributor fault or wrong timing; pilot line blocked or leaking
Engine turns on air but will not fire Fuel problems (no fuel, low fuel temperature, air in the fuel system); low starting speed from low air pressure; fuel limiter or governor setting
One starting branch pipe hot after starting Air start valve leaking past its seat, or sticking open, letting combustion gas back into the line
Rapid pressure drop or high air consumption Leaking valves or joints; main starting valve not closing fully; air start valve stuck open
Water at drains or indicator cocks Leaking compressor cooler; poor draining; leaking cylinder cover or liner (investigate before starting)
Bursting disc ruptured or relief valve lifted on a branch Explosion or severe pressure surge in the starting line. Treat this as serious

Worked scenario: hot starting air pipe

You are on standby manoeuvring. A temperature check shows that the No. 4 starting air branch is markedly hotter than the others, which are only warm. The air start valve on No. 4 is letting combustion gas into the branch.

  1. Inform the chief engineer and the bridge.
  2. If the engine is needed, use as few air starts as possible and keep monitoring that branch.
  3. At the first safe opportunity, stop the engine, isolate and vent the system, and change the valve for an overhauled spare.
  4. Inspect the branch and flame arrester for oil deposits or heat damage.
  5. Record the defect and the action taken in the PMS and the log.

Ignoring this is how starting air line explosions happen. Hot gas meets an oil film in the pipe, and the next start delivers fresh compressed air into it.

Safety: starting air line explosions and isolation

Why explosions happen

Oil carried over from the compressors coats the inside of the manifold and branches. If a cylinder valve leaks or sticks open, burning gas enters the pipe. When the oil film is hot enough and fresh air arrives, the film can ignite and the flame can travel along the line.

Defences built into the system

  • Non-return valve between the main starting valve and the manifold
  • Flame arresters on the branches
  • Bursting discs or relief valves to vent the pressure
  • Drains to remove oil and water
  • Interlocks to prevent starting with the turning gear engaged

Safe working rules

  • Before working on any part of the system, shut and lock the receiver outlet valves and the main starting valve. Vent the line and confirm zero pressure.
  • Engage the turning gear when working on the engine, and follow your company lock-out tag-out procedure.
  • Never stand in front of a receiver drain or a disconnected pipe unless you are certain it has been vented.
  • Receivers store a large amount of energy. Never gag or isolate their relief valves.
  • After an explosion, fully inspect the line before the system goes back into use. Check the bursting discs, arresters, pipework and all cylinder valves, and follow your SMS reporting requirements.

Oral exam questions and how to answer them

At MEO and CoC orals, examiners expect you to sketch the system and explain it, not recite it. Common questions:

  • "Draw the starting air system from compressor to cylinder." Include the compressors, receivers with their fittings, main valve, non-return valve, distributor, pilot lines, flame arresters and bursting discs, cylinder valves and the turning gear interlock.
  • "How does an air start valve open and close?" Pilot air on the actuating piston overcomes the spring. When the pilot air is vented, the spring closes the valve.
  • "When does it open, and why does it close before the exhaust opens?" It opens just after TDC on the power stroke to produce turning moment. It closes before the exhaust opens so air is not wasted.
  • "Causes and prevention of starting air line explosion?" The cause is a leaking cylinder valve plus an oil film in the pipe. Prevent it with maintained valves, minimal oil carry-over, regular draining, branch temperature checks and the protective devices.
  • "Why blow through before starting?" To reveal water, fuel or oil in the cylinders and avoid hydraulic lock.
  • "How is the engine reversed on air?" The distributor timing is changed so the cylinders receive air in the astern sequence. The fuel injection timing is reversed too. On some designs the exhaust valve timing also changes.
  • "How do you start from dead ship?" Use the emergency compressor, or another approved independent means, to charge a receiver. Then start a generator and restore normal services.
  • "Why must there be a minimum number of cylinders or overlap?" So that at least one valve can admit air at any crank position, letting the engine start wherever it stopped.

If the examiner asks for a figure, such as receiver pressure or the number of starts, give your last ship's value and say that it comes from the maker's manual and class. Do not quote a number you are unsure of.

Key points
  • Starting air turns the engine by pressurising cylinders in firing order during the power stroke until it fires on fuel.
  • Cylinder air start valves are spring-closed and opened by pilot air from the distributor.
  • Receiver capacity, number of starts and compressor recharge time come from class rules, so check your ship's documents.
  • A hot starting air branch after manoeuvring means a leaking air start valve, the classic cause of starting air line explosions.
  • Non-return valves, flame arresters, bursting discs or relief valves, drains and the turning gear interlock are the key safety defences.
  • Always blow through with indicator cocks open as procedures require, to detect liquid in the cylinders.
  • Isolate, lock and vent the system before any maintenance. Receivers store a large amount of energy.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the air start valve open after TDC and not before?

Air admitted before TDC would push against the rising piston and oppose rotation. Admitting it just after TDC on the power stroke produces turning moment in the chosen direction. The valve closes before the exhaust opens so the air is not wasted.

What causes a starting air line explosion?

The usual cause is a leaking or sticking cylinder air start valve. It lets hot combustion gas into a pipe coated with lubricating oil carried over from the compressors. Fresh compressed air on the next start can then ignite the oil film. Good valve maintenance, minimal oil carry-over and regular draining are the main preventions.

How many starts must the air receivers provide?

Classification society rules set a minimum number of consecutive starts without recharging. The number is higher for directly reversible engines than for non-reversible engines with a CPP. Check your class rules and the ship's machinery particulars for the figure that applies to your vessel.

How do I find a leaking air start valve?

After starting or manoeuvring, check the temperature of each cylinder starting air branch. Use an infrared thermometer if possible, or touch carefully with the back of the hand. A branch noticeably hotter than the others shows that combustion gas is leaking back past that valve. Report it, then change and overhaul the valve at the first safe opportunity.

Why can't the engine start with the turning gear engaged?

An interlock blocks starting air while the turning gear is engaged. Starting the engine with the turning gear engaged would damage it and could seriously injure anyone working on the engine. Never bypass this interlock.

Test yourself

What opens a typical pneumatically operated cylinder air start valve?

Pilot air from the distributor overcomes the spring to open the valve, and the spring closes it when the pilot air is vented (engine maker's manual).

A starting air branch pipe on one cylinder is much hotter than the others after manoeuvring. What is the most likely cause?

Hot gas passing a leaking or sticking air start valve heats that branch. This is a recognised precursor of starting air line explosions.

Why do engineers blow through with the indicator cocks open before starting after a long stop?

Liquid in a cylinder shows at the indicator cocks. Starting with liquid in a cylinder can cause hydraulic lock and serious damage (engine maker's instructions and company procedures).

Which document sets the required starting air receiver capacity for the main engine?

Receiver capacity, the number of consecutive starts and compressor capacity are class rule requirements. SOLAS Chapter II-1 separately requires the ability to start from dead ship condition.

What is the purpose of the non-return valve in the main starting air line?

The non-return valve protects the receivers and upstream pipework from a starting air line explosion. The distributor controls timing, and reducing valves lower the pressure.
