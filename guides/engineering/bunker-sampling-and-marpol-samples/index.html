Bunker Sampling and MARPOL Samples: A Practical Guide for Engineers

Quick answer

Every bunker delivery must come with a representative, sealed MARPOL sample. It is drawn continuously at the receiving ship's manifold, signed by both the supplier and the ship, and kept under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for at least 12 months. Separately, Annex VI now lets inspectors take in-use and onboard samples to check sulphur compliance, so you must know your designated sampling points and keep your sampling records tight.

Why bunker samples matter

A bunker sample is the only physical evidence of what actually came aboard. The Bunker Delivery Note (BDN) is the supplier's declaration. The sample is what a laboratory, a port State inspector or an arbitrator will test.

Samples serve two separate purposes. Keep them apart in your head and in your paperwork:

  • Regulatory compliance (MARPOL Annex VI): proving that the fuel met the applicable sulphur limit. That is 0.50% m/m globally, or 0.10% m/m inside an Emission Control Area.
  • Commercial protection: proving quality (ISO 8217 parameters such as viscosity, density, water, catalytic fines and flash point) and supporting claims against the supplier or charterer.

The MARPOL sample is for the Administration's compliance checks. It is not your sample to open for a quality dispute. Your commercial samples do that job.

The MARPOL delivered sample: the rules in brief

MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18 deals with fuel oil availability and quality. It requires the following:

  • BDN: every delivery is documented on a BDN containing the information listed in Appendix V. This includes product name, quantity, density at 15°C, sulphur content and a supplier's declaration of compliance. The BDN stays on board, readily available for inspection, for three years.
  • Representative sample: the BDN must be accompanied by a representative sample of the fuel delivered, taken in line with the IMO sampling guidelines.
  • Seal and signature: the sample is sealed and signed by the supplier's representative and by the master or the officer in charge of the bunker operation, on completion of bunkering.
  • Retention: the sample is kept under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for not less than 12 months from delivery.

The guidelines for drawing this sample are in IMO Resolution MEPC.182(59). Their key principle is that the sample is collected at the receiving ship's inlet bunker manifold. It is drawn continuously throughout the delivery, so it represents the whole parcel and not just the first or last few tonnes.

If the sample is tested, Annex VI, Appendix VI sets out the verification procedure. It allows a statistical margin based on the reproducibility of the test method. Do not quote a figure for this margin unless you have checked the current text.

Drawing the sample step by step

Exact steps are set by your company's bunkering procedure and checklist. The sequence below reflects common good practice:

  1. Pre-bunker meeting: agree with the barge on the sampling point, sampler type and who witnesses. Record the agreement. Some ports have their own bunkering standard that specifies sampling arrangements, so check local requirements.
  2. Fit and inspect the sampler: this is normally a drip sampler (needle valve plus a clean flexible container, or 'cubitainer') at the ship's manifold. Some ships use automatic flow-proportional samplers. Check the container is clean and empty, then seal the sampler in front of both parties. Note the seal numbers.
  3. Set the drip rate: open the needle valve once flow is steady. Aim for a constant drip that will collect enough volume over the whole delivery without overflowing early. Residual fuel may need the line warm to keep the valve from choking.
  4. Monitor throughout: check the drip at regular intervals, especially after rate changes, stoppages or tank changes on the barge. Log the checks.
  5. On completion: close the valve and remove the container in front of the supplier's representative. Mix it thoroughly by shaking or rolling until it is homogeneous.
  6. Divide into bottles: pour into the required bottles in stages, filling each a little at a time in rotation so every bottle is equally representative. Do not overfill. Leave ullage for expansion.
  7. Seal, label and sign: use tamper-evident seals. Typical label content includes ship name and IMO number, port and date, barge or supplier, grade, sampling location and method, seal number, and the names and signatures of both representatives.
  8. Distribute: the MARPOL sample stays on board. Commercial samples (ship's retained, supplier's retained and laboratory) go where the company and charter party require. Most companies want seal numbers cross-referenced on the BDN or bunker receipt.
  9. Send the lab sample promptly: where the company uses a fuel testing service, dispatch the sample promptly. Segregate the new fuel and avoid burning it until results arrive, wherever tank arrangements allow.

In-use and onboard samples

Amendments to Annex VI that entered into force in 2022 added two more sample types to Regulation 14. Inspectors use them to check the fuel actually being burned or carried:

  • In-use sample: drawn from the ship's fuel system while the fuel is being used. It is taken at a designated sampling point in the service line feeding the combustion units.
  • Onboard sample: drawn from a fuel tank to check fuel carried for use. Taking it must be practicable and must not compromise safety.

Ships must have designated in-use sampling points. The dates by which existing ships had to fit them depend on construction date and survey cycle, so check your flag's interpretation. Guidance on location and design is in MEPC.1/Circ.864/Rev.1. In short, the point should be:

  • readily accessible and safe to use
  • clear of hot surfaces and moving parts
  • located so the sample is representative of the fuel in use
  • clearly marked, and shown on a sketch kept on board

In an exam, be ready to point out on a fuel system sketch where your in-use sampling point sits and to explain why it is there.

Supplier's retained Supplier's evidence Barge Per supplier terms
Laboratory sample Quality analysis Testing lab Per lab / company
In-use / onboard sample Inspector's compliance check Taken by or for the Administration / PSC Per authority

Storage of the MARPOL sample (per the IMO guidelines):

  • a sheltered, cool location outside the accommodation
  • out of direct sunlight
  • where crew are not exposed to vapours
  • many ships use a dedicated sample locker on deck

Records to keep:

  • a sample register showing bottle and seal numbers, date, port, grade and disposal date
  • BDNs filed for three years
  • bunkering entries in the logs required by Annex VI and your SMS
  • fuel changeover records when entering or leaving an ECA

At PSC or an internal audit, inspectors commonly cross-check four things: BDN sulphur value, sample seal numbers, the register, and the actual bottle in the locker.

Common faults, disputes and a worked scenario

Faults you will meet:

  • Drip valve choked with cold residual fuel, leaving a thin or unrepresentative sample.
  • Drip rate wrong: too fast and the container is full halfway through; too slow and there is not enough volume to fill all bottles.
  • Sampler not witnessed or sealed at the start, so the ship cannot vouch for its integrity.
  • Poor mixing before division, so bottles differ. This matters for water and catalytic fines, which settle.
  • Missing or wrong labels, or seal numbers that do not match the BDN.
  • Samples left in the engine room or in the sun, or discarded before the retention period ends.

Worked scenario: the barge arrives with sealed, pre-filled bottles and says sampling was done at the barge's own manifold. Your actions:

  1. Do not sign for those bottles as the MARPOL sample. The guideline sampling point is the receiving ship's manifold, though some port standards specify otherwise, so check the local rules.
  2. Draw your own continuous drip sample at your manifold. Invite the barge representative to witness and countersign.
  3. If they refuse to witness or sign, issue a Letter of Protest stating the facts.
  4. Inform the chief engineer, the master and the company. Record the event in the bunker documents.
  5. Retain everything.

If later testing shows non-compliant sulphur, follow your company procedure. This normally means notifying the flag Administration and the relevant port authority, and segregating the fuel.

Safety when sampling

  • Hydrogen sulphide (H2S): residual fuels can release H2S, especially when you open sampler containers or tanks. SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5-1 requires a Safety Data Sheet (SDS) for marine fuel oil. Read the hazards before bunkering, and carry a personal H2S detector if your SMS requires one.
  • Hot fuel: heated residual fuel causes burns. Wear gloves, eye protection and overalls.
  • Spills: check drip trays, plug scuppers and keep SOPEP gear ready at the manifold. A dripping sampler valve is a pollution source too.
  • Fire: no naked lights or hot work during bunkering. Follow your bunkering checklist.
  • Manual handling: keep sample lockers secured and do not carry bottles on ladders without a free hand.

Oral exam questions

Examiners like these. Practise answering them in your own words:

  • Where is the MARPOL sample drawn, and why continuously? At the receiving ship's inlet manifold, throughout the delivery, so it represents the whole parcel.
  • Who seals and signs it? The supplier's representative together with the master or the officer in charge of bunkering.
  • How long do you keep it? Until the fuel is substantially consumed, and not less than 12 months from delivery.
  • How long do you keep the BDN? Three years.
  • What is an in-use sample, and where is your sampling point? Be specific to your last ship.
  • Can you open the MARPOL sample for a quality dispute? No. Use the commercial samples.
  • What do you do if the barge will not let you witness sampling? Sample at your own manifold and issue a Letter of Protest, then report.
  • What hazards exist when sampling residual fuel? H2S, burns, spills and fire.
Key points
  • The MARPOL delivered sample is drawn continuously at the receiving ship's inlet manifold throughout bunkering.
  • It is sealed and signed by the supplier's representative and the master or the officer in charge of bunkering.
  • Retain it under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and not less than 12 months.
  • BDNs are kept on board for three years; record the MARPOL sample seal numbers on the BDN or bunker receipt so they can be cross-checked.
  • In-use samples come from designated sampling points; onboard samples come from tanks.
  • The MARPOL sample is for compliance only; use commercial samples for quality claims.
  • Witness, mix, divide evenly, label and log; protest in writing if procedures are not followed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the MARPOL sample and the commercial samples?

The MARPOL sample exists so the Administration or port State can verify compliance with Annex VI, mainly sulphur content. Commercial samples (ship's, supplier's and laboratory) are used to check quality against the contract and support claims. You should not open the MARPOL sample for a commercial dispute.

How long must a ship keep the MARPOL bunker sample?

Under MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18, it stays under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for at least 12 months from delivery. Many companies keep samples longer as policy, so follow your SMS.

Can the supplier take the MARPOL sample at the barge manifold?

The IMO guidelines specify the receiving ship's inlet bunker manifold. Some ports have local bunkering standards with their own sampling arrangements, so check local requirements. If sampling is not done properly or not witnessed, record it and issue a Letter of Protest.

What is an in-use fuel sample?

It is a sample drawn from a designated sampling point in the fuel service line while that fuel is being used. It lets inspectors check the sulphur content of the fuel actually being burned. Designated sampling points are required under Regulation 14 of Annex VI.

What should I do if the barge refuses to sign the sample labels?

Complete the sampling at your manifold anyway and have the master or officer in charge of bunkering seal and sign it. Note on the label that the supplier refused to sign, since Reg. 18 requires both signatures. Then issue a Letter of Protest stating what happened, inform your company, and keep all samples and documents.

Test yourself

For how long must the MARPOL delivered sample be retained?

Annex VI requires retention until the fuel is substantially consumed and in any case not less than 12 months. Three years is the BDN retention period. (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg. 18)

According to the IMO sampling guidelines, where should the MARPOL sample be drawn?

The guidelines specify continuous sampling at the receiving ship's inlet manifold so the sample represents the whole delivery. (MEPC.182(59))

How long must the Bunker Delivery Note be kept on board?

The BDN must be kept on board, readily available for inspection, for three years. Twelve months is the minimum sample retention. (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg. 18)

Where is an in-use fuel oil sample taken?

In-use samples are drawn from designated sampling points serving the fuel actually in use. Tank samples are 'onboard samples'. (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg. 14; MEPC.1/Circ.864/Rev.1)

Who seals and signs the MARPOL delivered sample?

Both the supplier's representative and the master or officer in charge of bunkering must seal and sign the sample on completion. (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg. 18)
