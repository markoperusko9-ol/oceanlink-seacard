Engine Room Log Book and Oil Record Book: Practical and Exam Guide

Quick answer

The engine room log book is the watch-by-watch record of machinery operation, required by flag state law and the company SMS. The Oil Record Book Part I is a MARPOL Annex I record of every machinery-space oil operation, entered by code letter. The engineer in charge signs each completed operation, the master signs each completed page, and the book stays on board for three years after the last entry. Port State Control may check it against tank soundings and other records.

Two different books, two different purposes

Cadets often treat these as one job. They are not.

Engine room log book (engine log) - Records how the plant was run on each watch: main engine and generator parameters, running hours, fuel consumption, alarms, UMS periods, soundings, and orders received from the bridge. - No single international convention prescribes its format. Its content and retention are set by the flag Administration's national rules and the company SMS. - The chief engineer's standing orders usually state what must be logged and how often. - It supports the principles of the engineering watch in STCW, including handover, the state of machinery, and events that occurred.

Oil Record Book (ORB) - A statutory record required under MARPOL Annex I. Its format is laid down in Appendix III to the Annex. - Part I (machinery space operations) applies to every oil tanker of 150 GT and above, and to every other ship of 400 GT and above. - Part II (cargo/ballast operations) applies to oil tankers of 150 GT and above. It is normally kept by the deck side. - It is evidence. PSC officers, flag inspectors and, in some jurisdictions, prosecutors read it to decide whether oil went over the side illegally.

The engine log describes the machinery. The ORB accounts for the oil. If the two disagree, an inspector will notice.

Oil Record Book Part I: the code letters

Every Part I entry starts with a date, a code letter and an item number, as set out in Appendix III. Memorise the codes. Examiners ask for them.

Code Covers
A Ballasting or cleaning of fuel oil tanks
B Discharge of dirty ballast or cleaning water from fuel oil tanks
C Collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge)
D Non-automatic starting of discharge overboard, transfer or disposal otherwise of machinery-space bilge water
E Automatic starting of discharge overboard, transfer or disposal otherwise of bilge water
F Condition of the oil filtering equipment (failures and return to service)
G Accidental or other exceptional discharges of oil
H Bunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oil
I Additional operational procedures and general remarks

IMO has issued guidance on recording operations in Part I (MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2). It shows which code and items to use for common operations. It also expects a weekly inventory of the sludge tanks listed in section 3.1 of the IOPP Supplement, recorded under code C item 11.

Tank names in the ORB must match the tank names and capacities in the Supplement to the IOPP Certificate (sections 3.1 and 3.3). A mismatch here is one of the most common PSC findings.

How to make a correct entry, step by step

General rules (MARPOL Annex I, Appendix III and MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2) - Enter the operation when it is completed, in chronological order, without blank lines. - The officer(s) in charge of the operation sign each completed operation. The master signs each completed page. - Use the code letter and item number exactly as in the book. Write tank names as in the IOPP Supplement. Give quantities in m³, except bunkering (code H), where the quantity added and the total tank content are given in tonnes, as in the book's printed items. - Ships holding an IOPP Certificate must make entries in at least English, French or Spanish. If the flag's official national language is also used, that version prevails in a dispute. - Errors: strike through with a single line so the original stays legible, then sign and date the correction (per MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2). Never use correction fluid, never tear out pages, never rewrite a page. - Keep the book readily available for inspection and on board for three years after the last entry. Check whether your flag or company requires longer.

Worked scenario: bilge water pumped through the 15 ppm equipment 1. Before starting, sound the bilge holding tank and record the quantity in the engine log. 2. Confirm the ship is en route and outside any area where discharge is prohibited. 3. Run the separator. Note the start time and position. 4. On completion, note the stop time and position, and the quantity discharged (from soundings before and after). 5. Enter under code D: quantity discharged, start and stop times and positions, method (through 15 ppm equipment), and the tank emptied with its quantity retained. 6. The engineer in charge signs. The entry must agree with the engine log, the bridge positions and the OWS records.

Worked scenario: sludge burnt in the incinerator Enter under code C. Give the tank identity, the quantity disposed of, the method (incineration), and the quantity retained. The Garbage Record Book under MARPOL Annex V may also need an entry for incinerator use, depending on what was burnt. Follow your company SMS.

The engine log: what goes in and how to check it

The content depends on the flag and the company, but a typical engine log records the following:

  • Main engine: revolutions, load indicator or fuel rack, exhaust temperatures per unit, scavenge air pressure and temperature, turbocharger speed, lube oil and cooling water pressures and temperatures.
  • Generators: which sets are on load, kW, running hours.
  • Boilers, fresh water generator, purifiers, steering gear, compressors, refrigeration.
  • Tank soundings: fuel, lube oil, sludge, bilge, fresh water.
  • Fuel consumption per day, and changeovers between fuels.
  • Alarms, faults and remedial action. UMS start and end times.
  • Orders from the bridge, stand-by and full away times, and the movement (bell) book where kept separately.

Good practice and checks - Record actual readings. Don't copy the previous watch's figures. - Compare readings against the shop trial, sea trial or manufacturer's reference figures for that load, not against memory. A slow trend in exhaust temperature or scavenge pressure tells you more than one reading does. - Unusual readings need a remark explaining the cause and the action taken. - The chief engineer reviews and countersigns the log as the company SMS requires.

Other machinery records you will meet - Fuel changeover log: MARPOL Annex VI requires the changeover into and out of an emission control area to be recorded in a log book prescribed by the Administration. The record covers time, position and fuel volumes in each tank. - Bunker delivery notes: retained on board for three years under Annex VI. The MARPOL sample is kept separately. - Ozone-depleting substances record book: required under Annex VI for applicable systems. - Record book of engine parameters: required under the NOx Technical Code for applicable engines. - Annex VI fuel consumption data: collected for the IMO data collection system.

Faults, discrepancies and what inspectors look for

Problems in record books are less about machinery and more about the numbers not adding up.

Symptom What it may indicate
ORB tank names or capacities differ from IOPP Supplement Book set up wrongly, or modifications not reflected in the certificate
Sludge tank quantity drops with no code C entry Unrecorded disposal, possibly illegal
Sludge generated looks low for the fuel burnt Sludge going somewhere it shouldn't, or purifiers not being run properly
Bilge holding tank always near empty, little OWS running time Possible bypass ('magic pipe')
OWS overboard discharge times overlap port stays or prohibited areas False entry or illegal discharge
15 ppm alarm or OWS failure with no code F entry Unrecorded defect
Engine log soundings differ from ORB figures Careless record keeping, or falsification

The competent authority of a port State may inspect the ORB. It may take copies of entries and ask the master to certify them as true copies. Several countries treat a falsified ORB as a serious criminal matter in its own right, separate from any discharge. Individual engineers, not only the company, have been prosecuted.

Accidental discharge (code G): record the time, the position, the approximate quantity and type of oil, and the circumstances and reasons. Also follow the SOPEP and the reporting requirements. Recording the incident truthfully is your protection, not a confession of something worse.

Safety and integrity: why honest records matter

  • The pen is part of pollution prevention. An honest ORB proves the OWS, incinerator and sludge disposal arrangements are working. A false one hides a defect that should have been fixed or reported.
  • Never sign for an operation you did not supervise or cannot verify. Your signature makes you answerable for that entry.
  • If you are asked to make a false entry or bypass the OWS, refuse and escalate. Use the company's designated person ashore (DPA) under the ISM Code. Bypassing the OWS is not a grey area.
  • Report OWS or 15 ppm monitor defects promptly. Record the defect under code F and retain bilge water on board until the equipment is repaired. Don't let the bilge holding tank reach a level where someone is tempted to cut corners.
  • Keep soundings real. Sounding pipes, gauges and remote level readings should be cross-checked. A faulty level transmitter makes the whole record book unreliable.

Oral exam: questions examiners commonly ask

Q: Which ships carry ORB Part I? Every oil tanker of 150 GT and above, and every other ship of 400 GT and above, under MARPOL Annex I.

Q: Who signs the ORB? The officer(s) in charge sign each completed operation. The master signs each completed page.

Q: How long is it kept? On board, readily available, for three years after the last entry.

Q: You bunkered fuel. What do you write? Code H: place, time, type and quantity of fuel, and the tanks bunkered with the quantity added and total content of each.

Q: You made a wrong entry. What do you do? Single line through it, leaving it legible. Write the correct entry, then sign and date the correction. No erasing, no correction fluid, no removed pages.

Q: The 15 ppm alarm failed. What do you do? Stop discharging overboard and retain bilge water on board. Record the failure under code F, and record its return to service when repaired. Inform the chief engineer and follow the SMS.

Q: What document must your ORB tank list agree with? The Supplement to the IOPP Certificate.

Q: Can the ORB be electronic? MARPOL now allows electronic record books if they are approved by the Administration in line with IMO guidelines. Check the flag's acceptance and carry the approval on board.

Tip for the orals: examiners like candidates who explain why the entry matters, meaning PSC, prosecution risk and the audit trail, not just the code letter.

Key points
  • The engine log records machinery operation; its format and retention come from flag rules and the company SMS.
  • ORB Part I applies to oil tankers of 150 GT and above and to all other ships of 400 GT and above under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Learn codes A to I. C is sludge, D and E are bilge water, F is filtering equipment, G is accidental discharge, H is bunkering.
  • The officer in charge signs each operation, the master signs each page, and the book is kept for three years after the last entry.
  • Correct errors with a single line through them, signed and dated. Never erase or remove pages.
  • ORB tank names and capacities must match the IOPP Supplement, and every entry must agree with soundings and the engine log.
  • False entries can bring personal criminal liability in several jurisdictions, so refuse and escalate to the DPA if asked to falsify.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for maintaining the Oil Record Book Part I?

The master is ultimately responsible and signs each completed page. In practice the chief engineer manages the book, and the engineer who carried out or supervised each operation makes and signs that entry. Many companies assign day-to-day keeping to the second engineer.

How long must the Oil Record Book be kept on board?

MARPOL Annex I requires it to be kept readily available for inspection on board for three years after the last entry. Some flags or companies may require longer retention ashore. Check your SMS.

Is transferring bilge water from bilge wells to the holding tank recorded in the ORB?

Yes. IMO guidance on recording Part I operations places transfer of machinery-space bilge water under code D, as a transfer to a holding tank. Record the quantity, the times and the tank, consistent with your soundings.

Can I use an electronic Oil Record Book?

MARPOL was amended to allow electronic record books where they are approved by the flag Administration in line with IMO guidelines. Use one only if your flag has approved the specific system, and keep the approval document on board.

What happens if the ORB does not match the tank soundings during a PSC inspection?

Expect detailed questioning, a deficiency, and possibly a wider investigation into illegal discharge. In some countries this has led to detention and criminal proceedings. Regular cross-checks between soundings, the engine log and the ORB prevent this.

Test yourself

For how long must the Oil Record Book Part I be retained on board?

MARPOL Annex I requires the ORB to be kept readily available on board for three years after the last entry. (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17)

Under which ORB Part I code is the bunkering of fuel oil recorded?

Code H covers bunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oil. C is sludge, I is general remarks, and G is accidental discharge. (MARPOL Annex I, Appendix III)

Who must sign each completed page of the Oil Record Book Part I?

Officers in charge sign each operation, and the master signs each completed page. (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17)

An engineer makes a wrong entry in the ORB. What is the correct way to fix it?

The original entry must stay readable, with the correction signed and dated. Altered or missing pages destroy the book's evidential value. (MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2 guidance)

Which ships are required to carry an Oil Record Book Part I?

The applicability is split by ship type: 150 GT for oil tankers and 400 GT for other ships. (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17.1)
