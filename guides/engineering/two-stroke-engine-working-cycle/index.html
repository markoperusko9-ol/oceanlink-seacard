Two-stroke engine working cycle: how a slow-speed marine diesel completes its cycle

Quick answer

A two-stroke diesel completes compression, combustion, expansion, exhaust and scavenging in one crankshaft revolution, so every cylinder fires once per turn. On modern slow-speed crosshead main engines this is done by uniflow scavenging: air enters through ports at the bottom of the liner and leaves through an exhaust valve in the cylinder cover. Know the sequence, the timing logic and what each fault does to the cycle, and you will handle both the watch and the oral exam.

What the two-stroke cycle is and why ships use it

In a two-stroke engine the piston makes two strokes, one up and one down, for every working cycle. That is one crankshaft revolution. A four-stroke engine needs two revolutions for the same job because it uses separate strokes to push exhaust out and draw air in.

The two-stroke engine gets rid of those extra strokes by scavenging. Pressurised air from the turbocharger sweeps the cylinder clean and refills it near bottom dead centre (BDC), while the piston is changing direction.

Slow-speed two-strokes drive most deep-sea cargo ships for practical reasons:

  • High power per cylinder at low rpm. The engine can turn a large, efficient propeller directly, with no gearbox.
  • Crosshead design. A diaphragm and piston rod stuffing box separate the scavenge space from the crankcase. The crosshead design keeps combustion residues out of the crankcase and lets cylinder oil be matched to the fuel independently. This makes burning the heaviest residual fuels at low speed practical.
  • Separate cylinder lubrication. Lubricators or quills feed cylinder oil to the liner. The oil's base number is matched to the fuel's sulphur content, independent of the crankcase oil.
  • Direct reversing. The engine is reversed by changing fuel and starting air timing, so no reverse gear is needed.

The uniflow cycle step by step

This describes a typical uniflow-scavenged crosshead engine. The scavenge ports sit around the lower part of the liner and are opened and closed by the piston. The exhaust valve sits in the cylinder cover and is opened hydraulically, either cam-driven or electronically controlled depending on the design. Start with the piston near BDC and the cylinder full of fresh air.

  1. Compression (upstroke). The rising piston covers the scavenge ports, and the exhaust valve closes. The trapped air is compressed, and its temperature rises well above the self-ignition temperature of the fuel. Effective compression only starts once the cylinder is sealed.
  2. Fuel injection. Shortly before top dead centre (TDC), fuel is injected through the fuel valves as a fine atomised spray. The exact start point is set by the engine builder and varies with load on engines with VIT or electronic control.
  3. Combustion. The classic phases are:
    • ignition delay;
    • rapid pressure rise as the fuel already injected burns;
    • controlled combustion while injection continues;
    • afterburning as the piston moves down.
  4. Expansion (power stroke). The hot gas drives the piston down, and the work goes through the piston rod, crosshead and connecting rod to the crankshaft.
  5. Exhaust blowdown. Well before BDC, the exhaust valve opens. Cylinder pressure falls quickly as gas rushes to the exhaust receiver and the turbocharger. Cylinder pressure must drop below scavenge air pressure before the ports open, or exhaust gas will blow back into the scavenge space.
  6. Scavenging. The descending piston uncovers the scavenge ports. Pressurised air enters, and the ports are angled so the air swirls. The air pushes the remaining exhaust gas upward and out through the exhaust valve, which is why it is called uniflow. Some air goes straight out with the exhaust. This excess helps clean and cool the valve and liner.
  7. Recharging and the next compression. After BDC the piston rises and covers the ports again. The exhaust valve closes and compression begins again.

All of this happens in one revolution, so each cylinder delivers one power stroke per revolution.

Scavenging methods and timing logic

Older and smaller engines used ported scavenging without an exhaust valve. You will meet them in exam questions even if you never sail with one.

Method Air path Exhaust out through Key point
Uniflow Ports at liner bottom, one direction upward Exhaust valve in cover Most effective scavenging; allows long stroke; standard on modern slow-speed engines
Loop Ports on one side; air loops up and back down Exhaust ports above scavenge ports, same side No exhaust valve; some mixing of air and gas
Cross Ports on one side, across the cylinder Exhaust ports on opposite side Simple but poorest scavenge efficiency

Why the exhaust valve matters for timing. Ports are opened and closed by the piston, so their timing is always symmetrical about BDC. A fully ported engine therefore has fixed, symmetrical exhaust timing. Some designs added rotary valves or other devices to work around this.

An exhaust valve can be timed independently. It can open early enough for proper blowdown and close at whatever point the designer chooses. This is called asymmetric timing, and it gives better scavenging and better control of the trapped air mass.

On electronically controlled engines, the exhaust valve closing point can be varied with load. This is one way the effective compression ratio and compression pressure are managed.

Any change to injection or exhaust valve timing must stay within the settings in the engine's NOx Technical File, which forms part of its certification under MARPOL Annex VI.

Timing diagram. In the exam, draw a circle with TDC at the top. Mark:

  • fuel injection start and end around TDC;
  • exhaust valve open, then ports open, before BDC;
  • ports closed, then exhaust valve closed, after BDC.

Show the port-open arc as symmetrical about BDC. Do not quote crank angles unless you are referring to your own engine's manual, because they differ by make, model and tuning.

Monitoring the cycle: what normal looks like

Normal values are engine-specific. Use the shop test and sea trial records and the maker's manual as your reference, not a textbook figure. What you are checking is the trend and the balance between cylinders.

Key parameters

  • Compression pressure (Pcomp). Shows how well the cylinder traps and compresses air. It depends on scavenge air pressure, ring and liner condition, exhaust valve tightness and exhaust valve closing timing.
  • Maximum pressure (Pmax). Shows injection timing and fuel quality. It is limited by the maker for mechanical reasons.
  • Mean indicated pressure. Shows the work done per cylinder. Use it to check load balance.
  • Exhaust temperature after each cylinder. A quick daily balance check. A cylinder running hotter or colder than its neighbours needs investigating.
  • Scavenge air pressure and temperature. Compare both with the turbocharger speed and the engine load.

Tools

  • Indicator cards. Older engines use a mechanical indicator to draw cards. Many ships now use electronic pressure analysers or the engine's built-in combustion monitoring.
  • Diagrams. The pressure–volume diagram gives the work done. The pressure–crank angle diagram is better for judging injection timing, ignition delay and compression.

Worked check. One unit shows lower Pcomp and a higher exhaust temperature than the others, while scavenge air pressure and temperature are normal.

  1. Normal scavenge air pressure and temperature, with only one unit affected, point away from the turbocharger and air cooler, which affect all units.
  2. Suspect that unit: leaking or sticking rings, a leaking exhaust valve, or liner wear.
  3. Confirm with a scavenge port inspection and an exhaust valve check before adjusting fuel or timing.

Checks and maintenance tied to the cycle

Every part of the cycle has hardware that wears. Typical routine work, with intervals set by the maker and your planned maintenance system:

  • Scavenge port inspection. Look through the ports with the engine on turning gear. Check:
    • piston rings for free movement, breakage and collapse;
    • liner condition, looking for scuffing, polishing or lacquering;
    • the piston crown and skirt;
    • deposits on the ports and in the scavenge space.
  • Scavenge space drains. Check them regularly. Oily or dirty drain flow points to blow-by or over-lubrication. Water points to the air cooler or poor water mist separation.
  • Scavenge air cooler and water mist catcher. A fouled cooler raises air temperature and reduces air density. If the air is overcooled in humid conditions, condensation increases.
  • Turbocharger. Wash the turbine and compressor as specified, and check filters. A dirty turbocharger reduces scavenge pressure, which affects the whole cycle.
  • Auxiliary blowers. At low load the turbocharger cannot supply enough air, so electric blowers cut in automatically. Test them so they work at manoeuvring loads.
  • Fuel valves. Pressure test them and check the spray pattern. Poor atomisation gives late burning and high exhaust temperatures.
  • Exhaust valves. Overhaul the seat and spindle as scheduled. Check rotation where fitted, and check the closing mechanism (for example, the air spring on many designs).
  • Cylinder lubrication. Set the feed rate and match the oil grade to the fuel in use. Too little oil causes scuffing. Too much, or the wrong base number, causes deposits and can lead to scavenge fires. Too low a base number for the fuel's sulphur content causes cold corrosion of the liner.

Faults and their symptoms

Fault Effect on the cycle Typical symptoms
Broken or sticking piston rings Poor sealing in compression and expansion Low Pcomp on that unit, blow-by, dirty scavenge drains, high scavenge temperature locally
Leaking exhaust valve Charge lost during compression; hot gas burns the seat Low Pcomp, high exhaust temperature, poor combustion
Fouled scavenge ports or air cooler Less air trapped, poor scavenging Low scavenge pressure or high scavenge air temperature, high exhaust temperatures, smoke
Late injection or worn fuel pump Combustion moves into the expansion stroke Low Pmax, high exhaust temperature, reduced output
Early injection Pressure peaks too soon High Pmax, knocking, load on bearings
Defective fuel valve (dribbling, poor spray) Afterburning, carbon formation High exhaust temperature, smoke, deposits on piston crown and ports
Turbocharger fouled or surging Air supply unstable or reduced Surging noise, scavenge pressure fluctuation, high exhaust temperatures

Blow-by plus oil-soaked deposits in the scavenge space is the classic recipe for a scavenge fire. Signs to watch for:

  • a rising exhaust temperature on the affected unit;
  • a high scavenge space temperature alarm;
  • sparks or smoke from the scavenge drains;
  • loss of power.

The usual first response is:

  1. Reduce load (slow down).
  2. Cut fuel to the affected unit where the manual allows.
  3. Increase cylinder lubrication.
  4. Follow the maker's and company instructions.

Crankcase explosion risk is a separate issue. It comes from oil mist caused by a hot spot in the running gear, not from the scavenging process. However, a severe scavenge fire can damage the stuffing box and create a hot spot, so check the oil mist detector and crankcase after any scavenge fire.

Safety when working on the cycle components

Most inspection of the cycle happens inside the scavenge space or at the cylinder cover. These are hazardous jobs. Follow your company SMS and permit-to-work system. The usual principles are:

  • Isolate before you open.
    • Obtain bridge permission.
    • Shut the main starting air valve and vent the system.
    • Engage and lock the turning gear.
    • Lock out and tag the auxiliary blowers and the engine control.
  • Let it cool and ventilate. Open scavenge doors only after the space has cooled and any fire is fully out. Opening too early can feed air to smouldering deposits.
  • Treat the scavenge space as hazardous. Use good lighting. Remove loose deposits. Keep fire-fighting equipment ready. Apply enclosed space precautions if your SMS classes the space as one.
  • Turning the engine. Communicate clearly with the person on the turning gear before anyone puts hands near the ports or piston.
  • High pressure. Fuel and hydraulic exhaust valve actuation systems run at high pressure. Depressurise them before breaking any connection.

Oral exam questions and how to answer them

Q: Explain the two-stroke cycle of your main engine using a timing diagram. Draw it. Mark TDC, BDC, injection, exhaust valve open, ports open, ports closed and exhaust valve closed. Explain each event in order. Say clearly that port timing is symmetrical about BDC because the piston controls it.

Q: Why must the exhaust valve open before the scavenge ports? For blowdown. Cylinder pressure must fall below scavenge air pressure before the ports open, otherwise hot gas blows back into the scavenge space. That fouls the space and creates a fire risk.

Q: Why are auxiliary blowers needed? At low load there is too little exhaust energy for the turbocharger to supply enough scavenge air. The blowers make up the difference until the turbocharger can take over.

Q: Compression pressure is low on one unit. What do you check? First compare scavenge pressure. If it is normal, suspect that unit's rings, liner, exhaust valve tightness or exhaust valve timing. Confirm with a scavenge port inspection.

Q: Advantages of uniflow over loop scavenging? - Better scavenge efficiency and less mixing of air and gas. - It suits long-stroke engines. - Exhaust timing can be asymmetric. - It gives freedom to vary exhaust valve closing.

Q: Why is a crosshead engine well suited to the heaviest residual fuels? The crosshead design keeps combustion residues out of the crankcase and lets cylinder oil be matched to the fuel independently, which makes burning the heaviest residual fuels at low speed practical. The diaphragm and stuffing box keep acidic residues out of the system oil.

Examiners expect answers related to the engine you actually sailed on. Know its make, its model and its control system.

Key points
  • A two-stroke engine completes its working cycle in one revolution, so each cylinder fires once per turn.
  • Modern slow-speed engines use uniflow scavenging: air in through liner ports, exhaust out through a valve in the cover.
  • The exhaust valve opens before the ports for blowdown, so exhaust gas does not blow back into the scavenge space.
  • Port timing is always symmetrical about BDC; an exhaust valve allows asymmetric, and on electronic engines variable, timing.
  • Judge Pcomp, Pmax and exhaust temperatures against your engine's own shop trial and trend data, not textbook figures.
  • Blow-by and oily deposits in the scavenge space lead to scavenge fires; regular port inspections and clean drains prevent them.
  • Before scavenge space work, engage and lock the turning gear, and lock out starting air, blowers and engine control, per the SMS and permit to work.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between a two-stroke and a four-stroke marine engine?

A two-stroke completes its cycle in one crankshaft revolution by scavenging with pressurised air near BDC. A four-stroke needs two revolutions because it uses separate exhaust and intake strokes. Slow-speed main engines are usually two-stroke; generators and medium-speed propulsion are usually four-stroke.

What is uniflow scavenging?

Air enters through ports around the bottom of the liner and travels upward in one direction, leaving with the exhaust gas through the valve in the cylinder cover. The ports are angled to swirl the air. This gives better cleaning of the cylinder than loop or cross scavenging.

Why does a two-stroke engine need a turbocharger or blower to run?

There is no intake stroke to draw air in, so scavenge air must be supplied above cylinder pressure at BDC. The turbocharger does this at normal loads. Electric auxiliary blowers cover low loads, when exhaust energy is too low.

What causes low compression pressure on one cylinder?

Common causes are worn, broken or sticking piston rings, liner wear, a leaking exhaust valve or wrong exhaust valve closing timing. If scavenge air pressure is low on all units, look at the turbocharger, air cooler or filters instead. Confirm by scavenge port inspection and exhaust valve checks.

Do I need to know exact crank angles for the oral exam?

Know the order of events and the reasons behind them. Timing figures vary by make, model and tuning, so if you quote numbers, make them your own engine's, from its manual. Examiners care more about understanding than memorised angles.

Test yourself

On a uniflow-scavenged two-stroke engine, which event normally happens first as the piston moves down after combustion?

The exhaust valve opens before the ports are uncovered, so blowdown drops cylinder pressure below scavenge air pressure and there is no blow-back into the scavenge space. Reference: maker's timing diagram for uniflow engines.

Why is port timing always symmetrical about BDC?

The piston crown uncovers and covers the ports at the same distance from BDC on the way down and up, so the timing is inherently symmetrical. An exhaust valve (or other added device such as a rotary valve) is needed for asymmetric timing.

Why are electric auxiliary blowers used on a two-stroke main engine?

At low load there is too little exhaust energy for the turbocharger to provide enough scavenge air, so the blowers cut in. They are not a crankcase ventilation or valve-cooling system.

One unit shows high Pmax compared with the others while load is balanced. What is the most likely cause?

Early injection moves combustion earlier, giving a higher peak pressure. Worn rings and a leaking exhaust valve lower compression. A fouled cooler affects all units, not one.

In a crosshead engine, what separates the scavenge space from the crankcase?

The diaphragm with the piston rod stuffing box keeps scavenge space residues out of the crankcase. That is why cylinder oil can be matched to the fuel independently of the system oil on crosshead engines.
