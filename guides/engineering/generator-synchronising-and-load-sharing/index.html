Generator synchronising and load sharing: a practical guide for marine engineers

Quick answer

To parallel an incoming generator, match its voltage, frequency, phase sequence and phase angle to the busbar, then close the breaker just before the in-phase point with the incoming set running slightly fast. Once it is on the board, the governors share active load (kW) and the AVRs share reactive load (kVAr). Get either wrong and you risk reverse power trips, circulating currents or a blackout.

Why we parallel generators and what can go wrong

Most ships run more than one diesel generator at once. The usual reasons are manoeuvring, cargo operations, starting heavy consumers such as bow thrusters and cargo pumps, and redundancy in restricted waters. Changing over from one set to another without a blackout also needs a brief period in parallel.

Paralleling joins two AC sources so that they behave as one supply. The incoming machine must match the busbar closely at the moment the breaker closes. If it does not, the voltage difference across the closing contacts drives a large current surge. The rotor is also jerked into step with the busbar.

The possible results of a bad close include:

  • Heavy mechanical shock to the crankshaft, coupling and alternator rotor.
  • Damage to stator windings and breaker contacts.
  • Overcurrent or reverse power protection tripping one or both sets.
  • A blackout, which is the worst outcome when it happens during pilotage.

This is why examiners treat synchronising as a core watchkeeping skill and not just a switchboard routine.

The four conditions for paralleling

Before the incoming breaker closes, these four conditions must be met:

Condition How you check it What happens if it is wrong
Same phase sequence Phase sequence indicator after any work on cables or terminals; normally fixed at installation Closing is effectively a short circuit between phases. This is severe damage, not just a trip
Same (or very nearly the same) voltage Voltmeter with selector switch, comparing incoming against busbar Reactive circulating current flows and the sets share kVAr unevenly
Same (or very nearly the same) frequency Frequency meters, and how fast the synchroscope pointer rotates One set is driven hard onto load, or is motored, at the instant of closing
In phase at the moment of closing Synchroscope pointer at the in-phase mark, or synchronising lamps Current surge and torque shock that increase with the phase angle error

Phase sequence is normally proven during commissioning or after maintenance. Day to day, the watchkeeper deals with voltage, frequency and phase angle.

The permitted differences in voltage, frequency and phase angle are set by the manufacturer, class and the check-synchronising relay. Use the figures in your switchboard manual, not a number remembered from college.

Step by step: manual synchronising with a synchroscope

The sequence below is the general method. Always follow your ship's switchboard instructions and SMS.

  1. Check the load and the engine. Confirm that you need the extra set. Check that the prime mover is ready: lube oil pressure, cooling water, fuel, no alarms, and that it has been prepared and warmed through as the maker requires.
  2. Start the generator and let it run up to rated speed. Confirm that voltage has built up and is stable.
  3. Match the voltage. Use the voltmeter selector to compare incoming and busbar voltage. On most modern sets the AVR holds this automatically. Adjust only if your system provides a manual voltage trim.
  4. Switch on the synchroscope for the incoming generator. It is short-time rated on many boards, so do not leave it switched on longer than needed.
  5. Adjust the frequency using the governor raise/lower control. Aim for the incoming set to run slightly faster than the busbar, so the pointer rotates slowly in the FAST direction (clockwise on most instruments; check the dial marking).
  6. Watch the pointer. It should turn slowly and steadily enough for you to judge the closing moment. If it is spinning quickly, or jerking, the frequencies are too far apart or a governor is hunting.
  7. Close the breaker just before the pointer reaches the in-phase (12 o'clock) mark. The small lead allows for the breaker's closing time, so the contacts actually make at the in-phase point.
  8. Confirm the set has taken load. The incoming kW meter should show a small positive load and no reverse power alarm. Switch off the synchroscope.
  9. Share the load. Raise the governor on the incoming set and lower it on the running set until the kW is shared as required. Keep the bus frequency at its rated value.
  10. Check reactive sharing. Compare ammeters and power factor meters. They should be close if kW is balanced and the AVRs are working correctly.

Why slightly fast? At the moment of closing, a faster incoming set picks up a little load and acts as a generator. A slower one would be driven by the busbar as a motor. That draws reverse power and may trip on the reverse power relay before you can correct it.

Synchronising lamps are a common backup to the synchroscope. With the dark-lamp method, the lamps go dark when the two supplies are in phase. Their weakness is that a lamp can look dark while there is still some voltage across it, so the bright-lamp or mixed (two bright, one dark) arrangements are sometimes preferred. Lamps also give a phase sequence indication on some arrangements.

Check-synchronising relays are fitted on many boards. They block a manual close unless voltage, frequency and phase are within limits. They are a safeguard, not a substitute for watching the synchroscope. Auto-synchronisers in a power management system (PMS) do the whole job automatically. You still need to be able to do it by hand when the PMS is faulty.

Load sharing: kW and kVAr are controlled separately

This is the point examiners probe most.

  • Active load (kW) is shared by the governors. More fuel to one engine makes it take more kW. On the board, it cannot speed up because it is locked to the busbar frequency, so it pushes the others' share down.
  • Reactive load (kVAr) is shared by the AVRs / excitation. Raising one set's excitation makes it carry more reactive current. It does not change its kW share in any meaningful way.

Speed droop. Governors on paralleled sets usually have droop: their speed falls slightly as load rises. When two sets with similar droop settings are in parallel, they share load changes in a stable, predictable way. If one set ran isochronous (constant speed) and another on droop, the isochronous set would try to take every load change. Electronic governors with a load-sharing link, or the PMS, can run sets isochronously while still sharing kW correctly.

Reactive sharing uses the same idea. AVRs are given voltage droop (quadrature droop) or cross-current compensation, so that a set taking more than its share of kVAr lowers its own excitation.

What good sharing looks like:

  • kW shared in proportion to set rating, or as the PMS dictates.
  • Ammeter readings that follow the kW split.
  • Power factor meters reading about the same on each set.

If the kW are equal but one ammeter reads clearly higher and the power factors differ, you have a reactive sharing problem. The cause is in the excitation, not the governor.

Taking a generator off the board

Taking a set off is the reverse process, and the mistakes are just as costly.

  1. Check capacity. Confirm that the remaining sets can carry the full load with a sensible margin. Remember the large consumers that may start without warning.
  2. Transfer the load. Lower the governor on the outgoing set and raise it on the remaining sets. Hold the bus frequency steady as you go.
  3. Open the breaker at very low load. Bring the outgoing set down to a small positive load, then open its breaker.
    • Do not open it at full load. Throwing the load onto the other sets causes a frequency dip and possibly a preferential or overload trip.
    • Do not overshoot into reverse power, which will trip it anyway and shows poor control.
  4. Run it off load for the cooling-down period recommended by the engine maker, then stop it. Leave it on standby or auto-start as your SMS requires.

With a PMS, load transfer and opening the breaker are usually automatic when you select the set to stop. Watch that the transfer actually completes.

Checks, maintenance and common faults

Routine checks and maintenance. The exact jobs and intervals depend on the ship's planned maintenance system and the maker.

  • Test the synchroscope, sync lamps and check-sync relay.
  • Test the protective relays: reverse power, overcurrent, under-voltage and preferential trips. These are often done by a class-approved service engineer or under the ship's planned maintenance system.
  • Check the ACB: spring charging, closing coil, undervoltage release and contact condition.
  • Look over the governor actuator and linkage (where mechanical) and check the speed control response.
  • Check AVR settings and connections, and the condition of the switchboard terminals.

Faults and their likely causes:

Symptom Likely area to investigate
Synchroscope pointer will not slow or keeps hunting Governor hunting, sticky fuel linkage, poor fuel or a misfiring cylinder on the incoming set
Breaker will not close Check-sync relay blocking; spring not charged; undervoltage release not energised (no voltage on the incoming side); interlock; control supply fault
Set trips on reverse power just after closing Incoming set slower than the busbar when closed; governor fault; fuel starvation
kW sharing drifts after a load change Unequal droop settings; governor fault; load-sharing line or PMS fault
kW equal but unequal amps and power factors AVR fault, wrong droop compensation, excitation problem
Sharp bang and kick on closing, voltage dip Closed out of phase: report it and have the engine coupling, alternator and breaker inspected as required

If a close went badly, log it and tell the 2nd Engineer or Chief Engineer. Do not just try again.

Safety points on the switchboard

  • Dead bus closing is different. When the busbar is dead, for example the first set on the board or after a blackout, there is nothing to synchronise to. The breaker is closed on to a dead bus. Auto-start and blackout recovery logic handle this, and some check-sync relays have a dead-bus permissive. Never assume the bus is dead without checking the busbar voltmeter.
  • Never bypass a check-sync relay or interlock except under a documented procedure with the Chief Engineer's authority.
  • Treat the main switchboard as live. Follow your permit-to-work and lock-out/tag-out procedures for any work behind the panels. High-voltage installations need the specific HV safety rules and training.
  • Communicate with the bridge. Tell the bridge before deliberate changes in restricted waters. Keep enough running capacity during manoeuvring, as your company SMS requires.
  • Know the preferential trip sequence so you understand what the board will shed if a set is overloaded.

Oral exam questions and how to answer them

Examiners at operational level want a clear sequence and the reasons behind it. Practise saying these answers aloud.

  • "What conditions must be met before paralleling?" Same phase sequence, voltage and frequency, and in phase at the moment of closing. Then say how you check each one.
  • "Why close with the incoming set slightly fast?" So that it takes load as a generator straight away, and is not motored into a reverse power trip.
  • "Why close just before twelve o'clock?" To allow for the breaker's closing time, so the contacts make at the in-phase point.
  • "How do you share kW? And kVAr?" kW with the governors, kVAr with the AVRs/excitation. Mention droop.
  • "kW is shared equally but ammeters differ. Why?" Unequal excitation causing circulating reactive current. Check AVRs.
  • "What does the reverse power relay protect?" It protects the prime mover from being motored, and the board from a set that has lost its drive, for example through fuel failure.
  • "What happens if you close out of phase?" Large current surge, mechanical shock to the engine and alternator, possible trips and blackout.
  • "The synchroscope fails. What now?" Use the synchronising lamps if fitted, or the auto-synchroniser, as your board allows. Report the defect.

A good habit in the oral is to finish each answer with what you would report and to whom.

Key points
  • Four conditions: same phase sequence, voltage and frequency, and in phase at closing.
  • Run the incoming set slightly fast and close just before the in-phase mark.
  • Governors share active load (kW); AVRs share reactive load (kVAr).
  • Equal kW with unequal amps and power factors points to an excitation problem.
  • Take a set off by transferring load first, then open the breaker at low positive load.
  • Reverse power protection stops a generator being motored by the busbar.
  • Use maker and class settings for tolerances; never bypass check-sync interlocks.

Frequently asked questions

Which way should the synchroscope rotate before closing the breaker?

It should turn slowly in the FAST direction, meaning the incoming generator is slightly faster than the busbar. On most instruments this is clockwise, but always check the marking on your own dial. Close just before the pointer reaches the in-phase mark.

What happens if I close the generator breaker out of phase?

The voltage difference across the contacts drives a heavy current surge, and the rotor is jerked into step with the busbar. This can damage windings, couplings and breaker contacts, and may trip one or both sets, causing a blackout. Report any bad close so the equipment can be inspected.

Why is load not shared equally even though both generators are the same size?

If kW is unequal, look at governor settings, especially droop, and at the governor or PMS load-sharing function. If kW is equal but amps and power factors differ, the problem is reactive sharing in the AVRs or excitation.

Why did my generator trip on reverse power right after paralleling?

Usually it was running slower than the busbar when the breaker closed, so the board drove it as a motor. It can also point to a governor or fuel supply problem on that engine. Check before you try to parallel again.

Do I need to synchronise when the switchboard is dead after a blackout?

No. With a dead bus there is nothing to synchronise to, so the first generator is closed on to the dead bus. Confirm the bus really is dead on the busbar voltmeter before closing. Every set added after that must be synchronised normally.

Test yourself

Which control mainly determines how active load (kW) is shared between paralleled generators?

kW sharing depends on the fuel input to each prime mover, which the governor controls. Excitation governs reactive (kVAr) sharing. (Operational-level electrical competence, STCW Code Table A-III/1)

Two paralleled generators show equal kW but different ammeter readings and power factors. What is the most likely cause?

Equal kW means the governors are sharing correctly. Differing current and power factor point to uneven kVAr sharing from the AVRs. (STCW Code Table A-III/1)

Why is the incoming generator run slightly faster than the busbar before closing?

A slightly faster set picks up positive load on closing. A slower one is driven by the busbar and may trip on reverse power. (Switchboard practice; STCW Code Table A-III/1)

When should the breaker be closed during manual synchronising?

Closing slightly early allows for the breaker's closing time, so the contacts make in phase. Six o'clock is fully out of phase. (Switchboard practice; STCW Code Table A-III/1)

What is the correct way to take a generator off the board?

Load is transferred using the governor controls first, which avoids a frequency dip, overload or a needless reverse power trip. (Switchboard practice; STCW Code Table A-III/1)
