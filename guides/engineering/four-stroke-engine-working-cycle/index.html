Four-stroke engine working cycle: a practical guide for marine engineers

Quick answer

A four-stroke diesel completes one working cycle (intake, compression, power, exhaust) in two crankshaft revolutions, so each cylinder fires once every 720° of crank rotation and the camshaft turns at half crankshaft speed. On board you meet it mainly in generator engines and in medium-speed propulsion engines. You need to understand valve timing, overlap, tappet clearance and how the cycle shows up in exhaust temperatures and pressures, because examiners commonly test these points.

What the four-stroke cycle is and where you meet it on board

In a four-stroke engine each cylinder needs four piston strokes, which means two crankshaft revolutions, to complete one working cycle. Only one of those four strokes produces power. The other three are spent pumping air in, compressing it and pushing exhaust gas out.

On merchant ships you will find four-stroke engines in:

  • Auxiliary generator engines. These are the most common case on almost every ship type, usually medium or high speed.
  • Medium-speed main propulsion. Ferries, ro-ros, offshore vessels, tugs, dredgers and many smaller cargo ships use them, normally through a reduction gearbox and often driving a controllable pitch propeller.
  • Emergency generators and lifeboat engines. These are typically small high-speed units.

Most marine four-strokes are trunk-piston engines. There is no crosshead, so the piston side thrust is taken directly by the liner. The crankcase oil splashes and is thrown up onto the liner walls (some larger engines add cylinder lubricators fed with the same system oil), so cylinder and crankcase lubrication share the same oil. This single fact explains a lot of four-stroke maintenance, including oil contamination, oil consumption and lube oil analysis.

The four strokes, step by step

Follow one cylinder through a full cycle, starting with the piston at top dead centre (TDC) at the end of the exhaust stroke.

  1. Intake (suction) stroke: TDC to BDC. The inlet valve is already open. The piston moves down and draws in air. On a turbocharged engine the charge air is pushed in under boost pressure through the charge air cooler. The exhaust valve closes shortly after TDC.
  2. Compression stroke: BDC to TDC. The inlet valve closes after BDC. Both valves are now shut and the piston compresses the air. Its pressure and temperature rise well above the self-ignition temperature of the fuel.
  3. Power (expansion) stroke: TDC to BDC. Fuel is injected shortly before TDC. After a brief ignition delay it burns. Gas pressure peaks just after TDC and drives the piston down, turning the crankshaft. This is the only stroke that delivers work.
  4. Exhaust stroke: BDC to TDC. The exhaust valve opens before BDC. Hot gas blows down to the exhaust manifold and turbocharger turbine. The rising piston then pushes out the remaining gas. Near TDC the inlet valve opens again and the cycle repeats.

Key numbers you can state with confidence:

Item Four-stroke Two-stroke
Crank degrees per cycle 720° 360°
Power strokes per cylinder per revolution ½ 1
Camshaft speed Half crankshaft speed Same as crankshaft speed
Gas exchange Piston pumping plus turbocharger Scavenge air through ports, no pumping stroke

For an in-line engine with evenly spaced firing, the firing interval is 720° divided by the number of cylinders. A six-cylinder in-line four-stroke therefore fires every 120°.

Valve timing, overlap and the camshaft

The valves never open and close exactly at TDC or BDC. Gas has inertia and valves take time to lift, so the events are spread out to fill and empty the cylinder as fully as possible.

  • Inlet opens before TDC. The valve is already well open when the piston starts down.
  • Inlet closes after BDC. The moving air column keeps flowing in even as the piston starts to rise. This is the ram effect, and it improves volumetric efficiency.
  • Exhaust opens before BDC. You give up a little expansion work. In return, cylinder pressure blows down before the piston starts its exhaust stroke, which cuts pumping losses and delivers energy to the turbocharger.
  • Exhaust closes after TDC. The outgoing gas keeps flowing and helps clear the clearance volume.

Valve overlap is the period around TDC, between the exhaust and intake strokes, when both valves are open together. Charge air sweeps through the combustion space. This purges residual gas and cools the exhaust valve, piston crown and cylinder head. Turbocharged engines generally have a larger overlap than naturally aspirated ones, because boost pressure drives the scavenging flow.

The exact timing angles are set by the engine builder and are given in the maker's manual and on the flywheel or timing marks. Do not quote one engine's figures as if they were universal. In an exam, describe the principle and say the actual degrees come from the maker.

The camshaft is gear- or chain-driven from the crankshaft at half speed, so each cam operates its valve once per 720°. Cam followers, push rods and rocker arms transmit the lift. Where one rocker opens two valves, a valve bridge (yoke) is used. Fuel pump cams on jerk-pump engines sit on the same camshaft, so camshaft timing also fixes injection timing. Common-rail engines time injection electronically.

What normal looks like: reading the cycle in service

You cannot see the cycle, but you can see its results. Always compare against the maker's test-bed or sea-trial data, the engine's own trend and the other units. Do not compare against a figure remembered from another ship.

  • Exhaust gas temperatures per cylinder. These should be close to each other at a given load. A single unit drifting away from the mean is the classic first sign of trouble. Turbocharger inlet and outlet temperatures complete the picture.
  • Charge air pressure and temperature. Boost should match load. High charge air temperature after the cooler points to cooler fouling or cooling water problems.
  • Compression and maximum combustion pressures. These are taken with a peak pressure indicator or electronic monitoring where the engine has indicator cocks or sensors. Low compression with normal timing points to leakage or low charge air. Changed maximum pressure with normal compression points to fuel or timing.
  • Lube oil pressure and temperature, cooling water temperatures. These must stay within the maker's bands. Watch the trends.
  • Crankcase condition. Check oil mist detector or bearing temperature readings where fitted, and crankcase pressure.
  • Smoke and sound. A clean exhaust under steady load and an even running note are both normal. A knocking unit or uneven note is not.

On generator engines, also watch load sharing and frequency stability. A unit that will not take its share of load may have a combustion problem rather than a governor one.

Routine checks and maintenance

Running hours, the maker's manual and your company planned maintenance system set the intervals. The usual items tied directly to the working cycle are:

  • tappet (valve) clearances
  • injector overhaul and pressure testing
  • cylinder head and valve overhaul, including seat grinding and checking valve rotators
  • unit overhauls, which cover piston, rings, liner gauging, connecting rod and bearings
  • turbocharger washing and overhaul
  • charge air cooler cleaning
  • lube oil analysis
  • crankcase inspections

Checking tappet clearance: typical sequence

  1. Stop the engine and let it cool. Clearances are specified cold unless the maker states otherwise.
  2. Close and isolate the starting air and block the start. Engage the turning gear and apply lock-out tag-out.
  3. Turn the engine until the unit is at TDC on the firing (end of compression) position. Both valves are closed and both rockers have free play. You can confirm this when the valves of the paired cylinder are in overlap.
  4. Where a valve bridge is fitted, set the bridge first so it presses both valves evenly.
  5. Check each clearance with a feeler gauge of the maker's thickness. Adjust it, lock the nut and recheck, because tightening the lock nut can change the clearance.
  6. Repeat unit by unit in firing order. Remove the turning gear and restore air only when everyone is clear.

Some engines use hydraulic valve lash adjusters, which need no feeler gauge check. Know which type your engine has.

Why the clearance matters:

  • Too small. As the valve stem heats and expands, the valve may not seat fully. Hot gas leaks past and burns the valve and seat.
  • Too large. The valve opens late and closes early, with less lift and more noise. Hammering then wears the cam, follower and rocker.

Bumping clearance is the gap between the piston crown and the cylinder head at TDC. On many engines it is checked with lead wire after an overhaul. It affects compression ratio and therefore starting and combustion.

Faults and symptoms

Symptom Likely causes to investigate
One cylinder exhaust temperature high Leaking or dribbling injector, poor atomisation, late injection, leaking or burnt exhaust valve, valve not seating because of tight clearance
One cylinder exhaust temperature low Injector or fuel pump not delivering, sticking fuel rack, faulty thermocouple (check this first)
All exhaust temperatures high Overload, fouled turbocharger or charge air cooler, high charge air temperature, restricted air intake or exhaust back pressure
Black smoke Incomplete combustion from overload, air shortage, poor injection or turbocharger problems
Blue or grey-blue smoke Lube oil burning from worn rings or liner, or oil carry-over from the turbocharger or valve guides
White smoke Water in fuel or in the cylinder, or unburnt fuel on a cold start
Low compression pressure Worn rings or liner, leaking valves, low charge air pressure, excessive bumping clearance
Knocking Early injection, poor fuel ignition quality, mechanical looseness, or a bearing problem (stop and investigate)
Rising crankcase oil mist or bearing temperature Hot spot or bearing failure developing. This is a crankcase explosion risk.
Lube oil viscosity rising or falling, water present Falling viscosity: fuel dilution. Rising viscosity: soot/insolubles, HFO contamination or oxidation. Water present: cooling water leak or condensation. Confirm by analysis.

Work from the simplest check outward. Verify the sensor, compare with the other units and the trend, then open up.

Safety around four-stroke engines

  • Crankcase explosion risk. An oil mist alarm or rising bearing temperature means slow down or stop according to your procedures, and do not open crankcase doors straight away. Wait the cooling period given by the maker and your SMS before opening, so a hot spot cannot ignite the oil mist when fresh air rushes in. Keep everyone clear of the explosion relief valves.
  • Unexpected rotation. Before any work on running gear, valves or injectors, isolate the starting air, block the start, engage the turning gear and apply lock-out tag-out. Generator engines on standby and auto-start are a particular trap, so make sure the power management system cannot start the engine.
  • High-pressure fuel. Injector leaks and fuel pipe failures can cause spray fires on hot exhaust surfaces. Keep jacketed pipes and hot-surface insulation intact. Never bypass leak-off alarms.
  • Hot surfaces and pressurised systems. Exhaust manifolds, turbochargers, cooling water and lube oil all carry burn and pressure hazards.
  • Hydraulic tools. Cylinder head and main bearing studs are often tensioned hydraulically at very high pressure. Use the maker's tools and values and stand clear of the line of the jack.

Always follow the maker's instructions and your company SMS. Where they differ from general guidance, they take precedence for your engine.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Answer each one in plain words and link it to practice on board.

  • Why does the camshaft run at half crankshaft speed? Each valve opens once per cycle, and a cycle takes two revolutions.
  • Draw the valve timing diagram and explain each event. State the principle (inlet opens before TDC and closes after BDC, exhaust opens before BDC and closes after TDC). Say that the actual angles come from the maker.
  • What is valve overlap and why is it bigger on turbocharged engines? It is the period around TDC when both valves are open. It scavenges residual gas and cools the hot parts. Boost pressure makes the scavenging flow effective.
  • What happens if tappet clearance is too small, or too large? Too small causes a burnt valve and seat. Too large causes late opening, reduced lift and mechanical wear.
  • How do you find TDC on firing for a given unit? Use the flywheel marks, then check that both valves are closed with free rockers, or that the paired cylinder is in overlap.
  • One unit shows high exhaust temperature. What do you do? Check the thermocouple, compare loads, check the injector and valve clearance, and take a pressure reading if possible.
  • Compare four-stroke and two-stroke engines. Cover cycle length, gas exchange, trunk piston versus crosshead, shared versus separate cylinder lubrication, and typical shipboard uses.
  • Why is lube oil contamination a bigger issue on a trunk-piston engine? The same system oil lubricates the liner and the crankcase, so combustion products, unburnt fuel and acids reach the crankcase oil directly.

Examiners want to see that you have actually set clearances and read exhaust temperature trends. Bring your own engine into your answers.

Key points
  • A four-stroke cycle takes 720° of crank rotation, giving one power stroke per cylinder every two revolutions.
  • The camshaft runs at half crankshaft speed. Firing interval for an evenly fired in-line engine is 720° divided by the number of cylinders.
  • Inlet opens before TDC and closes after BDC. Exhaust opens before BDC and closes after TDC. Exact angles come from the maker.
  • Valve overlap around TDC scavenges residual gas and cools hot components, and is larger on turbocharged engines.
  • Set tappet clearance cold, at TDC on firing, with starting air isolated and turning gear engaged. Too tight burns valves.
  • Watch exhaust temperature deviation between units and against trend. It is often the first sign of injector or valve faults.
  • Do not open a crankcase after an oil mist alarm until the maker's and SMS cooling period has passed.

Frequently asked questions

How many revolutions does a four-stroke engine take to complete one cycle?

Two crankshaft revolutions, or 720° of rotation. Each cylinder fires once in that period, which is why the camshaft is geared to turn at half crankshaft speed.

Why are most ship generators four-stroke engines?

Medium- and high-speed four-strokes are compact for their power. They run at speeds that suit direct coupling to an alternator and they handle load changes well. Two-stroke slow-speed engines are better suited to driving a large propeller directly, so each type is used where it fits best.

What is valve overlap in a four-stroke engine?

It is the period around TDC, between the exhaust and intake strokes, when the inlet and exhaust valves are open together. Incoming charge air sweeps out remaining exhaust gas and cools the exhaust valve, piston crown and cylinder head. Turbocharged engines usually have more overlap than naturally aspirated ones.

Why is tappet clearance checked with the piston at TDC on firing?

At that point both valves are closed and the cam followers sit on the base circle of the cams, so the rockers have their full free play. Checking anywhere else may give a false reading because a cam could be starting to lift its valve. Always confirm you are on the firing TDC and not the overlap TDC.

What does blue smoke from a four-stroke auxiliary engine mean?

Blue or grey-blue smoke usually means lubricating oil is being burnt. Common causes are worn piston rings or liners, worn valve guides, or oil leaking past turbocharger seals. Check lube oil consumption and crankcase pressure, and plan an inspection according to the maker's guidance.

Test yourself

At what speed does the camshaft of a four-stroke engine rotate relative to the crankshaft?

Each valve operates once per cycle and a four-stroke cycle takes two revolutions, so the camshaft turns at half crank speed. Basic four-stroke principle covered under STCW Code Table A-III/1.

When does valve overlap occur in a four-stroke engine?

Both valves are open together near TDC as the exhaust stroke ends and the intake stroke begins, giving scavenging and cooling. At TDC on compression both valves are closed.

What is the main risk of setting tappet clearance too small?

With too little clearance, thermal expansion of the valve stem can hold the valve slightly open, so hot gas leaks past and burns it. Late opening and noise are symptoms of excessive clearance.

Why does the exhaust valve open before BDC on the power stroke?

Early exhaust opening gives up a little expansion work but lets the gas blow down, so the piston does not have to push against high pressure on the exhaust stroke. It also supplies energy to the turbocharger.

What is the firing interval of an evenly fired six-cylinder in-line four-stroke engine?

Firing interval for an in-line four-stroke is 720° divided by the number of cylinders, so 720 ÷ 6 = 120°. 60° would apply to a six-cylinder two-stroke (360 ÷ 6).
