Turbocharger Operation and Surging: A Practical Guide for Marine Engineers

Quick answer

A turbocharger uses exhaust gas energy to drive a compressor that supplies the engine with more air than it could draw in alone, giving more power and cleaner combustion. Surging is an unstable reversal of airflow through the compressor, heard as barking or howling. It happens when the compressor is asked to produce more pressure than it can sustain at the airflow passing through it, usually because of fouling, restrictions, unequal cylinder loading or sudden load changes.

What a turbocharger does and why the engine depends on it

A diesel engine's power is limited by how much air it can get into the cylinder. A turbocharger recovers energy from exhaust gas that would otherwise go up the funnel. That energy drives a compressor, which pushes a denser charge of air into the engine.

More air means:

  • more fuel can be burnt completely, so more power from the same cylinder size
  • better combustion and lower specific fuel consumption
  • on a two-stroke, effective scavenging of exhaust gas from the cylinder

A modern large slow-speed two-stroke has no pumping stroke, so it cannot scavenge itself and depends entirely on the turbocharger and auxiliary blowers for scavenge air. At low load there is not enough exhaust energy to drive the turbocharger hard enough, so electrically driven auxiliary blowers cut in automatically to supply scavenge air. If you lose the turbocharger at sea, the engine's available output drops sharply. That is why the turbocharger gets so much attention in watchkeeping, maintenance and orals.

Two arrangements are common:

  • Constant pressure system. All cylinders exhaust into a large receiver, and the turbine gets a steady flow. This is typical on modern two-strokes.
  • Pulse system. Short, narrow exhaust pipes deliver the pressure pulse of each cylinder to the turbine. It responds better at part load and is found on many four-strokes and older designs.

How it works, step by step

Follow the gas and the air through a typical two-stroke installation.

Gas side

  1. Exhaust leaves each cylinder through the exhaust valve into the exhaust receiver.
  2. Gas passes through the nozzle ring, which accelerates it and directs it onto the turbine blades at the correct angle.
  3. The gas expands through the turbine (axial on large two-stroke units, radial on many smaller units) and turns the rotor.
  4. Gas leaves through the turbine outlet to the exhaust gas economiser, then the funnel.

Air side

  1. Engine room air is drawn in through the filter and silencer.
  2. The compressor impeller, on the same shaft as the turbine, accelerates the air. The inducer guides it in smoothly.
  3. The diffuser and volute casing convert the air's velocity into pressure.
  4. Compression heats the air. The charge (scavenge) air cooler cools it to increase density and keep combustion temperatures under control.
  5. A water mist catcher removes condensed moisture before the air reaches the scavenge receiver and cylinders.

Bearings and seals

  • Many modern large units use plain bearings lubricated from the engine's lube oil system or a dedicated supply.
  • Older and some smaller designs use rolling-element bearings with their own oil sumps.
  • Labyrinth seals, often supplied with sealing air from the compressor, keep oil out of the gas and air paths and keep gas out of the bearing housing.

The arrangement depends on the maker and model, so always check the instruction manual for your unit.

Normal parameters: what to watch and how to judge them

There is no universal "correct" figure. Normal values depend on the engine, the turbocharger model, the load and the ambient conditions. Judge the readings against the shop test and sea trial records, the maker's performance data, and your own trend.

Parameters to log and trend:

Parameter What a change can tell you
Turbocharger speed Compare with load. Speed rising for the same load can point to a fouled nozzle ring. Speed falling can point to a fouled or damaged turbine.
Scavenge air pressure Low for the load suggests air-side fouling or a turbine problem.
Exhaust temperature before and after the turbine The temperature drop across the turbine shows how much energy is being recovered.
Exhaust temperature of each cylinder A large spread points to unequal loading, which is a classic surge trigger.
Differential pressure across the air filter Rising means the filter is clogging.
Differential pressure across the air cooler (air side) Rising means the cooler fins are fouled.
Cooling water temperature into and out of the cooler, and scavenge air temperature Shows how well the cooler is performing.
Lube oil pressure and temperature (where fitted) Bearing health.
Vibration (where monitored) Rotor imbalance or bearing wear.

Practical tip: correct readings to comparable conditions (load, ambient temperature, back pressure) before you conclude anything. A hot engine room in the tropics alone shifts several of these values.

Surging: what it is, why it happens and what to do

What it is. Every compressor has a stable operating range, shown on the maker's compressor map. The boundary on the low-flow, high-pressure-ratio side is the surge line. If the operating point crosses that line, the compressor can no longer hold the pressure against the flow. Air flow breaks down and briefly reverses back through the impeller, then recovers, and the cycle repeats.

Symptoms

  • loud barking, coughing or howling from the turbocharger
  • fluctuating scavenge air pressure and turbocharger speed
  • rising exhaust temperatures
  • possibly black smoke and engine speed fluctuation

Common causes

  • Air side restriction: dirty filter, fouled compressor or diffuser, or a fouled air cooler.
  • Gas side: a fouled or partly blocked nozzle ring can push the turbocharger to a higher speed and pressure ratio. Damage to the turbine or nozzle ring upsets the balance between turbine and compressor.
  • Engine side: unequal cylinder loading, misfiring, faulty fuel injection, or a cylinder cut out without following the maker's guidance. Fouled scavenge ports, a sticking exhaust valve or a scavenge fire can also cause it.
  • Sudden load changes: crash manoeuvres, emergency stops and propeller racing in heavy weather.
  • Heavy-running propeller: hull fouling or heavy weather moves the operating point towards the surge line.
  • Multi-turbocharger engines: one unit performing worse than the others.
  • Mismatching after engine modifications or derating. The turbocharger and its nozzle ring are NOx-influencing components, so any change must stay within the engine's Technical File or be approved under the NOx Technical Code 2008.

Worked scenario. You are on watch in heavy weather. The turbocharger barks intermittently each time the stern lifts.

  1. Inform the chief engineer and the bridge.
  2. Reduce engine load. If the engine's control system allows, consider whether its heavy-weather or anti-racing function is in use.
  3. Check the exhaust temperature of each cylinder for any that are abnormal.
  4. Check the scavenge pressure and the differential pressures across the filter and cooler.
  5. Check the scavenge space drains for oil discharge, and check the scavenge air space temperatures and alarms for signs of a scavenge fire.
  6. Once conditions allow, find and fix the underlying cause, for example washing, cleaning or a fuel injector. Do not simply accept a lower load permanently.

Repeated surging stresses the compressor blades and bearings and can lead to failure. Never ignore it.

Checks, washing and maintenance

Routine checks

  • Log the parameters above every watch and compare them with the trend.
  • Listen for changes in noise. Check for oil leaks and exhaust gas leaks around the casing and expansion joints.
  • Check that the auxiliary blowers start and stop automatically at the correct scavenge pressure.
  • Drain the air cooler and water mist catcher, and check that the drains are clear.

Washing. Follow the maker's procedure exactly. The load, the water quantity, the duration and the drains to open are all specified.

  • Compressor side: water injected while running. Done at the load the maker specifies.
  • Turbine side, wet wash: usually done at reduced load so the blades are not thermally shocked.
  • Turbine side, dry wash: solid granules are blown in while running at service load. This is common on engines burning heavy fuel.

Washing at the wrong load, or forgetting the drains, can cause blade damage or corrosion.

Planned maintenance

  • Clean the air filter, or replace the elements.
  • Clean the charge air cooler on both the air side and the water side.
  • Inspect and overhaul the turbocharger at the running-hour intervals the maker sets in the PMS. This covers bearings, the nozzle ring, the rotor, seals and clearances, recorded against the maker's limits.
  • Take oil samples where the unit has its own lubrication.
  • Have the rotor balanced by an approved service centre after repair.

Emergency operation. Most makers give a procedure for running with a damaged turbocharger. Depending on the design, this may mean locking the rotor, or removing the cartridge and fitting blanking covers. The engine then runs at a reduced load on the auxiliary blowers or the remaining turbochargers. The permitted load and the method come only from the engine and turbocharger manuals.

Faults and symptoms at a glance

Symptom Likely causes to check
Surging or barking Fouled filter, compressor or cooler; fouled nozzle ring; unequal cylinder loading; sudden load change; heavy propeller
High exhaust temperatures, low scavenge pressure Fouled turbine or compressor; dirty air cooler; low turbine efficiency
High turbocharger speed for the load Fouled or partly blocked nozzle ring
Low turbocharger speed for the load Fouled or damaged turbine blades; exhaust leakage before the turbine
Increased vibration Rotor imbalance from deposits or blade damage; bearing wear
Oil in the scavenge air or exhaust Worn seals; low sealing air; high oil level or pressure in the bearing housing
High scavenge air temperature Fouled cooler; low cooling water flow or high inlet temperature
Water in the scavenge space Leaking cooler tubes; ineffective mist catcher; blocked drains

Treat a sudden change more seriously than a slow drift. A slow drift usually means fouling. A sudden change suggests damage, such as a broken blade or a failed bearing.

Safety around turbochargers

  • Hot surfaces and fire. Casings and exhaust pipes run very hot. Keep insulation and lagging intact. Repair fuel and lube oil leaks near hot surfaces at once, because oil spraying onto an unlagged casing is a classic engine room fire.
  • Rotor failure. Class rules require turbochargers above a certain size to be type-tested for containment of a burst rotor, but overspeed and blade failure remain serious hazards. Stand clear during abnormal running.
  • Before working on it, stop the engine and isolate it under the permit-to-work and lock-out tag-out procedures. Make sure the rotor cannot be turned by draught through the exhaust or by other turbochargers on a common receiver. Lock the rotor where the maker provides a means.
  • Let it cool before opening it. Use lifting gear rated and certified for rotor and cartridge removal.
  • Washing involves hot surfaces and water under pressure. Wear PPE and follow the maker's sequence.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: Define surging.
Unstable operation of the compressor in which airflow breaks down and briefly reverses. It happens when the operating point crosses the surge line on the compressor map.

Q: Give three causes and your immediate action.
Causes: a fouled filter or cooler, unequal cylinder loading, and propeller racing. Immediate action: reduce load, inform the chief engineer and bridge, then find the cause by checking cylinder temperatures, differential pressures, the scavenge space drains for oil, and the scavenge air space temperatures and alarms.

Q: Why is the turbine washed at reduced load?
To avoid thermal shock to the hot blades and to stay within the maker's conditions for safe water injection.

Q: What happens if a turbocharger fails at sea on a single-turbocharger two-stroke?
You follow the maker's emergency procedure: lock the rotor or remove it and blank it off. The engine then runs at a heavily restricted load with air supplied by the auxiliary blowers.

Q: Why are auxiliary blowers fitted?
At low load there is not enough exhaust energy to supply scavenge air, and a modern two-stroke has no pumping stroke to scavenge itself.

Q: How does a fouled nozzle ring show on your log?
Turbocharger speed rises for the same load. Pressure and temperature on the gas side change too, and the unit moves nearer to surge.

Examiners want a logical sequence and a sense of safety. Saying "follow the maker's instructions" is correct, but only after you have shown you understand why.

Key points
  • A turbocharger uses exhaust energy to compress intake air, raising power and improving combustion.
  • Surging is flow breakdown and reversal when the compressor's operating point crosses the surge line.
  • Main surge triggers are air-side fouling, a fouled nozzle ring, unequal cylinder loading and sudden load changes such as propeller racing.
  • On surging, reduce load first, then trace the cause; never accept repeated surging.
  • Judge parameters against shop test, sea trial and trend data, not a single fixed number.
  • Washing load, method and intervals come from the maker's manual and the PMS.
  • Before any work, isolate the unit and lock the rotor against turning by draught or other turbochargers.

Frequently asked questions

What is turbocharger surging on a ship's main engine?

Surging is unstable compressor operation in which airflow breaks down and briefly reverses through the impeller. It is heard as barking or howling and shows as fluctuating scavenge pressure and turbocharger speed. It happens when the compressor must produce more pressure than it can sustain at the airflow passing through it.

What should the engineer on watch do if the turbocharger starts surging?

Reduce engine load and inform the chief engineer and the bridge. Then check the exhaust temperature of each cylinder, the scavenge pressure, and the differential pressures across the filter and cooler. Check the scavenge space drains for oil discharge and the scavenge air space temperatures and alarms for signs of a scavenge fire. Once the engine is stable, correct the underlying cause, such as fouling or a faulty injector.

Why is a turbocharger turbine washed at reduced load?

Injecting water onto very hot turbine blades at full load risks thermal shock and blade damage. Makers therefore specify a reduced load and a set procedure for wet washing. Dry washing with granules is normally done at service load, again following the maker's instructions.

Can a two-stroke main engine run if the turbocharger fails?

Usually yes, at a heavily restricted load. Following the maker's emergency procedure, the rotor is locked or removed and blanked off, and the auxiliary blowers or remaining turbochargers supply air. The permitted load and method are given in the engine and turbocharger manuals.

How often is a marine turbocharger overhauled?

There is no single interval. Overhauls follow the running-hour intervals the maker sets for that model, built into the ship's planned maintenance system. Condition is also taken into account, such as vibration, performance trends and oil analysis.

Test yourself

Which condition defines turbocharger compressor surge?

Surge is unstable compressor flow beyond the surge line on the compressor map; overspeed and low oil pressure are separate faults. Reference: turbocharger maker's instruction manual; STCW Code, Table A-III/1.

The engine load is unchanged but turbocharger speed has gradually risen. What is the most likely cause?

A fouled nozzle ring reduces the flow area, raising gas velocity and turbocharger speed, whereas fouled turbine blades and gas leakage lower speed. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-III/2 (operation, surveillance and performance assessment of machinery).

What is the first action when the main engine turbocharger surges repeatedly in heavy weather?

Reducing load moves the operating point away from the surge line; washing or stopping the engine is not the immediate response. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2 (engineering watch) and the maker's instructions.

Why are auxiliary blowers fitted on large two-stroke engines?

At low load the turbocharger cannot supply enough scavenge air, so the blowers cut in automatically. Reference: engine maker's instruction manual; STCW Code, Table A-III/1.

Before opening a turbocharger on an engine with several turbochargers on a common exhaust receiver, what must you ensure?

Draught or gas flow can turn an isolated rotor, so it must be secured under the permit-to-work and isolation procedures. Reference: Company SMS permit-to-work and isolation procedures (ISM Code, Section 7) and the maker's instructions.
