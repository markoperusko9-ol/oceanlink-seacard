Planned Maintenance System (PMS) on ships: how it works and what examiners ask

Quick answer

A planned maintenance system (PMS) is the shipboard system that schedules every maintenance job by running hours, calendar interval or condition. It records what was done and flags anything overdue or deferred. It is how a company shows it meets ISM Code Section 10, and examiners expect you to explain its structure, the handling of critical equipment and what to do when a job cannot be done on time.

What a PMS is and why it exists

A planned maintenance system is the ship's structured plan for keeping hull, machinery and safety equipment in working order. It replaces the old approach of fixing things when they broke. It is usually software, sometimes shore-linked, though paper systems still exist on some older tonnage.

The legal backbone is ISM Code Section 10. In paraphrase, the company must set up procedures so the ship is maintained in line with the relevant rules and regulations and with any additional requirements the company sets. It must also ensure that:

  • inspections are held at appropriate intervals
  • non-conformities are reported, with possible causes where known
  • corrective action is taken
  • records of these activities are kept

Section 10 also requires the company to identify equipment whose sudden operational failure could create a hazardous situation, and to promote its reliability. This includes regular testing of standby arrangements and of equipment not in continuous use.

Other instruments feed jobs into the PMS:

  • SOLAS Chapter II-2: maintenance plans for fire protection systems and fire-fighting appliances.
  • SOLAS Chapter III: maintenance, weekly and monthly inspections, and servicing of life-saving appliances.
  • Class rules and maker's instructions: overhaul intervals for engines, pumps and other machinery.

The PMS is where all of these requirements come together in one place.

How a PMS is structured

Most systems, whatever the software brand, follow the same logic.

1. Equipment hierarchy. The ship is broken down into systems, sub-systems and components. An example path is main engine, then cylinder unit No. 3, then exhaust valve. Each component has a code that links it to:

  • its jobs
  • its spare parts
  • its maker's manual references

2. Job definitions. Each component carries one or more jobs. A job card normally contains:

  • the task description
  • the interval
  • the responsible rank
  • reference to the manual or procedure
  • safety requirements such as risk assessment, permit to work and isolation
  • required spares and tools
  • fields for findings and measurements

3. Interval types.

Type Trigger Typical examples
Running hours Counter hours since last job Generator engine overhauls, purifier overhauls, compressor valves
Calendar Fixed time period Safety equipment checks, emergency equipment tests, tank inspections
Condition-based Measured condition reaches a limit Lube oil analysis results, vibration readings, bearing temperatures, insulation resistance trends
Unplanned / corrective Breakdown or defect found Repairs raised as defect reports or work orders

Whatever the trigger, intervals come from the maker's recommendations, class requirements, statutory requirements and company experience. Do not quote numbers in an exam unless you know them from the specific maker's manual.

4. Critical equipment list. The SMS identifies critical equipment, as required by ISM Section 10. Typical items are:

  • steering gear
  • emergency generator
  • emergency fire pump
  • main engine safety devices
  • fire detection
  • navigation equipment

The exact list is company-specific. Critical items usually carry stricter rules on deferral and on minimum spares.

Running the PMS day to day: step by step

In practice the chief engineer owns the engine department PMS, and the second engineer runs it day to day. Jobs are allocated to the rank responsible for each machine. Deck and safety equipment may sit with the chief officer or a designated officer, depending on the company.

A typical cycle runs like this:

  1. Update running hours. Enter counter readings at the interval the SMS sets, often monthly. Wrong hours mean wrong due dates, and this is a common audit finding.
  2. Review the due list. Look ahead at jobs due and overdue. Plan them around port calls, cargo work, manoeuvring and the availability of spares and manpower.
  3. Prepare the job. Read the job card and the manual. Carry out the risk assessment. Raise a permit to work where required, for example for hot work, enclosed space entry or electrical isolation. Apply lock-out/tag-out. Draw the spares.
  4. Carry out the work. Record actual findings and measurements, such as clearances, wear-down readings and insulation values, not just "done". Trend them against previous entries.
  5. Test and return to service. Run the machine and check its parameters against the maker's normal ranges. Remove isolations and close the permit.
  6. Close the job in the PMS. Enter the date, running hours, spares consumed and remarks. Attach photos or reports if the system allows. The next due date then resets.
  7. Report defects. Anything found that cannot be fixed becomes a defect or non-conformity report under the SMS, with corrective action tracked to closure.
  8. Reorder spares. Update the inventory and raise requisitions when stock falls below the minimum the company sets.

Postponements, overdue jobs and class links

Sometimes a job cannot be done on time. The usual reasons are lack of spares, operational constraints or the need for shore support.

Postponement. A job should not simply be left overdue. Most SMSs require a formal postponement request to the office, giving:

  • the reason
  • a risk assessment
  • any interim measures
  • a new target date

Shore approval is required, typically from the technical superintendent. Critical equipment postponements are usually more tightly controlled. Who can approve what is set by your company SMS.

Overdue jobs are visible. The following all look at the PMS overdue list and at whether postponements were properly approved:

  • internal audits
  • flag state inspections
  • port state control inspectors
  • charterer and oil-major vetting inspectors

A long overdue list on critical equipment is a classic route to an ISM non-conformity, or worse.

Continuous survey and class-approved PMS. Classification societies offer continuous survey schemes for machinery. Items are surveyed in rotation over the class cycle. Where the ship's PMS has been approved by class, some items may be examined by the chief engineer and credited after review or confirmatory survey by a surveyor.

The eligibility conditions, which items qualify, and the records required all differ between class societies. Check your own class's rules and the PMS approval documents on board.

Common PMS faults and their symptoms

A PMS fails quietly. The paperwork looks fine until a machine fails or an auditor digs in.

  • Running hours not updated or entered wrongly. Overhauls fall due too early or too late. Counter readings in the PMS do not match the engine room log book.
  • "Done" with no data. Jobs closed with no measurements make trend analysis impossible. Auditors spot identical remarks copied across many jobs.
  • Back-dated or pencil-whipped entries. Jobs are shown as done on dates when the ship's operations make that impossible, or while the machine was running. This is an integrity issue, not just poor record-keeping.
  • Spares mismatch. The PMS shows stock that is not physically on board, or spares used are never deducted. You find out during a breakdown.
  • Hierarchy not matching the ship. Equipment that was replaced or retrofitted still carries the old maker's jobs. New systems such as ballast water treatment or scrubbers are missing jobs altogether.
  • Ignored condition data. Oil analysis or vibration reports arrive but nobody changes the maintenance plan.
  • Standby equipment never tested. This undermines the ISM Section 10 requirement for regular testing of standby and intermittently used equipment, and it is often the reason a standby pump fails on demand.
  • Unauthorised postponements. Due dates are changed in the system without office approval.

Safety during planned maintenance

Planned work is where many engine room injuries happen, because people feel it is routine. The PMS job card should point you to the controls, but you must apply them.

  • Risk assessment and toolbox talk before the job, especially when the task is non-routine or the crew is unfamiliar with it.
  • Isolation. Isolate electrically, mechanically and on pressure, then lock out and tag out. Verify zero energy before opening anything. Watch for:
    • stored air
    • hot oil
    • residual pressure in pipelines and accumulators
  • Permits. Hot work, enclosed space entry, working aloft or overside, and high-voltage work all need permits as your SMS requires.
  • Engine turning gear. Engage and lock the turning gear before working on the main engine running gear. Isolate starting air and confirm the bridge knows.
  • Lifting. Check that lifting gear is certified and inspected, and that overhead cranes and chain blocks are within their safe working load.
  • Restoration. Remove all tools and rags. Refit guards and close the permit formally. Test before declaring the machine available.
  • Bridge communication. Inform the bridge before taking propulsion, steering or power generation out of service. Get the master's agreement where the SMS requires it.

Worked scenario: critical spare not on board

The emergency fire pump's scheduled test, at the interval set by the ship's maintenance plan, is due. During the test the pump fails to build pressure, and the impeller wear ring needed is not on board, although the PMS shows one in stock.

What a competent second engineer does:

  1. Report to the chief engineer, who informs the master. The ship's fire-fighting capability is reduced.
  2. Record the failed test and findings in the PMS. Raise a defect or non-conformity report as the SMS requires.
  3. Correct the stock record and investigate why the inventory was wrong.
  4. Request urgent supply through the office, giving the part number from the maker's manual.
  5. Agree interim measures with the master and office in line with the SMS, for example checking that the main fire pumps and their supply are available, and briefing the crew.
  6. Consider notifications. Flag and class may need to be told, depending on flag requirements and the defect's effect on certification. The office normally handles this.
  7. Repair, re-test and close the defect with records. Raise a lessons-learned point on spares control.

Oral exam questions on PMS

Expect these, usually phrased practically.

  • Q: Which part of the ISM Code covers maintenance, and what does it require? A: Section 10. You need procedures for maintenance, inspections at appropriate intervals, reporting of non-conformities, corrective action and records. Critical equipment must be identified, with measures to promote its reliability, including testing of standby and non-continuous-use equipment.
  • Q: Name the maintenance strategies. A: Running-hours based, calendar based, condition-based and breakdown/corrective. Explain where each fits on your last ship.
  • Q: A job is due but you have no spares. What do you do? A: Do not let it go silently overdue. Raise a postponement request with reason, risk assessment and interim measures. Get office approval as per the SMS, and requisition the spares.
  • Q: What is critical equipment? Give examples. A: Equipment whose sudden failure could cause a hazardous situation, for example steering gear, emergency generator and emergency fire pump. Explain the extra controls that apply to it.
  • Q: What is continuous survey of machinery? A: A class scheme where machinery items are surveyed in rotation over the class cycle. With an approved PMS, some items can be examined by the chief engineer and credited under class conditions.
  • Q: How do you use lube oil analysis results? A: Trend them, compare them with the lab's and maker's limits, and adjust maintenance, for example by bringing forward a purifier or bearing inspection. Record the action taken.

At management level, examiners also ask how you would audit a PMS on joining as chief or second engineer. The answer is to check:

  • running-hours accuracy
  • the overdue list
  • open postponements and their approvals
  • critical equipment status
  • spares reconciliation
  • whether recorded findings are genuine
Key points
  • PMS is how the company meets ISM Code Section 10: planned maintenance, inspections, defect reporting, corrective action and records.
  • Jobs are triggered by running hours, calendar intervals or measured condition; breakdowns are handled as corrective work orders.
  • Critical equipment and standby or intermittently used machinery need specific reliability measures, including regular testing.
  • Overdue jobs need a formal, approved postponement with a risk assessment; never just change the due date.
  • Record real findings and measurements so trends mean something; "done" alone is a weak record.
  • Running-hours updates and spares reconciliation are common audit and PSC findings.
  • Every PMS job still needs risk assessment, permits and isolation where applicable.

Frequently asked questions

Is a planned maintenance system mandatory on ships?

The ISM Code, Section 10, requires companies to establish maintenance procedures, inspections, defect reporting and records for ships to which it applies. It does not prescribe a particular software, but in practice a documented PMS is how companies meet the requirement. SOLAS also requires maintenance plans and records for fire-fighting and life-saving equipment.

Who is responsible for the PMS in the engine room?

The chief engineer is responsible overall, and the second engineer usually manages it day to day and allocates jobs. Each engineer carries out and records the jobs for the machinery assigned to them. The exact split is set by your company SMS and the chief engineer's standing orders.

What happens if PMS jobs are overdue during a PSC inspection?

Inspectors may look at overdue jobs, particularly on critical and safety equipment, and at whether any postponement was properly approved. Unexplained overdue critical items can lead to deficiencies and point to an ISM-related failing. Properly documented and approved postponements with interim measures are treated very differently from items simply ignored.

What is the difference between planned and condition-based maintenance?

Planned maintenance is done at fixed running-hour or calendar intervals regardless of condition. Condition-based maintenance uses measured data, such as oil analysis, vibration or temperature trends, to decide when work is actually needed. Most ships use a mix, and the condition data should feed back into the PMS intervals.

Can the chief engineer carry out class surveys under the PMS?

Under some classification societies' continuous survey schemes, where the ship's PMS has been approved by class, the chief engineer may examine certain machinery items for credit. These are subject to class review or confirmatory survey. Which items qualify and the conditions vary by class society, so check your class rules and the approval documents on board.

Test yourself

Which instrument is the primary basis for a company's shipboard maintenance system?

ISM Code Section 10 requires procedures for maintenance, inspections, non-conformity reporting, corrective action and records (ISM Code, Section 10).

A PMS job on critical equipment cannot be completed by its due date because spares have not arrived. What is the correct action?

Deferral must be controlled and approved through the SMS; falsifying or silently editing records breaches ISM requirements on maintenance and records (ISM Code, Section 10).

Which of these is an example of condition-based maintenance?

Condition-based maintenance acts on measured condition, such as oil analysis trends, rather than on fixed intervals or after failure. Results should be recorded and used to adjust the PMS.

Under the ISM Code, what must the company do for equipment whose sudden failure could create a hazardous situation?

The Code requires identification of such equipment and measures to promote reliability, including regular testing of standby and non-continuously used equipment (ISM Code, Section 10).

During an internal audit, PMS running hours for No. 2 generator do not match the engine room log book. What is the main risk?

Due dates for hour-based jobs depend on accurate counter entries, and records must reflect actual maintenance status (ISM Code, Section 10).
