Bearings on Ships: Types, Clearances and Failure Signs

Quick answer

Marine machinery uses two families of bearing. Plain bearings run on a pressurised oil film and are found in main and auxiliary engines, thrust blocks and stern tubes. Rolling-element bearings are found mainly in pumps, motors and some turbochargers. Clearance limits always come from the maker's manual, and the early signs of trouble are rising temperatures, oil mist alarms, falling lube oil pressure, metal in filters and changes in noise or vibration.

Why bearings matter and how a plain bearing actually works

A bearing locates a moving part and carries its load while keeping friction and wear low. In the engine room, a failed bearing is not a maintenance nuisance. It can lead to a crankshaft write-off, a crankcase explosion or a dead ship.

Most heavy-duty marine bearings are plain (journal) bearings running on hydrodynamic lubrication. The process works like this:

  1. At rest, the journal sits on the bottom of the bearing with metal-to-metal contact, apart from a thin residual film.
  2. As it starts to rotate, the journal drags oil into the converging gap between itself and the shell. This creates a wedge-shaped film.
  3. Pressure builds in that wedge until it lifts the journal clear of the shell. The journal then runs slightly eccentric, with the thinnest film (minimum oil film thickness) on the loaded side.
  4. The clearance lets oil flow through the bearing, which carries away heat and dirt. That is why clearance must be neither too small nor too large.

The dangerous periods are start-up and slow running, when the film has not formed and the bearing runs in boundary lubrication. This is why pre-lubrication before starting, and proper slow-turning, are not optional.

The crosshead bearing on a two-stroke engine is the hardest case. It only oscillates, so it never gets a full rotating wedge. On a two-stroke the load on it stays largely in one direction, so the bearing is never unloaded to let fresh oil in. Designers deal with this using generous bearing area and accurate geometry. On some engine designs they also add a separate higher-pressure lube oil supply to the crosshead.

Types of bearing you will meet on board

Bearing Where you find it Typical construction
Main bearings Crankshaft, main and auxiliary engines Steel-backed shells lined with white metal or tri-metal
Crankpin (bottom end) Connecting rod big end Thin-wall or thick-wall shells
Crosshead Two-stroke engines Large-area white metal or tri-metal shells
Thrust bearing Main engine or separate thrust block Tilting pads (Michell type) on a thrust collar
Stern tube bearing Propeller shaft White metal (oil-lubricated) or polymer/composite (water-lubricated)
Intermediate shaft bearings Shaft line Plain bearings, often with an oil ring or local sump
Rolling-element bearings Pumps, electric motors, fans, purifiers, some turbochargers Ball, cylindrical roller, spherical roller, taper roller

Bearing materials:

  • White metal (tin-based babbitt): soft and conformable, and it embeds small dirt particles. Its fatigue strength is limited and it softens quickly with heat.
  • Tri-metal: a steel back, an intermediate layer (for example lead-bronze or copper-lead) and a thin soft overlay. It carries higher loads but tolerates dirt and misalignment less well once the overlay is worn through.
  • Aluminium-tin: common in medium-speed engines and a good compromise between strength and conformability.

Thick-wall shells are sometimes scraped or fitted. Thin-wall shells are precision parts that rely on crush in the housing and must never be scraped, filed or shimmed.

Tilting-pad thrust bearings work because each pad pivots and forms its own oil wedge. That lets a short bearing carry the full propeller thrust. They have pads on both sides of the collar, for ahead and astern thrust.

Clearances: what they are and how they are measured

Clearance figures, wear limits and measurement procedures are engine-specific. Always take them from the maker's instruction manual and the PMS. Never use a number remembered from another ship.

What happens if the clearance is too small:

  • Too little oil flows through the bearing to remove heat.
  • Temperature rises, white metal softens and the bearing can wipe.
  • There is little margin for thermal growth or misalignment.

What happens if the clearance is too large:

  • Lube oil pressure drops, and the bearings further along the supply line are starved.
  • Oil film pressure and stiffness fall, and the bearing can knock under load reversal.
  • Shock loading speeds up fatigue cracking.

Common measurement methods:

  • Feeler gauges: used at accessible points, for example the top clearance of some main and crosshead bearings, or thrust axial float.
  • Lead wire or plastic gauge: the wire or strip is placed across the journal, the cap is tightened to the correct torque, then removed. The squashed thickness gives the clearance.
  • Micrometer and bore gauge: used on thin-wall shells. You measure the journal diameter and the shell bore with the cap torqued, then subtract.
  • Dial gauge or jack-up method: the journal, or the bearing housing relative to the journal, is jacked through its clearance and the movement is read on a dial gauge. Some makers specify this for main or crankpin bearings.
  • Thrust axial float: measured with a dial gauge or feeler gauges while the shaft is pushed fully ahead and then astern.
  • Stern tube wear-down: read with a poker gauge in drydock, or with a fitted wear-down measuring device where one exists.

Crankshaft deflection is related but different. A dial gauge is placed between the crank webs and readings are taken at set crank positions. The results show main bearing alignment: uneven wear or a low bearing appears as an abnormal deflection pattern. It does not measure clearance directly, but it is the standard way to spot a worn or misaligned main bearing without opening every one. Take readings under the conditions the maker specifies, record engine temperature and draught or loading, and compare against the maker's limits and previous records.

Rolling-element bearings: fitting and care

Rolling-element bearings fail differently from plain bearings. They are more often damaged by poor fitting, inadequate lubrication or contamination than by normal fatigue from hard work.

Fitting:

  • Interference fits are made by heating the bearing with an induction heater or a controlled oil bath, never with a flame. Local overheating ruins the hardness.
  • Press only on the ring that carries the interference. Pushing an inner ring on through the balls brinells the races.
  • Internal clearance groups exist, for example C3, which has greater than normal radial internal clearance. Motors and pumps that run warm often specify one. Always replace with the same designation.

Lubrication:

  • Over-greasing is as bad as under-greasing. Too much grease churns, overheats and pushes past seals.
  • Follow the maker's grease type, quantity and interval. Do not mix incompatible greases.

Condition monitoring:

  • Vibration analysis and shock pulse measurement pick up race defects long before you can hear them.
  • Temperature trend, noise (use a stethoscope or screwdriver to the ear) and grease condition are the basic watchkeeping checks.

Failure signs and what causes them

On watch, plain bearings give you these warnings:

  • Rising bearing or outlet oil temperature on one unit compared with the others.
  • An oil mist detector (OMD) alarm, or a bearing temperature monitoring alarm where fitted.
  • Falling lube oil pressure, or a rising pressure drop across the filter.
  • White metal flakes or particles in the filters or in a lube oil sample.
  • Knocking, or a change in the engine's sound.
  • On stopping, a hot spot found on a crankcase feel-round, where the SMS allows one.

Damage you find when the bearing is opened:

Damage Appearance Usual cause
Wiping Smeared, melted-looking white metal Oil starvation, too little clearance, overload
Fatigue cracking Network of cracks, pieces of lining falling out Overload, excessive clearance, misalignment, ageing
Scoring Circumferential grooves Hard particles in the oil, poor filtration
Cavitation erosion Pitted areas, often near oil grooves Collapse of vapour bubbles from rapid pressure changes
Corrosion Dark or etched lining Water or acidic contamination of the oil
Spark erosion Fine pitting on the bearing or journal Stray current through the shaft, often from a defective shaft earthing device
Fretting Marks on the shell back Loss of crush, slack bolts, movement in the housing

Rolling-element bearings:

  • Spalling: flaking of the race from fatigue or overload.
  • True brinelling: dents caused by impact or bad fitting.
  • False brinelling: wear marks at ball spacing, caused by vibration while the bearing is stationary. Standby pumps next to running machinery are typical victims.
  • Electrical fluting: washboard marks caused by current passing through the bearing, seen especially with variable frequency drives.
  • Off-centre wear path: a sign of misalignment.

Worked scenario: OMD alarm at sea

You are the duty engineer. The main engine OMD alarms, and the deviation points to one unit. Follow your company procedure and the maker's instructions. The sequence below shows the logic most of them share.

  1. Treat it as real. Do not assume a false alarm until it has been proved false.
  2. Inform the bridge and the chief engineer. Request a slowdown, and a stop as soon as it is navigationally safe.
  3. Keep people away from the crankcase doors and relief valves. If the mist ignites, that is where the flame will vent.
  4. Do not open the crankcase. Admitting air to hot oil mist can cause a secondary explosion, which is usually worse than the primary.
  5. After stopping, follow the maker's guidance on keeping lube oil circulating and on turning the engine with the turning gear.
  6. Wait the full cooling period your procedure specifies. Before entry, isolate starting air, engage and lock the turning gear, issue a permit, and have fire-fighting equipment ready.
  7. Inspect. Look for the hot spot and check bearings, guides and the piston rod stuffing box. Look for discoloured or wiped white metal. Check filters and take an oil sample.
  8. Find the root cause before restarting. A bearing that wiped once will wipe again if the oil supply problem is still there.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • Why does a journal bearing need clearance at all? Clearance lets the oil wedge form, provides oil flow for cooling and allows for thermal expansion and minor misalignment.
  • Why is the crosshead bearing the most difficult to lubricate? It only oscillates, so there is no full hydrodynamic wedge. On a two-stroke the load stays largely in one direction, so oil is not drawn in by load reversal.
  • What does crankshaft deflection tell you? It shows the alignment of the main bearings and the condition of the bedplate and crankshaft line, not the clearance directly.
  • Why must thin-wall shells never be scraped? They rely on precise geometry and crush. Scraping destroys the overlay and the fit.
  • What causes spark erosion and how is it prevented? Current passing through the shaft and its bearings causes it. It is prevented by a properly maintained shaft earthing device.
  • Why do we wait before opening a crankcase after a hot spot? Fresh air entering hot oil mist can ignite it and cause a secondary explosion.
  • What is false brinelling? It is wear at ball spacing caused by vibration while the bearing is stationary.
Key points
  • Plain bearings rely on a hydrodynamic oil wedge. Start-up and slow running are the high-wear periods.
  • Clearance limits come only from the maker's manual and PMS, never from memory or another ship.
  • Too little clearance causes overheating and wiping. Too much causes low oil pressure, knocking and fatigue.
  • Crankshaft deflection checks main bearing alignment, not clearance.
  • Rising temperature, an OMD alarm, low LO pressure and metal in filters are the key warning signs.
  • Never open a crankcase until the specified cooling period has passed after a hot spot or OMD alarm.
  • Fit rolling bearings with induction or oil-bath heating, never a flame.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between white metal and tri-metal bearings?

White metal is a soft tin-based alloy that conforms well and embeds dirt but has limited fatigue strength. Tri-metal shells have a steel back, a stronger intermediate layer and a thin soft overlay, so they can carry higher loads. Which one your engine uses is set by the maker, and the two are not interchangeable.

How do you check main bearing clearance on a ship?

It depends on the engine. Methods include feeler gauges, lead wire, micrometer and bore measurement, or a jack-up and dial gauge procedure. The maker's manual specifies the method, the conditions and the limits. Record the results in the PMS so you can follow the wear trend.

Why can an oil mist detector alarm indicate a bearing problem?

An overheating bearing or other hot spot vaporises lube oil, and the vapour condenses into a fine mist in the crankcase. The OMD detects rising mist density before it reaches an explosive concentration, which gives early warning of bearing damage and a possible crankcase explosion. The hot spot may also be a piston, guide, stuffing box or chain drive, so the whole crankcase must be inspected.

What causes a bearing to wipe?

Wiping happens when the oil film breaks down and the white metal overheats and smears. Common causes are loss of oil pressure, blocked oil passages, too little clearance, contaminated oil, overload or misalignment. Always find and fix the cause before fitting a new shell.

Can I reuse a bearing shell after inspection?

Only if it is within the maker's wear and thickness limits and shows no cracking, wiping, scoring or loss of overlay beyond acceptable levels. Shells must go back into the same position and orientation. Company SMS and maker guidance decide, and if in doubt the chief engineer decides.

Test yourself

What lifts the journal clear of the shell in a hydrodynamic plain bearing?

Rotation drags oil into the converging gap, and the pressure built in that wedge supports the load. Pump pressure only supplies the oil.

What does crankshaft deflection measurement mainly indicate?

The change in distance between the webs shows how the main bearings support the crankshaft. Compare readings with the maker's limits.

After an OMD alarm and engine stop, what is the correct approach to the crankcase?

Admitting air to hot oil mist can cause a secondary explosion. Maker's instructions and the SMS set the waiting time.

A standby pump bearing shows wear marks at ball spacing although the pump rarely runs. What is the likely cause?

Vibration transmitted from nearby running machinery wears the races of a stationary bearing at ball positions. Rotating the pump periodically helps prevent it.

Why are thin-wall bearing shells never scraped or shimmed?

Thin-wall shells are precision parts held by crush in the housing. Altering them destroys the fit and the overlay.
