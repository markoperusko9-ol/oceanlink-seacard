Weather Fax and GRIB Files on Board: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Weather fax (radiofacsimile) charts are prepared by national meteorological services and broadcast on HF. GRIB files are compressed grids of raw numerical model output, received by email, satellite data or a routing service. Use both to plan and monitor a passage, but official warnings and Maritime Safety Information take priority over a GRIB file, because GRIB output is not checked by a forecaster and often understates severe weather.

What each product actually is

Weather fax and GRIB both show weather on a chart. They come from different places and carry different authority.

Weather fax (radiofacsimile, HF fax) - Prepared by national meteorological services and broadcast on HF on fixed schedules. - A human forecaster has analysed and checked each chart before broadcast. - Typical products include: - surface analysis and surface prognosis (prog) charts - wind and wave analyses and forecasts - upper-air (500 hPa) charts - tropical cyclone warnings and track charts - sea surface temperature, ice charts and satellite images - Each chart is a picture. You cannot zoom in to get more detail than was drawn.

GRIB (GRIdded Binary) - A WMO-standardised data format (editions GRIB1 and GRIB2) for model output. - Holds values at grid points and time steps. Common fields are: - mean sea level pressure - 10 m wind, stored as U and V components - significant wave height - precipitation - Usually generated from a global numerical model, such as NOAA's GFS. The values come straight from the computer, not from a forecaster. - Displayed in a viewer, routing software or an ECDIS/ECS overlay. - Files are small enough to request by email or over a satellite data link.

Receiving a weather fax: step by step

  1. Find the schedule. Check the Admiralty List of Radio Signals Volume 3, Maritime Safety Information Services (NP283), or the issuing service's own published schedule. Note the:
    • station
    • frequencies
    • chart types
    • broadcast times in UTC
  2. Pick a frequency for the time of day and range. HF propagation changes with distance, season and day or night. If one frequency is noisy, try another listed one.
  3. Set up the receiver. A dedicated fax receiver does this for you. If you use an SSB receiver with fax software:
    • set USB mode
    • tune 1.9 kHz below the listed assigned frequency, unless the schedule or equipment manual states otherwise
  4. Match the drum settings. The common settings are 120 lines per minute and IOC 576. Some broadcasts differ, so set what the schedule states. A wrong speed gives a slanted or smeared chart.
  5. Let it start automatically. The start tone and phasing signal align the image. Joining a broadcast part-way through usually leaves the chart offset.
  6. Check the header before using the chart. Confirm:
    • chart type
    • issuing service
    • analysis or valid time (UTC)
    • area covered
  7. File and record it. Log or file the chart as your SMS requires. Pass anything significant to the master and the OOW.

Reading the charts and GRIB data correctly

Fax charts - Analysis vs prog. An analysis shows conditions at a past time. A prog chart shows a forecast for its valid time (often marked VT). - A common error is reading a 48-hour prog as if it showed present conditions. - Issue time vs valid time. Both are in UTC. Work out the difference from ship's time before you brief anyone. - Isobars. The interval is commonly 4 hPa, but check the chart legend. - Close spacing means strong gradient wind. - Remember that the same spacing gives a stronger geostrophic wind at lower latitudes. - Fronts and centres. Note the: - position, central pressure and forecast movement of each low - labels such as DEVELOPING, GALE, STORM or HURRICANE FORCE - Wind barbs. A half barb is 5 kt, a full barb is 10 kt and a pennant is 50 kt. The shaft points towards the direction the wind is blowing from, and the barbs and pennants are drawn at that end. - Wave charts. These usually show combined sea and swell (significant height). Individual waves can be much higher.

GRIB files - Check the: - model name and run time (for example 00Z or 12Z) - grid spacing and time step - Wind is model mean wind at 10 m. Gusts and squalls are not shown unless a gust field is included, and even then they are model estimates. - Coarse grids smooth out sharp features. Expect fronts, small intense lows, coastal acceleration zones and tropical cyclone cores to be understated. - Values between time steps are interpolated by the viewer. A fast-moving system can be in the wrong place between steps. - One deterministic run is one possible outcome. Comparing successive runs shows how confident the model is.

Worked scenario: winter North Atlantic

A loaded bulk carrier is two days west of the Channel, heading east-north-east. On the 1200 UTC watch the 2nd Officer receives two products.

What the products show - GRIB, 00Z run: a low passing north of track in 36 hours. The ship's track shows 30-35 kt from the south-west, and the grid shows 4.5 m waves. - Fax, 48-hour surface prog: the same low, but deeper. Its isobars are tightly packed and it is labelled STORM. The track crosses the gradient south of the centre. - NAVTEX or SafetyNET: a storm warning for the area has also been received.

What a competent OOW does 1. Treats the official warning and the fax as the governing information. The GRIB gives timing and a general pattern only. 2. Assumes the real wind and sea will be stronger than the GRIB shows. Coarse model output under-reads deepening lows, and squalls behind the cold front will be stronger again. 3. Plots the forecast positions of the low against the ship's planned positions at the same UTC times. 4. Watches the barometer and observed wind trend. Compares them with the forecast to judge whether the low is ahead of or behind schedule. 5. Reports to the master with options: - adjust course or speed to pass further from the centre - delay - prepare to heave to 6. Once the master decides, starts heavy weather precautions in good time. These include lashings, closing openings, a ballast and stability check, and briefing the crew. 7. Records the information received and the decisions made, as the SMS requires.

If the ship meets winds of force 10 or above for which no warning has been received, the master must send a danger message (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31).

Where these fit in the regulations and in the SMS

SOLAS Chapter V - Regulation 5: contracting governments must arrange for meteorological services, warnings and forecasts for shipping. Radiofacsimile is listed among the means of distributing them. - Regulation 34: the master must have a voyage plan that takes account of relevant information. Weather is part of that. The IMO voyage planning guidelines (Resolution A.893(21)) list meteorological information among what should be considered. - Regulations 31 and 32: set out the danger message duty and what the message must contain.

SOLAS Chapter IV - Covers reception of official Maritime Safety Information. This means NAVTEX and the recognised EGC services. - A GRIB file is not MSI and does not replace it. - Weather fax is also not part of the GMDSS. It is a valuable supplement to MSI, not a substitute.

STCW - The Code (Section A-II/1 for OOW, A-II/2 for master and chief mate) expects officers to: - apply available meteorological information - understand weather systems - at management level, forecast weather and oceanographic conditions - Examiners therefore expect you to interpret a fax chart, not just recognise one.

Company SMS and equipment - The SMS usually sets: - which products must be received and how often - who reviews them - how they are recorded - Whether a ship carries a fax receiver, and whether GRIB comes from a contracted routing service, depends on the company. Follow your SMS rather than a general rule.

Common mistakes on board

  • Reading the wrong time. Using a prog chart or GRIB time step without converting UTC to ship's time, or confusing the analysis time with the valid time.
  • Trusting the GRIB number. Treating 30 kt in the GRIB as the wind you will get. It is a smoothed mean, and gusts and front squalls are higher.
  • Ignoring warnings because the GRIB looks calm. Official warnings take priority.
  • Using old data. Using a GRIB from a model run more than a day old when a newer run is available. Or keeping an old fax on the chart table after a fresh one has arrived.
  • Accepting a bad chart. Using a slanted or noisy fax without checking the header and area. Retune or wait for the rebroadcast.
  • Reading too much into the grid. Relying on a coarse grid near land, in narrow seas or close to a tropical cyclone, where local effects dominate.
  • No cross-check against the real weather. Not comparing the forecast with the barometer trend, observed wind, swell direction and cloud. Your own observations show whether the forecast is holding.
  • Leaving it to software. Letting routing or ECDIS overlays make the decision. The OOW and master must still judge the weather and own the decision.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

Q: What is the difference between a weather fax and a GRIB file? Fax is a chart prepared and checked by a met service and broadcast on HF. GRIB is raw gridded model output with no forecaster input and no warnings.

Q: Which do you trust more if they disagree? The official forecast and warnings, which include the fax and MSI. GRIB tends to understate intense, small or fast-developing systems.

Q: Where do you find fax schedules? In the Admiralty List of Radio Signals Volume 3, Maritime Safety Information Services (NP283), or the issuing service's published schedule.

Q: What settings does a fax broadcast need? The correct frequency (USB, normally 1.9 kHz below the assigned frequency on a general receiver), and the correct drum speed and IOC. These are commonly 120 lpm and IOC 576, but you take them from the schedule.

Q: Why might real wind exceed the GRIB value? Three reasons: the GRIB shows mean 10 m wind, the grid smooths sharp features, and gusts and frontal squalls are not resolved.

Q: You meet force 10 with no warning received. What must you do? The master sends a danger message under SOLAS V/31 to ships in the vicinity and to the competent authorities, with the information listed in V/32.

Q: How do you use a prog chart for collision with weather? Plot the forecast positions of the system against the ship's planned positions at the same UTC times. Assess the closest approach and the dangerous sector. Then advise the master on course and speed options.

Key points
  • Weather fax is forecaster-checked; GRIB is raw model output with no warnings and no human review.
  • Official warnings and MSI (NAVTEX, EGC) override any GRIB file.
  • GRIB wind is smoothed mean 10 m wind: expect stronger gusts, squalls and deeper lows than shown.
  • Always check issue time, valid time and model run time, all in UTC.
  • Fax schedules are in ALRS Volume 3, Maritime Safety Information Services (NP283), or the issuing service's schedule.
  • Cross-check every forecast against the barometer trend and observed weather.
  • Report force 10 or above with no warning received as a danger message under SOLAS V/31.

Frequently asked questions

Is a GRIB file an official weather forecast?

No. A GRIB file is gridded output from a numerical model, delivered without forecaster review or warnings. It is useful for planning and timing but is not Maritime Safety Information. Official forecasts and warnings take priority.

Why is the weather fax chart slanted or blurred?

Usually the drum speed (lines per minute) or IOC setting does not match the broadcast. It can also happen if reception started after the phasing signal. Poor HF propagation and a wrong tuning offset cause noise. Check the settings against the schedule, try another listed frequency, or wait for the next broadcast.

Do all ships have to carry a weather fax receiver?

Weather fax is not part of the GMDSS carriage requirements, so carriage depends on the flag administration, the trade and the company. Many companies require it or provide equivalent weather data through a routing service. Check your SMS and the ship's equipment list.

Can I use GRIB data for a tropical cyclone?

Only with great caution. Global models often underestimate the intensity and tightness of a tropical cyclone and can misplace its track. Use the official tropical cyclone warnings and track forecasts, and apply the avoidance rules from your training and company procedures.

What is the difference between an analysis and a prognosis chart?

An analysis shows observed conditions at a past time, drawn from reports and model data. A prognosis (prog) is a forecast for a stated future valid time, for example 24, 48 or 96 hours ahead. Always read the valid time on the chart before using it.

Test yourself

A GRIB file shows 30 kt on your track while a storm warning is in force for the same area. What should you plan for?

Official warnings take priority over unreviewed model output, which smooths intense systems and omits gusts. Reference: SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 5 and Chapter IV on MSI.

On a general SSB receiver with fax software, how is a fax broadcast normally tuned?

Fax tones sit around 1.9 kHz, so on USB you tune that amount below the assigned frequency unless the schedule or equipment manual states otherwise. Reference: the issuing service's broadcast schedule.

What does the wind value in a typical GRIB file represent?

GRIB wind is model 10 m wind (U and V components) averaged over the grid. Gusts are not included unless a separate field is supplied. Reference: WMO Manual on Codes (WMO-No. 306), GRIB format.

You receive a 48-hour surface prognosis chart. What does its valid time tell you?

A prog chart shows the forecast situation at its valid time, which is not the issue or observation time. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-II/1 (meteorology competence).

Your ship meets wind of force 10 and no warning has been received. What is required?

Winds of force 10 or above without a prior warning must be reported as a danger message. Reference: SOLAS Chapter V, Regulations 31 and 32.
