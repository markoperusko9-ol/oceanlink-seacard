Beaufort Scale and Sea State: Recognising, Logging and Acting on Wind and Sea

Quick answer

The Beaufort scale estimates wind force from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane, 64 knots and over) by what the sea surface looks like. Sea state is a separate wave description, usually on the Douglas scale from 0 (calm, glassy) to 9 (phenomenal). Log true wind, not apparent wind. Treat wave heights in the tables as open-sea guidance, not fixed values. A rising force should trigger your heavy weather precautions and, at force 10 or above with no storm warning received, a SOLAS danger message.

What the Beaufort scale is and what it is not

The scale started with Francis Beaufort in the early 1800s as a way to describe wind for a sailing warship. Today it is the standard way an observer estimates wind force at sea without instruments. You judge it from the appearance of the sea surface.

The points examiners want to hear:

  • It is a wind scale. Each force has a speed band in knots and a description of the sea that goes with it.
  • The "probable wave height" column is guidance for the open sea, remote from land, once the wind has blown long enough. It is not a definition.
  • The sea lags behind the wind. A freshly risen wind has not yet built its full sea. After the wind drops, the sea and swell take time to go down.
  • Fetch, duration, current, shallow water and existing swell all change what you see. Do not use the scale in reverse, reading off wind speed from wave height alone.

Sea state is described separately, usually on the Douglas scale. Swell is a third element. Logbooks and weather reports normally record wind, sea and swell as three different things.

The Beaufort scale at a glance

Force Description Speed (kn) Sea criteria (open sea) Probable wave ht (m)
0 Calm <1 Sea like a mirror 0
1 Light air 1-3 Ripples like scales, no foam crests 0.1
2 Light breeze 4-6 Small wavelets, glassy crests, not breaking 0.2
3 Gentle breeze 7-10 Large wavelets, crests begin to break, scattered white horses 0.6
4 Moderate breeze 11-16 Small waves becoming longer, fairly frequent white horses 1
5 Fresh breeze 17-21 Moderate, longer waves, many white horses, chance of spray 2
6 Strong breeze 22-27 Large waves forming, extensive white foam crests, probably some spray 3
7 Near gale 28-33 Sea heaps up, foam begins to be blown in streaks 4
8 Gale 34-40 Moderately high, longer waves, crest edges break into spindrift, well-marked foam streaks 5.5
9 Strong gale 41-47 High waves, dense foam streaks, crests topple and roll over, spray may affect visibility 7
10 Storm 48-55 Very high waves with long overhanging crests, sea looks white, heavy shock-like tumbling 9
11 Violent storm 56-63 Exceptionally high waves, sea covered in long white foam patches 11.5
12 Hurricane 64+ Air full of foam and spray, sea completely white, visibility very seriously affected 14

Useful recognition anchors:

  • Force 3-4: the first white horses appear.
  • Force 7: foam starts streaking downwind.
  • Force 8: spindrift comes off the crests.
  • Force 9 and above: spray starts to cut visibility.

Foam streaks lie along the wind direction. In a gale they are often a better guide to true wind direction than the anemometer.

Sea state and swell: the Douglas scale

Sea state describes the wind waves at your position. The Douglas scale used in weather reporting runs from 0 to 9:

Code Description Wave height (m)
0 Calm (glassy) 0
1 Calm (rippled) 0-0.1
2 Smooth (wavelets) 0.1-0.5
3 Slight 0.5-1.25
4 Moderate 1.25-2.5
5 Rough 2.5-4
6 Very rough 4-6
7 High 6-9
8 Very high 9-14
9 Phenomenal Over 14

The heights describe the general wave field, essentially the significant wave height. Individual waves are regularly much higher, and can approach roughly twice the significant height.

Swell is wave energy generated somewhere else. It arrives with long, regular, rounded crests and a longer period. Its direction may have nothing to do with the local wind. Report it separately, with its direction, and describe its height and length (low, moderate or heavy; short, average or long) as your company's or the met service's format requires.

Swell running against or across the wind sea gives a confused sea. A strong current running against the wind steepens waves sharply. The Agulhas Current under south-westerly gales is the classic example.

Estimating from the bridge: true wind, not apparent wind

The anemometer and the flags show the apparent wind, which includes the effect of your own speed. The log and any weather report need the true wind.

Worked scenario. The ship is heading 000° T at 15 knots. The anemometer shows apparent wind from 030° at 25 knots, which looks like force 6.

  1. Convert the apparent wind into a velocity vector. Wind from 030° moves air towards 210°. Its components are 12.5 kn west and 21.65 kn south.
  2. Add your own velocity. Your 15 kn northward motion is what made the wind seem stronger, so add 15 kn north. The result is 12.5 kn west and 6.65 kn south.
  3. Work out the true wind speed. √(12.5² + 6.65²) ≈ 14.2 kn.
  4. Work out the true wind direction. The air is moving towards about 242°, so the true wind is from about 062°.
  5. Convert to Beaufort. 14 knots is force 4, not force 6.

A quick sanity check: with the wind right ahead, true speed is roughly apparent minus ship speed. With the wind right astern, it is roughly apparent plus ship speed.

The practical method is to work the triangle on a plotting sheet or use the true-wind calculation in your instruments. Then cross-check against the sea surface. If the table says force 4 and you can see foam streaking, something is wrong. The sea is the independent witness.

At night, you lose most visual cues. Use:

  • the anemometer, corrected for your own motion;
  • the sound of the wind in the rigging;
  • spray over the bow;
  • the ship's motion;
  • barometer trends.

Record in the log that the estimate was made in darkness if your format allows.

What the ship should do as the numbers climb

The Beaufort number by itself is not an order to do anything. The trend is what drives action. The thresholds for calling the Master, reducing speed or altering course come from the Master's standing orders and the company SMS, and they vary.

A typical progression:

  • Rising wind or falling barometer, forecast gale (force 8) on NAVTEX/SafetyNET:
    • Inform the Master as per standing orders.
    • Review the passage plan and weather routing.
    • Start heavy weather precautions: secure loose gear, check lashings, close openings, restrict deck access, check ballast and stability, brief the engine room.
  • Force 6-7 with a building head or quartering sea:
    • Watch for slamming, green seas on deck and propeller racing.
    • Watch for synchronous or parametric rolling. The warning signs are a natural roll period close to the wave encounter period (synchronous), or close to twice the encounter period, i.e. encounter period about half the roll period (parametric).
    • Reduce speed or adjust heading in good time, not after the damage.
  • Force 8 and above:
    • Heave-to or run as the Master decides. That depends on ship type, loading condition and sea direction.
    • Keep no one on deck unless essential, and only under the SMS controls.
  • Force 10 or above with no storm warning received:
    • SOLAS Chapter V requires the Master to send a danger message to nearby ships and the competent coastal authorities.
    • SOLAS sets the content of these messages. Include position, wind, barometer and sea state information so others can act on it.

Many ships also make routine weather observations for national met services as Voluntary Observing Ships. Your Beaufort and sea state estimates go into forecasts other ships rely on, so log honestly.

Common mistakes examiners and Masters see

  • Logging apparent wind as true wind. This is the classic error. It overstates wind ahead and understates it from astern.
  • Ignoring anemometer limitations. The mast sits above the sea surface and in airflow disturbed by the superstructure and cargo. The readings need sense-checking.
  • Mixing up swell and wind sea. A long heavy swell in force 2 does not make it force 6.
  • Reading the scale backwards. Assigning a force from wave height alone fails in fetch-limited waters near the coast, early in a blow, or in a current.
  • Misjudging height from a high bridge. Height of eye flattens the waves. Judge height against something you know, such as the forecastle or a ship nearby, not just your impression.
  • Treating the table heights as the maximum. They are probable figures. The biggest individual waves will be well above them.
  • Acting on the number, not the trend. A steady force 7 may be manageable. A rapid climb from force 5 to force 7 with a falling barometer is the real warning.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: What is the Beaufort scale and what does it measure? It is an empirical scale, force 0 to 12, for estimating wind force from the sea surface. Each force has a defined speed band in knots.

Q: At what force does foam start to be blown in streaks? Force 7, near gale. At force 8 the crests break into spindrift.

Q: Can you find wind force from wave height? Not reliably. Wave height depends on fetch, duration, current, depth and swell, and it lags behind the wind. The table heights are open-sea guidance only.

Q: Difference between sea and swell? Sea is wind waves generated locally. Swell is generated elsewhere: longer period, more regular, and possibly from a different direction.

Q: You are steaming into a 30-knot apparent wind right ahead at 14 knots. What do you log? True wind of about 16 knots from ahead, which is force 4. Then confirm against the sea surface.

Q: When must you send a danger message about wind? When you meet winds of force 10 or above and no storm warning has been received, under SOLAS Chapter V. Other danger messages cover dangerous ice, derelicts, tropical storms and severe ice accretion.

Q: What does Douglas sea state 6 mean? Very rough, with waves of about 4 to 6 metres.

Key points
  • Beaufort runs from force 0 to 12. Force 12 (hurricane) is 64 knots and over.
  • The wave heights in the Beaufort table are probable open-sea values, not definitions. Never use the scale in reverse.
  • Sea state (Douglas 0-9) and swell are recorded separately from wind.
  • Always convert apparent wind to true wind before logging or reporting.
  • Foam streaks appear at force 7, lie along the wind, and are a good guide to true wind direction.
  • Act on the trend and the standing orders, not on a single Beaufort number.
  • Winds of force 10 or above with no storm warning received require a SOLAS danger message.

Frequently asked questions

What wind speed is a gale on the Beaufort scale?

Force 8, gale, is 34 to 40 knots. Force 9, strong gale, is 41 to 47 knots. Marine forecasts generally issue gale warnings when force 8 or more is expected, but the exact warning terms depend on the issuing met service.

What is the difference between the Beaufort scale and the Douglas sea scale?

Beaufort estimates wind force from 0 to 12. Douglas describes the state of the wind waves from 0 (calm, glassy) to 9 (phenomenal, over 14 m). They are related but independent. A strong wind over a short fetch can give a lower sea state than the Beaufort table suggests.

Should I log true or apparent wind in the deck log?

True wind. Apparent wind includes your own speed and course. Use a vector triangle or the true-wind function of your instruments, then check the result against the sea surface. Follow your company's log format for how wind, sea and swell are entered.

Why does the sea look rougher than the wind force suggests?

The usual causes are swell from distant weather, a current running against the wind, shallow water, or a wind that has just dropped while the sea has not yet gone down. These are exactly why the Beaufort wave heights are only guidance.

Who decides when to reduce speed or heave-to in heavy weather?

The Master. The OOW's job is to recognise deterioration early and call the Master in line with the standing orders and company SMS. Thresholds vary by company and by ship, so learn your own ship's orders on joining.

Test yourself

At what Beaufort force does white foam from breaking waves first begin to be blown in streaks along the wind?

Force 7 (near gale, 28-33 kn) is described as the sea heaping up with foam beginning to be blown in streaks. Ref: Beaufort scale as used by WMO.

Your ship makes 15 knots heading north. Apparent wind is 25 knots from right ahead. What is the true wind?

With the wind dead ahead, true speed is roughly apparent speed minus ship speed: 25 − 15 = 10 kn from ahead. Logging 25 kn is the classic apparent-wind error.

Why should you not assign a Beaufort force from observed wave height alone?

The table's probable heights assume open sea and a fully developed wind sea. Fetch, duration, current, depth and swell all alter them, so the scale must not be used in reverse.

Under SOLAS Chapter V, which wind condition requires a danger message when no storm warning has been received?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31 includes winds of force 10 or above on the Beaufort scale for which no storm warning has been received.

On the Douglas sea scale, what does sea state 5 describe?

Douglas sea state 5 is 'rough', 2.5-4 m. State 4 is moderate, 6 is very rough and 7 is high. Ref: WMO state of sea code.
