Reading a Synoptic Weather Chart: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

A synoptic chart shows the pressure pattern, fronts and observed weather over a wide area at one moment in time, always given in UTC. Read the isobars for wind direction and strength, the fronts for the weather sequence coming your way, and the forecast charts for where systems are heading. Then decide what your ship should do about it.

What a synoptic chart is, and which one you are looking at

"Synoptic" means a view of the whole picture at the same instant. A surface synoptic chart plots pressure, fronts and weather observations from ships, buoys and land stations at a fixed time, so that the pattern can be analysed.

Before you read anything else, check the header block:

  • Analysis or prognosis. An analysis shows what was happening at the stated time. A prognosis (forecast) chart shows the expected pattern at T+24, T+48 and so on. Mixing them up is one of the commonest mistakes on the bridge.
  • Valid time in UTC. Convert to ship's time. A chart received at 1400 LT may already be several hours old.
  • Issuing service and area. Different met services cover different areas, and their symbols and colours are not always identical.
  • Isobar interval. Many charts draw isobars every 4 hPa, but some use other intervals. Read the legend rather than assuming.
  • Projection and scale. These matter when you measure isobar spacing against the geostrophic wind scale.

On board you will usually get charts by HF radiofacsimile, by email or satellite data service, or through a weather routing provider. Treat a forecast chart as the forecaster's best estimate, not as a guarantee.

Isobars, pressure systems and wind

Isobars join points of equal pressure, reduced to mean sea level. The patterns they make tell you the type of system:

Feature Isobar shape Typical weather
Depression (low) Closed, lowest in centre Strong winds, cloud, rain, fronts
Anticyclone (high) Closed, highest in centre Light winds, generally settled; fog or low stratus possible
Trough V or U-shaped extension of a low Unsettled, showers, wind shift on passage
Ridge Extension of a high Fair interval, light winds
Col Slack area between two highs and two lows Light, variable winds; fog possible

Direction of circulation:

  • Northern Hemisphere: anticlockwise around a low, clockwise around a high.
  • Southern Hemisphere: clockwise around a low, anticlockwise around a high.

Buys Ballot's law. Stand with your back to the true wind. In the Northern Hemisphere the low is on your left. In the Southern Hemisphere it is on your right. Examiners will expect you to state this without hesitating, for both hemispheres.

Strength. The closer the isobars, the steeper the pressure gradient and the stronger the wind. To estimate the speed, use the geostrophic wind scale printed on the chart:

  1. Measure the spacing between adjacent isobars at right angles to them, at your position.
  2. Lay it on the scale at your latitude.
  3. Read off the geostrophic wind.

For the same spacing, the geostrophic wind is stronger at lower latitudes. That is why the scale is latitude-dependent.

Surface wind is not the geostrophic wind. Friction slows the surface wind and turns it across the isobars towards the low. Over the open sea, a common rule of thumb is that the surface wind is backed by about 10-15° from the isobars in the Northern Hemisphere and is somewhat less than the geostrophic value. Over land the effect is larger. Treat these figures as rough guidance, and check them against observed wind and the official forecast.

Remember the definitions in both hemispheres: veering is a clockwise change in wind direction, and backing is an anticlockwise change.

Fronts and the weather sequence they bring

Fronts mark the boundaries between air masses. On colour charts the conventional symbols are as follows, and the symbols point in the direction the front is moving:

  • Warm front: semicircles, usually red.
  • Cold front: triangles, usually blue.
  • Occluded front: triangles and semicircles on the same side, usually purple.
  • Stationary front: triangles and semicircles on opposite sides.

Classic sequence for a frontal depression passing to the north of a ship in the Northern Hemisphere:

Stage Pressure Wind Cloud and weather Visibility
Warm front approaching Falling steadily Backing, freshening Cirrus, then cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus; rain becomes continuous Deteriorating in rain
Warm front passes Fall slows or stops Veers Rain eases Often poor
Warm sector Steady or slow fall Steady direction Low stratus, drizzle Poor; fog possible over cold sea
Cold front passes Sharp rise Veers sharply, often squally Cumulonimbus, heavy showers, possible thunder Poor in showers, then good
Behind the cold front Rising Gusty Showers, bright intervals Good outside showers

Real depressions rarely match the textbook exactly, but the pattern is reliable enough to plan around. An occlusion usually indicates a mature depression, which is often slowing down and starting to fill. Do not assume this means it has become harmless.

Movement. A useful rule is that a frontal depression tends to move roughly parallel to the isobars in its warm sector. Compare successive analyses and the prognosis charts to confirm the track and speed. Watch for small secondary lows forming on trailing fronts, because they can deepen quickly.

Station plots and wind barbs

Analysis charts often show plotted observations. You do not need to decode every element at sea, but you must be able to read wind, pressure and present weather.

  • Wind barb: the shaft points towards the direction the wind is blowing from.
  • Full feather: 10 knots.
  • Half feather: 5 knots.
  • Pennant (solid triangle): 50 knots.
  • Example: a pennant plus two full feathers is 70 knots.
  • Pressure and tendency: the station model shows pressure and the change over the preceding hours. A rapid fall near your position is a stronger warning than the isobar pattern alone.
  • Present weather symbols: for example dots for rain, commas for drizzle, asterisks for snow, and horizontal lines for fog.

The layout of the station model follows WMO conventions, but individual services present it differently. Learn the version you actually receive.

Worked scenario: what the ship should do

Situation. You are on passage eastbound in the North Atlantic in winter, at 12 knots. The 0000 UTC analysis shows a depression of about 975 hPa roughly 300 nm to your north-west. Its warm sector isobars run SW-NE, and the cold front trails south-westwards behind it. The isobars over your position are closely spaced. The T+24 prognosis shows the low deepening and moving north-east, with the cold front sweeping across your track tomorrow afternoon.

Reading it, step by step:

  1. Hemisphere and circulation. Northern Hemisphere, so the circulation is anticlockwise. You are south-east of the centre, so expect a southerly to south-westerly wind now.
  2. Strength. Measure the isobar spacing on the geostrophic scale at your latitude. Allow for the surface wind being backed and somewhat lighter than the geostrophic value. Then compare with the official forecast and your own observations.
  3. Track. The warm-sector isobars run SW-NE and the prognosis agrees, so the centre will pass to your north.
  4. Sequence. You are likely in or near the warm sector, so expect poor visibility and drizzle. The cold front will then bring a sharp veer to westerly or north-westerly, squalls, and a rapid rise in pressure. A heavy swell will build behind it.
  5. Ship's action, in line with the company SMS and the Master's standing orders:
    • Inform the Master early.
    • Review the passage plan.
    • Carry out heavy weather preparations: lashings, deck openings, loose gear, and restricting access to the open deck.
    • Consider adjusting speed or course so the sea is not on a dangerous heading.
    • Increase the frequency of barometer readings.
    • Prepare for restricted visibility in the warm sector.
  6. Monitor. Compare each new chart with the last one. If the observed barometer and wind depart from the forecast, trust your observations and re-evaluate.

If you meet storm-force winds for which no warning has been issued, SOLAS requires the master to send a danger message. The detailed reporting procedure is set by the administration and the company.

Common mistakes on the bridge

  • Treating an old analysis as current. Always check the valid time in UTC.
  • Confusing a prognosis with an analysis, or reading a forecast chart as though it were certain.
  • Reading the geostrophic scale at the wrong latitude, or measuring the isobar spacing along the track instead of at right angles to the isobars.
  • Taking geostrophic wind as surface wind without allowing for friction.
  • Applying Northern Hemisphere rules south of the equator.
  • Reading isobar charts near the equator the same way as at mid-latitudes. At low latitudes pressure gradients are weak, and the isobar picture is a poor guide. Tropical revolving storms need separate treatment and the latest warnings.
  • Relying on GRIB files alone. GRIB data is raw model output without a forecaster's analysis. It can smooth out frontal and squall winds. Use it alongside official charts and warnings, not instead of them.
  • Ignoring the barometer. A falling barometer and a backing wind on your own bridge beat any chart.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • State Buys Ballot's law for both hemispheres. Back to the wind: the low is on your left in the Northern Hemisphere and on your right in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Why does the geostrophic scale change with latitude? The Coriolis effect weakens towards the equator. A given pressure gradient therefore produces a stronger balanced wind at lower latitudes.
  • Describe the weather as a warm front, then a cold front, pass to your north. Give the sequence from the table above:
    • pressure falling, then steady, then rising sharply;
    • wind backing, then veering, then veering sharply;
    • cloud changing from cirrus to nimbostratus, then stratus, then cumulonimbus.
  • What is a col, and what weather do you expect? It is a slack area between two highs and two lows. Expect light, variable winds and possible fog.
  • How would you predict the movement of a depression from the chart? Use the warm-sector isobars, successive analyses and the prognosis charts.
  • What are your actions on receiving a chart showing a deepening low near your track? Brief the Master, review the passage plan, make heavy weather preparations and monitor closely.
  • When must a ship send a danger message for weather? Explain the SOLAS Chapter V obligations, including the case of storm-force winds for which no warning has been received.

Examiners want reasoning, not recitation. Sketch a frontal depression on paper and talk through what your ship would feel and do at each stage.

Key points
  • Check whether the chart is an analysis or a prognosis, and read its valid time in UTC before anything else.
  • Back to the wind: the low is on your left in the Northern Hemisphere and on your right in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Closer isobars mean stronger wind; measure the spacing on the geostrophic scale at your own latitude.
  • Surface wind over the sea blows somewhat lighter than geostrophic and crosses the isobars towards the low.
  • A warm front brings falling pressure and continuous rain; a cold front brings a sharp veer, squalls and rising pressure.
  • A frontal depression tends to move roughly parallel to the isobars in its warm sector.
  • Use charts alongside official warnings and your own barometer, never GRIB data alone.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find wind direction from a synoptic chart?

Wind blows roughly along the isobars: anticlockwise around lows and clockwise around highs in the Northern Hemisphere, and the reverse in the Southern Hemisphere. At the surface it is turned a little across the isobars towards the low pressure. Buys Ballot's law is a quick cross-check.

How do I work out wind speed from the isobars?

Measure the distance between adjacent isobars at right angles to them, at your position. Apply it to the geostrophic wind scale on the chart at your latitude. The surface wind over the sea will usually be somewhat less than this value, so cross-check with forecasts and your own observations.

What is the difference between an analysis chart and a prognosis chart?

An analysis shows the observed pattern at a stated past time. A prognosis is a forecast of the pattern at a future time, such as T+24 or T+48. Use analyses to see the trend and prognoses to plan ahead.

Can I rely on GRIB files instead of fax charts?

GRIB files are useful, but they are raw computer model output without a forecaster's analysis or warnings. They can understate winds in fronts, squalls and small intense systems. Good practice is to use official forecasts and warnings as well. Check your company SMS for its specific procedures.

What does an occluded front mean for the ship?

An occlusion forms when the cold front catches up with the warm front. It usually indicates a mature depression, which may be slowing and filling. It can still bring prolonged rain, poor visibility, strong winds and a significant wind shift, so prepare as you would for any active front.

Test yourself

In the Northern Hemisphere, standing with your back to the true wind, where is the area of low pressure?

Buys Ballot's law: in the Northern Hemisphere the low is on your left. It is on your right in the Southern Hemisphere.

What do closely spaced isobars on a surface chart indicate?

Closely spaced isobars mean a steep pressure gradient, which produces stronger wind. Light and variable winds go with widely spaced isobars, as in a col or high.

In the Northern Hemisphere, what does the wind typically do as a cold front passes the ship?

Cold front passage in the Northern Hemisphere brings a sharp veer, often with squalls and a rapid rise in pressure. Backing is associated with the approach of a warm front.

Over the open sea, how does the surface wind compare with the isobars?

Surface friction reduces wind speed and turns the wind across the isobars towards lower pressure. Exact geostrophic flow occurs only above the friction layer.

Two places have identical isobar spacing, one at 30°N and one at 60°N. Where is the geostrophic wind stronger?

The Coriolis effect is weaker at lower latitudes, so the same pressure gradient produces a stronger geostrophic wind at 30°N. This is why the geostrophic scale is latitude-dependent.
