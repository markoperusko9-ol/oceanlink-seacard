Swell, wind waves and parametric rolling: a practical guide for deck officers

Quick answer

Wind waves (sea) are raised by the local wind and are short, steep and irregular. Swell has left its generating area and is long, regular and often comes from a different direction. Parametric rolling is a dangerous roll build-up in head or following seas. It happens when the wavelength is close to the ship's length and the encounter period is close to half (or equal to) the natural roll period. The remedy is to change course and/or speed early.

Wind waves and swell: what the difference actually is

Wind waves (sea) are generated by the wind blowing where you are. Their size depends on three things:

  • Wind speed
  • Fetch, the distance of open water over which the wind blows
  • Duration, how long the wind has blown

If fetch or duration is limited, the sea cannot reach its full height for that wind speed. Once all three are sufficient, the sea is described as fully developed.

Swell is wave energy that has travelled out of the generating area. Away from the wind, the short, steep components die out. What remains is long-crested, regular and of longer period. Swell can run for hundreds or thousands of miles and often arrives from a direction quite different from the local wind.

Feature Wind waves (sea) Swell
Source Local wind Distant wind system
Period Short Long
Shape Steep, irregular, breaking crests Rounded, regular, long crests
Direction Roughly with the local wind Independent of local wind
Ship effect Spray, slamming, short pitching Heavy rolling and pitching, resonance risk

When estimating wind force from the Beaufort scale, judge the sea, not the swell. A long, low swell under a light breeze does not mean a strong wind.

The numbers you should be able to use

In deep water (depth greater than about half the wavelength), simple relationships link wave period (T, seconds), wavelength (L) and wave speed (C):

  • L ≈ 1.56 × T² metres
  • C ≈ 1.56 × T m/s, which is about 3 × T knots

So a 10-second swell is about 156 m long and travels at about 30 knots. A 15-second swell is roughly 350 m long and moves at about 45 knots.

Measuring period on board. Pick a patch of foam or a bit of flotsam some distance from the ship. Time several successive crests passing it with a stopwatch and divide by the number of intervals. Because the object drifts with the water, this gives approximately the true wave period. Timing crests passing the ship itself (for example the bow) gives the encounter period, unless the ship is stopped.

Height. Forecasts give significant wave height (Hs), the mean height of the highest one-third of the waves. An experienced observer's visual estimate is close to Hs. Individual waves much higher than Hs are expected over a few hours, and the largest can approach twice Hs. Plan for those, not for the average.

Combined sea and swell. Total significant height is commonly taken as the square root of (sea height² + swell height²). For example, a 3 m sea with a 4 m swell gives about 5 m, not 7 m.

Long swell as a warning. Longer-period waves travel faster, so long swell outruns the storm that generated it. An unexplained long, low swell, especially with a falling barometer, is a classic early sign of a tropical revolving storm.

Encounter period: why your course and speed change everything

The ship does not feel the true wave period. It feels the encounter period (Te), the time between successive crests reaching the ship.

  • Head seas: you meet crests faster, so Te is shorter than the true period.
  • Following seas: crests overtake you more slowly, so Te is longer. If your speed component along the wave direction approaches the wave speed, Te becomes very long. This is the surf-riding and broaching zone.
  • Beam seas: Te is about equal to the true period.

Roll response depends on how Te compares with the ship's natural roll period (Tr). The rough rule is Tr ≈ 2CB/√GM, where B is the beam and C is a coefficient typically around 0.35–0.45 (2008 IS Code). This is equivalent to the rule of thumb Tr ≈ 0.7–0.8 × B/√GM. A large GM gives a short, stiff roll and a small GM gives a long, slow roll.

Time Tr yourself in a calm-ish sea. Also check what the stability booklet or loading computer gives. Some company procedures require Tr to be recorded at departure.

Two resonance conditions matter:

Condition Typical heading Ratio
Synchronous rolling Beam to quartering seas Te ≈ Tr
Parametric rolling Head or following seas (and near them) Te ≈ Tr/2, sometimes Te ≈ Tr

Parametric rolling: the mechanism

Parametric rolling does not need a wave pushing the ship sideways. It comes from stability that changes as waves pass along the hull:

  • Wave trough amidships, crests at bow and stern. The waterplane widens where the hull flares. GM increases and the ship is stiff.
  • Wave crest amidships. The ends lift out of the water and the waterplane narrows. GM can fall sharply, even close to zero for a moment.

Suppose GM cycles twice for each full roll, which means Te is about half Tr. The ship then rolls out while stability is low and is snapped back while it is high, gaining energy each cycle. Large angles can develop in just a few rolls. That is what catches watchkeepers out.

Risk factors:

  • Wavelength comparable to ship length
  • Pronounced bow flare and wide, flat stern, typical of large container ships and car carriers, but not limited to them
  • Low roll damping, for example small bilge keels or low speed
  • Wave height above a threshold, which differs by ship
  • Head or following seas, including oblique angles near them

IMO MSC.1/Circ.1228 includes parametric rolling among the dangerous phenomena the master should be guided on. Ship-specific vulnerability can be assessed using the interim second-generation intact stability criteria (MSC.1/Circ.1627, applied on a voluntary basis), together with any operational guidance the company, builder or class provides.

Worked scenario: spotting the 2:1 trap

Situation: A 250 m container ship is heading into a swell from right ahead at 16 knots. The forecast swell period is 12 s. Natural roll period at departure was timed at about 17 s.

Step 1. Wavelength. L ≈ 1.56 × 12² ≈ 225 m. This is close to ship length, which is the first warning.

Step 2. Wave speed. C ≈ 1.56 × 12 ≈ 18.7 m/s. Ship speed 16 kn ≈ 8.2 m/s.

Step 3. Encounter period (head seas). Te ≈ L / (C + V) = 225 / (18.7 + 8.2) ≈ 8.4 s.

Step 4. Compare with Tr. 17 / 8.4 ≈ 2.0. This is the parametric rolling condition.

What you would see: the ship pitches steadily, then starts to roll. The rolls grow over four or five cycles, with one full roll for every two pitch cycles.

Action: change course and/or speed to move Te away from Tr/2. Check the new ratio using the same method or the ship's polar diagram or decision-support tool if fitted. Do not assume a speed reduction alone will cure it. In head seas, slowing down lengthens Te. Depending on the numbers, that can move you towards the critical ratio rather than away from it.

What the officer of the watch should do

If parametric or synchronous rolling begins:

  1. Call the master immediately. Heavy weather and unusual ship behaviour are standard call-the-master items in most bridge standing orders.
  2. Change the encounter conditions.
    • Alter course so the sea is well off the bow or quarter.
    • Adjust speed.
    • Consider both together.
    • Use manual steering if the autopilot cannot hold the ship.
  3. Warn the ship. Use the PA so people stop work, secure themselves and keep clear of deck and lashing areas. Inform the engine room.
  4. Monitor and record.
    • Roll angles from the inclinometer
    • Roll and pitch periods
    • Heading, speed and sea and swell observations in the log
  5. Check cargo and securing once it is safe to do so.

Preventive measures are mainly for the master and chief officer at the planning stage:

  • Know Tr for the departure condition.
  • Study the wave forecast for swell period and direction, not just height.
  • Use weather routing.
  • Avoid planning long legs with long swell right ahead or astern where the ship is known to be susceptible.

Changing GM at sea by ballasting is a planned decision for the master and chief officer, not an OOW quick fix. Slack tanks introduce free surface effect, and ballasting in heavy weather has its own hazards.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Common mistakes

  • Believing that head seas mean pitching only, so no roll is expected.
  • Judging wind force by the swell height.
  • Watching the local sea and ignoring a long swell from a distant system.
  • Treating speed reduction as the universal cure.
  • Not knowing the ship's natural roll period for the current loading condition.
  • Confusing synchronous rolling (Te ≈ Tr, usually beam seas) with parametric rolling (Te ≈ Tr/2, head or following seas).

Typical oral questions, with model answers

  • Define swell and sea. Swell has left its generating area and is long, regular and low-steepness. Sea is raised by the local wind and is short, steep and irregular.
  • What governs the height of wind waves? Wind speed, fetch and duration.
  • What is significant wave height? The mean height of the highest third of the waves. Expect individual waves well above it.
  • What is encounter period, and how do course and speed affect it? It is the period of waves as experienced by the moving ship. It is shortened in head seas and lengthened in following seas.
  • Explain parametric rolling. GM varies between wave crest and wave trough amidships. When Te is about Tr/2, the roll amplifies over a few cycles. It is most likely when wavelength is close to ship length, in head or following seas, on hulls with flare.
  • Your ship starts rolling heavily in a head swell. What do you do? Call the master. Alter course and/or speed to change the encounter period. Warn the crew, inform the engine room and log the event.
  • Which IMO guidance covers this? MSC.1/Circ.1228, the revised guidance to the master on avoiding dangerous situations in adverse weather and sea conditions.

Examiners vary by administration. Expect follow-up questions on GM, roll period and heavy weather precautions in the same answer.

Key points
  • Wind waves depend on wind speed, fetch and duration. Swell is energy that has left the generating area.
  • In deep water, wavelength is about 1.56 × T² metres and wave speed is about 3 × T knots.
  • The ship responds to the encounter period, which changes with course and speed.
  • Parametric rolling risk is highest when the encounter period is about half the natural roll period and the wavelength is close to ship length.
  • A classic sign of parametric rolling is one roll for every two pitch cycles in head or following seas, building fast.
  • Cure it by changing course and/or speed. A speed reduction alone may make it worse.
  • An unexplained long swell with a falling barometer can be the first warning of a tropical revolving storm.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between swell and wind waves?

Wind waves are generated by the wind where you are and are short, steep and irregular. Swell consists of waves that have travelled away from the area where they were generated. They are longer, more regular and often come from a different direction to the local wind.

What causes parametric rolling on ships?

It is caused by the periodic change in transverse stability as wave crests and troughs pass along the hull. When the encounter period is roughly half the ship's natural roll period, the roll gains energy each cycle and can reach large angles within a few rolls. It is most likely in head or following seas with a wavelength similar to ship length.

Which ships are most at risk of parametric rolling?

Ships with large bow flare and wide, flat sterns, especially large container ships and car carriers, are the best known cases. Fishing vessels and other hull forms can also be affected. The ship-specific risk depends on hull form, loading condition, roll damping and speed, so check your stability booklet and any operational guidance on board.

How do I find the ship's natural roll period?

Time several full rolls with a stopwatch in moderate conditions and take the average. Compare the result with the value from the loading computer or stability booklet. A short roll period indicates a large GM and a long one indicates a small GM, so it changes with every loading condition.

Should I reduce speed if the ship starts parametric rolling?

Not automatically. The aim is to move the encounter period away from the critical ratio, which usually means changing course, speed or both. In head seas a speed reduction lengthens the encounter period and can move you closer to the danger zone, so check the result and inform the master straight away.

Test yourself

Which statement best describes swell?

Swell is wave energy that has left its generating area, becoming longer and more regular. Local wind-raised waves are the sea. Meteorology competence, STCW Code Table A-II/1.

In deep water, approximately how long is a wave with a period of 10 seconds?

Deep-water wavelength is about 1.56 × T², so 1.56 × 100 ≈ 156 m. The figure of 30 m confuses wavelength with wave speed in knots (about 3 × T = 30 kn).

Parametric rolling is most likely when:

Parametric rolling results from GM varying between crest and trough, typically at Te ≈ Tr/2 in head or following seas. Beam seas with Te ≈ Tr describe synchronous rolling (IMO MSC.1/Circ.1228).

Which observation most strongly suggests parametric rolling has started?

A 2:1 relationship between pitch and roll, with rapid build-up in head or following seas, is the classic signature. Rolling in step with beam waves points to synchronous rolling (IMO MSC.1/Circ.1228).

The OOW notices parametric rolling developing in a head swell. What is the correct immediate action?

Changing heading and/or speed moves the encounter period away from the critical ratio, and the master must be called. Unplanned ballasting adds free surface and other risks, and slowing down in head seas can make things worse (IMO MSC.1/Circ.1228; SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34).
