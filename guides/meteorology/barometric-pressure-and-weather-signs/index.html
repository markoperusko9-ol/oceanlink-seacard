Barometric Pressure and Weather Signs: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

The barometer gives the bridge its earliest warning of bad weather. A steady or rapid fall, especially when combined with a swell from an unusual direction, changing cloud and a shifting wind, tells you a depression or tropical storm is approaching. To use it properly you must correct the reading for index error and height, allow for diurnal variation, watch the tendency rather than single readings, and report and act early.

Pressure basics every watchkeeper must know

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air column above you. On ships it is measured in hectopascals (hPa), which are numerically the same as millibars. Standard pressure at mean sea level is 1013.25 hPa. Older instruments in inches of mercury convert at about 33.86 hPa per inch.

What matters on the bridge is not the absolute figure but three things:

  • The corrected reading compared with the normal value for that area and season. You can find normal values in climatic atlases, routeing charts and sailing directions.
  • The tendency, meaning how much the pressure has changed and in which direction over the last three hours.
  • The pressure gradient shown on synoptic charts. Closely spaced isobars mean strong winds.

Wind blows roughly along the isobars. Near the surface, friction turns it slightly inwards towards low pressure. In the northern hemisphere it circulates anticlockwise round a low and clockwise round a high. In the southern hemisphere the directions are reversed.

Buys Ballot's law. In the northern hemisphere, stand with your back to the true wind and low pressure lies on your left, a little forward of abeam. In the southern hemisphere it lies on your right. This gives you a first rough bearing of a depression centre from your own observation alone.

Shipboard instruments and correcting the reading

Most ships carry a precision aneroid barometer and a barograph. Some VOS-participating ships carry an electronic barometer supplied by a national meteorological service. Mercury barometers are now rare.

Reading the aneroid properly:

  1. Tap the glass gently first. This overcomes friction in the linkage.
  2. Read at eye level to avoid parallax.
  3. Apply the index error from the instrument's certificate or the last comparison. Port meteorological officers can check your barometer against a standard.
  4. Apply the height correction to reduce the reading to mean sea level. Pressure falls with height. Near sea level the correction is roughly 0.12 hPa per metre, which is about 1 hPa for every 8 m. The correction is always added for an instrument above sea level. Check the correction table supplied with the instrument or your company's met observation guidance.

Precision aneroids are temperature-compensated. Only mercury barometers need temperature and gravity (latitude) corrections.

The barograph is often more useful than the barometer. Its trace shows the shape of the change at a glance. Check that the clock is set correctly, that the pen is inking and that the chart is changed on schedule. Compare it with the corrected barometer regularly.

The interval for logging pressure, and whether you log the raw or corrected value, is set by your company SMS. Many companies require an entry every watch, and hourly entries once weather is deteriorating.

Diurnal variation and pressure tendency

Pressure has a regular twice-daily tide. It is highest around 1000 and 2200 local time and lowest around 0400 and 1600.

  • In the tropics the daily range can be up to about 3 hPa.
  • In high latitudes the range is small and usually masked by passing weather systems.

This matters most in the tropics. A fall of 2 hPa between 1000 and 1600 may be entirely normal. The same fall between 1600 and 2200, when pressure should be rising, is a warning.

Tendency is the change over the past three hours. It is reported as part of a ship's synoptic observation. The UK Met Office uses these terms in shipping forecasts:

Term Change in 3 hours
Steady less than 0.1 hPa
Rising or falling slowly 0.1 to 1.5 hPa
Rising or falling 1.6 to 3.5 hPa
Rising or falling quickly 3.6 to 6.0 hPa
Rising or falling very rapidly more than 6.0 hPa

In mid-latitudes, a fall of more than a few hectopascals in three hours generally means a gale or worse is coming.

Remember that a moving ship measures more than the weather system's own change. If you are steaming towards a low, your barograph will fall faster than a fixed station would record. If you are steaming away, it may show a slower fall or even a rise. Always read the tendency together with your course and speed.

Recognising an approaching depression or tropical storm

Mid-latitude depression (northern hemisphere, ship on the equatorward side of the track):

  1. Pressure begins to fall steadily. Cirrus thickens to cirrostratus, often with a halo round the sun or moon. Altostratus follows, then nimbostratus with continuous rain. The wind backs and freshens.
  2. At the warm front: the wind veers, the rate of fall slows or the pressure steadies, the temperature rises, and visibility is often poor in drizzle or fog.
  3. At the cold front: the wind veers sharply, often with squalls and heavy showers. Pressure rises sharply, temperature drops and visibility improves.

If the centre passes on your equatorward side instead, the wind backs steadily throughout. In the southern hemisphere, reverse the backing and veering.

A depression is said to deepen explosively when its central pressure falls by roughly 24 hPa in 24 hours, a figure scaled for latitude. These systems can outrun forecasts, so your own barograph is the check.

Tropical revolving storm (TRS). The Mariner's Handbook (NP100) gives a widely taught rule. Compare your corrected reading, after allowing for diurnal variation, with the normal pressure for that place and season:

  • 3 hPa or more below normal: suspect a TRS.
  • 5 hPa or more below normal: there is little doubt a storm is near.

Other warning signs include:

  • A long, low swell from an unusual direction, often felt well before the storm.
  • The diurnal variation disappearing from the barograph trace.
  • Unusually clear visibility, or a brassy or red sky at sunrise or sunset.
  • Cirrus converging towards a point on the horizon.
  • A change in the direction or strength of the trade wind or monsoon.

Worked scenario: deciding whether to call the Master

Figures are illustrative only.

You are the 2/O in the Bay of Bengal in October, on the 0800-1200 watch. At 1000 local time you read the following:

  • Aneroid reading: 1001.5 hPa
  • Index error: +0.4 hPa
  • Barometer height: 24 m, giving a height correction of about +2.9 hPa
  • Corrected reading: about 1004.8 hPa
  • Normal pressure for the area and month, from the routeing chart: about 1008 hPa

Assessment:

  • You are about 3 hPa below normal. That alone is enough to suspect a TRS.
  • It is 1000, when the diurnal tide is near its peak. Pressure ought to be above its daily mean, so the true departure is worse than the raw comparison suggests.
  • The barograph shows no normal morning rise.
  • A low swell is coming from the south-east, against the wind sea.

Actions:

  1. Call the Master.
  2. Check the latest NAVTEX and SafetyNET weather bulletins and any warnings.
  3. Start hourly pressure, wind and swell observations.
  4. Use Buys Ballot's law to estimate the bearing of the centre. Watch whether the wind veers, backs or stays steady to judge which semicircle you are in.
  5. Plot the forecast track against your passage plan.
  6. Begin heavy weather preparations.

If you encounter a tropical storm, or have good reason to believe one is developing or exists nearby, SOLAS V/31 and V/32 require a danger message whether or not a warning has been issued (see below).

What the ship must do: reporting and preparation

Reporting. SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31 requires the master to report a tropical storm met with. Regulation 32 adds that this should be interpreted broadly, including when there is good reason to believe a tropical storm is developing or exists nearby. Regulation 31 also requires a report of winds of force 10 or above for which no storm warning has been received. Regulation 32 lists the information to include. For a tropical storm, this includes the barometric pressure (preferably corrected, stating the units and whether corrected), its tendency, the true wind direction and force, the sea and swell, and the ship's position, true course and speed. The master must communicate the report by all means at his disposal to ships in the vicinity and to the competent authorities.

Under Regulation 5, Contracting Governments undertake to encourage ships to collect and exchange weather observations, for example through the Voluntary Observing Ships (VOS) scheme. Good barometer practice is the core of a useful observation.

Preparation. Exact checklists come from your company SMS. Typical items are:

  • Secure deck cargo, stores and moveable items.
  • Check lashings, closing appliances and weathertight doors.
  • Review stability and free surface.
  • Brief the engine room on possible speed reduction and manual steering.
  • Restrict access to open decks.
  • Update the passage plan with an alternative route or sea room.

For TRS avoidance, apply the semicircle rules and keep well clear of the dangerous semicircle and the track. Act early: sea room disappears quickly once the swell and wind build.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Mistakes examiners and Masters see:

  • Logging the raw aneroid reading but comparing it with sea-level chart values, without the height correction.
  • Treating a normal afternoon diurnal fall as a warning, or missing a real fall because it was hidden by the morning rise.
  • Reacting to a single reading instead of the three-hour tendency and the barograph trace.
  • Trusting a forecast issued hours ago over a falling barometer on board.
  • Forgetting the ship's own movement towards or away from the system.
  • A barograph clock several hours out, or a dry pen.

Typical oral questions:

  • How do you correct an aneroid reading to mean sea level?
  • What is diurnal variation, when are the highs and lows, and why does it matter in the tropics?
  • State Buys Ballot's law for both hemispheres.
  • Describe the sequence of cloud, wind, pressure and weather as a warm and then a cold front pass.
  • What barometric signs indicate a TRS? Which other signs support it?
  • When must the master send a danger message, and what weather information does it contain?
  • How does the ship's own movement affect the observed tendency?
Key points
  • Always correct the aneroid for index error and height before comparing with chart or normal values.
  • Diurnal variation peaks around 1000 and 2200 and dips around 0400 and 1600; it matters most in the tropics.
  • Tendency and the barograph trace tell you more than any single reading.
  • In the tropics, 3 hPa below normal suggests a TRS and 5 hPa below makes one almost certain.
  • Back to the wind: low pressure is on your left in the northern hemisphere and on your right in the southern.
  • SOLAS V/31 requires a danger message for a tropical storm, or for force 10 winds with no warning received.
  • Allow for the ship's own movement when reading the tendency on passage.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I add to the ship's barometer reading for height?

Near sea level, add roughly 0.12 hPa per metre of barometer height above the sea, which is about 1 hPa per 8 m. Use the correction table supplied with the instrument or your met observation guidance where available. The correction is always added for an instrument above sea level.

What is a dangerous rate of pressure fall at sea?

There is no single legal figure. UK shipping forecasts call a 3.6 to 6.0 hPa fall in three hours 'falling quickly' and more than 6.0 hPa 'falling very rapidly'. In mid-latitudes a fall of several hectopascals in three hours usually means a gale is coming. In the tropics, even a small fall below normal can signal a TRS.

Why does the barometer go up and down twice a day in the tropics?

This is diurnal variation, a semi-diurnal atmospheric tide. Pressure peaks around 1000 and 2200 local time and dips around 0400 and 1600. The range can be up to about 3 hPa in the tropics. Allow for it before deciding whether pressure is genuinely falling.

How do I find the direction of a depression centre without a weather chart?

Use Buys Ballot's law. In the northern hemisphere, with your back to the true wind, the low lies to your left and slightly ahead. In the southern hemisphere it lies to your right. Track how the wind shifts and how the barometer changes to judge whether the centre is approaching and on which side it will pass.

Do I have to report a tropical storm even if a warning is already out?

Yes. SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31 requires masters to communicate information on a tropical storm met with, and Regulation 32 extends this to cases where there is good reason to believe one is developing or exists nearby. The 'no storm warning received' condition applies only to winds of force 10 or above. Your company SMS and the Master decide the exact procedure, and your observations are valuable to forecasters either way.

Test yourself

A precision aneroid barometer is mounted 16 m above sea level. Which correction is needed to reduce its reading to mean sea level?

Pressure falls by roughly 1 hPa per 8 m near sea level, so about 2 hPa is added. Temperature compensation does not remove the height correction (WMO-No. 8; NP100).

In the northern hemisphere you stand with your back to the true wind. Where does the low pressure lie?

Buys Ballot's law places the low on the left in the northern hemisphere and on the right in the southern; surface friction turns the wind slightly inwards (NP100).

In the tropics, the corrected barometer reads 5 hPa below the seasonal normal after allowing for diurnal variation. What should you conclude?

The Mariner's Handbook guidance is that 3 hPa below normal suggests a TRS and 5 hPa below makes one almost certain (NP100).

A northern hemisphere ship is on the equatorward side of a depression's track. What happens to the wind as the cold front passes?

At a northern hemisphere cold front the wind veers sharply, pressure rises, temperature drops and showers or squalls occur (The Mariner's Handbook, NP100).

Under SOLAS V/31, which weather condition requires a danger message?

Regulation 31 lists, among other dangers, tropical storms and winds of force 10 or above for which no storm warning has been received (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31).
