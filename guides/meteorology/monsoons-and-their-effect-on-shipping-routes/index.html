Monsoons and their effect on shipping routes

Quick answer

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of the prevailing wind. It is caused mainly by uneven heating of large land masses and the sea, and by the seasonal shift of the ITCZ. For ships, the SW monsoon in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal (roughly June to September) brings strong winds, heavy swell, reversed currents, rain and reduced visibility. These conditions affect route choice, speed, load line zones, cargo safety and port operations. The NE monsoon is generally milder, except in the South China Sea, where it can be strong.

What a monsoon is and why it happens

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of the prevailing wind over a large region. It is not just "the rainy season". Examiners want the mechanism, not the weather report.

  • Differential heating: In the northern summer, the Asian land mass heats far more than the surrounding ocean. Low pressure develops over the subcontinent and the Arabian Peninsula. Air flows in from the cooler, higher-pressure ocean.
  • Shift of the ITCZ: The Intertropical Convergence Zone moves well north of the equator over South Asia in summer.
  • Coriolis effect: The SE trades of the southern hemisphere are drawn across the equator. Once in the northern hemisphere they are deflected to the right and become the SW monsoon.
  • Northern winter: The land cools and high pressure builds over central Asia. The flow reverses and you get the NE monsoon, a drier outflow from the land.

The same principle drives other monsoon systems:

  • the East Asian monsoon (South China Sea, East China Sea)
  • the northern Australian monsoon (NW in the southern summer)
  • the West African monsoon

The Indian Ocean system is the one most Indian and Filipino officers deal with, and the one examiners usually ask about.

The Indian Ocean monsoon year at a glance

Dates shift from year to year. Always check the current forecast and the monthly routeing chart rather than relying on the calendar.

Period (approx.) North Indian Ocean conditions Practical effect
Dec to Mar: NE monsoon Moderate NE winds, generally fair weather in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal Usually the easiest passages. Westward-setting currents north of the equator
Apr to May: transition Light, variable winds. Rising sea temperatures Tropical cyclone risk in both the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea
Jun to Sep: SW monsoon Strong SW to W winds, heavy rain, long swell, haze Heavy weather, speed loss, port restrictions, eastward-setting currents
Oct to Nov: transition SW monsoon withdraws, winds variable Second tropical cyclone peak, especially in the Bay of Bengal

Key features of the SW monsoon:

  • Somali (Findlater) jet: A low-level wind maximum off the Horn of Africa. Winds near Socotra are often the strongest in the Arabian Sea at the height of the season, and gale force is not unusual there.
  • Somali Current: It reverses to flow strongly north-east along the Somali coast in the SW monsoon. It is among the fastest open-ocean currents and can run at several knots. Allow for it in DR and EP.
  • Monsoon currents: North of the equator the general set is eastward in the SW monsoon and westward in the NE monsoon.
  • Swell: Long, heavy SW swell persists across the Arabian Sea. It reaches the west coast of India and Sri Lanka even where the local wind is moderate.
  • Visibility: Visibility can drop in heavy rain squalls and in dust haze carried off Arabia and the Horn of Africa. Radar performance also suffers in heavy rain.

How monsoons change routes and ship operations

Route choice and passage planning

During the appraisal stage, check these sources for the month of the voyage:

  • the routeing chart
  • Ocean Passages for the World
  • the relevant Sailing Directions

They show prevailing wind, current and swell, and the routes recommended for each season. Many companies also use a shore-based weather routeing service. The master remains responsible for the final decision.

Load line zones

Parts of the northern Indian Ocean are seasonal areas under Annex II of the Load Line Convention. The permitted freeboard depends on the date. In some areas the Tropical mark may be used for part of the year, but only the Summer mark during the summer seasonal period fixed in Annex II. These calendar dates do not exactly match the meteorological monsoon, so always use the Annex II dates, not the weather.

  • Check the zone chart and the exact dates for every area on your route.
  • Do this especially when loading on the west coast of India, in the Gulf or in East Africa for a voyage that crosses the Arabian Sea.

Ports and anchorages

  • Several ports and anchorages exposed to the SW swell restrict or suspend some operations during the monsoon. This applies particularly to some smaller ports on India's west coast.
  • Restrictions vary by port and by year. Check local port notices and the agent's advice.
  • Expect surge alongside, parting moorings, difficult pilot boarding and poor holding in exposed anchorages.

Cargo

Cargo is often loaded in heavy monsoon rain at Indian and South-East Asian ports. Iron ore fines, nickel ore and similar Group A cargoes can then exceed their transportable moisture limit (TML).

  • The IMSBC Code puts the responsibility on the shipper to declare moisture content.
  • The master can refuse cargo that appears too wet.
  • Liquefaction losses have occurred during monsoon seasons.

Machinery

Head seas and swell cause:

  • propeller racing
  • engine overload and higher fuel consumption
  • slamming

The chief engineer should know the expected conditions so that bunker reserves and engine load limits can be planned.

Security

The SW monsoon sea state makes small-craft attacks off Somalia harder. Industry security guidance has noted higher risk in the calmer transition periods. Do not treat the monsoon as protection; follow current BMP and UKMTO advice.

Recognising the onset and keeping track at sea

Officers should be able to recognise the monsoon developing or strengthening, not just read about it after the event.

Signs on board

  • Wind veering and freshening from the SW to W over the Arabian Sea, sustained over days rather than in short gusts.
  • A long swell from the SW arriving ahead of the wind. Swell period and height increase.
  • Large cloud development, frequent rain squalls and falling visibility.
  • The usual diurnal pressure variation in the tropics is still visible. A marked departure from it, or a steady fall, suggests a depression or cyclone. Treat that seriously, particularly in the transition months.
  • Set and drift building up in the EP, especially near the Somali coast.

Information sources

  • NAVTEX and SafetyNET forecasts and warnings for the METAREA you are in. India is the issuing service for the northern part of METAREA VIII.
  • Weather fax and GRIB files. Remember that GRIB data is model output without a forecaster's judgement and can underestimate squalls and local jet strength.
  • Weather routeing service updates.

Record keeping

  • Keep proper weather observations in the deck log: wind, sea, swell direction and period, pressure and visibility.
  • Compare each observation with the forecast. A trend you have logged yourself is often the first warning that the forecast is wrong.

Worked scenario: Gulf of Aden to Colombo in July

A loaded Supramax leaves the Gulf of Aden eastbound for Colombo in mid-July. The 2nd Officer is preparing the passage plan.

  1. Appraisal: Check the July routeing chart, Ocean Passages and Sailing Directions. Expect strong SW winds and heavy swell, worst in the area around Socotra. Expect a strong set from the Somali Current and the SW monsoon current.
  2. Load line: Confirm which marks apply in each zone crossed on the voyage dates. Plan the departure draught so that the ship will not be loaded beyond the applicable mark in any zone at the time she is in it. Allow for fuel and water consumed en route and for the fresh water allowance at the loading port.
  3. Route options: Compare a direct route with any track the routeing service suggests. Weigh the shorter distance against exposure to the strongest winds. Brief the master with the forecast for each option. The final decision is the master's.
  4. Sea direction: Eastbound, the wind and swell are mainly on the quarter or astern. Watch for:
    • broaching tendency
    • reduced stability on a wave crest in following seas
    • synchronous or parametric rolling

Apply the company's heavy weather guidance and the IMO guidance to masters on avoiding dangerous situations in adverse weather and sea conditions. 5. Heavy weather preparation before entering the area: - secure deck stores and the anchors - check hatch cover cleats and seals - check that vents and air pipes are closed where required - plan ballast, avoiding slack tanks - restrict access to the open deck and record it 6. Execution: - Monitor rolling period against encounter period. - Reduce speed or alter course early if rolling builds up. - Update the ETA. - Inform the company and the engine room of expected propeller racing. 7. Arrival: Check Colombo port notices for swell or pilotage restrictions. Plan for a possible wait at anchor.

Same voyage westbound: The ship heads into the sea and swell. Expect slamming, green water on the forecastle, significant speed loss and higher fuel consumption. Reducing speed early is better seamanship than trying to hold the schedule.

Common mistakes

  • Treating monsoon dates as fixed. Onset and withdrawal vary by weeks. Plan using the current forecast as well as climatology.
  • Forgetting the load line seasonal zone when loading for a voyage that crosses the Arabian Sea during the monsoon months.
  • Ignoring swell because the local wind is moderate. SW monsoon swell travels a long way and causes rolling, surge at berths and pilot boarding problems.
  • Confusing the monsoon with a tropical cyclone. The monsoon is a seasonal wind regime. Cyclones in the North Indian Ocean are most frequent in the transition months either side of it. Each needs its own response.
  • Not allowing for the reversed currents. A strong set off Somalia makes DR positions inaccurate unless you correct for it.
  • Accepting wet bulk cargo because the paperwork looks correct, when the cargo in the hold and the rain on the quay suggest otherwise.
  • Relying only on GRIB data for wind strength near Socotra and in monsoon squalls.

Oral exam questions to prepare

Examiners at OOW and Chief Mate level commonly ask the following. Answer in your own words and explain the reasoning.

  • Explain the cause of the SW monsoon. Cover differential heating, the Asian thermal low, the shift of the ITCZ, and the SE trades crossing the equator and being deflected by Coriolis.
  • Why is the SW monsoon stronger than the NE monsoon over the Arabian Sea? Mention the large summer pressure gradient, the long sea fetch, the moisture supply and the Somali jet. The NE monsoon is a weaker, drier outflow from the land.
  • What happens to the Somali Current during the SW monsoon? It flows strongly north-east along the coast. Mention the upwelling of cooler water.
  • When are tropical cyclones most likely in the Bay of Bengal? Mainly in the transition periods before and after the SW monsoon.
  • You are loading on the west coast of India in July for a voyage across the Arabian Sea. Which load line do you use? Consult Annex II and the zone chart for the dates and areas on the route. Ensure the ship will not be loaded beyond the applicable mark in each zone when she is in it, allowing for fuel and water consumption en route and for fresh water allowance at the loading port.
  • Which publications would you consult when planning a passage in the monsoon season? Routeing charts, Ocean Passages for the World, Sailing Directions, METAREA forecasts, and the weather routeing service if one is used.
  • What risks does the monsoon season pose to solid bulk cargo? Rain while loading can raise moisture content above the TML, leading to a risk of liquefaction. Mention the shipper's declaration and the master's right to refuse.
Key points
  • A monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal driven by land and sea heating differences and the seasonal shift of the ITCZ.
  • The SW monsoon (roughly June to September) brings strong winds, heavy swell, rain and haze, and is worst near Socotra.
  • The Somali Current and the North Indian Ocean monsoon currents reverse with the seasons; allow for them in DR and EP.
  • Parts of the northern Indian Ocean are seasonal load line areas; check Annex II and the zone chart for the voyage dates.
  • North Indian Ocean cyclone risk peaks in the transition months either side of the SW monsoon.
  • Monsoon rain at loading ports raises liquefaction risk for Group A bulk cargoes.
  • Plan using routeing charts, Ocean Passages, Sailing Directions and live METAREA forecasts, not calendar dates alone.

Frequently asked questions

When does the SW monsoon start and end for shipping in the Arabian Sea?

It typically sets in around early June and withdraws from late September into October. The strongest winds usually come in July and August. Onset and withdrawal vary from year to year, so use current forecasts as well as climatological averages.

Why is the area near Socotra so rough in the SW monsoon?

A low-level wind maximum, often called the Somali or Findlater jet, runs off the Horn of Africa during the SW monsoon. Winds there are often the strongest in the Arabian Sea, with a heavy sea and swell on top. Routeing charts and Sailing Directions show this clearly for the summer months.

Does the monsoon affect which load line mark a ship can use?

Yes, in some areas. Parts of the northern Indian Ocean are seasonal areas under Annex II of the Load Line Convention, and only the Summer mark may be used there during the summer seasonal period fixed in Annex II. These calendar dates do not exactly match the meteorological monsoon, so always use the Annex II dates, not the weather. Check the zone chart and dates for every area on the route before finalising the departure draught.

Are cyclones more likely during the monsoon?

In the North Indian Ocean, tropical cyclones are most frequent in the transition periods, roughly April to May and October to November. They can still form at other times. Monsoon depressions in the Bay of Bengal during the SW monsoon can also bring severe weather, so monitor warnings at all times.

Why do some Indian ports restrict operations during the monsoon?

Exposed ports and anchorages on the west coast receive heavy SW swell and frequent squalls. This makes pilot boarding, mooring and cargo work unsafe. The extent of any restriction depends on the individual port, so check local port notices and the agent's advice for the season.

Test yourself

What is the main reason the SE trade winds become south-westerly after crossing the equator in the northern summer?

Air drawn north across the equator towards the Asian thermal low is deflected to the right by the Coriolis effect, turning SE trades into SW winds. This is part of the meteorology knowledge expected under STCW Code, Table A-II/1.

When are tropical cyclones most frequent in the Bay of Bengal?

North Indian Ocean cyclone frequency peaks in the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon transition months. Warnings are issued under the SOLAS Chapter V meteorological services arrangements.

A ship loading in a seasonal tropical area of the northern Indian Ocean during the SW monsoon months should check which instrument for the freeboard mark that applies?

Load Line Convention Annex II defines the zones and seasonal areas, including the periods when Tropical or Summer freeboard applies.

How does the Somali Current behave during the SW monsoon?

The Somali Current reverses with the monsoon and becomes a strong north-easterly flow in the SW monsoon. Seasonal current data in routeing charts supports voyage planning under SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34.

Iron ore fines are loaded during heavy monsoon rain. What is the main safety concern?

Group A cargoes can liquefy if moisture content exceeds the transportable moisture limit. The IMSBC Code requires the shipper to declare moisture content, and the master may refuse cargo that appears unsafe.
