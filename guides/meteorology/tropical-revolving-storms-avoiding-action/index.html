Tropical Revolving Storms: Avoiding Action for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Avoiding a tropical revolving storm (TRS) means using forecasts, swell, the barometer and wind shifts to fix where the centre is and which semicircle you are in, then manoeuvring away early. In the northern hemisphere the right-hand semicircle is dangerous, a veering wind means you are in it, and the standard action is to put the wind fine on the starboard bow and make best speed. The southern hemisphere is the mirror image.

What a TRS is and where you meet it

A tropical revolving storm is an intense, compact low-pressure system that forms over warm tropical oceans. The local name depends on the ocean basin:

  • Hurricane: North Atlantic, Caribbean, eastern North Pacific
  • Typhoon: western North Pacific, including the South China Sea
  • Cyclone: North Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea), South Indian Ocean, Australian waters and the South Pacific

They usually form between about 5° and 20° of latitude over sea surface temperatures of roughly 26–27°C or more. They do not normally form within a few degrees of the equator because the Coriolis effect there is too weak to start the rotation.

Circulation: winds blow anticlockwise and inwards in the northern hemisphere and clockwise and inwards in the southern hemisphere.

Typical track: in the northern hemisphere a storm generally moves westward or west-north-westward at first. It often then recurves poleward and north-eastward, and frequently speeds up once it has recurved. Southern hemisphere storms usually move westward or west-south-westward and then recurve to the south-east. The recurvature point is where forecasts are most uncertain and where ships most often get caught.

Each basin has its own seasons (the Bay of Bengal, for example, has two peaks around the monsoon transitions). Check the Sailing Directions, routeing charts and the Mariner's Handbook for the area you are trading in.

Recognising an approaching storm

Today most ships first hear about a storm through NAVTEX, SafetyNET, weather fax or GRIB data, or from the company or a weather routeing service. The traditional signs still matter. Forecasts carry track errors, small storms can be missed, and the oral examiner will expect you to know them.

Swell. A long, low swell from an unusual direction is often the earliest sign. It can reach you well before any change in the weather. The swell direction gives a rough bearing of the storm.

Barometer. In the tropics the pressure normally follows a regular twice-daily rise and fall (diurnal variation). Always correct the reading for diurnal variation and compare it with the normal pressure for that place and season, taken from climatic tables or routeing charts.

  • Corrected pressure about 3 hPa or more below normal: suspect a TRS.
  • About 5 hPa or more below normal: treat a TRS as present and act.

An uncorrected reading can hide a real fall or invent a false one.

Other signs:

  • Cirrus spreading from one direction and thickening to cirrostratus and then heavy cumulonimbus
  • Oppressive, humid air and unusually vivid sunrise or sunset colours
  • Wind that freshens and departs noticeably from the expected trade or monsoon direction
  • Rapid changes in weather as the main cloud bank (the bar of the storm) appears on the horizon

Once a storm is suspected, record pressure, wind direction and force, and swell direction at least hourly.

Finding the centre and your semicircle

Bearing of the centre: Buys Ballot's law. In the northern hemisphere, face the true wind and the low pressure lies on your right. In a TRS the centre bears roughly 8 to 12 points (about 90°–135°) to the right of the wind direction. The angle is nearer 12 points when you are far out and nearer 8 points close in. In the southern hemisphere the centre is on your left.

Semicircles. Look along the storm's direction of travel:

Northern hemisphere Southern hemisphere
Dangerous semicircle Right-hand Left-hand
Navigable semicircle Left-hand Right-hand
Most dangerous part Leading (advance) quadrant of the dangerous semicircle Same

The dangerous semicircle has stronger winds, because the storm's forward speed adds to the circulation. The wind there also tends to blow a ship towards the path of the centre.

Which semicircle are you in? Watch the shift in true wind direction over time. Use true wind, not apparent wind, and allow for your own course and speed. Some masters heave to briefly to get a clean observation.

Observed true wind Northern hemisphere Southern hemisphere
Veering (shifting clockwise) Dangerous semicircle Navigable semicircle
Backing (shifting anticlockwise) Navigable semicircle Dangerous semicircle
Steady direction, falling barometer, increasing wind In or near the path, ahead of the centre Same
Steady direction, rising barometer In or near the path, behind the centre Same

Avoiding action: the standard manoeuvres

The text below is the traditional guidance for power-driven ships found in the Mariner's Handbook and standard seamanship texts. Your company's heavy weather procedures and the master's judgement of sea room and ship behaviour come first.

Northern hemisphere

  • Dangerous semicircle: put the wind 1 to 4 points (about 11°–45°) on the starboard bow and proceed as fast as practicable. Alter course to starboard as the wind veers. If you cannot make way, heave to with the wind and sea on the starboard bow.
  • Navigable semicircle: put the wind well on the starboard quarter, note the course and proceed at best speed. Alter course to port as the wind backs.
  • In the path, ahead of the centre: put the wind well on the starboard quarter and run at best speed into the navigable semicircle. Then follow the navigable-semicircle procedure.

Southern hemisphere: reverse everything

  • Dangerous semicircle: wind 1 to 4 points on the port bow. Alter to port as the wind backs.
  • Navigable semicircle or in the path: wind well on the port quarter. Alter to starboard as the wind veers.

A simple memory aid: in either hemisphere, the standard action keeps the bow pointing away from the centre's path and the wind steadily shifting in the direction you are turning.

In every case the aim is to increase distance from the centre as early as possible. Avoid courses that cross ahead of the storm's track, because the forecast track can shift or the storm can speed up after recurving.

Modern practice, including US forecast-agency guidance for mariners, also adds an allowance for forecast track error. That allowance widens with forecast time, and you plan to stay clear of the 34-knot wind field plus that margin. Whatever method you use, plan against the whole forecast cone, not the line drawn down its middle.

Worked scenario: northern hemisphere

Situation. You are in the Bay of Bengal in early November on passage south. A cyclone warning places a depression well to the south-east of you. The wind is north-east force 6. A long south-easterly swell has set in. The barometer, corrected for diurnal variation, is 5 hPa below normal and falling.

Step 1: bearing of the centre. Face the wind (045°). In the northern hemisphere the centre lies 8 to 12 points to the right, so it bears roughly 135° to 180°. This agrees with the swell direction and the warning.

Step 2: semicircle. Over the next two hours the true wind shifts from north-east to east-north-east and then to east. The wind is veering, so you are in the dangerous semicircle.

Step 3: action. Put the wind 1 to 4 points on the starboard bow. With the wind from 090°, that means a heading of roughly 045° to 079°. Proceed at the best speed the sea allows. As the wind continues to veer, alter course to starboard to keep it on the bow.

Step 4: plot and report. Plot the estimated centre every hour from the wind bearing and the latest warnings. Watch for a steadying of the wind with a still-falling barometer, because that suggests the storm is curving onto your track. Prepare the ship: secure for heavy weather, check stability and slack tanks, and brief the engine room. Send the SOLAS Chapter V danger message (Reg. 31/32) to ships in the vicinity and the competent authorities. This is required on meeting a tropical storm even if warnings are already in force.

Abandoning a planned port call or delaying departure is often the best avoiding action of all. Leaving port late to beat a storm, or anchoring off in its path, has lost ships.

Reporting obligations and common mistakes

Reporting. Under SOLAS Chapter V, the master of a ship that meets a tropical storm must report it to ships in the vicinity and to the competent authorities. The same applies to winds of Beaufort force 10 or above for which no storm warning has been received.

The danger message should include:

  • A statement that a TRS has been encountered
  • Time and date (UTC) and position
  • Barometric pressure (stating whether corrected or uncorrected) and its tendency
  • True wind direction and force
  • State of sea and swell
  • Ship's true course and speed

While you remain under the storm's influence, further observations should be sent frequently. That means hourly if practicable and at intervals of not more than three hours.

Common mistakes

  • Using an uncorrected barometer reading. In the tropics the diurnal range alone can mask the early fall.
  • Using apparent wind to judge veering or backing, or ignoring the effect of your own course alterations.
  • Trying to cross ahead of the storm to save time, or trusting the forecast track line.
  • Acting too late. Once the sea builds, a full-powered ship may be unable to hold the course or speed the plan depends on.
  • Treating the navigable semicircle as safe. It is only less dangerous. It can still produce hurricane-force winds near the centre.
  • Forgetting recurvature. A storm moving away westward can turn back across your track.
  • Reversing the hemisphere rules. Cadets in the southern hemisphere often apply northern hemisphere actions out of habit.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Q: How would you know a TRS is approaching if you had no forecast? A: Unusual long swell, a barometer 3 hPa or more below normal after correcting for diurnal variation, changing cloud from cirrus to a heavy bar, oppressive weather, and the wind departing from its expected direction and freshening.

Q: You face the wind in the northern hemisphere. Where is the centre? A: About 8 to 12 points on the right. It is nearer 8 points when close to the centre.

Q: Northern hemisphere, the wind is veering. What does that mean and what do you do? A: The ship is in the dangerous semicircle. Bring the wind 1 to 4 points on the starboard bow, proceed as fast as practicable, and alter course to starboard as the wind veers.

Q: The wind direction is steady, the barometer is falling and the wind is increasing. A: The ship is in or near the path, ahead of the centre. In the northern hemisphere, bring the wind well on the starboard quarter and run into the navigable semicircle.

Q: Why is the right-hand semicircle dangerous in the northern hemisphere? A: The storm's forward speed adds to the wind speed there. The wind also tends to drive a ship into the path ahead of the centre.

Q: What must you report and under which regulation? A: The encounter with the tropical storm, under SOLAS Chapter V Regulations 31 and 32, with the content listed in Regulation 32.

Examiners often switch hemispheres partway through a question. Practise answering every question for both.

Key points
  • Correct the barometer for diurnal variation: 3 hPa below normal means suspect a TRS, 5 hPa means act.
  • Buys Ballot: face the wind and the centre is 8–12 points to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern.
  • Northern hemisphere: a veering wind means the dangerous semicircle and a backing wind means the navigable semicircle. Reverse both in the southern hemisphere.
  • Northern hemisphere, dangerous semicircle: wind 1–4 points on the starboard bow, best speed, alter to starboard as the wind veers.
  • Northern hemisphere, navigable semicircle or in the path: wind well on the starboard quarter, best speed.
  • Plan against the whole forecast cone, never cross ahead of the track, and allow for recurvature.
  • SOLAS Chapter V requires a danger message on meeting a tropical storm; Regulation 32 recommends follow-up observations hourly if practicable, and at intervals of not more than three hours, while under its influence.

Frequently asked questions

Which is the dangerous semicircle of a cyclone?

Looking along the storm's direction of travel, it is the right-hand semicircle in the northern hemisphere and the left-hand semicircle in the southern hemisphere. The leading quadrant of that semicircle is the most dangerous part. Winds there are stronger and tend to drive a ship towards the path of the centre.

How do I know if I am in the dangerous or navigable semicircle?

Watch the change in true wind direction over time. In the northern hemisphere a veering wind means the dangerous semicircle and a backing wind means the navigable one. The southern hemisphere is the reverse. A steady wind direction with a falling barometer means you are in or near the path ahead of the centre.

Why must the barometer be corrected for diurnal variation in the tropics?

Tropical pressure follows a regular twice-daily rise and fall. That cycle can be large enough to hide the early fall caused by an approaching storm, or to suggest a fall that is not there. Only the corrected reading compared with the seasonal normal is meaningful.

Is the navigable semicircle safe?

No. It is only less dangerous than the other side. Close to the centre it can still produce violent winds and very high seas, so the aim is always to gain distance from the centre early.

Do I have to report a tropical storm I encounter?

Yes. Under SOLAS Chapter V the master must report a tropical storm encountered, giving position, barometric pressure (stating whether corrected) and tendency, wind, sea, swell, and the ship's course and speed. Follow-up observations should be sent frequently while you remain under its influence.

Test yourself

Northern hemisphere: facing the true wind, roughly where does the centre of a TRS lie?

By Buys Ballot's law, low pressure lies on the right in the northern hemisphere. In a TRS the centre bears about 8–12 points to the right of the wind direction (Mariner's Handbook; standard meteorology texts).

Northern hemisphere: the true wind is steadily veering and the barometer is falling. Which semicircle are you in?

A veering wind in the northern hemisphere indicates the right-hand, dangerous semicircle. In the southern hemisphere a veering wind indicates the navigable semicircle.

Northern hemisphere, dangerous semicircle. What is the standard avoiding action for a power-driven ship?

Keeping the wind fine on the starboard bow and altering to starboard as it veers works the ship away from the centre's path. Wind on the starboard quarter is the action for the navigable semicircle (Mariner's Handbook guidance).

What corrected barometer reading should make you suspect a TRS?

Traditional guidance treats a reading 3 hPa or more below normal, after correcting for diurnal variation, as grounds for suspicion, and 5 hPa or more as confirmation. Absolute values and small raw falls are not reliable indicators in the tropics.

Under SOLAS Chapter V, how often should follow-up observations be sent while a ship remains under the influence of a tropical storm?

SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 32 provides for frequent follow-up observations, hourly if practicable and in any case at intervals of not more than three hours.
