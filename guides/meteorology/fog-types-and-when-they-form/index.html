Fog Types and When They Form: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Fog forms when air near the surface is cooled to its dew point or has enough moisture added to saturate it. At sea the type that matters most is advection (sea) fog, where warm moist air flows over water colder than its dew point. Radiation, frontal and steam fog are the other types you must recognise. If you know the type, you can predict when fog will arrive, how long it will last, and how to prepare the bridge.

What fog is and why it forms

Fog is a cloud in contact with the surface. It forms when the air becomes saturated and water vapour condenses on tiny particles such as salt, smoke and dust. The internationally used meteorological definition is visibility below 1,000 metres due to suspended water droplets or ice crystals. When visibility is reduced by droplets but stays at 1,000 m or more, the term is mist. Haze is reduced visibility caused by dry particles, not water.

Air can reach saturation in two ways:

  • Cooling to the dew point. The air is cooled by contact with a cold surface, by radiation at night, or by rising up a slope.
  • Adding moisture. Water evaporates into the air from warm water or from falling rain until the air saturates.

Every fog type below is one of these two mechanisms in a particular setting. In an oral exam, start with this principle and then name the type. It shows the examiner you understand fog and have not just memorised a list.

Forecast terms for visibility vary between met services. As one example, UK shipping forecasts use very poor for under 1,000 m, poor for 1,000 m to 2 NM, moderate for 2 to 5 NM and good for over 5 NM. Check the definitions used by the service whose forecast you are reading.

Advection fog (sea fog): the one that matters most

How it forms: warm, moist air moves horizontally (advects) over a sea surface whose temperature is at or below the dew point of that air. The lowest layer cools by contact and saturates.

Typical conditions:

  • Airflow from a warm sea area towards a cold current or cold coastal water.
  • Light to moderate winds. Some wind is needed to keep feeding moist air over the cold water. Sea fog can persist even in fresh winds. Stronger winds tend to mix the layer upwards, which lifts the fog into low stratus.
  • Common in spring and early summer in mid-latitudes, when the sea is still cold and the air masses are warming.
  • The warm sector of a depression, where tropical maritime air flows over cooler water.

Well-known areas:

  • The Grand Banks of Newfoundland, where air from over the Gulf Stream crosses the cold Labrador Current.
  • The coasts of California, Peru and Chile, Namibia and north-west Africa, where cold currents and upwelling cool the sea surface.
  • The Western Approaches, English Channel and North Sea in spring and early summer.
  • The Yellow Sea, the East China Sea and the waters off northern Japan and the Kurils.

Key behaviour: sea fog does not reliably clear at sunrise. The sea surface temperature hardly changes between day and night, so advection fog can last for days. It clears when the airflow changes, when a front passes and brings drier or colder air, or when the wind strengthens enough to lift it.

Radiation fog: a land fog that comes to the ship

How it forms: on a clear night the ground loses heat by radiation. The air in contact with it cools to the dew point.

Conditions needed:

  • Clear skies, so heat escapes freely.
  • Light winds of a few knots. In a dead calm you tend to get dew or frost rather than deep fog. Too much wind mixes the cooled layer and gives low stratus or nothing at all.
  • Moist air near the surface, for example after rain or over damp ground.
  • Long nights. This makes autumn and winter under a slow-moving anticyclone the classic setting.

Why it concerns ships: radiation fog rarely forms over the open sea, because the sea surface temperature changes little between day and night. It does form over land and drains down river valleys into estuaries, rivers, ports and anchorages. A ship arriving at a river port at dawn may find the river blanketed in fog while the sea outside is clear.

Clearing: it usually thins and lifts after sunrise as the land warms, or when the wind increases. In winter, under a strong high, it can persist all day. Do not plan pilotage on the assumption that it will clear by a fixed time.

Frontal, steam and other fog types

Type Mechanism Where and when
Frontal (precipitation) fog Rain falls from warmer air aloft into colder air below and evaporates, which saturates the cold layer Ahead of a warm front or occlusion, in a belt of steady rain
Steam fog (sea smoke, arctic sea smoke) Very cold air moves over much warmer water; evaporation saturates the air Near ice edges and leads, and off cold coasts in winter with offshore winds; also over rivers and lakes on cold autumn mornings
Hill / upslope fog Moist air is forced up rising ground and cools as it expands Coastal high ground; often hides headlands and conspicuous marks used for fixing
Ice fog Suspended ice crystals in extreme cold High latitudes in severe winter conditions

Points examiners like:

  • Frontal fog is moisture-addition fog. Advection fog is cooling fog. Both can occur in the same depression: frontal fog ahead of the warm front, and advection fog in the warm sector behind it.
  • Steam fog is the reverse of advection fog. Advection fog is warm air over cold water. Steam fog is cold air over warm water. Steam fog is often shallow, but it can be dense enough to hide small craft and ice. In polar conditions it comes with sub-zero air and the risk of ice accretion on the ship.
  • Haze and mist are not fog. Haze is dry particles. Mist is fog-type droplets with visibility of 1,000 m or more.

Predicting fog on board: worked scenarios

Fog does not have to be a surprise. You have the tools to see it coming.

What you need:

  1. Dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures from the psychrometer or screen. Convert them to dew point using the hygrometric tables on board.
  2. Sea surface temperature. A bucket sample is closest to the true surface temperature. The engine-room sea-water inlet reading comes from below the surface and may be warmed, so treat it with caution.
  3. The synoptic picture. Look at the airflow direction, any fronts, and whether you are heading towards a cold current. NAVTEX and other forecasts will give visibility warnings.

Scenario 1: spring, North Sea, south-westerly airflow

  • Air temperature 12 °C, dew point 10 °C, sea temperature 8 °C.
  • The sea is colder than the air's dew point, and the wind is carrying that air over the water.
  • Conclusion: advection fog is likely. Expect it to persist until the airflow changes. Warn the master and prepare the bridge now, not when the fog arrives.

Scenario 2: autumn, arriving at a river port at 0500

  • There is a large anticyclone, the night was clear and the wind is light. The outer anchorage has good visibility.
  • Conclusion: radiation fog has probably formed inland and in the river. Expect reduced visibility in the pilotage waters, likely improving after sunrise but with no guarantee. Check with VTS and the pilot station about delays or restrictions.

Scenario 3: winter, leaving a Scandinavian or Canadian port, strong offshore wind well below freezing, open water

  • Conclusion: steam fog over the water. It is often patchy and shallow, so the bridge may be clear while small craft and growlers are hidden. Treat it as restricted visibility.

Rule of thumb, not a regulation: watch the trend. If the gap between air temperature and dew point is closing, and the dew point is at or above the sea temperature, fog is close. Log the readings regularly when you are approaching known fog areas.

Bridge actions when fog is expected or encountered

Fog brings the restricted-visibility rules into force. The detail of what you do comes from the master's standing orders and the company SMS, but the core is the same everywhere.

  • Call the master. The STCW Code (Section A-VIII/2) requires the OOW to notify the master immediately when restricted visibility is encountered or expected. Do not wait until visibility is already bad.
  • Safe speed (Rule 6). Set a speed appropriate to the visibility, traffic and your stopping distance.
  • Engines ready (Rule 19). Power-driven vessels must have engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. In practice, put the engine on standby and inform the engine room.
  • Look-out (Rule 5). Post an extra look-out, including a listening watch on the bridge wings. Shut wheelhouse doors only if it does not stop you hearing signals.
  • Steering. Change to manual steering as per orders.
  • Radar (Rule 7). Run both radars, with systematic plotting and ARPA. Use different ranges and adjust clutter controls with care. Do not rely on AIS alone, because many small craft do not transmit.
  • Sound signals (Rule 35). A power-driven vessel making way sounds one prolonged blast at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. Underway but stopped, it sounds two prolonged blasts at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • Lights. Navigation lights on (Rule 20(c)).
  • Other preparations. Close watertight doors where the SMS requires it, and check the position by all available means. Log the time of each action.

Remember that Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another in or near an area of restricted visibility. A fog bank ahead puts you in Rule 19 before you enter it.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting sea fog to burn off at sunrise. That is radiation fog behaviour. Advection fog over the open sea can last for days.
  • Taking dew point readings only after fog has arrived. The trend is your early warning.
  • Trusting the inlet temperature as the sea surface temperature. It can mislead you about how close you are to the fog threshold.
  • Treating sea smoke as harmless steam. It hides small craft and ice, and in the Arctic it comes with icing.
  • Relying on AIS targets. Fishing boats, yachts and small craft may show up on neither AIS nor radar in clutter.
  • Delaying Rule 19 actions until inside the fog bank. By then, the vessel approaching from the other side is already close.
  • Calling the master late because visibility "is only patchy". Patchy fog is restricted visibility; the STCW Code and standing orders require the master to be informed when it is encountered or expected.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Define fog and give its causes. A: Visibility below 1,000 m due to suspended droplets or ice crystals. It forms when air is cooled to its dew point or when enough moisture is added to saturate it.

Q: Explain advection fog and name two areas where it is common. A: Warm moist air passes over sea colder than its dew point and cools by contact. Two common areas are the Grand Banks, over the Labrador Current, and the Californian coast, where upwelling cools the sea.

Q: Why is radiation fog uncommon over the open sea? A: The sea surface temperature changes very little between day and night, so the surface does not cool the air enough overnight.

Q: What is the difference between steam fog and advection fog? A: Steam fog is very cold air over warmer water, which saturates the air by evaporation. Advection fog is warm moist air over colder water, which cools the air to its dew point.

Q: How would you forecast fog on board? A: Find the dew point from dry- and wet-bulb readings and compare it with the sea temperature. Check the airflow and fronts on the synoptic chart, and read the forecasts and NAVTEX. Watch the trend over time.

Q: You are OOW and visibility is reducing. What do you do? A: Call the master and put the engine on standby. Post extra look-outs and change to manual steering if ordered. Start the sound signals, set a safe speed, and use radar plotting on both radars. Then comply with Rule 19 and log every action.

Key points
  • Fog forms when air is cooled to its dew point or saturated by added moisture; visibility below 1,000 m.
  • Advection fog (warm moist air over colder sea) is the main sea fog and does not reliably clear at sunrise.
  • Radiation fog forms over land on clear, light-wind nights and drifts into rivers, estuaries and ports.
  • Steam fog (sea smoke) is very cold air over warmer water and can hide small craft and ice.
  • Compare the dew point from psychrometer readings with the sea temperature to predict fog.
  • Call the master when restricted visibility is encountered or expected, and apply Rules 5, 6, 7, 19 and 35.
  • Rule 19 applies in or near restricted visibility, so act before entering a fog bank.

Frequently asked questions

What visibility counts as fog at sea?

Fog is visibility below 1,000 metres caused by suspended water droplets or ice crystals. When droplets reduce visibility but it stays at 1,000 m or more, the term is mist. Forecast wording for visibility categories varies between met services, so check the definitions used in the forecast you are reading.

Will sea fog clear when the sun comes up?

Usually not. Advection fog depends on the sea temperature, which barely changes between day and night, so it can last for days. It clears when the airflow changes, when a front brings drier or colder air, or when stronger winds lift it into low cloud. Radiation fog over land is the type that tends to clear after sunrise.

What is sea smoke or arctic sea smoke?

It is steam fog. It forms when very cold air moves over much warmer water and evaporation saturates the air. It is common near ice edges and off cold coasts in winter. It can be shallow but still hide small craft and ice.

How do I calculate the dew point on board?

Take dry-bulb and wet-bulb readings from the psychrometer or screen and enter them in the hygrometric tables carried on board. Compare the result with the sea surface temperature. If the dew point is at or above the sea temperature and moist air is flowing over that water, advection fog is likely.

When should the OOW call the master in fog?

The STCW Code (Section A-VIII/2) requires the OOW to notify the master immediately when restricted visibility is encountered or expected. The master's standing orders may set a specific visibility threshold. Calling early is always correct.

Test yourself

Which type of fog is most commonly met by ships on the open sea?

Advection fog forms when moist air moves over colder water, which is the typical open-sea setting. Radiation fog needs a surface that cools strongly overnight, which the sea does not do.

What conditions produce advection fog?

Advection fog is contact cooling of moist air over cold water. The other options describe steam fog, a misconception about radiation fog at sea, and frontal fog.

Steam fog (arctic sea smoke) forms when:

Evaporation from relatively warm water into very cold air saturates the air and produces steam fog.

Why does radiation fog rarely form over the open sea?

Radiation fog needs strong overnight cooling of the surface. The sea's diurnal temperature range is small, so this does not happen.

In restricted visibility, you hear the fog signal of another vessel apparently forward of your beam, and you have not determined that no risk of collision exists. What does COLREG require?

COLREG Rule 19(e) requires you to reduce speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course, take all way off if necessary, and navigate with extreme caution until the danger of collision is over.
