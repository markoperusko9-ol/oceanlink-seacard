COLREG Rule 11 – Application (Section II): When the 'In Sight' Rules Apply

Quick answer

Rule 11 states that the rules in Part B, Section II (Rules 11 to 18) apply only to vessels in sight of one another. 'In sight' means one vessel can be observed visually from the other (Rule 3(k)). A radar or AIS contact alone does not count. When vessels are not in sight in or near restricted visibility, Rule 19 governs instead, and there is no stand-on or give-way vessel.

What Rule 11 says

Rule 11 is one of the shortest rules in the COLREGs. In paraphrase, it says the Rules in this Section apply to vessels in sight of one another.

That single line decides which part of Part B you use. Part B is split into three sections:

Section Rules When it applies
I – Conduct of vessels in any condition of visibility 4 to 10 Always, clear or thick
II – Conduct of vessels in sight of one another 11 to 18 Only when vessels are in sight of one another
III – Conduct of vessels in restricted visibility 19 Vessels not in sight of one another when navigating in or near an area of restricted visibility

Section II contains the rules most watchkeepers think of as 'the Rules of the Road':

  • Rule 12: sailing vessels
  • Rule 13: overtaking
  • Rule 14: head-on situation
  • Rule 15: crossing situation
  • Rule 16: action by give-way vessel
  • Rule 17: action by stand-on vessel
  • Rule 18: responsibilities between vessels

None of these applies unless the two vessels are in sight of one another. That includes the whole idea of a stand-on and a give-way vessel. Rule 11 is the switch that turns them on.

What 'in sight of one another' actually means

The definition is in Rule 3(k). Vessels are deemed to be in sight of one another only when one can be observed visually from the other.

Practical points for the watchkeeper:

  • Visual means visual. Seeing the hull, the lights or the shapes counts. A radar echo, an ARPA vector, an AIS target on the ECDIS or a VHF position report does not make vessels 'in sight'.
  • Night is not restricted visibility. On a clear night you are in sight of a vessel once you can see her lights. Section II applies from that moment.
  • Restricted visibility does not switch Section II off. In fog, rain or haze, once the other vessel comes into view, you are in sight of one another and Rules 12–18 apply. Rule 19 governs only vessels not in sight.
  • Range is not fixed. No distance in the Rules says when vessels are in sight. In good visibility it may be many miles; in dense fog it may be a few cables.

The definition does not say that both vessels must actually see each other. Where visibility is patchy, one vessel may see the other before she is seen herself. This is a grey area. The safe practice is to assume the other ship may not yet have you visually. Do not rely on stand-on status until you are sure she has seen you and is acting on it.

How Section I and Section II fit together

A common exam trap is to treat Section II as standing alone. It does not. Section I applies in any condition of visibility, so it applies alongside Section II whenever you are in sight of another vessel:

  • Rule 5: proper look-out
  • Rule 6: safe speed
  • Rule 7: risk of collision
  • Rule 8: action to avoid collision
  • Rule 9: narrow channels
  • Rule 10: traffic separation schemes

Some rules also cut across the sections:

  • Rule 13 (overtaking) applies notwithstanding anything in Sections I and II. An overtaking vessel keeps out of the way, even if she is otherwise privileged under Rule 18.
  • Rule 18 (responsibilities between vessels) opens with an exception: it applies except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 otherwise require.
  • Rule 2 (responsibility) sits over everything. It covers ordinary seamanship and departing from the Rules where necessary to avoid immediate danger.

Section II also links to Part D:

  • Rule 34(a): the manoeuvring signals (one short blast for 'altering to starboard' and so on) are for a power-driven vessel underway that is manoeuvring as authorised or required by the Rules, when vessels are in sight of one another.
  • Rule 34(d): the 'at least five short and rapid blasts' doubt signal is also for vessels in sight of one another.
  • Rule 35: fog signals apply in or near restricted visibility, whether by day or night. They do not stop just because one vessel has come into view.

Worked scenarios on the bridge

Scenario 1 – Clear night, crossing. You are on a power-driven vessel. You see a masthead light broad on your starboard bow at about 8 miles. As she closes you also see her red sidelight, and the compass bearing remains steady.

  • You can see her lights, so you are in sight of one another and Section II applies.
  • Rule 15 makes you the give-way vessel.
  • Rule 16 requires early and substantial action. Normally this is a bold alteration to starboard to pass astern of her.
  • Sound one short blast under Rule 34(a) as you alter.

Scenario 2 – Fog, radar contact only. Visibility is about 0.5 nm. Radar shows a target 20° on your starboard bow at 6 miles, closing, CPA 0.3 nm. Nothing is visible.

  • You are not in sight of one another, so Rule 15 does not apply. There is no give-way or stand-on vessel.
  • Rule 19 applies. Proceed at safe speed with engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  • Decide whether a close-quarters situation is developing. If it is, take avoiding action in ample time.
  • Rule 19(d) says to avoid, so far as possible, an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken. A starboard alteration or a speed reduction is the usual answer.
  • Sound fog signals under Rule 35.

Scenario 3 – Fog, the target comes into view. In the same situation, the other vessel suddenly appears out of the fog at 1.5 nm, still on your starboard bow.

  • You are now in sight of one another, so Section II applies.
  • Once she is visible, confirm her aspect from her lights or hull. If it is a crossing with her on your starboard side, Rule 15 makes you give-way.
  • At this range there is little time. Rule 8 still demands effective action.
  • If you were the stand-on vessel instead, Rule 17(b) would require you to act once collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone.
  • Your Rule 35 fog signals continue while you remain in restricted visibility.

Scenario 4 – Small craft not on radar. In clear daylight you see a small fishing boat fine on the port bow that is not painting on radar.

  • She is in sight, so Section II applies. If she is engaged in fishing within Rule 3(d), Rule 18 also applies.
  • You assess risk visually, for example by taking compass bearings under Rule 7(d).
  • Poor radar detection changes nothing about which rules apply.

Scenario 5 – Patchy visibility. You can see a vessel's lights through a gap in a rain squall. You have no indication that she can see you.

  • Treat Section II as applicable to your own conduct.
  • Do not sit on stand-on status. She may still be navigating under Rule 19 and could alter to port.
  • Watch her closely and consider an early call on VHF, without letting it replace action under the Rules.
  • Be ready to act under Rule 17 or Rule 2.

Common mistakes

  • Applying crossing or head-on rules to a radar-only target. This is the classic error. It leads to the 'I'm stand-on, I'll hold course' mindset in fog, which Rule 19 does not support.
  • Altering to port for a radar target ahead in fog because 'it would be head-on'. Rule 14 is a Section II rule. In fog you are under Rule 19, which steers you away from port alterations for vessels forward of the beam.
  • Thinking Section II only applies in good visibility. It applies whenever vessels are in sight, including in fog at close range.
  • Using Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals for a vessel you cannot see. These signals are for a power-driven vessel underway when vessels are in sight of one another. In restricted visibility, Rule 35 signals apply.
  • Stopping fog signals once one ship is visible. Rule 35 applies in or near restricted visibility. Others may still be out there unseen.
  • Treating AIS identification as 'in sight'. Knowing her name and course is not visual observation.
  • Forgetting Section I. Look-out, safe speed, risk of collision and Rule 8 apply at all times. Being stand-on under Section II does not excuse an improper look-out or unsafe speed.

Oral exam points

Examiners often open with Rule 11 because it tests whether you understand the structure of Part B, not just individual rules. Be ready to answer crisply.

  • Q: What does Rule 11 say? A: The rules in Section II apply to vessels in sight of one another.
  • Q: Define 'in sight of one another'. A: Rule 3(k). Vessels are deemed to be in sight only when one can be observed visually from the other.
  • Q: Which rules are in Section II? A: Rules 11 to 18.
  • Q: Does Rule 15 apply in fog? A: Only once the vessels are in sight of one another. Before that, Rule 19 applies and there is no give-way or stand-on vessel.
  • Q: Which rules apply in any condition of visibility? A: Section I, Rules 4 to 10. They apply together with whichever of Section II or Section III is relevant.
  • Q: You see a vessel on radar in fog, 30° on your port bow, closing with risk. What do you do? A: Rule 19. Use safe speed and take action in ample time. Avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam. Typically alter to starboard and/or reduce speed, and keep sounding Rule 35 signals.
  • Q: Can you sound one short blast for a vessel you cannot see? A: No. Rule 34(a) applies to a power-driven vessel underway manoeuvring as authorised or required by the Rules, when vessels are in sight of one another.

A good candidate links the rules together. Mention Rule 3(k) when defining Rule 11, Section I when explaining scope, and Rule 2 as the overriding duty. Expect follow-ups on the change from Rule 19 to Section II as visibility clears or a target emerges at close range.

Key points
  • Rule 11: Rules 11–18 apply only to vessels in sight of one another.
  • 'In sight' means one vessel can be observed visually from the other (Rule 3(k)); radar and AIS do not count.
  • No stand-on or give-way vessel exists between vessels not in sight; Rule 19 governs in or near restricted visibility.
  • Section II applies in fog once the other vessel comes into view.
  • Section I (Rules 4–10) applies in all visibility, alongside Section II or III.
  • Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals are for power-driven vessels underway when in sight; Rule 35 fog signals continue in restricted visibility.
  • In patchy visibility, never assume the other ship has seen you.

Frequently asked questions

Does a radar contact count as 'in sight' under the COLREGs?

No. Rule 3(k) defines 'in sight' as one vessel being observable visually from the other. A radar echo, ARPA plot or AIS target does not meet that definition. Until you can see the vessel or her lights, Section II does not apply. In or near restricted visibility, Rule 19 applies instead.

Is there a stand-on vessel in restricted visibility?

Not between vessels that are not in sight of one another. Rule 19 places a duty on each vessel to determine whether a close-quarters situation is developing and, if so, to take avoiding action in ample time. Stand-on and give-way roles come from Section II, which only applies once the vessels are in sight.

Do the crossing and head-on rules apply at night?

Yes, provided visibility is not restricted and you can see the other vessel's lights. Darkness alone is not restricted visibility. Once you see her lights, you are in sight of one another and Rules 13 to 18 apply as normal.

What happens when a vessel appears out of fog at close range?

From the moment she is in sight, Section II applies and the normal crossing, head-on or overtaking rules govern. Because the range is likely to be short, Rule 8 and Rule 17(b) become critical. Fog signals under Rule 35 continue while you are in restricted visibility.

Which COLREG rules apply in any condition of visibility?

Part B, Section I, which is Rules 4 to 10. These cover look-out, safe speed, risk of collision, action to avoid collision, narrow channels and traffic separation schemes. They apply together with Section II when vessels are in sight, or with Rule 19 when they are not.

Test yourself

Under Rule 3(k), when are vessels deemed to be in sight of one another?

Rule 3(k) requires visual observation. Radar, AIS and set distances play no part in the definition (COLREG Rule 3(k)).

In dense fog you have a radar target fine on the starboard bow, closing, with risk of collision. Nothing is visible. Which rule primarily governs your conduct?

The vessels are not in sight, so Section II does not apply. Rule 19 governs vessels not in sight in or near restricted visibility (COLREG Rules 11 and 19).

Which group of rules makes up Part B, Section II?

Section II runs from Rule 11 to Rule 18. Section I is Rules 4–10 and Section III is Rule 19 alone (COLREG Part B).

In restricted visibility, a vessel emerges from the fog and is clearly visible at 1.5 nm. What is the correct position?

Rule 19 applies only to vessels not in sight. Once in sight, Section II applies, whatever the general visibility (COLREG Rules 11 and 19(a)).

When may a vessel use the Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signal of one short blast?

Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals are for a power-driven vessel underway when vessels are in sight of one another. They are not for radar-only targets and do not replace Rule 35 signals (COLREG Rule 34(a)).
