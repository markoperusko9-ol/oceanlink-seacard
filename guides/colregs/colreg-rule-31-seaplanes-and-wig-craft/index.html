COLREG Rule 31 – Seaplanes and WIG Craft

Quick answer

Rule 31 allows a seaplane or wing-in-ground (WIG) craft that cannot practically show the standard lights and shapes to show ones as close as possible in character and position. It is not an exemption from carrying lights. The rule makes sense only when read with Rule 3 (definitions), Rule 18(e) and (f) (responsibilities) and Rule 23(c) (the WIG craft's flashing red light).

What Rule 31 actually says

Rule 31 is one of the shortest rules in Part C (Lights and Shapes), and it does one job.

If a seaplane or a WIG craft cannot practically show lights and shapes with the characteristics, or in the positions, that Part C prescribes, it must show lights and shapes as closely similar in characteristics and position as is possible.

Three things follow from that:

  • It is not an exemption. The craft still has to show lights and shapes. Only the exact specification is relaxed, and only as far as is truly impracticable.
  • The test is impracticability. An aircraft hull has wings, floats, a tail and no mast. A sidelight may end up on a wingtip and a masthead light may sit lower than Annex I would want. The standard is the closest you can get, not whatever is convenient.
  • It covers characteristics as well as position. Colour, arc and range should match the prescribed lights as nearly as the craft allows.

The original 1972 rule covered seaplanes only. WIG craft were added by the amendments adopted in 2001, which also inserted the WIG definition in Rule 3 and the WIG provisions in Rules 18 and 23.

Definitions you must have straight (Rule 3)

Rule 31 depends on the definitions, and examiners often start there.

Term Paraphrase of Rule 3
Vessel (3(a)) Every description of water craft used or capable of being used for transport on water. This expressly includes non-displacement craft, WIG craft and seaplanes.
Power-driven vessel (3(b)) Any vessel propelled by machinery.
Seaplane (3(e)) Any aircraft designed to manoeuvre on the water.
WIG craft (3(m)) A multimodal craft which, in its main operational mode, flies close to the surface using surface-effect action.

The point that matters most: both seaplanes and WIG craft are vessels for COLREG purposes. When they are on the water, the Rules apply to them and to you in respect of them.

IMO has also issued separate guidelines on the design and operation of WIG craft. These classify craft by how far they can climb out of ground effect. When a craft operates well clear of the surface, aviation regulation rather than the maritime regime applies. That level of detail is rarely asked at OOW level, but know that the distinction exists.

The rules that work alongside Rule 31

On its own, Rule 31 only deals with lights. Behaviour and identification come from other rules.

Rule 18(e) – seaplane on the water - In general, a seaplane on the water must keep well clear of all vessels and avoid impeding their navigation. - Where risk of collision exists, however, it must comply with the steering and sailing rules (Part B).

Rule 18(f) – WIG craft - When taking off, landing and in flight near the surface, a WIG craft must keep well clear of all other vessels and avoid impeding their navigation. - When operating on the water surface, it must comply with Part B as a power-driven vessel.

Rule 23(c) – WIG craft lights - A WIG craft shows the normal power-driven vessel lights of Rule 23(a). - Only when taking off, landing and in flight near the surface, it also shows a high-intensity all-round flashing red light.

Do not confuse this with Rule 23(b). An air-cushion vessel operating in non-displacement mode shows an all-round flashing yellow light. Red flashing means WIG craft near the surface. Yellow flashing means hovercraft off the cushion.

Applying it on the bridge

You will rarely meet these craft in open ocean. Seaplanes are common in some coastal, island and lake areas, and you may meet them near resort anchorages, in sheltered harbours and in some pilotage waters. WIG craft are still uncommon, but the Rules already provide for them.

Practical points for the OOW:

  1. Expect lights that look wrong. Under Rule 31 the sidelights may be widely spaced (wingtips) and the white lights low or oddly placed. Do not reject an unusual pattern as a mistake. Identify the craft by the best available means: binoculars, radar, AIS if fitted, VHF and local knowledge from the pilot or sailing directions.
  2. Watch closing speeds. A WIG craft in flight near the surface, or a seaplane on its take-off run, moves far faster than ordinary traffic. Trial manoeuvres and ARPA vectors can lag behind reality. Keep a proper look-out (Rule 5) and assess risk of collision early (Rule 7).
  3. "Keep well clear" is their duty, not your licence. Rule 18(e) and 18(f)(i) put the burden on the seaplane or WIG craft. You still have the Rule 2 duty of good seamanship. If risk of collision develops, Part B applies in full.
  4. Treat a WIG craft on the surface as a power-driven vessel. That means the head-on, crossing and overtaking rules (Rules 13–15) and Rules 16–17 apply in the normal way.
  5. Do not assume every flashing red light is a WIG craft. Aircraft carry anti-collision beacons, and shore installations show red lights. Correlate what you see with radar and other information before acting.
  6. Record and report. If you are unsure what a craft is, or its behaviour is unclear, tell the Master. Follow your company SMS standing orders on calling the Master.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: night, coastal waters, unusual lights fine on the starboard bow. You see a red and a green light widely separated at about the same height, with a low white light between them. The radar shows a small, slow target. This is a likely seaplane taxiing, showing lights under Rule 31.

  • Plot it as you would any target.
  • If it is steady in bearing and closing, risk of collision exists.
  • Rule 18(e) says the seaplane should keep well clear, but once risk exists it must comply with Part B.
  • A seaplane on the water propelled by its engines is a vessel propelled by machinery. Apply the normal power-driven rules and act on your obligations.
  • Do not stand on merely because "aircraft give way".

Scenario 2: high-intensity all-round flashing red light, closing fast. You also see sidelights and masthead light(s), and the radar target has a very high speed. This is consistent with a WIG craft taking off, landing or in flight near the surface (Rule 23(c)).

  • Under Rule 18(f)(i) it must keep well clear and avoid impeding you.
  • Keep a close watch.
  • If its action is in doubt, use the Rule 34(d) signal of at least five short and rapid blasts. Be ready to act under Rule 17 if it becomes apparent it is not taking appropriate action.

Scenario 3: same craft, now slow, flashing red light extinguished. The WIG craft has come off the plane and is on the surface. It now complies with Part B as a power-driven vessel (Rule 18(f)(ii)).

  • If it is crossing from your starboard side with risk of collision, you are the give-way vessel under Rule 15.

Scenario 4: daytime, seaplane at anchor off a beach. It may not be practicable for the seaplane to hoist a black ball where a ship would. Expect a shape as close as possible in form and position under Rule 31, or one placed less prominently. Treat it as an anchored vessel and pass at a safe distance and speed.

Common mistakes

  • Reading Rule 31 as an exemption. It only relaxes characteristics and position as far as is impracticable. The craft must still show lights and shapes.
  • Mixing up red and yellow flashing lights. Red is a WIG craft near the surface (Rule 23(c)). Yellow is an air-cushion vessel in non-displacement mode (Rule 23(b)).
  • Thinking the WIG red light is always shown. It is shown only when taking off, landing and in flight near the surface.
  • Believing ships never give way to seaplanes. Rule 18(e) is a general duty on the seaplane. When risk of collision exists, Part B applies to both of you.
  • Forgetting that seaplanes and WIG craft are "vessels". Rule 3(a) says so expressly.
  • Confusing Rule 31 with Rule 1(e). Rule 1(e) covers vessels of special construction or purpose and depends on a determination by the Government concerned. Rule 31 applies directly to seaplanes and WIG craft.

Oral exam: how examiners test Rule 31

Expect short, linked questions. Answer the rule first, then show the links.

  • "State Rule 31." Paraphrase it accurately. Where impracticable, a seaplane or WIG craft shows lights and shapes as closely similar in characteristics and position as possible.
  • "Is a seaplane a vessel?" Yes, under Rule 3(a). Then give the Rule 3(e) definition.
  • "Define a WIG craft." Give Rule 3(m) in your own words: multimodal, main mode flying close to the surface using surface effect.
  • "What lights does a WIG craft show?" Rule 23(a) lights, plus a high-intensity all-round flashing red light only when taking off, landing and in flight near the surface (Rule 23(c)).
  • "Who keeps clear?" Rule 18(e) for seaplanes, and Rule 18(f) for WIG craft in each mode.
  • "You see an all-round flashing yellow light. What is it?" An air-cushion vessel in non-displacement mode. This is the classic trap question.
  • "Does Rule 18(e) let you stand on regardless?" No. Once risk of collision exists, Part B applies, and Rule 2 always applies.

Keep answers precise and in your own words. Examiners listen for the conditions ("impracticable", "in general", "only when") as much as the headline.

Key points
  • Rule 31 lets seaplanes and WIG craft show lights and shapes as close as possible to those prescribed, only where exact compliance is impracticable.
  • Seaplanes and WIG craft are vessels under Rule 3(a), so the Rules apply to them and to you in respect of them.
  • A seaplane on the water must in general keep well clear (Rule 18(e)), but must follow Part B once risk of collision exists.
  • A WIG craft keeps well clear when taking off, landing or flying near the surface, and acts as a power-driven vessel on the surface (Rule 18(f)).
  • A high-intensity all-round flashing red light marks a WIG craft near the surface (Rule 23(c)); flashing yellow marks an air-cushion vessel (Rule 23(b)).
  • Expect odd light layouts on these craft and identify them with every available means before acting.
  • Their duty to keep clear never removes your Rule 2 and Part B obligations.

Frequently asked questions

Does a seaplane on the water have to show navigation lights under COLREG?

Yes. A seaplane is a vessel under Rule 3(a) and must show the lights and shapes of Part C. Rule 31 only allows it to show them as closely similar in characteristics and position as possible where exact compliance is impracticable.

What does a high-intensity all-round flashing red light mean at sea?

Under Rule 23(c), it is shown by a WIG craft when taking off, landing and in flight near the surface, in addition to normal power-driven vessel lights. Confirm what you see with radar and other means, because aircraft and shore installations can also show red flashing lights.

Do ships have to give way to seaplanes?

Not as a general rule. Rule 18(e) requires a seaplane on the water to keep well clear of all vessels and avoid impeding them. Once risk of collision exists, however, the seaplane must comply with Part B, and both vessels act under the normal steering and sailing rules.

How is a WIG craft treated when it is on the water surface?

Rule 18(f)(ii) requires a WIG craft operating on the water surface to comply with Part B as a power-driven vessel. The flashing red light of Rule 23(c) is not shown in this mode, because it applies only when taking off, landing or in flight near the surface.

What is the difference between Rule 31 and Rule 1(e)?

Rule 1(e) covers vessels of special construction or purpose and depends on a determination by the Government concerned that the vessel cannot fully comply. Rule 31 applies directly to seaplanes and WIG craft wherever exact compliance with Part C is impracticable.

Test yourself

Under Rule 31, what must a seaplane do if it cannot practicably show the prescribed lights in the prescribed positions?

Rule 31 requires lights and shapes as closely similar in characteristics and position as is possible. It is not an exemption from showing lights. (COLREG Rule 31)

You sight a high-intensity all-round flashing red light with sidelights and a masthead light. What is it most likely to be?

Rule 23(c) prescribes this light for WIG craft near the surface. An air-cushion vessel in non-displacement mode shows a flashing yellow light under Rule 23(b).

How must a WIG craft operating on the water surface comply with the steering and sailing rules?

Rule 18(f)(ii) requires a WIG craft operating on the water surface to comply with Part B as a power-driven vessel.

A seaplane on the water is closing on a steady bearing and risk of collision exists. Which statement is correct?

Rule 18(e) makes keeping well clear a general duty, but once risk of collision exists the seaplane must comply with Part B. It is a vessel under Rule 3(a).

Where in COLREG are seaplanes and WIG craft defined?

Rule 3(e) defines a seaplane and Rule 3(m) defines a WIG craft. Rule 3(a) includes both in the definition of a vessel.
