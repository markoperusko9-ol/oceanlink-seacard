COLREG Rule 38 – Exemptions: What It Covers and Why It Still Matters

Quick answer

Rule 38 allowed vessels that complied with the 1960 Collision Regulations, and whose keels were laid before COLREG 1972 entered into force on 15 July 1977, to be exempted from certain new requirements for lights, light positions and sound signal appliances. Most of these exemptions were time-limited and expired in 1981 or 1986. Three are permanent: light repositioning caused by metric conversion, masthead light horizontal repositioning on vessels under 150 m, and repositioning of all-round lights. None of them exempts any vessel from the steering and sailing rules.

What Rule 38 actually says

Rule 38 is a transitional rule. When COLREG 1972 replaced the 1960 Regulations, many ships already afloat had lights and sound appliances fitted to the old standard. Re-rigging every mast and replacing every whistle overnight was not practical, so the Convention gave existing ships breathing space.

Paraphrased, it works like this:

  • Who qualifies: a vessel, or class of vessels, that complies with the 1960 Regulations and whose keel was laid, or which was at a corresponding stage of construction, before COLREG 1972 entered into force.
  • Entry into force date: 15 July 1977.
  • What can be exempted: only specific equipment and fitting requirements, listed in paragraphs (a) to (h). These cover light ranges, light colours, the positioning of masthead lights, sidelights and all-round lights, and sound signal appliances.
  • Wording: such vessels may be exempted. The exemption is not automatic. It depends on the flag Administration.

Rule 38 does not touch Parts B or C in substance. A vessel holding a Rule 38 exemption must still show the lights the Rules require and must comply fully with look-out, safe speed, risk of collision and every other conduct rule.

The eight exemptions at a glance

"Expired" means the time limit ran out years ago. Every qualifying vessel should have complied by those dates.

Para Subject Duration
(a) Installing lights with the ranges in Rule 22 4 years after entry into force (expired 1981)
(b) Installing lights with the colour specifications in Annex I, Section 7 4 years (expired 1981)
(c) Repositioning lights because of conversion from Imperial to metric units and rounding of figures Permanent
(d)(i) Repositioning masthead lights on vessels under 150 m, resulting from Annex I, Section 3(a) Permanent
(d)(ii) The same repositioning on vessels of 150 m or more 9 years (expired 1986)
(e) Repositioning masthead lights resulting from Annex I, Section 2(b) 9 years (expired 1986)
(f) Repositioning sidelights resulting from Annex I, Sections 2(g) and 3(b) 9 years (expired 1986)
(g) Sound signal appliances in Annex III 9 years (expired 1986)
(h) Repositioning all-round lights resulting from Annex I, Section 9(b) Permanent

An easy way to remember the pattern:

  • 4 years covers performance of the lights, meaning range and colour.
  • 9 years covers rebuilding work, meaning moving lights and replacing sound gear.
  • Permanent covers cases where the change was small or costly for little safety gain: rounding errors from metrication, horizontal masthead spacing on smaller ships, and all-round light placement.

The Annex I section numbers matter for the exam. Section 3(a) deals with the horizontal positioning of masthead lights. Section 2(b) deals with the vertical separation of masthead lights. Sections 2(g) and 3(b) deal with sidelight height and position. Section 9(b) deals with how all-round lights are placed so they are not obscured.

Applying it on the bridge

For a watchkeeper, Rule 38 has almost no effect on day-to-day decisions. It still has a practical lesson, though.

1. Lights may not sit exactly where the textbook diagram puts them.

A pre-1977 vessel under 150 m may legally carry its masthead lights closer together horizontally than Annex I would require on a new ship. On such a ship:

  • the range light offers a weaker aspect cue;
  • your judgement of her heading from the masthead light alignment is less reliable.

2. Older tonnage is now rare, but not extinct.

Qualifying ships had their keels laid before 15 July 1977, so they are now close to, or more than, half a century old. You are still most likely to meet them in coastal and island trades, among old ferries and small cargo vessels, and as heritage or training vessels. Many smaller craft also operate under national rules with their own allowances.

3. The conduct rules do not change.

An odd light arrangement is exactly the scanty information that Rule 7 warns against. Use these tools to build the full picture:

  • radar and ARPA trial and plotted data;
  • a series of compass bearings;
  • AIS, used as an aid only;
  • your binoculars.

Do not base your action on an assumed aspect.

4. Your own ship's equipment is a separate question.

If you serve on a very old vessel, any Rule 38 exemption should be supported by flag State documentation. The form this takes varies by Administration, so check the certificate file and ask the Master if in doubt.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Confusing aspect at night

You are 3rd Officer on the 0000–0400 watch in coastal waters. You sight a vessel fine on the starboard bow. She shows two masthead lights that appear almost in line, with only a small horizontal gap, plus both sidelights intermittently.

Her AIS shows a small general cargo ship built in the early 1970s.

Correct approach: 1. Recognise that a permanent exemption under Rule 38(d)(i) may explain the close masthead spacing. 2. Treat her heading, as judged from the lights, as uncertain. 3. Take compass bearings and acquire her on ARPA. 4. Assess CPA and TCPA. 5. If you have any doubt about a head-on situation, Rule 14 tells you to assume one exists. 6. Take early, substantial action, and call the Master in line with standing orders.

Scenario 2: The examiner's trap

The examiner says: "Your ship was built in 1992. During a survey it is found that the sidelights are placed forward of the forward masthead light. Does Rule 38 cover you?"

Answer: No, for two reasons. - Rule 38 applies only to vessels whose keels were laid before 15 July 1977 and which complied with the 1960 Regulations. - Even for those vessels, the sidelight exemption under (f) expired nine years after entry into force.

A 1992 ship must comply in full and have the sidelights repositioned. Rule 1(e) closest possible compliance is available only to vessels of special construction or purpose, and that is a different provision.

Scenario 3: Whistle on an old vessel

A cadet notices that a 1970s-built training ship has a whistle that looks old-fashioned and asks whether it is exempt from Annex III.

Answer: The Annex III exemption under Rule 38(g) ended in 1986. The whistle must meet Annex III today, however old it looks. Age of appearance proves nothing either way.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking Rule 38 still offers broad relief. Paragraphs (a), (b), (e), (f), (g) and sub-paragraph (d)(ii) all expired decades ago. Only (c), (d)(i) and (h) remain.
  • Confusing Rule 38 with Rule 1(e).
    • Rule 38 is transitional and tied to vessel age.
    • Rule 1(e) concerns vessels of special construction or purpose. For these, the government decides on the closest possible compliance with lights, shapes and sound signals.
  • Confusing it with Rule 1(c). Rule 1(c) preserves special additional station or signal lights for warships, vessels in convoy and fishing vessels in a fleet.
  • Believing exempt vessels follow different conduct rules. They do not. Rules 2 to 19 apply in full.
  • Assuming exemption is automatic. The rule says a vessel may be exempted, so the decision lies with the Administration.
  • Quoting the wrong base date. The clock starts at entry into force, 15 July 1977, not at adoption in 1972.

Oral exam points

Examiners rarely spend long on Rule 38, but they like to test whether you know where the line sits between equipment and conduct. Have crisp answers ready.

  • What is Rule 38 for? It gave ships built to the 1960 Regulations, with keels laid before 15 July 1977, time to bring lights and sound appliances into line with COLREG 72.
  • Which exemptions are permanent? There are three:
    • light repositioning due to Imperial-to-metric conversion;
    • masthead light repositioning under Annex I 3(a) for vessels under 150 m;
    • all-round light repositioning under Annex I 9(b).
  • Which were four-year exemptions? Light ranges under Rule 22 and light colours under Annex I Section 7.
  • Does an exempt vessel follow different steering rules? No. Rule 38 deals only with fitting and positioning of equipment.
  • How would you handle a target whose lights do not match the textbook?
    • Do not assume her aspect.
    • Use all available means under Rules 5 and 7.
    • Apply Rule 2 good seamanship.
    • Act early.
  • Who grants the exemption? The vessel's flag Administration. Documentation and procedure vary by flag.

A tip from the examiner's side: keep each answer short and accurate, then stop. Volunteering a wrong date or the wrong Annex section does more harm than saying "I would check the text of Rule 38".

Key points
  • Rule 38 is transitional: it applies only to vessels complying with the 1960 Regulations and laid down before 15 July 1977.
  • Exemptions cover fitting and positioning of lights and sound appliances only, never the conduct rules.
  • Light range (Rule 22) and colour (Annex I Section 7) exemptions lasted 4 years, expiring in 1981.
  • Sidelight repositioning, masthead repositioning on vessels of 150 m or more, Annex I 2(b) masthead repositioning and Annex III sound appliance exemptions lasted 9 years, expiring in 1986.
  • Permanent exemptions: metric conversion repositioning, Annex I 3(a) masthead repositioning for vessels under 150 m, and Annex I 9(b) all-round light repositioning.
  • An odd light arrangement is scanty information: confirm aspect with radar, bearings and look-out before acting.
  • Rule 38 (vessel age) is not the same as Rule 1(e) (special construction or purpose).

Frequently asked questions

Do any COLREG Rule 38 exemptions still apply today?

Yes. Three are permanent: repositioning of lights due to metric conversion and rounding, repositioning of masthead lights under Annex I Section 3(a) on vessels under 150 m, and repositioning of all-round lights under Annex I Section 9(b). They apply only to qualifying pre-July 1977 vessels that hold the exemption from their flag Administration.

When did COLREG 1972 enter into force?

COLREG 1972 entered into force on 15 July 1977. The time limits in Rule 38 run from that date, so the four-year exemptions ended in 1981 and the nine-year exemptions ended in 1986.

Can a ship built after 1977 use Rule 38?

No. Rule 38 is limited to vessels that complied with the 1960 Regulations and whose keel was laid, or which was at a corresponding stage of construction, before COLREG 1972 entered into force. A newer vessel with special construction may instead be considered by its flag State under Rule 1(e).

Does a vessel with a Rule 38 exemption have different right-of-way rules?

No. Rule 38 exempts only certain equipment and positioning requirements for lights and sound signal appliances. All the steering and sailing rules apply to that vessel exactly as to any other.

What is the difference between Rule 38 and Rule 1(e)?

Rule 38 is a transitional allowance based on when the vessel was built. Rule 1(e) applies to vessels of special construction or purpose that cannot fully comply with the lights, shapes and sound signal requirements. For those, the government determines the closest possible compliance, regardless of build date.

Test yourself

Which vessels could be exempted under Rule 38?

Rule 38 applies to vessels complying with the 1960 Regulations whose keel was laid before entry into force. Special construction falls under Rule 1(e) and warships under Rule 1(c). Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 38.

For how long could a qualifying vessel be exempted from fitting lights with the ranges in Rule 22?

Light ranges and light colours both had four-year exemptions, which expired in 1981. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 38(a) and (b).

Which of these Rule 38 exemptions is permanent?

Metric conversion repositioning is one of three permanent exemptions. The other three options were all nine-year exemptions. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 38(c), (d), (f) and (g).

At night you sight an old vessel whose masthead lights are closer together horizontally than usual. What is the correct response?

Rule 38 may explain the arrangement, but Rule 7 forbids assumptions based on scanty information. Exempt vessels gain no special status. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rules 7 and 38(d)(i).

What does a Rule 38 exemption NOT relieve a vessel from?

Rule 38 covers only equipment and positioning of lights and sound appliances. Conduct under Part B applies in full. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 38 and Part B.
