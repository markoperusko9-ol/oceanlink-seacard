COLREG Rule 25 – Sailing vessels underway and vessels under oars

Quick answer

Rule 25 requires a sailing vessel underway to show sidelights and a sternlight. A vessel under 20 m may combine them in a masthead tricolour, and any sailing vessel may add optional all-round red-over-green lights at the masthead, but not with the tricolour (Rule 25(c)). Small sailing craft and rowing boats may instead have a white torch or lantern ready, and a vessel under sail that is also being propelled by machinery must show a cone, apex downwards, by day because she is then a power-driven vessel.

What Rule 25 actually requires

Rule 25 sits in Part C (Lights and Shapes). Its lights apply from sunset to sunrise and in restricted visibility, and its shape applies by day (Rule 20). In paraphrase:

  • 25(a): A sailing vessel underway shows sidelights and a sternlight. She shows no masthead light, because she is not a power-driven vessel.
  • 25(b): If she is under 20 m, she may combine the sidelights and sternlight in one lantern at or near the top of the mast. This is the tricolour.
  • 25(c): She may also show two all-round lights in a vertical line at or near the masthead, red over green. These are optional. They must not be shown together with the tricolour.
  • 25(d)(i): A sailing vessel under 7 m should show the lights in (a) or (b) if practicable. If she does not, she must have a white electric torch or lighted lantern ready at hand, shown in time to prevent collision.
  • 25(d)(ii): A vessel under oars may show sailing-vessel lights. If she does not, she must have the same white torch or lantern ready at hand.
  • 25(e): A vessel under sail and also propelled by machinery shows a conical shape, apex downwards, forward where it can best be seen.

The key definition comes from Rule 3(c). A vessel is a sailing vessel only while under sail with any propelling machinery not being used. Once the engine is driving the propeller she is a power-driven vessel for every purpose: lights, steering rules and sound signals. That is why 25(e) exists. An engine idling out of gear, for example to charge batteries, does not change her status.

Reading the lights and shape from the bridge

The sector arcs are the same as any other vessel's (Rule 21). Each sidelight covers 112.5° from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam, and the sternlight covers 135° astern. A tricolour shows exactly these arcs from one lantern at the masthead.

Practical points:

  • Ranges are short. Under Rule 22, a vessel under 12 m needs sidelights visible to only 1 mile. Yachts are also poor radar targets in a seaway. Detection may come late, so the look-out (Rule 5) and safe speed (Rule 6) matter more than usual.
  • Height tells you something. A tricolour sits far higher than deck-level sidelights. A red or green light high up with nothing below it usually means a small yacht, not a large ship at long range.
  • Mnemonic: "Red over green, sailing machine." Do not confuse it with green over white (trawling) or red over white (fishing other than trawling) under Rule 26.

How Rule 25 links to the steering and sailing rules

Rule 25 only tells you what the vessel is. Your obligations come from Part B.

  • Rule 18: A power-driven vessel underway keeps out of the way of a sailing vessel, subject to Rules 9, 10 and 13.
  • Rule 13: If you are overtaking a sailing vessel, you keep clear. Being under sail gives her no duty to keep clear of you.
  • Rules 9(b) and 10(j): A sailing vessel must not impede a vessel that can safely navigate only within a narrow channel or fairway, or a power-driven vessel following a traffic lane.
  • Rule 8(f): The vessel required not to impede must act early to leave sea room. If risk of collision develops anyway, both vessels must comply with the steering rules.
  • Rule 12: This governs two sailing vessels meeting, not Rule 25.
  • Rule 35: In restricted visibility, a sailing vessel sounds one prolonged blast followed by two short blasts at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. Small vessels have a relaxation and may make some other efficient sound signal instead.
  • Rule 34(a): Manoeuvring signals (one, two or three short blasts) are for power-driven vessels in sight of one another. A sailing vessel under sail does not use them. However, the Rule 34(c) overtaking signals in narrow channels and the Rule 34(d) five-short-blast doubt signal apply to all vessels, including sailing vessels.

Vessels under oars are a special case. Rule 18 gives them no place in its list of who keeps clear of whom. Rules 14 and 15 apply only between power-driven vessels. Rule 13 (overtaking) still applies, and as a vessel of less than 20 m she must not impede under Rules 9(b) and 10(j). Otherwise Rules 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 and good seamanship govern. In practice, the ship has the manoeuvrability and the larger margin for error, and good seamanship says she gives room early.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: night, open sea. You are a power-driven vessel on passage. A low red light appears fine on your starboard bow with no white light above it. A few minutes later you make out red over green above it. The compass bearing is steady and the range is closing.

  1. Identify her as a sailing vessel showing her port side, carrying the optional 25(c) lights.
  2. Check whether you are overtaking. You are not, so Rule 18(a) applies and you keep out of the way.
  3. Take early, substantial action under Rule 8. A bold alteration to starboard to pass astern of her is normally better than crossing ahead of a vessel whose speed and heading can change with a wind shift.
  4. Monitor until she is past and clear. Yachts tack and gybe without warning.

Scenario 2: daylight, coastal. A yacht crosses from your port side. Her sails are set but there is no cone. Through binoculars you see the sails slatting and a steady wake, and she is holding a course straight into the wind.

  • She is very probably motoring, which makes her a power-driven vessel. Rule 15 would then make you the stand-on vessel.
  • Rule 7(c) warns against assumptions based on scanty information. Do not hold your course on the strength of a guess. Call on VHF if identification is possible. If doubt remains, act as if risk of collision exists, and remember Rule 17 lets you act once it is clear she is not taking action.

Scenario 3: TSS lane at night. You are following the lane. A tricolour shows green fine on your port bow, crossing the lane.

  • Under Rule 10(j) she must not impede you. That duty does not make you stand-on by default.
  • Rules 8(f)(ii) and (iii) apply. Once risk of collision exists, both vessels must comply with Part B. The sailing vessel is not relieved of her duty not to impede, but you must not rely on it. Take early action to avoid collision, reducing speed or altering in good time rather than standing on and hoping.

Scenario 4: a white flash close ahead. In an anchorage approach at dusk, a weak white light flicks on and off low on the water.

  • It may be a kayak, dinghy or rowing boat using 25(d). Do not dismiss it as a stern light you are slowly overtaking.
  • Reduce speed and put the look-out on it. If needed, sound Rule 36 signals or use the searchlight carefully, without dazzling. Give a wide berth.

Common mistakes, on both sides

Mistakes by yachts that a watchkeeper should be ready for:

  • Tricolour left on while motoring. She then appears to be a sailing vessel with no masthead light. The correct display when motoring is power-driven lights under Rule 23. The tricolour is permitted only for a sailing vessel under 20 m (or a vessel under oars under 25(d)(ii)), never for a vessel under power.
  • Tricolour and deck sidelights both lit. This is not the display the rule describes and gives a misleading picture of size and range.
  • Red-over-green shown with the tricolour. Rule 25(c) expressly forbids this combination.
  • No cone when motor-sailing. Many leisure craft omit it, so judge by what you observe, not by the absence of the shape.

Mistakes by ship's officers:

  • Assuming a sailing vessel always has right of way. Rule 13 (overtaking) and Rules 9(b)/10(j) (not to impede) change the picture.
  • Assuming red over green is mandatory. It is optional, and most sailing vessels you meet will not show it.
  • Assuming a rowing boat is stand-on under Rule 18. She is not listed there. Rule 13 and the not-to-impede duties of Rules 9(b) and 10(j) still apply, and otherwise the general rules and good seamanship govern.
  • Late detection. A small yacht at 1 mile or less, low on radar, gives little time. Proper look-out and a safe speed are the real defence.

Local rules can differ. Rule 1(b) allows special rules for harbours, rivers and inland waters, and some administrations relax shape requirements for small craft there. Check the local regulations for the waters you are in.

Oral exam: what the examiner will push you on

Expect questions like these at an OOW or Chief Mate oral. Answer in your own words, but get the facts exact.

  • "Define a sailing vessel." Any vessel under sail, provided her propelling machinery, if fitted, is not being used (Rule 3(c)).
  • "What lights does a sailing vessel show?" Sidelights and a sternlight. Under 20 m she may use a masthead tricolour. Optionally she may show red over green all-round at the masthead, but never with the tricolour.
  • "Why no red-over-green with a tricolour?" Rule 25(c) prohibits it outright. Be ready to state that clearly.
  • "Day signal for a yacht under sail and also propelled by her engine?" A cone, apex downwards, forward. By law she is a power-driven vessel.
  • "Sound signal for a sailing vessel in fog?" One prolonged blast followed by two short blasts, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes (Rule 35).
  • "Does a sailing vessel always stand on against a ship?" No. Give the examples of overtaking, narrow channels and TSS lanes.
  • "What must a rowing boat carry at night?" Either sailing-vessel lights, or a white torch or lantern ready at hand, shown in time to prevent collision.
  • "You see a single green light high up, nothing below. What is it?" Probably a sailing vessel under 20 m with a tricolour, showing her starboard side. Confirm by bearing, range and radar before acting.

Examiners often show a light board or model and rotate it. Practise naming the vessel, her aspect and your action for each arc of a tricolour.

Key points
  • A sailing vessel underway shows sidelights and a sternlight and never a masthead light.
  • Under 20 m, the lights may be combined in a masthead tricolour.
  • Red over green all-round at the masthead is optional and must never be shown with the tricolour.
  • Sailing craft under 7 m and rowing boats may instead keep a white torch or lantern ready to show in time.
  • Under sail with the engine driving makes her power-driven, and she shows a cone, apex down, by day.
  • Rule 25 identifies the vessel. Rules 9, 10, 12, 13 and 18 decide who keeps clear.
  • Rowing boats get no Rule 18 privilege. Rule 13 and Rules 9(b)/10(j) apply, and otherwise Rules 2, 7 and 8 and early action govern.

Frequently asked questions

What does red over green mean at sea?

Two all-round lights in a vertical line at the masthead, red above green, show a sailing vessel underway under Rule 25(c). They are optional and are shown in addition to her sidelights and sternlight. They are never shown with a tricolour.

Can a yacht show a tricolour while motoring?

No. The tricolour under Rule 25(b) is allowed only for a sailing vessel under 20 m. Once the engine is propelling her she is a power-driven vessel. She must show Rule 23 power-driven lights: a masthead light, or for a vessel under 12 m an all-round white light in lieu of masthead light and sternlight, plus sidelights.

What is the cone apex downwards on a sailing yacht?

It shows a vessel proceeding under sail while also being propelled by machinery (Rule 25(e)). She is legally a power-driven vessel and the steering rules apply to her as such. Many small craft fail to hoist it, so judge by observation as well.

Does a rowing boat have right of way over a ship?

Rule 18 does not include vessels under oars in its list of who keeps clear of whom, and Rules 14 and 15 apply only between power-driven vessels. Rule 13 (overtaking) still applies. As a vessel of less than 20 m, a rowing boat must not impede under Rules 9(b) and 10(j). Otherwise the general duties in Rules 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 apply, and in practice the ship should give early, generous room.

What sound signal does a sailing vessel make in restricted visibility?

One prolonged blast followed by two short blasts, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes, under Rule 35. Small vessels have a relaxation allowing some other efficient sound signal at the same interval.

Test yourself

Which display is NOT permitted for a sailing vessel of 15 m underway at night?

Rule 25(c) forbids showing the optional red-over-green lights together with the combined lantern permitted by Rule 25(b).

A yacht has sails set and her engine driving the propeller. What is her status under the COLREG?

Under Rule 3(c), a sailing vessel's machinery must not be in use. With the engine propelling her she is power-driven and shows the cone under Rule 25(e).

What shape does a vessel under sail and machinery show by day?

Rule 25(e) requires a conical shape, apex downwards, exhibited forward where it can best be seen.

Your power-driven vessel is overtaking a sailing vessel in open water. Who keeps out of the way?

Rule 18 is expressly subject to Rule 13. Any overtaking vessel keeps out of the way of the vessel being overtaken.

What must a rowing boat that is not showing sailing-vessel lights have at night?

Rule 25(d)(ii) allows a vessel under oars to show sailing-vessel lights. If she does not, she must have a white torch or lantern ready to show in sufficient time.
