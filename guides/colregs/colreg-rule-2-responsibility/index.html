COLREG Rule 2 – Responsibility: What It Means on the Bridge

Quick answer

Rule 2 has two parts. Part (a) says nothing in the Rules exonerates a vessel, or her owner, master or crew, from the consequences of neglecting to comply with the Rules, or of neglecting any precaution required by the ordinary practice of seamen or by the special circumstances of the case. Part (b) says you must account for all dangers and special circumstances, including vessel limitations, and allows a departure from the Rules only when it is necessary to avoid immediate danger.

What Rule 2 says, in plain terms

Rule 2 sits in Part A (General) of the COLREGs. It governs how every other Rule is read and applied, including the steering and sailing rules, lights and shapes, and sound signals.

Rule 2(a): no excuse for neglect. Nothing in the Rules protects a vessel, or her owner, master or crew, from the consequences of neglect. That neglect can be any of three kinds:

  • failing to comply with the Rules themselves;
  • failing to take any precaution required by the ordinary practice of seamen (good seamanship);
  • failing to take any precaution required by the special circumstances of the case.

Rule 2(b): when departure may be necessary. When you read and apply the Rules, you must take account of all dangers of navigation and collision. You must also consider any special circumstances, including the limitations of the vessels involved. Such circumstances may make it necessary to depart from the Rules to avoid immediate danger.

The two halves pull in different directions on purpose:

  • 2(a) stops you hiding behind the Rules ("I was the stand-on vessel, so I held on").
  • 2(b) stops you being trapped by them when obeying would put you into danger.

Neither half is a general licence to ignore the Rules.

Rule 2(a): good seamanship is part of compliance

The phrase "ordinary practice of seamen" means what a competent, prudent mariner would normally do in the situation. The Rules do not list these precautions, but courts, investigators and examiners expect them. Typical examples on the bridge:

  • Calling the master early when traffic, visibility or the situation is outside your comfort zone or the standing orders.
  • Posting an additional lookout. Putting the helm in hand steering in congested waters.
  • Having engines on standby, and the anchor cleared away in pilotage waters where your SMS or the passage plan calls for it.
  • Keeping a sensible passing distance rather than the bare minimum. Allowing for interaction, squat and set.
  • Making your intentions obvious early, rather than relying on small course changes the other ship may not detect.
  • Using every available means to assess the situation, including radar plotting, AIS and visual bearings. Not relying on a single sensor.

Rule 2(a) applies to the owner, master and crew as well as the vessel. A watchkeeper cannot transfer responsibility to the pilot, the master's night orders or the other ship. Many of these precautions also overlap with the specific duties in Rule 5 (look-out), Rule 6 (safe speed) and Rule 8 (which itself refers to good seamanship).

Rule 2(a) also covers "special circumstances of the case". These are features of the particular situation that the general Rules do not address. Examples include a known strong cross-set, a fishing fleet working across a TSS, or a vessel behaving erratically. Recognising them is part of your duty.

Rule 2(b): departure from the Rules – the narrow exception

Rule 2(b) is sometimes called the general prudential rule. Examiners and investigators read it narrowly. Before you rely on it, every element should be present:

Element What it means in practice
Dangers of navigation and collision Shoals, traffic density, other vessels, hazards to the planned action
Special circumstances Multi-vessel encounters, vessels not behaving as the Rules expect, restricted sea room
Limitations of the vessels involved Draught, manoeuvrability, a small craft's inability to see or be seen, a large ship's turning circle
Departure is necessary Complying with the normal Rule would itself create danger
To avoid immediate danger The danger is close and real, not a matter of saving time or distance

If compliance with the specific Rule would safely resolve the situation, there is no justification for departing from it.

Rule 2(b) also does not replace the specific provisions that already deal with the stand-on vessel. Rule 17 sets out when the stand-on vessel may act, and when she shall take best action because the give-way vessel alone can no longer avoid collision. In accepted practice and case law, Rule 17 is applied first. Rule 2(b) covers situations the specific Rules do not foresee.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Three-ship encounter in open water

You are a power-driven vessel. Ship A, also a power-driven vessel, crosses from your port side, so under Rule 15 you are the stand-on vessel. Ship B is overtaking close on your starboard quarter. Ship A is not taking action.

  • The steering and sailing rules are written mainly for two-vessel encounters. With three ships, you must consider the whole picture under Rule 2(b).
  • An alteration to starboard to help A may close B dangerously. Rule 17(c) requires that, if the circumstances of the case admit, you shall not alter course to port for a power-driven vessel on your own port side.
  • A substantial reduction of speed may be the action that resolves both encounters. You would then work through Rule 17 in sequence: sound the doubt signal under Rule 34(d), then act when permitted or required.
  • Call the master, record the actions taken and keep plotting.

Scenario 2: Small craft in a narrow channel

You are a deep-draught vessel that can only navigate safely within the channel. A small craft starts crossing ahead.

  • Rules 9(b) and 9(d) say the small craft (under 20 m, or a sailing vessel) shall not impede you, and must not cross if doing so impedes you. Under Rule 8(f)(iii) and Rule 2(a), you remain fully obliged to comply with the steering and sailing rules when risk of collision exists, and to take the ordinary precautions.
  • Sound at least five short and rapid blasts if in doubt about its intentions (Rule 34(d)), and reduce speed early.
  • Have the anchor and engines ready, and inform VTS if your local procedures require it.
  • Your limitations are a Rule 2(b) consideration. So is the small craft's possible inability to judge your speed.

Scenario 3: The VHF "agreement"

In a crossing situation, the other ship calls and proposes a starboard-to-starboard passing that contradicts what the Rules require.

  • Agreeing does not change your obligations under the Rules. Rule 2(a) means you remain answerable if it goes wrong.
  • VHF calls can be time-consuming, misidentified or misunderstood.
  • If there is no immediate danger requiring departure, the Rules apply.
  • Many company SMS procedures restrict VHF use for collision avoidance. Follow your own SMS and the master's standing orders.

Common mistakes watchkeepers make

  • Believing the stand-on vessel has "right of way". The Rules impose duties on both vessels. Under Rule 2(a), holding on into a collision because "it was his duty to give way" is no defence.
  • Using Rule 2(b) as a convenience clause. Cutting a corner, departing from a TSS lane or breaking a Rule to save time is not a departure to avoid immediate danger.
  • Treating vessel limitations as one-way. Your own ship's limitations count, but so do the other vessel's. A yacht may not see you, and a loaded VLCC cannot stop quickly.
  • Waiting too long. Many casualties involve a stand-on vessel that recognised danger late. Know where Rule 17(a)(ii) and 17(b) start to apply for your ship's handling characteristics.
  • Relying on one sensor. Trusting AIS heading, or a single radar target without plotting, is a failure of ordinary practice.
  • Assuming the pilot carries responsibility. The pilot advises. The master and the bridge team remain responsible, and the OOW should challenge anything that looks wrong.

Oral exam: how examiners test Rule 2

Expect Rule 2 questions at every level from OOW to master. Examiners often bring it into scenario questions on Rules 9, 10, 17 and 19. Typical questions and the points they want to hear:

  • "State Rule 2 in your own words." Cover both parts: no exoneration for neglect of the Rules, good seamanship or special circumstances; and departure only when necessary to avoid immediate danger, considering all dangers and vessel limitations.
  • "Who does Rule 2 apply to?" The vessel and her owner, master and crew.
  • "Give examples of the ordinary practice of seamen." Extra lookout, hand steering, engines on standby, anchor ready, calling the master, safe passing distance, early and obvious action.
  • "When may you depart from the Rules?" Only when special circumstances or dangers make it necessary to avoid immediate danger. Stress "necessary" and "immediate".
  • "How does Rule 2 relate to Rule 17?" Rule 17 is the specific provision for the stand-on vessel and comes first. Rule 2(b) deals with circumstances the specific Rules do not cover, such as multi-vessel encounters.
  • "Is a VHF agreement a special circumstance?" Not on its own. It does not relieve either vessel of its obligations.

A good answer always ends with the practical actions: call the master, reduce speed if in doubt, plot, sound signals and record.

Key points
  • Rule 2(a): compliance with the Rules never excuses neglect of good seamanship or the special circumstances of the case.
  • Responsibility rests on the vessel, owner, master and crew, not just on the 'give-way' ship.
  • Rule 2(b) allows departure from the Rules only when necessary to avoid immediate danger.
  • The limitations of all vessels involved, including your own, must be taken into account.
  • Specific Rules such as Rule 17 come first; Rule 2(b) covers situations they do not foresee, such as multi-ship encounters.
  • A VHF agreement does not change either vessel's obligations under the Rules.
  • There is no 'right of way' in the COLREGs, only duties on every vessel.

Frequently asked questions

What is COLREG Rule 2 about?

Rule 2 deals with responsibility. It says no one is excused for neglecting the Rules, good seamanship or the special circumstances of a situation. It also allows a departure from the Rules when that is necessary to avoid immediate danger.

What does 'ordinary practice of seamen' mean in Rule 2?

It means the precautions a competent, prudent mariner would normally take even though no Rule spells them out. Examples include posting extra lookouts, having engines on standby, calling the master early and keeping a safe passing distance. Failing to take such precautions can amount to neglect under Rule 2(a).

Can I break a COLREG rule if I think it is safer?

Only within the narrow terms of Rule 2(b). Special circumstances or dangers must make the departure necessary to avoid immediate danger. Departing for convenience, to save time, or because another ship suggested it on VHF is not covered.

Does the stand-on vessel have right of way under the COLREGs?

No. The COLREGs place duties on each vessel, not rights. The stand-on vessel must keep course and speed but may act under Rule 17(a)(ii), and must act under Rule 17(b) when collision can no longer be avoided by the give-way vessel alone. Rule 2(a) means holding on into danger is no defence.

How does Rule 2 apply when more than two vessels are involved?

The steering and sailing rules are written mainly for encounters between two vessels. In multi-ship situations, Rule 2(b) requires you to consider all the dangers together. This may make an action necessary that differs from what a single two-ship encounter would call for, such as a large speed reduction.

Test yourself

Under Rule 2(b), when may a vessel depart from the Rules?

Departure is permitted only when special circumstances make it necessary to avoid immediate danger. COLREG 1972, Rule 2(b).

Who does Rule 2(a) say is not exonerated from the consequences of neglect?

Rule 2(a) extends responsibility to the vessel and her owner, master and crew. COLREG 1972, Rule 2(a).

A stand-on vessel holds course and speed until collision because the other ship 'had to give way'. How does Rule 2 treat this?

Rule 2(a) removes any excuse based on rigid compliance, and Rule 17(b) requires best action when collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone. COLREG 1972, Rules 2(a) and 17(b).

Which of these is a 'limitation of the vessels involved' to be considered under Rule 2(b)?

Rule 2(b) refers to the limitations of the vessels involved, such as draught and manoeuvrability; commercial pressures are not vessel limitations. COLREG 1972, Rule 2(b).

As stand-on vessel, how should Rule 17 and Rule 2(b) be applied?

In accepted practice and case law, Rule 17, the specific provision for the stand-on vessel, is applied first. Rule 2(b) addresses special circumstances such as multi-vessel encounters. COLREG 1972, Rules 2(b) and 17.
