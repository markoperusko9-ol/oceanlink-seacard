COLREG Rule 1 – Application: Where and to Whom the Collision Regulations Apply

Quick answer

Rule 1 applies the COLREGs to all vessels on the high seas and in all waters connected to them that seagoing vessels can navigate. It also allows four exceptions or additions: local rules for harbours, rivers and inland waters, extra signals for warships, convoys and fishing fleets, IMO-adopted traffic separation schemes, and 'closest possible compliance' for vessels of special construction. On the bridge, always check which local rules apply: many only supplement the COLREGs, but some, such as the US Inland Rules, replace them within their limits.

What Rule 1 says, in plain terms

Rule 1 is the gateway to the whole of the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea 1972, as amended. It does not tell you how to manoeuvre. It tells you where the Rules apply, to whom, and what other rules may sit alongside them. It has five paragraphs:

Para Paraphrase
1(a) The Rules apply to all vessels on the high seas and in all waters connected with the high seas that seagoing vessels can navigate.
1(b) An appropriate authority may make special rules for roadsteads, harbours, rivers, lakes or inland waterways connected with the high seas and navigable by seagoing vessels. The COLREGs do not interfere with these rules. Such rules must conform as closely as possible to the COLREGs.
1(c) A government may make special rules for additional station or signal lights, shapes or whistle signals for warships and vessels in convoy. It may also make special rules for additional lights or shapes for fishing vessels fishing as a fleet. These extras should, as far as possible, not be mistakable for any signal in the Rules.
1(d) The IMO may adopt traffic separation schemes for the purposes of the Rules.
1(e) A vessel of special construction or purpose may be unable to comply fully with the requirements for lights, shapes or sound-signalling appliances. If so, its own government decides what counts as the closest possible compliance, and the vessel follows that.

The key word in 1(a) is all. 'Vessel' is defined broadly in Rule 3. It covers every description of water craft used or capable of being used as a means of transport on water, including non-displacement craft, WIG craft and seaplanes. Warships, fishing boats, yachts, tugs and dredgers are all inside the net.

The geography: high seas and connected waters

Paragraph 1(a) sets two tests for waters other than the high seas:

  • the water must be connected with the high seas, and
  • it must be navigable by seagoing vessels.

This covers territorial seas, approaches, most estuaries and many ports and rivers. The COLREGs follow you in from the open ocean. They do not stop at the 12-mile limit.

What falls outside the COLREGs:

  • Enclosed lakes with no navigable connection to the sea.
  • Inland waterways that seagoing ships cannot reach.

National or regional rules govern these waters instead. Few deep-sea officers ever navigate them. Crews on river-sea and inland craft do meet this distinction regularly.

The practical point is the overlap. Many waters connected with the high seas also have local rules under 1(b). Many local rules supplement the COLREGs, which then still govern anything the local rules do not cover. Some regimes, such as the US Inland Navigation Rules, are complete codes that apply in place of the COLREGs within their limits. Check which type applies.

Local and special rules under 1(b) and 1(c): how they work on the bridge

Port, river and canal authorities often have their own regulations. Some well-known examples:

  • the US Inland Navigation Rules, which apply on the inland side of published demarcation lines;
  • the rules of navigation for major canals such as Panama and Suez;
  • regulations for busy rivers and port approaches.

Some of these rules supplement the COLREGs. Others are complete codes in their own right. Either way, they can differ from the COLREGs in places, such as manoeuvring and whistle signals, lights for certain craft, speed limits, reporting points, or right-of-way in particular channels.

How a watchkeeper handles this:

  1. At the appraisal stage of the passage plan, find out which local regulations apply along the route. Use the Sailing Directions, port guides, local notices and the agent's port information.
  2. Note in the plan where the regime changes, for example a demarcation line or a canal entrance. Note what changes there: signals, VHF reporting, speed, or anchoring restrictions.
  3. Brief the bridge team before entering the area. Make sure the OOW knows any signals that differ from the COLREGs.
  4. Use the pilot's local knowledge. It does not replace your own reading of the regulations. The Master and OOW remain responsible.
  5. Check which type of local rules applies. Many local rules supplement the COLREGs, which then still govern anything the local rules do not cover. Some regimes, such as the US Inland Navigation Rules, are complete codes that apply in place of the COLREGs within their limits.

For 1(c), expect to see unfamiliar additional lights, for example from warships, vessels in naval convoy, or fishing fleets working together. Their governments may authorise these signals. The rule asks that they be designed so they cannot be mistaken for COLREG signals, but you cannot rely on recognising them. Treat any unexplained light pattern with caution, keep a proper lookout, and use all available means (Rules 5 and 7).

Traffic separation schemes and 'special construction' vessels: 1(d) and 1(e)

1(d) – IMO-adopted TSS. Rule 1(d) gives the IMO the authority to adopt traffic separation schemes. Rule 10 then applies to TSS adopted by the Organization.

Not every routeing scheme you see on a chart is IMO-adopted. Some schemes are established nationally or locally. In those, the conduct required may come from local regulations rather than from Rule 10. Check the chart notes, Sailing Directions and routeing publications to find out which applies.

1(e) – closest possible compliance. Some vessels cannot physically carry lights, shapes or sound-signalling appliances exactly as Annex I and Annex III require. Examples include:

  • aircraft carriers and some other warships;
  • submarines;
  • certain high-speed or unusually configured craft.

Under 1(e), the vessel's own government decides on the closest possible compliance. Two points follow:

  • 1(e) covers only the number, position, range or arc of visibility of lights or shapes, and the arrangement and characteristics of sound-signalling appliances. It does not excuse a vessel from the steering and sailing rules.
  • The decision rests with the flag government. The owner, the Master or the yard cannot decide it.

On your own ship, you will never use 1(e) yourself. You will see it on others, as lights that look 'wrong' on a warship or special craft. Your response is the same as for 1(c): extra caution and early action.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Crossing a demarcation line. You are inbound to a US port. Your passage plan marks the demarcation line where the US Inland Rules take over from the COLREGs. Before crossing it, the Master briefs the team on the differences that matter for this port, especially whistle signals in meeting and crossing situations. Under 1(b), this switch is entirely legitimate. Not knowing the local rules is a planning failure, not a defence.

Scenario 2 – Unusual lights at night. You are on the 0000–0400 watch. Ahead you see a cluster of white and coloured lights that does not match any Annex I pattern. AIS shows nothing. The vessel could be a naval unit under 1(c) or 1(e), a fishing fleet with additional lights, or simply a vessel showing incorrect lights. Do not try to 'solve' the lights before acting:

  • assess risk of collision by radar and compass bearing (Rule 7);
  • reduce speed if appropriate (Rule 6);
  • give a wide berth;
  • call the Master if in doubt, as your standing orders require.

Scenario 3 – Harbour with a speed limit but no right-of-way rules. The port's regulations set speed limits and VHF reporting but say nothing about crossing situations. These regulations supplement the COLREGs rather than replacing them, so Rules 15 to 17 of the COLREGs still govern your encounter with a crossing tug. Confirm this when you research the port: if the waters were covered by a complete code, such as the US Inland Rules, that code would govern instead.

Scenario 4 – Routeing scheme on a chart. A scheme in a coastal approach is marked on the chart. The chart notes say it is not IMO-adopted and is governed by national regulations. Rule 10 does not apply automatically. Follow the national requirements set out in the publications, and the COLREGs for everything else.

Common mistakes

  • 'The COLREGs only apply on the high seas.' Wrong. They also apply in all connected waters navigable by seagoing vessels, including territorial seas and most ports.
  • 'Local rules always replace the COLREGs completely.' Not necessarily. Many only supplement them, and the COLREGs still govern anything the local rules do not cover. Some, such as the US Inland Rules, are complete codes that apply in place of the COLREGs within their limits. Either way, local rules must conform as closely as possible to the COLREGs.
  • 'Warships are exempt.' Wrong. Warships are vessels under Rule 1(a). Rule 1(c) only allows additional signals, and 1(e) only covers lights, shapes and sound-signalling arrangements. Steering and sailing obligations still apply.
  • 'Every TSS on the chart is a Rule 10 scheme.' Not necessarily. Rule 10 applies to IMO-adopted schemes. Others depend on national or local regulation.
  • 'The pilot knows the local rules, so I don't need to.' The pilot advises. The Master and the bridge team keep their responsibility, as Rule 2 makes clear for consequences of neglect.
  • Confusing Rule 1(e) with Rule 38. Rule 1(e) covers vessels of special construction or purpose, with arrangements set by their government. Rule 38 gives exemptions, some time-limited and some permanent, for vessels whose keels were laid (or which were at a corresponding stage of construction) before the Rules entered into force.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Rule 1 is a favourite opening question because it shows whether a candidate understands the structure of the Rules. Be ready to answer briefly and in your own words.

  • Q: To which vessels and waters do the Rules apply? All vessels, on the high seas and in all connected waters navigable by seagoing vessels. Mention the Rule 3 definition of 'vessel'.
  • Q: Can a port authority make its own rules? Yes, under 1(b), for roadsteads, harbours, rivers, lakes and inland waterways connected with the high seas. Such rules must conform as closely as possible to the COLREGs. Give a real example, such as the US Inland Rules or a canal authority's regulations.
  • Q: What if local rules are silent on a situation? It depends on the type of local rules. If they supplement the COLREGs, the COLREGs govern anything the local rules do not cover. If they are a complete code, such as the US Inland Rules, that code governs within its limits.
  • Q: What is Rule 1(c) about? Governments may authorise additional lights, shapes or whistle signals for warships and convoys, and additional lights or shapes for fishing fleets. These should not be mistakable for COLREG signals.
  • Q: Who adopts traffic separation schemes for the purpose of the Rules? The IMO, under 1(d). Link this to Rule 10.
  • Q: A submarine cannot carry her masthead light at the required height. What applies? Rule 1(e). Her government determines the closest possible compliance. This affects only lights, shapes and sound-signalling arrangements, not her conduct.
  • Q: How do you prepare for local rules in practice? Through the passage plan: identify the regulations, mark where they take effect, and brief the team.

Examiners look for a candidate who can link Rule 1 to Rules 2, 3, 10 and 38 and to passage planning. Reciting the text is not enough.

Key points
  • Rule 1(a): the COLREGs apply to all vessels on the high seas and in all connected waters navigable by seagoing vessels.
  • Local rules under 1(b) may supplement the COLREGs or, like the US Inland Rules, replace them within their limits; check which type applies.
  • Governments may authorise additional signals for warships, convoys and fishing fleets under 1(c), so expect unfamiliar lights.
  • Only IMO-adopted traffic separation schemes come under 1(d) and Rule 10; check chart notes for the status of other schemes.
  • Rule 1(e) lets a flag government set the 'closest possible compliance' for lights, shapes and sound signals on special vessels, not for conduct.
  • Identify local regulations at the appraisal stage of the passage plan and brief the bridge team before entering the area.

Frequently asked questions

Do the COLREGs apply inside a port or river?

Yes, if the port or river is connected with the high seas and navigable by seagoing vessels. The port or river authority may also have special rules under Rule 1(b). Many such rules only supplement the COLREGs, which still govern anything the local rules do not cover. Some, such as the US Inland Rules, are complete codes that apply in place of the COLREGs within their limits.

Do warships have to follow the COLREGs?

Yes. Warships are vessels under Rule 1(a). Their governments may authorise additional signals under Rule 1(c), and closest possible compliance for lights, shapes and sound appliances under Rule 1(e). Neither paragraph exempts them from the steering and sailing rules.

What is the difference between the COLREGs and the US Inland Rules?

The US Inland Rules are special rules of the kind Rule 1(b) allows. They apply on the inland side of published demarcation lines. They are closely based on the COLREGs but differ in some details, such as certain sound signals. Check the current official text for the waters you are entering as part of your passage plan.

Does Rule 10 apply to every traffic separation scheme?

No. Rule 10 applies to traffic separation schemes adopted by the IMO, which Rule 1(d) provides for. Some schemes are established nationally or locally and governed by their own regulations. The chart notes and routeing publications will tell you which applies.

What does 'closest possible compliance' in Rule 1(e) mean?

Some vessels of special construction or purpose cannot meet the Rules for the number, position, range or arc of lights or shapes, or for sound-signalling appliances. In that case, the vessel's own government decides what arrangement comes closest to the Rules, and the vessel follows it. The decision belongs to the government, not the owner or the Master.

Test yourself

According to Rule 1(a), where do the COLREGs apply?

Rule 1(a) applies the Rules to all vessels on the high seas and in all connected waters navigable by seagoing vessels. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 1(a).

A harbour's local regulations supplement the COLREGs and cover speed limits and reporting, but say nothing about crossing situations. Which rules govern a crossing encounter inside the harbour?

These harbour rules supplement the COLREGs rather than replacing them, so the COLREGs govern what they do not cover, and Rules 15 to 17 apply. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 1(b).

Who determines the 'closest possible compliance' for lights on a vessel of special construction under Rule 1(e)?

Rule 1(e) gives this decision to the government concerned, not to class, the Master or the port state. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 1(e).

Under Rule 1(c), for which vessels may a government authorise additional signals?

Rule 1(c) covers additional signals for warships and vessels in convoy, and additional lights or shapes for fishing vessels fishing as a fleet. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rule 1(c).

Which statement about traffic separation schemes and Rule 1(d) is correct?

Rule 1(d) provides for IMO adoption of TSS, and Rule 10 applies to schemes adopted by the Organization. Reference: COLREG 1972, Rules 1(d) and 10.
