COLREG Rule 34 – Manoeuvring and Warning Signals

Quick answer

Rule 34 requires a power-driven vessel underway, when in sight of another vessel, to sound one short blast when altering to starboard, two when altering to port and three when operating astern propulsion. It also sets the narrow-channel overtaking signals, the 'five or more short and rapid blasts' doubt signal and the prolonged blast for bends and obstructions. The 34(a) signals announce what you are doing, not what you would like the other vessel to do.

What Rule 34 says, in plain terms

Rule 34 sits in Part D (Sound and light signals) and has six paragraphs. Paraphrased:

  • 34(a) Manoeuvring signals. When vessels are in sight of one another, a power-driven vessel underway must signal on her whistle any manoeuvre she makes that is authorised or required by the Rules:
    • one short blast: I am altering my course to starboard
    • two short blasts: I am altering my course to port
    • three short blasts: I am operating astern propulsion
  • 34(b) Light signals. Any vessel may back up the 34(a) whistle signals with matching flashes (one, two or three), repeated as needed while the manoeuvre is being carried out.
  • 34(c) Narrow channel overtaking. This works with Rule 9(e)(i). The overtaking vessel signals her intention and the vessel being overtaken signals agreement.
  • 34(d) Doubt or warning signal. A vessel approaching another in sight that does not understand the other's intentions, or doubts that the other is doing enough to avoid collision, must immediately sound at least five short and rapid blasts. She may back this up with at least five short and rapid flashes.
  • 34(e) Bends and obstructions. A vessel nearing a bend, or an area of a channel or fairway where other vessels may be hidden by an obstruction, sounds one prolonged blast. Any approaching vessel that may be within hearing on the other side answers with one prolonged blast.
  • 34(f) Multiple whistles. If the vessel has whistles fitted more than 100 metres apart, only one of them is used for manoeuvring and warning signals.

The definitions come from Rule 32. A short blast lasts about one second. A prolonged blast lasts four to six seconds.

Light signals under 34(b):

  • Each flash lasts about one second, with about one second between flashes.
  • There must be at least ten seconds between successive signals.
  • The light, where fitted, is an all-round white light visible at a minimum range of 5 miles.
  • It must be positioned in accordance with Annex I.

The light is optional. The whistle signal under 34(a) is not.

Applying it on the bridge

Three conditions must all be met before a 34(a) signal is mandatory:

  1. The vessels are in sight of one another, meaning visually, not just on radar or AIS (see Rule 3).
  2. Your vessel is power-driven and underway.
  3. You are making a manoeuvre authorised or required by the Rules, for example as give-way vessel, in a head-on situation, or as stand-on vessel acting under Rule 17.

Practical points:

  • Sound the signal as the manoeuvre is made, not before. Rule 34(a) describes action, not intention. 'I am altering' means the wheel is going over.
  • A small alteration still gets a signal if it is made for another vessel in sight. The fact that the alteration may not be readily apparent is a reason to signal, not a reason to stay quiet.
  • Sound a new signal for each change. If you alter to starboard and later come back to port to resume your track, the second change is also a manoeuvre: two short blasts.
  • Three short blasts means the engine is working astern. It does not mean the vessel is moving astern. A ship carrying headway with the engine going astern still sounds three short.
  • Use the light to reinforce at night or in high ambient noise.
  • If your vessel has whistles fitted more than 100 m apart (possible on very large ships), use only one of them for manoeuvring and warning signals (34(f)).
  • Check that it was heard. Watching the other vessel's response is part of keeping a proper look-out under Rule 5.

Whistle and light procedures, including testing the whistle and the manoeuvring light, will also be covered by your company SMS and the Master's standing orders. Follow those in addition to the Rule.

Worked scenarios

1. Crossing, give-way vessel. You are power-driven. Another power-driven vessel is on your starboard bow, in sight, on a steady bearing.

  • Rule 15 makes you give-way.
  • You put the wheel to starboard for a bold alteration and sound one short blast as she starts to swing.
  • At night, at the same time, you can give one flash on the manoeuvring light.

2. Head-on. Two power-driven vessels meet on reciprocal courses. Under Rule 14 each alters to starboard, and each sounds one short blast when she alters.

3. Stand-on vessel takes action. You are stand-on, the give-way vessel is not acting, and the range is closing.

  • First, sound at least five short and rapid blasts under 34(d).
  • If you then take action under Rule 17(a)(ii) and alter to starboard, sound one short blast for that manoeuvre.
  • Remember that under Rule 17(c), a power-driven vessel taking action under 17(a)(ii) in a crossing situation with another power-driven vessel shall, if the circumstances of the case admit, not alter course to port for a vessel on her own port side.

4. Crash stop. A fishing vessel crosses close ahead in daylight. You order full astern and sound three short blasts as the engine goes astern.

5. Narrow channel overtaking. You want to overtake a slower vessel on her port side, and she will need to move over to let you pass.

  • You sound two prolonged followed by two short.
  • If she agrees, she replies prolonged, short, prolonged, short and makes room.
  • If she is unhappy, she may sound the doubt signal under Rule 9(e)(i).
  • Rule 13 still applies to you throughout: you keep clear.

6. Blind bend in a river. Approaching the bend, you sound one prolonged blast. A vessel coming the other way, hidden by the bank, answers with one prolonged blast. Both of you now know the other is there.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

  • Treating one short blast as 'please pass me on my port side'. Under the International Regulations, the 34(a) signals report your own action. They are not proposals or agreements. Some local rules adopted under Rule 1(b), such as the US Inland Navigation Rules, use whistle signals differently. Read up on local rules before entering such waters.
  • Using VHF instead of the whistle. VHF calls are not required by the COLREGs. They can cause confusion over which vessel is being called. A VHF exchange never replaces a required sound signal or the steering and sailing rules.
  • Sounding 34(a) signals in fog when not in sight. Rule 34(a) applies only when vessels are in sight of one another. In restricted visibility, the fog signals of Rule 35 and the conduct required by Rule 19 apply.
  • Holding back the doubt signal out of politeness. Rule 34(d) says the vessel in doubt 'shall' sound it immediately. It is a mandatory safety signal, not an insult.
  • Thinking sailing vessels give 34(a) signals. Paragraph (a) is for power-driven vessels. The doubt signal in 34(d) and the bend signal in 34(e) are not limited to power-driven vessels.
  • Confusing short and prolonged blasts. A lazy four-second 'short' blast is ambiguous. Keep short blasts to about one second.
  • Mixing up Rule 34 and Rule 36. Rule 36 signals to attract attention must not be mistakable for any signal authorised elsewhere in the Rules.

Oral exam points

Examiners like short, precise answers. Expect questions like these.

  • Q: When must a power-driven vessel give manoeuvring signals? A: When underway, in sight of another vessel, and manoeuvring as authorised or required by the Rules.
  • Q: Is the manoeuvring light compulsory? A: No. Rule 34(b) says any vessel may supplement the whistle signals. Where fitted, it is an all-round white light visible at a minimum of 5 miles.
  • Q: Give the timing for the light signals. A: Each flash about one second, about one second between flashes, and not less than ten seconds between successive signals.
  • Q: What do you do if the give-way vessel is not acting? A: Sound at least five short and rapid blasts, optionally with flashes. Then consider action under Rule 17 and give the appropriate 34(a) signal when you manoeuvre.
  • Q: What signals do you give for overtaking in a narrow channel, and how does the other vessel reply? A: Two prolonged plus one short to overtake on her starboard side, or two prolonged plus two short for her port side. She replies prolonged-short-prolonged-short if she agrees.
  • Q: Which signals apply in restricted visibility? A: Rule 35 signals. Rule 34(a) does not apply unless vessels come into sight of one another.
  • Q: What does 34(f) cover? A: Whistles fitted more than 100 m apart. Only one is used for manoeuvring and warning signals.

Many candidates lose marks by saying 'intending to alter'. For 34(a), say 'altering'. Keep 'intend' for the 34(c) overtaking signals, which genuinely signal intention.

Key points
  • Rule 34(a): 1 short = altering to starboard, 2 short = altering to port, 3 short = operating astern propulsion.
  • 34(a) signals are mandatory for a power-driven vessel underway, in sight of another vessel, when manoeuvring under the Rules.
  • Signals describe action being taken, not intention, except the narrow-channel overtaking signals in 34(c).
  • At least five short and rapid blasts is the mandatory doubt signal and can be backed up with flashes.
  • Light signals are optional: about 1 s flashes, about 1 s apart, at least 10 s between signals, from an all-round white light visible at 5 miles.
  • One prolonged blast is used at bends and obstructions and is answered with one prolonged blast.
  • Rule 34(a)–(d) apply only when vessels are in sight of one another; in restricted visibility Rules 19 and 35 govern (the 34(e) bend signal applies in any visibility).

Frequently asked questions

What does one short blast mean under COLREGs?

One short blast, of about one second, from a power-driven vessel means 'I am altering my course to starboard'. It is sounded when vessels are in sight of one another and the alteration is being made. It is a statement of action, not a request to the other vessel.

What does five short blasts mean at sea?

At least five short and rapid blasts is the doubt or warning signal under Rule 34(d). It means you do not understand the other vessel's intentions, or doubt she is taking enough action to avoid collision. The vessel in doubt must sound it immediately and may back it up with at least five short and rapid flashes.

Does three short blasts mean the ship is going astern?

It means 'I am operating astern propulsion', so the engine or propeller is working astern. The vessel may still be carrying headway. That is why the signal is useful: it warns others that she is trying to stop or back.

Do I sound manoeuvring signals in fog?

Not under Rule 34(a) unless the vessels are in sight of one another. In restricted visibility you sound the Rule 35 fog signals and act under Rule 19. Once the other vessel comes into sight, Rule 34(a)–(d) apply. The 34(e) bend signal applies whatever the visibility.

Is the manoeuvring light mandatory on merchant ships?

Rule 34(b) makes light signals optional: any vessel may supplement the whistle with them. Where a manoeuvring light is fitted, it must be an all-round white light visible at least 5 miles and positioned as Annex I requires. Your company SMS or flag may set its own expectations on use.

Test yourself

A power-driven vessel in sight of another is altering course to port as required by the Rules. What signal must she give?

Two short blasts mean 'I am altering my course to port'. COLREG Rule 34(a).

What is the minimum interval between successive light signals under Rule 34(b)?

Flashes last about one second, about one second apart, with not less than ten seconds between successive signals. COLREG Rule 34(b)(ii).

In a narrow channel, which signal shows that a vessel agrees to being overtaken?

The vessel about to be overtaken shows agreement with one prolonged, one short, one prolonged, one short. COLREG Rules 34(c)(ii) and 9(e)(i).

When are the 34(a) manoeuvring signals required?

Rule 34(a) applies when vessels are in sight of one another. Radar-only contacts in restricted visibility are covered by Rules 19 and 35.

A ship has two whistles fitted 150 m apart. How should manoeuvring signals be given?

If whistles are fitted more than 100 m apart, only one is used for manoeuvring and warning signals. COLREG Rule 34(f).
