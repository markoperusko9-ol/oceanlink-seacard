COLREG Rule 32 – Definitions (Sound Signals): Whistle, Short Blast and Prolonged Blast

Quick answer

Rule 32 defines three terms used throughout Part D. A whistle is any sound appliance that can produce the prescribed blasts and meets Annex III. A short blast lasts about one second, and a prolonged blast lasts from four to six seconds. Every whistle signal in Rules 34 and 35 is built from these two blasts, so getting the timing right is what makes your signal mean what you intend.

What Rule 32 actually says

Rule 32 is the first rule of Part D (Sound and Light Signals). It is short and contains three definitions. In paraphrase:

  • (a) Whistle means any sound-signalling appliance that can produce the blasts prescribed in the Rules and that complies with the specifications in Annex III.
  • (b) Short blast means a blast of about one second's duration.
  • (c) Prolonged blast means a blast of four to six seconds' duration.

That is the whole rule. Do not underestimate it. Every whistle signal in Rules 34 and 35 is a combination of short and prolonged blasts. If you get the durations wrong, you are sending a different message, or no recognisable message at all.

Notice what Rule 32 does not define:

  • Bell and gong. Their carriage requirements are in Rule 33 and their technical details are in Annex III.
  • The interval between blasts. Intervals for particular signals are set in the rules that prescribe them, such as Rules 34 and 35.

The 'whistle' definition and the link to Annex III

The word 'whistle' is functional. It does not have to be a steam whistle or a particular type of horn. An air horn, an electrically driven diaphragm horn or an electronic system all count. The condition is that the appliance can make the prescribed blasts and meets Annex III.

Annex III sets the technical standard. In summary, it covers:

  • Frequency. The fundamental frequency band depends on the vessel's length. Larger vessels use lower frequencies. Roughly:
    • 70–200 Hz for vessels of 200 m or more
    • 130–350 Hz for vessels of 75 m but less than 200 m
    • 250–700 Hz for vessels under 75 m
  • Sound pressure and audibility range. Annex III gives an audibility range for information. It runs from 2 nautical miles for vessels of 200 m or more down to 0.5 nautical miles for vessels under 20 m. The Annex itself treats these figures as approximate. They depend on weather, background noise and the listener's position.
  • Directional properties of the whistle.
  • Positioning. The whistle should be sited as high as practicable and placed to reduce interference with the sound.
  • Multiple whistles fitted far apart, and combined whistle systems.

Rule 33 then says who must carry what:

Vessel length Required
12 m or more Whistle
20 m or more Whistle and bell
100 m or more Whistle, bell and gong
Under 12 m Not obliged to carry the above, but must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal

The gong may be replaced by other equipment with the same sound characteristics. Manual sounding of the required signals must always be possible.

As a watchkeeper, you will not test frequency on board. Your job is to make sure the whistle works from every control position the ship has. Surveys and approval of the equipment are a flag state matter.

How the definitions build every signal you will use

Once 'short' and 'prolonged' are fixed, the signals in Part D follow from them. These are the common ones.

Rule 34: vessels in sight of one another. The manoeuvring signals in (a) are given by power-driven vessels underway.

  • One short: I am altering my course to starboard.
  • Two short: I am altering my course to port.
  • Three short: I am operating astern propulsion.
  • Narrow channel overtaking:
    • Two prolonged and one short means I intend to overtake you on your starboard side.
    • Two prolonged and two short means I intend to overtake you on your port side.
    • Prolonged, short, prolonged, short means the vessel being overtaken agrees.
  • Doubt or warning: at least five short and rapid blasts.
  • Nearing a bend or obstructed area: one prolonged blast, answered with one prolonged blast.

Rule 35: restricted visibility (examples):

  • Power-driven vessel making way: one prolonged, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • Power-driven vessel underway but stopped: two prolonged, about 2 seconds apart, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • NUC, RAM, CBD, sailing, fishing, towing or pushing: one prolonged followed by two short, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • Last manned vessel of a tow: one prolonged followed by three short.
  • Pilot vessel on duty: may add an identity signal of four short blasts.

The practical point is simple. 'Prolonged, short, short' means something specific. Hold a blast for two seconds and nobody can tell whether it was short or prolonged.

Worked scenarios on the bridge

Scenario 1: crossing, clear weather. You are give-way in a crossing situation and alter 30° to starboard. You are a power-driven vessel in sight of the other vessel, so Rule 34(a) applies.

  1. Press the whistle for about one second.
  2. You may also show the manoeuvring light as a supplement, if fitted (Rule 34(b)).
  3. Watch the bearing and CPA to confirm the action is working.

A common fault is a nervous half-second tap. Count 'one-thousand' and release.

Scenario 2: fog, making way. Visibility drops and you start fog signals under Rule 35.

  1. Check that the automatic fog signal is set to the correct pattern.
  2. Check that the blast length falls within 4–6 seconds.
  3. Check that the interval is no more than 2 minutes.

Many automatic timers are adjustable, and some are set badly after maintenance. If you hear something like a 2-second blast, that is not a prolonged blast. Sound the signal manually until it is corrected, and report the defect as your company SMS requires.

Scenario 3: stopped in fog. You stop engines and lose way to wait for the pilot. Your signal changes from one prolonged to two prolonged about 2 seconds apart. If the timer is still sending the 'making way' pattern, you are telling other ships you are moving.

Scenario 4: doubt. Another vessel in sight is closing and its intentions are unclear. Sound at least five short, rapid blasts. Keep each blast short and the gaps tight, so it reads as 'doubt' and not as a jumble of single-short signals. Follow up on VHF if useful, but keep in mind the risks of VHF-assisted collisions.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 'prolonged' as 'long as you like'. Eight or ten seconds is outside the definition. Six seconds is the upper limit.
  • Cutting short blasts too short. A short blast is about one second, not a click. Very brief blasts may not carry at all.
  • Unclear blasts. In a combination signal, a short blast that drifts towards two or three seconds becomes ambiguous.
  • Wrong automatic signal mode. Forgetting to change from 'making way' to 'stopped', or the reverse.
  • Wrong rule. Using Rule 34 manoeuvring signals in restricted visibility when the vessels are not in sight of one another. Rule 34(a) applies only when vessels are in sight.
  • Assuming the Annex III audibility range is guaranteed. Wind, rain and engine noise can cut it sharply. A signal is no substitute for radar and a proper look-out.
  • Not testing every control position. Whistle tests before arrival and departure are normally part of the company checklist. Follow your SMS for frequency and recording.

Oral exam: what examiners look for

Expect Rule 32 to come up as a lead-in to Part D questions. Typical lines of questioning:

  • 'Define a short blast and a prolonged blast.' Answer cleanly: about one second, and four to six seconds. Do not hedge.
  • 'What is a whistle?' Any appliance that can produce the prescribed blasts and complies with Annex III. Mention that Annex III covers frequency, intensity and audibility.
  • 'What sound appliances must a 150 m vessel carry?' Whistle, bell and gong, under Rule 33. The gong is required at 100 m and over.
  • 'Demonstrate the signal for a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre in fog.' Many examiners will ask you to say or tap it out with timing: prolonged (4–6 s), short, short.
  • 'Where is the interval between signals specified?' In the rule for each signal, such as Rules 34 and 35. Rule 32 does not specify it.
  • 'Is a whistle signal ever optional?' Yes:
    • Vessels under 12 m are not obliged to give the Rule 35 signals, but must make some other efficient sound signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes (Rule 35(j)).
    • Vessels under 12 m have relaxed carriage requirements.
    • Some Rule 35 signals use 'may', for example the extra anchored signal and the pilot vessel identity signal.

The examiner wants to see that you link the definition to its use. Do not stop at reciting numbers.

Key points
  • Rule 32 defines three terms: whistle, short blast and prolonged blast.
  • A short blast lasts about one second; a prolonged blast lasts four to six seconds.
  • A 'whistle' is any appliance that produces the prescribed blasts and complies with Annex III.
  • Annex III sets frequency, intensity and audibility by ship length; the audibility ranges are approximate.
  • Rule 33 sets carriage: whistle from 12 m, bell from 20 m, gong from 100 m.
  • All whistle signals in Rules 34 and 35 are built from short and prolonged blasts, so timing errors change the meaning.
  • Check that automatic fog signal timers meet the 4–6 second prolonged blast and are in the correct mode.

Frequently asked questions

How long is a prolonged blast under COLREG?

Rule 32(c) defines a prolonged blast as lasting from four to six seconds. Anything shorter or longer falls outside the definition and may be misread. Aim for about five seconds when sounding manually.

How long is a short blast?

Rule 32(b) defines a short blast as lasting about one second. It should be clearly distinguishable from a prolonged blast, so avoid both very brief taps and blasts that stretch to two or three seconds.

Does an electric horn count as a whistle under the Rules?

Yes. Rule 32(a) defines a whistle by what it does, not how it works. Any sound-signalling appliance that can produce the prescribed blasts and complies with Annex III qualifies. Approval and survey of the installed equipment fall under the flag state.

Where are the intervals between sound signals defined?

Not in Rule 32. Each signal rule sets its own intervals. For example, Rule 35 specifies intervals of not more than 2 minutes for most fog signals. Rule 34(b) sets the timing for the supplementary light signals.

Do small boats need a whistle?

Under Rule 33, a vessel of 12 m or more must carry a whistle. A vessel under 12 m is not obliged to carry the Rule 33 appliances, but must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal. Some administrations add national requirements for small craft, so check local rules.

Test yourself

Under COLREG Rule 32, what is the duration of a prolonged blast?

Rule 32(c) defines a prolonged blast as four to six seconds. Longer or shorter blasts fall outside the definition. (COLREG Rule 32(c))

Under COLREG Rule 32, what is the duration of a short blast?

Rule 32(b) defines a short blast as about one second. Half-second taps are a common error. (COLREG Rule 32(b))

Which best describes a 'whistle' as defined in Rule 32?

The definition is functional and tied to Annex III. Audibility ranges in Annex III vary with ship length. (COLREG Rule 32(a), Annex III)

A power-driven vessel in sight of another sounds three short blasts. What does this mean?

Three short blasts mean 'I am operating astern propulsion'. Two short means altering to port. At least five short and rapid blasts signal doubt. (COLREG Rule 34(a) and (d))

In restricted visibility, a power-driven vessel underway but stopped and making no way sounds which signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes?

Rule 35(b) prescribes two prolonged blasts about 2 seconds apart. One prolonged is for making way. Bell ringing is the anchored signal. (COLREG Rule 35(a), (b), (g))
