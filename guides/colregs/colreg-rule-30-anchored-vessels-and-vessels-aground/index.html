COLREG Rule 30 – Anchored vessels and vessels aground

Quick answer

Under Rule 30, a vessel at anchor shows an all-round white light forward and a lower all-round white light aft by night, and one ball forward by day. A vessel under 50 m may show a single all-round white instead, and a vessel of 100 m or more must also light her decks. A vessel aground shows her anchor lights plus two all-round red lights in a vertical line by night, or three balls in a vertical line by day.

What Rule 30 says, in plain words

Rule 30 sits in Part C (Lights and Shapes). It sets out the signals for two situations in which a vessel is not underway within the meaning of Rule 3: lying at anchor, and aground.

At anchor, paraphrased:

  • (a) Show, where they can best be seen, an all-round white light or one ball in the fore part. At or near the stern, and lower than the forward light, show a second all-round white light.
  • (b) A vessel of less than 50 m may show one all-round white light where it can best be seen, instead of the two in (a).
  • (c) Any vessel at anchor may use her working or equivalent lights to light her decks. A vessel of 100 m or more shall do so.
  • (e) A vessel under 7 m need not show the anchor light or ball. This applies only when she is not in or near a narrow channel, fairway or anchorage, or where other vessels normally navigate.

Aground, paraphrased:

  • (d) Show the anchor lights from (a) or (b). In addition, where they can best be seen, show two all-round red lights in a vertical line by night, or three balls in a vertical line by day.
  • (f) A vessel under 12 m, when aground, need not show the two reds or three balls.

Rule 20 governs the timing. Lights are shown from sunset to sunrise, and also by day in restricted visibility. Shapes are shown by day.

Supporting technical points:

  • Light range (Rule 22). For vessels of 50 m or more, all-round white and red lights must be visible at 3 miles. For smaller vessels they must be visible at 2 miles.
  • Ball size and spacing (Annex I). A ball must be at least 0.6 m in diameter, and shapes in a vertical line at least 1.5 m apart. Vessels under 20 m may use smaller shapes commensurate with their size (Annex I, 6(c)).
  • Anchor light heights (Annex I). Where two anchor lights are carried, the forward one must be at least 4.5 m above the after one. On a vessel of 50 m or more, the forward anchor light must be at least 6 m above the hull.

Applying it on the bridge

Coming to anchor

  1. When the anchor is brought up and holding, the vessel is no longer underway.
  2. Switch off the masthead lights, sidelights and sternlight. Switch on the anchor lights.
  3. If she is 100 m or more, switch on the deck lights.
  4. By day, hoist the ball forward. Check it is not hidden behind the foremast, cranes or deck cargo.
  5. Log the times, set the AIS navigational status to 'at anchor', and make any VTS/port reports required, in line with your company SMS.

Heaving up

  • She becomes underway when she is no longer at anchor.
  • Your SMS will normally fix the switch-over point, often when the anchor is reported aweigh.
  • Do not leave the anchor lights or ball up while making way. Other vessels will plan their action on what you show.

Anchor watch

  • Keep a proper look-out (Rule 5), monitor position and weather, and watch for dragging, both your own and other ships'.
  • STCW Section A-VIII/2 sets out the watchkeeping principles at anchor. The detail lives in the Master's standing orders and the SMS.
  • Check periodically that every anchor light is actually burning. A failed after light makes a large ship look like a small one.

Sound signals in restricted visibility (Rule 35)

  • At anchor: ring the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds, at intervals of not more than one minute. A vessel of 100 m or more rings the bell forward and immediately afterwards sounds the gong aft.
  • Optional warning at anchor: you may also sound short–prolonged–short to warn an approaching vessel.
  • Aground: give the same bell (and gong) signal. Add three separate and distinct strokes on the bell immediately before and after the rapid ringing. An appropriate whistle signal may also be sounded.
  • Smaller vessels: these have alternatives under Rule 35. Check the rule for your vessel's length.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Approaching an anchorage at night.

You see two all-round whites, one higher forward and one lower aft, with a blaze of deck lights between them.

  • She is a vessel at anchor, probably a large one. Deck lights are mandatory at 100 m or more, but any vessel at anchor may show them, so they do not prove her length.
  • The higher light marks her bow, so you can tell roughly how she is lying. That indicates the tidal stream or wind direction at the anchorage.
  • Treat her as a fixed obstruction that can swing.
  • Pass at a distance that allows for her swinging circle and her anchor cable.

Scenario 2 – Two reds in a vertical line plus two whites.

  • Two all-round reds alone would suggest a vessel not under command (Rule 27).
  • The anchor lights with them tell you this vessel is aground. A NUC vessel making way would also show sidelights and a sternlight.
  • She marks a danger to navigation, not just a ship. Check your chart: you may be standing into the same shoal.

Scenario 3 – Your own ship touches bottom and remains fast.

  • Display the signals: anchor lights plus two reds by night, three balls by day.
  • Sound the aground signal in restricted visibility.
  • Inform the Master and engine room. Carry out the SMS emergency procedure: soundings, tank checks, damage assessment.
  • Make the appropriate broadcasts or reports to the coastal authority.
  • The signals tell traffic what you are. They do not replace a direct warning by VHF to a vessel standing into danger.

Scenario 4 – Day, good visibility, 180 m bulk carrier at anchor.

  • One ball forward is required, whatever her size. Only vessels under 7 m clear of channels, anchorages and traffic are exempt.
  • Deck lights are not required in daylight with good visibility. In restricted visibility by day, when lights are required under Rule 20, a 100 m+ vessel should light her decks.
  • "Everyone can see we're anchored" is not a defence. The shape is mandatory.

How Rule 30 interacts with other rules

  • Rule 27(a), NUC. NUC lights and shapes apply to a vessel underway. A broken-down vessel that has anchored shows anchor signals, not NUC signals.
  • Rule 27(b), RAM. A vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre shows her RAM signals and, when at anchor, the anchor lights or ball as well.
  • Rule 27(d), dredging or underwater operations. When at anchor, such a vessel shows the Rule 27 signals, including the obstruction and passing-side signals, instead of the Rule 30 anchor lights.
  • Rule 28, CBD. Constrained by draught is an underway status only. It is never shown at anchor.
  • Rule 29, pilot vessels. A pilot vessel on duty at anchor shows her pilot lights plus the anchor lights or shape.
  • Rule 26, fishing vessels. A vessel engaged in fishing shows her fishing signals whether underway or at anchor. This differs from the RAM arrangement.
  • Rule 20(b), other lights. Lights that could be mistaken for the prescribed ones are not allowed. Deck lighting at anchor is expressly permitted by Rule 30(c), but it should not hide or drown out the anchor lights.
  • Rule 3, definitions. A vessel at anchor or aground is not underway. Once she is underway, Part B steering and sailing duties apply in full.

Dragging anchor. A dragging vessel may still be showing anchor signals. If you are the one dragging:

  • Act early: veer cable, use the engine, or heave up and re-anchor.
  • Warn nearby ships by VHF and, in restricted visibility, by sound.
  • Change to underway lights once she is under way.

How the law treats a dragging vessel is a matter for the specific facts. Seamanship requires prompt action either way.

Common mistakes

  • Late changeover. Leaving the anchor lights, deck lights or ball up after heaving up and getting under way, or switching on navigation lights while still brought up.
  • Missing ball. No anchor ball by day, or a ball stowed out of sight behind cargo gear or containers.
  • NUC confusion. Showing NUC lights on top of anchor lights after an engine failure at anchor.
  • Aground versus NUC. Confusing aground (two reds with anchor lights) with NUC (two reds with sidelights and sternlight if making way, or two reds alone if not).
  • Dead after light. Not checking that the after anchor light works. The lamp often fails and goes unnoticed from the bridge.
  • Optional deck lights. Thinking deck lights are optional on a 100 m+ ship. They are mandatory.
  • CBD at anchor. Showing constrained-by-draught signals at anchor.
  • Wrong bell signal. Forgetting the gong aft on a 100 m+ vessel, or the three distinct bell strokes when aground.

Oral exam points

Examiners usually test Rule 30 by showing a light card or day-shape picture and asking you to identify it and say what you would do. Have crisp answers ready:

  • "Lights of a 150 m vessel at anchor?" Two all-round whites, the forward one higher. Deck lights are mandatory. The bell is rung forward and the gong sounded aft in restricted visibility.
  • "A 40 m vessel at anchor?" She may show a single all-round white where best seen. Deck lights are optional.
  • "Day signal for aground?" Three balls in a vertical line.
  • "Two reds in a vertical line and two whites, one higher than the other?" A vessel aground, probably 50 m or more, and the higher white marks her bow. Keep clear and check the chart for the shoal.
  • "Smallest vessel exempt?" At anchor, under 7 m, but only when clear of channels, fairways, anchorages and normal traffic. Aground, under 12 m is exempt from the reds and balls.
  • "Sound signal aground?" Three distinct bell strokes, then the rapid bell (and gong if 100 m+), then three distinct strokes again.
  • "Dredger at anchor?" Rule 27(d) signals instead of Rule 30 anchor lights.

Say "where they can best be seen" and give the rule number. It shows the examiner you know the source, not just the picture.

Key points
  • At anchor: one ball forward by day; by night an all-round white forward and a lower one aft.
  • Vessels under 50 m may show a single all-round white light at anchor.
  • Deck lights are optional at anchor but mandatory for vessels of 100 m or more.
  • Aground: anchor lights plus two all-round reds in a vertical line by night; three balls by day.
  • The anchor lights are what distinguish a vessel aground from a NUC vessel.
  • In restricted visibility at anchor, the bell rings forward and, on 100 m+ vessels, the gong sounds aft; aground adds three distinct bell strokes before and after.
  • Change over between anchor and underway signals at the correct moment; your SMS defines the exact procedure.

Frequently asked questions

Do anchor lights have to be shown during the day?

Not normally. By day a vessel at anchor shows one ball forward. Under Rule 20, lights are also shown by day in restricted visibility, so in fog the anchor lights should be on as well as the ball.

Is a vessel at anchor with engine failure 'not under command'?

No. NUC signals under Rule 27(a) are for a vessel underway that cannot manoeuvre as the rules require. A vessel at anchor shows Rule 30 anchor signals. She should warn nearby traffic by VHF if her situation becomes a danger, for example if she starts to drag.

Why is the forward anchor light higher than the after one?

The height difference lets an observer judge the anchored vessel's heading, and so how she is lying to the wind or tidal stream. Annex I requires the forward light to be at least 4.5 m above the after one when two are carried.

What sound signal does a vessel aground make in fog?

She gives the anchored vessel's bell signal (plus the gong aft if 100 m or more), with three separate and distinct bell strokes immediately before and after the rapid ringing. She may also sound an appropriate whistle signal under Rule 35.

Can I use deck lights at anchor on a small ship?

Yes. Rule 30(c) permits any vessel at anchor to light her decks, and makes it compulsory only for vessels of 100 m or more. Make sure the deck lighting does not obscure the anchor lights or create confusion.

Test yourself

By day, what shape does a vessel aground exhibit?

Rule 30(d)(ii) requires three balls in a vertical line. Two balls is NUC, and ball-diamond-ball is RAM (Rule 27).

For which vessels is illuminating the decks at anchor mandatory?

Rule 30(c) makes deck lights optional for all vessels at anchor but compulsory for those of 100 m and more.

At night you see two all-round red lights in a vertical line and two all-round white lights at different heights, with no sidelights. What is it?

Rule 30(d): anchor lights plus two all-round reds indicates a vessel aground. A CBD vessel shows three reds, and RAM shows red-white-red.

What may a vessel of less than 50 m show at anchor at night instead of two anchor lights?

Rule 30(b) allows a vessel under 50 m to show a single all-round white light. The under-7 m exemption in 30(e) does not apply in or near an anchorage.

A 200 m vessel at anchor in fog sounds which signal?

Rule 35(g) requires the bell forward and, for vessels of 100 m or more, the gong aft. The whistle signals given are those for power-driven vessels underway.
