COLREG Rule 9 – Narrow channels

Quick answer

Rule 9 requires a vessel proceeding along a narrow channel or fairway to keep as near to the outer limit on her starboard side as is safe and practicable. Small craft, sailing vessels, fishing vessels and crossing vessels must not impede vessels that can only navigate safely within the channel. The rule also covers overtaking by agreement using whistle signals, the one prolonged blast at bends, and avoiding anchoring in the channel.

What Rule 9 actually says (paraphrased)

Rule 9 sits in Section I of Part B. Section I applies in any condition of visibility (Rule 4), so it applies in fog as well as clear weather. It has seven paragraphs:

Para In plain words
9(a) A vessel going along a narrow channel or fairway keeps as near to the outer limit on her starboard side as is safe and practicable.
9(b) A vessel under 20 m in length, or a sailing vessel of any length, shall not impede a vessel that can safely navigate only within the channel.
9(c) A vessel engaged in fishing shall not impede the passage of any other vessel navigating within the channel.
9(d) No vessel shall cross a channel if the crossing impedes a vessel that can safely navigate only within it. If the channel vessel doubts the crosser's intentions, she may sound the doubt signal of Rule 34(d).
9(e) Where overtaking needs the vessel ahead to take action, the overtaking vessel signals her intention under Rule 34(c)(i). The vessel ahead, if she agrees, answers under Rule 34(c)(ii) and makes room. If in doubt she may sound Rule 34(d). The overtaking vessel is still bound by Rule 13.
9(f) Near a bend, or anywhere other vessels may be hidden by an obstruction, navigate with particular alertness and caution and sound the Rule 34(e) signal.
9(g) Any vessel shall avoid anchoring in a narrow channel if the circumstances of the case admit.

The Rules do not define "narrow channel" or "fairway". Whether a stretch of water counts is a question of fact: its width, depth, traffic, buoyage and how it is used. Local rules made under Rule 1(b) often settle the point for a particular port or river. Read the port's regulations and the sailing directions before arrival. Do not decide on the day.

Key terms the examiner will push you on

  • "Safe and practicable": 9(a) is not absolute. A deep-draught ship may need to stay near the centre line because the starboard edge is too shallow. Bank effect, squat or a pilot's advice may also justify a different position. You must be able to justify any departure from the starboard side.
  • "Can safely navigate only within": this is about the vessel's real limitation in that channel, usually draught against the available depth. The vessel does not need to be showing Rule 28 constrained-by-draught signals. A 300 m bulker in a dredged channel is such a vessel. A coaster with plenty of water outside the buoys may not be.
  • "Shall not impede": this term comes with Rule 8(f). The vessel required not to impede must take early action to leave enough sea room for safe passage. If the two vessels still get into risk of collision, both are fully bound by the steering and sailing rules. The vessel not to be impeded does not become a privileged stand-on vessel with no duties.
  • Rule 9 versus Rule 18: Rule 18 starts by saying it applies except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 otherwise require. A sailing vessel in a narrow channel therefore cannot rely on Rule 18 to make a confined power-driven vessel give way. Rule 9(b) overrides that.

Applying Rule 9 on the bridge: step by step

  1. Planning. At the appraisal stage, mark the channel limits, the planned track and the wheel-over points on the chart. Mark the bends and blind spots where 9(f) applies, and any no-anchoring or no-overtaking areas from local regulations. Plan your track toward the starboard side unless depth or squat says otherwise.
  2. Brief the team. Cover the pilot's intentions, the VTS reporting points and the expected traffic. Agree who sounds the whistle and who works the VHF.
  3. Execution. Keep to the starboard side. Use parallel indexing on channel edges or bridge piers so you know your cross-track position. Do not rely only on GNSS.
  4. Watch the small stuff. Fishing boats, yachts and launches often ignore 9(b) and 9(c). Track them early and make your restriction obvious. Use speed, aspect and, where useful, VHF.
  5. Use the whistle. Sound one prolonged blast approaching blind bends. Use at least five short and rapid blasts (Rule 34(d)) when in sight of a crosser or other approaching vessel whose intentions are unclear. A VHF agreement can supplement the sound signals required by the Rules, but it does not replace them or change your obligations under them.
  6. Keep speed under review. Rule 6 still applies. Too much speed in a channel increases squat and bank effect and reduces the time you have to react at bends.

Whistle signals used with Rule 9

Situation Signal (Rule 34) Meaning
Overtaking, wish to pass on her starboard side Two prolonged, one short "I intend to overtake you on your starboard side"
Overtaking, wish to pass on her port side Two prolonged, two short "I intend to overtake you on your port side"
Vessel ahead agrees Prolonged, short, prolonged, short (Morse C) Agreement
Doubt about the other vessel's intentions or actions At least five short and rapid blasts Doubt / warning
Nearing a bend or obstruction One prolonged blast Warning. An approaching vessel round the bend that hears it answers with one prolonged blast.

The overtaking and agreement signals under Rule 34(c) are for vessels in sight of one another in a narrow channel or fairway. Do not use them in open water. There, Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals apply instead. The five-short signal under Rule 34(d) is also for vessels in sight of one another.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: yacht in the channel. You are a loaded tanker in a dredged approach channel with a pilot on board. A 15 m sailing yacht is tacking across the channel ahead.

  • Under 9(b) and 9(d), the yacht must not impede you. Under Rule 8(f), she must act early.
  • If she keeps coming, sound at least five short blasts and reduce speed early. Call her on VHF if you can identify her.
  • If risk of collision develops, you are both bound by Part B. You cannot simply hold course and blame her. Take whatever action is safe within the channel.

Scenario 2: overtaking a slower ship. You are closing a slow coaster ahead in a channel too narrow to pass without her moving over. You want to pass on her port side.

  1. Sound two prolonged and two short blasts.
  2. If she agrees, she answers with the Morse C and edges to starboard.
  3. If she sounds five short, she is in doubt. Do not attempt the overtake. Drop back and talk on VHF.
  4. If she agrees, you remain the overtaking vessel under Rule 13. Keep clear until you are past and clear.

Scenario 3: blind bend. A river bend is masked by a high bank and warehouses.

  • Sound one prolonged blast on approach and listen for an answer.
  • Slow down and keep tight to the starboard side.
  • Do not cut the corner. A ship coming the other way may be doing the same, and you would meet on her side of the channel.

Scenario 4: engine failure. Your main engine trips in the channel.

  • Under 9(g), anchoring in the channel is to be avoided if circumstances admit. Losing propulsion near a bank or another ship may well justify it.
  • If you anchor, show the Rule 30 lights or shapes.
  • If you are not anchored and not under command, show the Rule 27 NUC signals.
  • Inform VTS or the port authority where one exists, as required by local regulations, and warn traffic on VHF.

Common mistakes

  • Treating "not impede" as "must give way" and stopping there. Rule 8(f) means the duty starts early, before risk of collision exists. Both vessels keep their Part B duties if risk develops.
  • Assuming only CBD vessels are protected. Rule 9(b) and (d) protect any vessel that can safely navigate only within the channel, whatever lights she is showing.
  • Fishing vessels thinking Rule 18 protects them. Under 9(c), a vessel engaged in fishing must not impede any vessel navigating in the channel, not only deep-draught ships.
  • Drifting to the middle or the port side. This often happens to take a "better line" through a bend or because the autopilot track was badly placed. It is a classic contributor to collisions in channels.
  • Using the overtaking signals as a demand. The vessel ahead may refuse. Five short blasts means wait.
  • Relying on a VHF agreement alone. VHF misidentification is a known hazard. The Rules and the sound signals still apply.
  • Forgetting the pilot changes nothing in law. The master and the OOW remain responsible for compliance with the Rules.

Oral exam: questions you should expect

  • "Define a narrow channel." The Rules give no definition. It is a matter of fact for each case, often clarified by local rules under Rule 1(b).
  • "Which side do you keep to, and when may you not?" Keep to the starboard side as far as is safe and practicable. Draught, squat, bank effect or obstructions may justify a different position.
  • "A 25 m sailing vessel is ahead in the channel. Who keeps clear?" She must not impede you if you are confined to the channel. Rule 9(b) covers sailing vessels of any length, and Rule 18 yields to Rule 9. If risk of collision develops, both must act under Part B.
  • "Give me the overtaking signals." Two prolonged and one short to pass on her starboard side. Two prolonged and two short to pass on her port side. She agrees with the Morse C. If she is in doubt, she sounds five short.
  • "Does agreement make the overtaken vessel give-way?" No. Rule 9(e)(ii) keeps the overtaking vessel bound by Rule 13.
  • "What do you sound at a blind bend, and what is the reply?" One prolonged blast. Any approaching vessel round the bend that hears it answers with one prolonged blast.
  • "Can you anchor in a channel?" Avoid it if circumstances admit. In an emergency you may, with the correct Rule 30 signals; inform VTS or the port authority where one exists and warn traffic, as required by local regulations and good seamanship.
Key points
  • Keep as near to the starboard outer limit of a narrow channel as is safe and practicable.
  • Vessels under 20 m and sailing vessels of any length must not impede a vessel confined to the channel.
  • Fishing vessels must not impede any vessel navigating within the channel.
  • "Not impede" means early action under Rule 8(f), and both vessels stay bound by Part B if risk of collision develops.
  • Overtaking needing the other ship's cooperation uses Rule 34(c) signals; the overtaking vessel remains bound by Rule 13.
  • Sound one prolonged blast at blind bends and navigate with particular caution.
  • Avoid anchoring in a narrow channel unless the circumstances leave no choice.

Frequently asked questions

What is considered a narrow channel under COLREG?

COLREG does not define it. Whether a waterway is a narrow channel or fairway depends on its width, depth, traffic and use, and is decided case by case. Many ports and rivers settle the matter through local rules under Rule 1(b), so check the port regulations and sailing directions.

Does a sailing vessel have right of way over a ship in a narrow channel?

Not where the ship can safely navigate only within the channel. Rule 9(b) says a sailing vessel must not impede such a ship, and Rule 18 expressly gives way to Rule 9. If risk of collision still develops, both vessels must comply with the steering and sailing rules.

What are the overtaking signals in a narrow channel?

Two prolonged blasts and one short means you intend to overtake on the other vessel's starboard side. Two prolonged and two short means her port side. The vessel ahead signals agreement with prolonged, short, prolonged, short, or sounds at least five short blasts if in doubt.

Does Rule 9 apply in restricted visibility?

Yes. Rule 9 is in Section I of Part B, which applies in any condition of visibility. In restricted visibility you also apply Rule 19 and the fog signals of Rule 35. Note that the Rule 34(c) overtaking signals are for vessels in sight of one another.

Can I anchor in a narrow channel if my engine fails?

Rule 9(g) says avoid anchoring in a narrow channel if the circumstances admit. A propulsion failure near banks or traffic may make anchoring the safest option. If you anchor, show the correct anchor signals, warn nearby traffic and inform VTS or the port authority where one exists, as local regulations require.

Test yourself

In a narrow channel, you wish to overtake a vessel ahead on her port side. Which signal do you sound?

Two prolonged and two short signals intent to overtake on the other vessel's port side; two prolonged and one short is for her starboard side. COLREG Rule 34(c)(i) with Rule 9(e).

Approaching a bend in a channel where vessels beyond may be obscured, what signal should you sound?

Rule 9(f) requires the Rule 34(e) signal of one prolonged blast near such a bend, answered by one prolonged blast from an approaching vessel. COLREG Rules 9(f) and 34(e).

A 40 m sailing vessel meets a deep-draught bulk carrier that can safely navigate only within the channel. What applies?

Rule 9(b) applies to sailing vessels of any length, and Rule 18 is subject to Rule 9. No CBD signals are needed. COLREG Rules 9(b) and 18.

After the vessel ahead agrees to be overtaken in a narrow channel, what is the overtaking vessel's position?

Rule 9(e)(ii) states the rule does not relieve the overtaking vessel of her obligation under Rule 13. COLREG Rules 9(e) and 13.

Where should a vessel proceeding along a narrow channel keep?

Rule 9(a) requires keeping near the starboard outer limit, qualified by "safe and practicable", so it is not an absolute line. COLREG Rule 9(a).
