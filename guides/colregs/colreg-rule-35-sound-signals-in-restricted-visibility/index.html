COLREG Rule 35 – Sound Signals in Restricted Visibility

Quick answer

Rule 35 sets out the sound signals you must make in or near restricted visibility, by day or night. A power-driven vessel making way gives one prolonged blast at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. When stopped and making no way she gives two prolonged blasts. Special categories, towed vessels, anchored and aground vessels each have their own signal, and you must be able to identify every one of them by ear.

What Rule 35 does and when it applies

Rule 35 tells every vessel what to sound when visibility is restricted. Other vessels can then work out roughly what you are and what you are doing, even when they cannot see you.

Three points decide when the rule applies:

  • In or near an area of restricted visibility. You do not have to be inside the fog bank. If you are in clear weather close to one, vessels inside it cannot see you, so you sound signals.
  • By day or by night. Fog signals have nothing to do with darkness.
  • Restricted visibility is defined in Rule 3. It means visibility reduced by fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms, sandstorms or any similar cause.

COLREG gives no visibility range at which signals must start. Many masters' standing orders and company SMS procedures set a figure, and these vary between companies. Follow your own ship's orders. The legal test remains whether visibility is restricted, not a number on a card.

Rule 35 works alongside other rules:

  • Rule 32 defines the blasts. A short blast lasts about one second. A prolonged blast lasts four to six seconds.
  • Rule 33 sets the equipment. A vessel of 12 m or more carries a whistle. A vessel of 20 m or more also carries a bell. A vessel of 100 m or more also carries a gong.
  • Rule 19 governs how you manoeuvre when vessels are not in sight of one another.

Signals for vessels underway

All intervals below are maximums. 'Not more than 2 minutes' means you may sound more often, but never less often.

Points worth knowing:

  • The (— · ·) signal replaces the power-driven signals. It is sounded whether the vessel is making way or not. A tug with a tow that is stopped in the water still sounds (— · ·), not two prolonged.
  • The towed vessel's signal should, when practicable, follow straight after the towing vessel's signal. A listener then hears the tug and tow as one group.
  • 'Making way through the water' is not the same as moving over the ground. A ship stopped in the water and drifting on a current is making no way. She sounds two prolonged blasts.

Signals at anchor, aground, and for small vessels

At anchor the interval is shorter: not more than one minute.

  • Any vessel at anchor: ring the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds.
  • Vessel of 100 m or more: ring the bell in the fore part of the vessel. Immediately afterwards, sound the gong rapidly in the after part for about 5 seconds.
  • Optional warning: an anchored vessel may also sound one short, one prolonged and one short blast (· — ·). This warns an approaching vessel of her position and the possibility of collision.

Exceptions to the anchor signal:

  • A vessel engaged in fishing at anchor sounds (— · ·) instead of the bell.
  • A vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre carrying out her work at anchor also sounds (— · ·) instead of the bell.

Aground:

  • Give the anchor bell signal, plus the gong if required by length.
  • Add three separate and distinct strokes on the bell immediately before and immediately after the rapid ringing.
  • You may also sound an appropriate whistle signal. The rule does not specify which one.

Small vessels:

  • 12 m or more but less than 20 m: not obliged to give the anchor or aground bell signals. If she does not, she must make some other efficient sound signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • Less than 12 m: not obliged to give any of the Rule 35 signals. If she does not, she must make some other efficient sound signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.

The practical lesson for a big-ship OOW: a small craft may be making a weak or non-standard sound, or one you cannot hear at all over your own noise. Radar and a proper lookout matter more than ever.

Applying Rule 35 on the bridge: step by step

When visibility closes in, sound signals are one item on a longer checklist. A typical sequence is below. The exact order and extra items depend on your SMS and master's orders.

  1. Call the master as required by standing orders.
  2. Start the fog signal. Most ships have an automatic whistle controller. Check that:
    • it is set to the correct signal for your current status;
    • the interval is within the maximum;
    • you can actually hear it on the bridge.
  3. Comply with Rule 6 and Rule 19. Proceed at a safe speed and have the engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  4. Post a lookout who can hear. Open a bridge wing door or use the sound reception system if fitted. Reduce bridge noise. A fog signal you cannot hear is no use to you.
  5. Change the signal when your status changes. Examples:
    • stopping in the water: from (—) to (— —);
    • taking a tow or starting restricted-manoeuvrability work: to (— · ·);
    • anchoring: switch whistle off and start the bell (and gong if 100 m or more).
  6. Log it. Record when signals started, changed and stopped, as required by your company procedures.
  7. Keep signalling while 'near' the fog, even after you break into clear weather, if other vessels may still be inside it.

STCW watchkeeping principles also expect the OOW to sound fog signals in accordance with COLREG and to inform the master when restricted visibility is encountered or expected.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: You hear a single prolonged blast, apparently forward of the beam.

  • This is a power-driven vessel making way. You have no reliable range or bearing.
  • Unless you have determined that risk of collision does not exist, Rule 19 requires you to:
    • reduce speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course;
    • if necessary, take all way off;
    • navigate with extreme caution until the danger of collision is over.
  • Sound travels unpredictably in fog. Never judge the other vessel's distance or direction by how loud the signal is.

Scenario 2: You hear (— · ·) followed immediately by (— · · ·).

  • This is almost certainly a vessel towing, followed by her manned tow.
  • Expect a long, slow, unmanoeuvrable combination.
  • Allow generous room on radar for the tow behind the tug.

Scenario 3: Your 180 m bulk carrier anchors off a port in fog.

  • Switch off the whistle signal.
  • At intervals of not more than one minute:
    • ring the bell forward rapidly for about 5 seconds;
    • then sound the gong aft for about 5 seconds.
  • If a radar target is closing your position, you may also sound (· — ·) to warn her.

Scenario 4: Your 70 m coaster grounds in fog.

  • Every minute or less, sound:
    • three distinct strokes on the bell;
    • then rapid ringing for about 5 seconds;
    • then three distinct strokes again.
  • No gong is required, because she is under 100 m.
  • You may add an appropriate whistle signal.

Scenario 5: You are a container ship and must stop to wait for the pilot.

  • Once you have no way through the water, change from one prolonged blast to two prolonged blasts.
  • The pilot boat may be sounding her own signal plus four short blasts as an identity signal. Do not confuse this with five short blasts, which is the Rule 34 doubt signal used between vessels in sight of one another.

Common mistakes

  • Using Rule 34 manoeuvring signals in fog. One, two or three short blasts for course alterations and astern propulsion are for vessels in sight of one another. Sounding them at a radar target you cannot see is wrong and can mislead.
  • Forgetting to change the signal. The ship stops for a pilot or drifts at the anchorage, but the automatic controller is still sounding one prolonged blast.
  • Thinking the signal gives you priority. Hearing (— · ·) does not create a stand-on or give-way relationship. Rules 12 to 18 apply only to vessels in sight of one another. In fog, Rule 19 applies to everyone.
  • Treating 'every 2 minutes' as exact. The requirement is 'not more than'. Sounding more often is allowed.
  • Switching off as soon as your own visibility improves while still close to a fog bank.
  • Assuming small craft will be heard. Vessels under 12 m may use only 'some other efficient sound signal', which can be easily missed.
  • Muffling the bridge. Closed doors and loud bridge noise mean signals go unheard. Under Rule 5, a proper look-out includes hearing.

Oral exam points

Examiners usually play or describe a signal and ask what it is, or describe a vessel and ask what she sounds. Be ready to answer quickly:

  • 'Define restricted visibility.' Give the Rule 3 definition, and say that the rule applies in or near such an area, by day or night.
  • 'You hear — · · · . What is it?' A manned vessel being towed (or the last vessel of a tow). It is usually heard straight after the towing vessel's signal.
  • 'A trawler at anchor in fog: what does she sound?' (— · ·), not the bell.
  • 'A 120 m ship aground: describe the full signal.'
    • Forward: three strokes on the bell, rapid ringing for about 5 seconds, three strokes.
    • Aft: immediately afterwards, the gong sounded rapidly for about 5 seconds.
    • Repeat at intervals of not more than one minute.
    • An appropriate whistle signal may be added.
  • 'Interval underway versus at anchor?' Not more than 2 minutes underway. Not more than 1 minute at anchor or aground.
  • 'Sailing vessel in fog?' (— · ·), the same group as NUC, RAM, CBD, fishing and towing.
  • 'You hear a fog signal forward of the beam. What do you do?' Answer with Rule 19, not with Rule 35. Examiners like candidates who link the rules together.

A final tip: if you cannot remember whether a category is in the (— · ·) group, think about it this way. That group covers vessels that cannot easily get out of your way, plus the sailing vessel.

Key points
  • Rule 35 applies in or near restricted visibility, by day or night.
  • Power-driven vessel making way: one prolonged blast; stopped and making no way: two prolonged; both at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • NUC, RAM, CBD, sailing, fishing and towing or pushing vessels sound one prolonged and two short, whether making way or not.
  • A manned towed vessel sounds one prolonged and three short, ideally straight after the tug's signal.
  • At anchor, ring the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than 1 minute; vessels of 100 m or more add the gong aft.
  • Aground: add three distinct bell strokes before and after the rapid ringing.
  • Rule 34 manoeuvring signals are not used when vessels are not in sight of one another; Rule 19 governs your actions in fog.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fog signal for a power-driven vessel underway?

If she is making way through the water, she sounds one prolonged blast (four to six seconds) at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. If she is underway but stopped and making no way, she sounds two prolonged blasts about 2 seconds apart, at the same maximum interval.

At what visibility do I have to start sounding fog signals?

COLREG does not set a distance. Signals are required in or near an area of restricted visibility as defined in Rule 3. Many companies and masters set a visibility figure in the SMS or standing orders, and these differ, so follow your ship's instructions as well as the rule.

What does one prolonged and two short blasts mean in fog?

It is the signal of a vessel not under command, restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, constrained by her draught, a sailing vessel, a vessel engaged in fishing, or a vessel towing or pushing. It is also used by a fishing vessel at anchor and by a RAM vessel at anchor carrying out her work. It does not tell you which of these she is.

How often is the anchor bell rung in restricted visibility?

At intervals of not more than one minute, ringing rapidly for about 5 seconds. A vessel of 100 m or more rings the bell forward and then immediately sounds the gong aft. She may also sound one short, one prolonged and one short blast to warn an approaching vessel.

Should I sound one short blast when altering to starboard in fog?

No. Rule 34 manoeuvring signals apply only to vessels in sight of one another. In restricted visibility you continue the appropriate Rule 35 signal and manoeuvre in accordance with Rule 19.

Test yourself

In fog you hear one prolonged blast followed by three short blasts. What is it?

One prolonged plus three short is the signal of a manned towed vessel, or the last vessel of a tow. COLREG Rule 35(e).

A tug towing a barge stops in the water in fog. What signal should the tug sound?

A vessel towing sounds one prolonged and two short instead of the power-driven signals, whether or not she is making way. COLREG Rule 35(c).

What is the maximum interval for the anchor bell signal in restricted visibility?

Vessels at anchor ring the bell at intervals of not more than one minute. Signals underway are at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. COLREG Rule 35(g).

What does a vessel engaged in fishing at anchor sound in fog?

A fishing vessel at anchor, and a RAM vessel at anchor carrying out her work, sound one prolonged and two short instead of the anchor signals. COLREG Rule 35(d).

Your vessel is stopped and drifting over the ground on a current, with no way through the water. Which signal applies?

The test is way through the water, not movement over the ground. A power-driven vessel underway making no way sounds two prolonged blasts. COLREG Rule 35(b).
