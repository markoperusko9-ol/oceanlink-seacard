COLREG Rule 33 – Equipment for Sound Signals

Quick answer

Rule 33 sets the sound-signalling equipment a vessel must carry by length. A whistle is required from 12 metres, a bell is added from 20 metres, and a gong is added from 100 metres, all to the Annex III specifications. A vessel under 12 metres need not carry these appliances, but it must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal.

What Rule 33 actually says

Rule 33 sits in Part D (Sound and light signals). It is purely an equipment rule. It tells you what must be fitted. It does not tell you when to sound it, which is covered by Rules 34 and 35.

Paraphrased, it requires the following.

Paragraph (a): vessels of 12 metres or more - A vessel of 12 m or more in length must be provided with a whistle. - A vessel of 20 m or more must also have a bell. - A vessel of 100 m or more must also have a gong. The gong's tone and sound must not be confusable with the bell. - The whistle, bell and gong must comply with Annex III. - The bell, the gong or both may be replaced by other equipment with the same sound characteristics. This is usually an electronic system. However, it must always be possible to sound the prescribed signals manually.

Paragraph (b): vessels under 12 metres A vessel under 12 m does not have to carry the appliances in (a). If she does not, she must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal.

Length Whistle Bell Gong
Under 12 m Not obliged, but needs an efficient alternative Not obliged Not obliged
12 m to under 20 m Required Not required Not required
20 m to under 100 m Required Required Not required
100 m and over Required Required Required

Rule 32 defines "whistle" as any appliance that can produce the prescribed short and prolonged blasts and that complies with Annex III. A whistle is therefore defined by its performance, not by whether it is driven by air, steam or electricity.

Annex III in brief: what makes a compliant whistle and bell

Rule 33 points you to Annex III for the technical standard. You will not be asked to recite every figure, but examiners expect you to know the pattern. Bigger ships need lower-pitched, louder whistles that carry further.

Whistle: fundamental frequency and indicative range

Vessel length Fundamental frequency 1/3-octave band level at 1 m (min) Audibility range (for information)
200 m or more 70–200 Hz 143 dB 2 nautical miles
75 m to under 200 m 130–350 Hz 138 dB 1.5 nautical miles
20 m to under 75 m 250–700 Hz 130 dB 1 nautical mile
Under 20 m 250–700 Hz 120 dB (180–450 Hz band; lower levels apply at higher frequency bands) 0.5 nautical mile

The audibility ranges are given for information only. They assume still air and average background noise at the listening post. In wind, rain or a noisy wheelhouse the real range can be much shorter.

Other whistle points from Annex III - Directional whistles have limits on how much the sound may drop off away from the forward axis. - A directional whistle used as the only whistle must point its maximum intensity straight ahead. - The whistle should be mounted as high as practicable, to reduce interception of the sound by obstructions. - It should also be sited to limit the noise level at the ship's own listening posts. - If whistles are fitted more than 100 m apart, they must be arranged so they are not sounded simultaneously. - A combined whistle system is used where obstructions would create a sound shadow. Its whistles are within 100 m of each other, sound together, and are treated as one whistle.

Bell and gong - Each must give a sound pressure level of at least 110 dB at 1 m. - Each must be made of corrosion-resistant material and give a clear tone. - For vessels of 20 m or more, the bell mouth diameter must be at least 300 mm. - The striker mass must be at least 3% of the bell's mass. - A power-driven striker is recommended where practicable, to give a constant force, but manual operation must remain possible.

If you are unsure whether a particular installation complies, check the ship's certificates and equipment documentation. Approval is a matter for the flag Administration.

Applying it on the bridge

Rule 33 compliance is mostly settled at build and survey stage. The watchkeeper's job is to make sure the equipment works when needed and that everyone knows how to use it.

Before departure and arrival Most company SMS checklists include a whistle test. Usually both the manual control and the automatic fog-signal timer are tested. Follow your own checklist, because the exact requirement is set by the company, not by Rule 33.

Know every control point Find out where the whistle can be operated from:

  • the wheelhouse console
  • the bridge wings
  • any manual lanyard or back-up push

In restricted visibility you may be at the wing, not the console.

Fog-signal timer - Check that the automatic programmer gives the correct signal for your situation. - Check that you can override it manually at once, for example to give a Rule 34 manoeuvring signal.

Bell and gong - On a typical ship the bell is forward and the gong is aft. - If an electronic system replaces them, find out how to sound them manually if the electronics fail. The manual capability is mandatory. - Confirm that the forecastle bell is free to swing and the striker is in place before anchoring.

Cold weather and icing - Where whistle heating is fitted, confirm it is on. - Check air whistles for water in the line.

Defects - Report any fault to the master immediately and record it in the deck log. - Inform the company through the SMS. - Absence or serious deterioration of sound-signalling appliances is listed among grounds that may lead to detention under PSC procedures.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: 18 m harbour workboat at anchor in fog - Under Rule 33 she must carry a whistle (12 m or more) but no bell (under 20 m). - Under Rule 35, a vessel of 12 m to under 20 m is not obliged to give the bell signals for anchored or aground vessels. - If she does not give them, she must make some other efficient sound signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. - The skipper might use the whistle or a portable bell to meet this.

Scenario 2: 180 m bulk carrier at anchor in restricted visibility - She carries a whistle, a bell and a gong. - Under Rule 35 she: - rings the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds in the fore part, at intervals of not more than one minute - sounds the gong rapidly in the after part immediately after the bell - She may also sound one short, one prolonged and one short blast on the whistle to warn an approaching vessel. - If the bell and gong are electronic and the system trips, someone must be able to sound them manually.

Scenario 3: 95 m general cargo ship - She needs a whistle and a bell, but not a gong. - At anchor in fog she rings the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than one minute; there is no gong signal. - A common exam trap is to give her a gong because she is a big ship. The threshold is 100 m.

Scenario 4: whistle fails in a busy strait 1. Inform the master. 2. Consider attempting an immediate repair. 3. Log the failure and report it to the company. 4. Consider informing VTS, so that other traffic knows you cannot give whistle signals. 5. Use the Rule 34 light signal to supplement manoeuvring intentions where fitted. It supplements whistle signals; it does not replace them. 6. Do not sail from the next port until the defect is rectified, or until the flag Administration has agreed how it will be handled.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up thresholds with lights rules. For sound equipment the key lengths are 12 m, 20 m and 100 m. The 50 m figure belongs to the lights rules.
  • Thinking under-12 m vessels can stay silent. They are only excused the specified appliances. They still need an efficient sound signal.
  • Treating electronic bells and gongs as a full substitute. They are allowed, but only if the prescribed signals can always be sounded manually.
  • Reading the Annex III audibility ranges as guaranteed. They are indicative, under ideal conditions. Never assume you have been heard.
  • Confusing equipment with use. Rule 33 says what to carry. Rule 34 covers manoeuvring and warning signals, Rule 35 covers restricted visibility signals, and Rule 36 covers signals to attract attention.
  • Assuming the gong is just a second bell. It must have a tone that cannot be confused with the bell. That is how others tell a long anchored vessel from a short one.

Oral exam: likely questions and model answers

Q: What sound signalling equipment must a 120 m vessel carry? A whistle, a bell and a gong, all complying with Annex III. The gong's tone must be distinguishable from the bell.

Q: Can the bell be replaced by a loudspeaker system? Yes, by equipment with the same sound characteristics, provided the prescribed signals can always be sounded manually.

Q: What does a 10 m launch need? She is not obliged to carry a whistle or bell. However, she must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal.

Q: Why do larger ships have lower-pitched whistles? Lower frequencies carry further. Annex III also links pitch to size, so a deep note warns other vessels that a large ship is nearby.

Q: Where would you find the technical specification for the whistle? In COLREG Annex III. It covers frequency, sound pressure level, directional properties and positioning.

Q: Your whistle is inoperative at sea. What do you do? - Inform the master and log the failure. - Attempt repair and report to the company. - Increase vigilance, and consider informing VTS and nearby traffic. - Use the manoeuvring light as a supplement where fitted. - Arrange repair before departure from the next port, liaising with the flag Administration as needed.

Key points
  • Whistle required from 12 m, bell added from 20 m, gong added from 100 m.
  • All three must meet the specifications in COLREG Annex III.
  • Bell or gong may be replaced by equivalent equipment, but manual sounding must always be possible.
  • Under 12 m: no prescribed appliances required, but an efficient means of sound signalling is mandatory.
  • Annex III audibility ranges are for information only; real range in wind and noise can be far less.
  • Rule 33 covers equipment only; when to sound it is set by Rules 34, 35 and 36.
  • An inoperative whistle, bell or gong is a serious deficiency: log it, report it and get it fixed.

Frequently asked questions

At what length does a ship need a bell under COLREG?

A bell is required for vessels of 20 metres or more, in addition to the whistle. Vessels of 12 m to under 20 m need only a whistle. Under Rule 35 they may use another efficient sound signal instead of the anchored or aground bell signals.

Which ships must carry a gong?

Vessels of 100 metres or more must carry a gong as well as a whistle and bell. The gong's tone must not be confusable with the bell. At anchor in restricted visibility it is sounded in the after part, immediately after the bell signal forward.

Can an electronic system replace the ship's bell and gong?

Yes. Rule 33 allows the bell or gong, or both, to be replaced by equipment with the same sound characteristics. The condition is that the prescribed signals can always be sounded manually. Know where that manual facility is before you need it.

Does a small boat under 12 metres need a whistle?

It is not obliged to carry the whistle and bell described in Rule 33(a). However, it must be provided with some other means of making an efficient sound signal. National rules for small craft may add further requirements, so check the local regulations.

How far should a ship's whistle be heard?

Annex III gives indicative ranges by length: 2 nautical miles for 200 m or more, down to 0.5 nautical mile for vessels under 20 m. These figures are for information only and assume still air and normal background noise. In practice you should never assume another vessel has heard you.

Test yourself

What is the minimum length at which a vessel must carry a bell in addition to a whistle?

Rule 33(a) requires a bell for vessels of 20 m or more. 12 m triggers the whistle requirement and 100 m triggers the gong. COLREG Rule 33(a).

Which vessels are required by Rule 33 to carry a gong?

The gong is required only for vessels of 100 m or more, in addition to the whistle and bell. COLREG Rule 33(a).

Under what condition may a vessel's bell be replaced by an electronic sound system?

Rule 33(a) permits replacement equipment with the same sound characteristics, provided manual sounding of the prescribed signals is always possible. COLREG Rule 33(a).

What does Rule 33 require of a vessel under 12 m that does not carry a whistle and bell?

Vessels under 12 m are excused the prescribed appliances, but not from having an efficient means of sound signalling. COLREG Rule 33(b).

What fundamental frequency range does Annex III specify for the whistle of a vessel 200 m or more in length?

Larger vessels have lower-pitched whistles: 70–200 Hz for 200 m or more. 130–350 Hz is for 75–200 m and 250–700 Hz for under 75 m. COLREG Annex III, Section 1.
