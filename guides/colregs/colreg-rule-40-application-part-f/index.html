COLREG Rule 40 – Application (Part F): What It Means for Seafarers

Quick answer

Rule 40 requires every Contracting Party (a government, not a ship) to use the IMO Instruments Implementation Code (III Code) when carrying out its obligations under COLREG. It places no direct duty on the master or officer of the watch. It is the reason flag states are held to account for how well they implement the collision regulations you work under.

What Rule 40 actually says

Rule 40 is one sentence long. In paraphrase, every Contracting Party must use the provisions of the Code for Implementation when it carries out its obligations and responsibilities under the Convention.

Three terms do the work:

  • Contracting Party: a state that has accepted COLREG 1972. It does not mean a ship, a company or an officer.
  • Code for Implementation: defined in Rule 39 as the IMO Instruments Implementation Code (III Code), adopted by IMO Resolution A.1070(28).
  • Obligations and responsibilities: what the state must do under COLREG. That covers giving the Rules effect in national law, surveying and enforcing compliance on its ships, deciding on equivalents and exemptions where the Rules allow it, and dealing with violations and casualties.

In plain terms, Rule 40 tells governments that they cannot implement COLREG however they like. They must follow a common IMO standard, and Rule 41 then audits them against it.

Where Part F fits and why it was added

COLREG is arranged in Parts:

Part Rules Subject
A 1–3 General
B 4–19 Steering and sailing
C 20–31 Lights and shapes
D 32–37 Sound and light signals
E 38 Exemptions
F 39–41 Verification of compliance

Part F was added by IMO Resolution A.1085(28) and entered into force on 1 January 2016. That date coincides with the IMO Member State Audit Scheme (IMSAS) becoming mandatory.

The background is that IMO had run a voluntary audit scheme for member states for some years. Audits kept finding that some administrations had ratified conventions without the laws, staff or procedures to enforce them. IMO's answer was to make audits compulsory. The same kind of 'verification of compliance' provisions went into the other main conventions at about the same time, for example SOLAS Chapter XIII and STCW Regulation I/16. Part F is COLREG's share of that package.

Within Part F the three Rules divide the work:

  • Rule 39 defines audit, audit scheme, Code for Implementation and audit standard.
  • Rule 40 obliges states to use the III Code.
  • Rule 41 makes every Contracting Party subject to periodic IMO audits and requires it to act on the findings.

What the III Code expects of a state

The III Code is not a navigation document. It sets out how a government should run its maritime administration. It is arranged in four parts: common areas, then flag state, coastal state and port state responsibilities.

The broad themes include:

  • Having a strategy and national legislation that give full effect to the mandatory IMO instruments, COLREG among them.
  • Having enough qualified staff, and controlling any work delegated to recognised organisations, such as classification societies carrying out statutory surveys.
  • Enforcing requirements on ships under its flag and acting on deficiencies and violations.
  • Investigating marine casualties and learning from them.
  • Communicating required information to IMO, and reviewing and improving its own performance.

For COLREG this means checking a number of things. The flag must actually require your lights, shapes and sound appliances to comply. Someone must check them. Decisions such as 'closest possible compliance' for vessels of special construction must be made properly. A collision involving one of its ships must be investigated and followed up.

How it reaches the bridge

As OOW you never 'apply' Rule 40 in the way you apply Rule 15 or Rule 19. Your conduct obligations come from Parts A to D. Rule 40 shapes the system around you:

  • Statutory surveys: navigation lights, shapes and sound-signalling appliances are normally checked during statutory surveys carried out by or on behalf of the flag. Well-run flag oversight makes these checks meaningful rather than a tick-box exercise.
  • Rule 1(e) decisions: where a vessel of special construction or purpose cannot fully comply with the lights, shapes or sound-signal provisions, the Government concerned determines the closest possible compliance. The III Code expects such decisions to be made and recorded properly. (Rule 38 transitional exemptions are set by the Rule itself and are now largely historical.)
  • Enforcement after an incident: if you are involved in a close-quarters situation or collision, the flag state's investigation and any proceedings are part of how it meets its COLREG obligations.
  • National rules: where a state uses Rule 1(b) to make special rules for its harbours or inland waters, those rules are made by an appropriate authority under that state, and the state remains responsible for its COLREG implementation as a whole.

The practical message for officers is this. Rule 40 does not change how you keep a lookout or take avoiding action. It is the reason someone checks that the state behind your certificate and your flag is doing its part.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: oral exam trap The examiner asks: 'Under Rule 40, what must you do as officer of the watch?'

  • The correct answer is nothing directly. Rule 40 applies to Contracting Parties.
  • Then explain the link. The state must implement COLREG in line with the III Code. Your duties come from Parts A to D and national law, and they are enforced by the flag.

Scenario 2: unusual vessel lights A new offshore construction vessel cannot place its masthead lights exactly as Annex I requires because of its crane arrangement.

  • Under Rule 1(e), the government concerned decides what closest possible compliance looks like.
  • Rule 40 means that decision should be taken within a properly run administration, consistent with the III Code. It should not be a quiet informal fix.
  • On board, the OOW should know the vessel's actual light arrangement and why it differs. The documentation should be available if questioned.

Scenario 3: collision investigation Your ship is in a collision in coastal waters.

  • The flag state, and possibly the coastal state, will look into it.
  • A state applying the III Code is expected to investigate casualties and act on the lessons learned. That can lead to new requirements for your fleet.
  • Your part is to preserve evidence, including the VDR, logbooks and ECDIS/radar records, and to cooperate. The same applies whatever Part F says.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking Part F changed the conduct rules. It did not. No steering, lighting or sound-signal requirement was altered by Rules 39–41.
  • Thinking the ship is audited under Rule 41. IMSAS audits governments. Your ship and company are audited under the ISM and ISPS Codes, and inspected by flag and port state control. Those are separate processes.
  • Confusing the III Code with the ISM Code. The III Code is for administrations. The ISM Code is for companies and ships.
  • Confusing Rule 40 with Rule 1. Rule 1 tells you which waters and vessels the Rules apply to. Rule 40 tells governments how to carry out their own obligations.
  • Using an old textbook. Older books stop the Rules at Rule 38 (before the Annexes). If your study material has no Part F, it is out of date. Check the amendments in force with your administration's current syllabus.
  • Calling the audit voluntary. It was voluntary before 2016. Rule 41 makes it mandatory for Contracting Parties.

Oral exam points

Examiners usually test Part F quickly. They want clear, accurate answers rather than detail. Be ready to state:

  1. Part F is titled verification of compliance and covers Rules 39, 40 and 41.
  2. It was added by Resolution A.1085(28) and entered into force on 1 January 2016.
  3. Rule 40 requires Contracting Parties to use the III Code (Resolution A.1070(28)) in carrying out their COLREG obligations.
  4. Rule 41 makes Contracting Parties subject to periodic IMO audits under the Member State Audit Scheme.
  5. None of Part F imposes duties on the master or OOW, and none changes Parts A to E.
  6. The link to the ship is indirect: legislation, surveys, exemptions and equivalents, enforcement and casualty investigation by the flag.

Some examiners and administrations give Part F little weight. Others expect you to name the III Code and the 2016 date. Preparation should follow your own administration's syllabus. A short, correct answer always beats a long guess.

Key points
  • Rule 40 applies to Contracting Parties (states), not to ships or watchkeepers.
  • It requires states to use the III Code when carrying out their COLREG obligations.
  • Part F (Rules 39–41) entered into force on 1 January 2016 via Resolution A.1085(28).
  • Part F changes no steering, lighting or sound-signal requirement.
  • Rule 41 makes IMO audits of Contracting Parties mandatory; the ship itself is not audited under Part F.
  • The effect on board is indirect: surveys, Rule 1(e) decisions, enforcement and casualty investigation by the flag.
  • A textbook whose Rules stop at Rule 38 is out of date.

Frequently asked questions

Does COLREG Rule 40 apply to the officer of the watch?

No. Rule 40 is addressed to Contracting Parties, meaning governments. The OOW's obligations come from Parts A to D of COLREG and national law. Rule 40 governs how the state implements and enforces those rules.

What is the Code for Implementation mentioned in Rule 40?

It is the IMO Instruments Implementation Code, known as the III Code, adopted by Resolution A.1070(28) and defined in Rule 39. It sets out how flag, coastal and port states should give effect to and enforce mandatory IMO instruments.

When did Part F come into COLREG?

Part F was added by Resolution A.1085(28) and entered into force on 1 January 2016. That is the date the IMO Member State Audit Scheme became mandatory.

Is my ship audited under COLREG Rule 41?

No. Rule 41 audits apply to Contracting Parties. Your ship and company are checked through statutory surveys, ISM and ISPS audits, and flag and port state inspections, which are separate processes.

Will Part F be asked in my CoC oral exam?

It depends on your administration and examiner. Some ask only whether you know Part F exists and who it applies to. Others expect you to name the III Code and the 2016 entry into force, so check your administration's current syllabus.

Which instrument is the 'Code for Implementation' referred to in Rule 40?

What effect did Part F have on the steering and sailing rules?
