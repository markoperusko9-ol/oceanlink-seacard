COLREG Rule 15 – Crossing situation: what it says and how to apply it on the bridge

Quick answer

Rule 15 says that when two power-driven vessels in sight of one another are crossing with risk of collision, the vessel that has the other on her own starboard side must keep out of the way. If circumstances allow, she must also avoid crossing ahead of the other vessel. The other vessel is the stand-on vessel under Rule 17, and the give-way vessel's action is governed by Rules 8 and 16.

What Rule 15 actually says (paraphrased)

Rule 15 is one of the shortest rules in the book, and one of the most argued about in collision cases. In plain words:

  • It applies to two power-driven vessels.
  • They are crossing in a way that involves risk of collision.
  • The vessel that has the other on her own starboard side must keep out of the way.
  • If the circumstances of the case admit, she must avoid crossing ahead of the other vessel.

That is the whole rule. Everything else you need to apply it comes from the rules around it:

Rule What it adds to a crossing situation
Rule 7 How you decide whether risk of collision exists
Rule 8 What any avoiding action must look like
Rule 11 Section II rules, including Rule 15, apply only to vessels in sight of one another
Rule 13 Overtaking overrides crossing
Rule 14 Head-on is a separate situation, not a crossing
Rule 16 Give-way vessel takes early and substantial action to keep well clear
Rule 17 What the stand-on vessel may do and must do
Rule 18 Responsibilities between different types of vessel
Rule 34 Manoeuvring and doubt signals when in sight

A useful memory aid that many cadets learn is "starboard side, I give way". If you can see the other ship's port sidelight (red) at night from a crossing aspect, you are normally the give-way vessel.

When Rule 15 applies and when it does not

Before you decide who gives way, confirm that Rule 15 is the right rule. Run through these checks in order:

  1. Are both vessels in sight of one another? Rule 15 sits in Section II, which only applies when vessels are in sight visually. A radar-only contact in fog is dealt with under Rule 19, and there is no stand-on or give-way vessel in that case.
  2. Are both power-driven vessels? If the other vessel is not under command, restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, engaged in fishing or under sail, Rule 18 decides responsibilities, not Rule 15. A power-driven vessel keeps out of the way of all four. Also consider a vessel constrained by her draught, which others should avoid impeding if circumstances admit.
  3. Is it actually an overtaking situation? A vessel coming up from more than 22.5° abaft the other's beam is overtaking. Rule 13 makes the overtaking vessel keep out of the way regardless of any later change in bearing. If in doubt, assume you are overtaking.
  4. Is it head-on? If the courses are reciprocal or nearly so, apply Rule 14. If you are in doubt whether it is head-on or crossing, assume head-on and both alter to starboard.
  5. Narrow channel or TSS? Rule 9 and Rule 10 add duties not to impede certain vessels, for example a vessel that can safely navigate only within a narrow channel. These operate alongside Rule 15. They do not simply replace it, and the interaction is a favourite exam topic.
  6. Local rules. Rule 1(b) allows special rules for harbours, rivers and inland waters made by an appropriate authority. These can modify crossing duties in specific waters. Check the pilot books and local regulations, because they vary by area.

Only when the situation is two power-driven vessels, in sight, crossing, with risk of collision, does Rule 15 decide the matter.

Applying it on watch: give-way and stand-on step by step

Establish risk of collision (Rule 7). Take a series of compass bearings and use radar plotting or ARPA. If the bearing is steady, or not changing appreciably, risk exists. Remember that risk may still exist with a bearing change. This is especially true with a very large vessel, a tow, or a vessel at close range. Don't make assumptions from scanty information, including scanty radar information. AIS is useful to support your assessment but must not replace it.

If you are the give-way vessel (target on your starboard side):

  • Act early. Don't wait to see if the other ship does something.
  • Make the action substantial and readily apparent to the other ship, visually or on her radar. Avoid a series of small alterations. The rules give no figure in degrees, so the test is whether the other OOW will clearly notice it.
  • The normal action is a bold alteration to starboard to pass astern of her, often bringing her onto your port bow.
  • Reducing speed, or stopping or reversing, is also a proper action (Rule 8(e)) and may be preferable in confined waters or with traffic to starboard. Remember that a speed change is less readily apparent than a bold course alteration.
  • Check that your action does not create a close-quarters situation with a third vessel (Rule 8).
  • In sight, sound the Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signal: one short blast for starboard, three short blasts for astern propulsion. Where it helps, supplement it with light signals.
  • Keep checking until the other vessel is finally past and clear. Do not resume course too early.

If you are the stand-on vessel (target on your port side):

  • Keep your course and speed (Rule 17(a)(i)). Unpredictable stand-on action is a classic cause of collisions.
  • Keep plotting. If you are in doubt whether the give-way vessel is taking sufficient action, sound at least five short and rapid blasts (Rule 34(d)).
  • You may take action to avoid collision by your own manoeuvre alone once it becomes apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action (Rule 17(a)(ii)).
  • Under Rule 17(c), a power-driven stand-on vessel in a crossing situation shall, if the circumstances of the case admit, not alter course to port for a vessel on her own port side. That would turn you towards her at the very moment she may finally alter to starboard.
  • If you get so close that the give-way vessel cannot avoid collision on her own, you must take the action that will best aid avoiding collision (Rule 17(b)).
  • Rule 17(d): nothing in Rule 17 relieves the give-way vessel of her obligation to keep out of the way. Any action by the stand-on vessel does not transfer that duty.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Clear give-way, open sea, daylight. Own ship is steering 000°. A container ship is sighted about 4 points on the starboard bow at 8 nm. Her bearing is steady and the ARPA shows a small CPA. You can see her port side.

  • She is on your starboard side, and you are both power-driven and in sight. Rule 15 applies and you give way.
  • Call the Master if the standing orders require it. Check astern and to starboard for other traffic.
  • Make a bold alteration to starboard so that she ends up on your port bow and you pass astern of her. Sound one short blast.
  • Monitor until she is past and clear, then return to your track.

Scenario 2: Stand-on vessel with a non-responsive give-way vessel. A bulk carrier is on your port bow, crossing, with a steady bearing. Range is closing and she shows no sign of action.

  • You stand on. Keep your course and speed and keep plotting.
  • When it becomes apparent she is not acting, sound five or more short rapid blasts. Use the Aldis lamp as well if useful.
  • If she still does nothing, act under Rule 17(a)(ii). A substantial alteration to starboard or a reduction of speed is the usual choice, depending on geometry and sea room. Do not alter to port towards her.
  • If collision can no longer be avoided by her action alone, take whatever action best aids avoiding collision, including a full astern or a round turn.

Scenario 3: Fine on the starboard bow at night. You see a masthead light nearly in line with another, plus both sidelights, almost dead ahead and slightly to starboard.

  • This is head-on (Rule 14), not crossing. Both vessels alter to starboard.
  • If you are in doubt whether it is head-on or a fine crossing, treat it as head-on.

Scenario 4: Restricted visibility, radar contact on the starboard bow. In fog, a contact is closing on the starboard bow with a steady bearing.

  • Rule 15 does not apply, because you are not in sight of one another. Apply Rule 19.
  • If close-quarters is developing, take avoiding action in ample time. Avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken.
  • Proceed at safe speed with fog signals sounding. Neither vessel is stand-on.

Common mistakes that end up in collision reports

  • Small, late alterations. A 5° or 10° nudge is often invisible to the other OOW. It also leaves the stand-on vessel guessing, and that is exactly when she may make her own move.
  • Altering to port as give-way vessel. Turning to port for a vessel on your starboard bow can put you across her bow. It can also meet her head-on if she takes late starboard action under Rule 17. The rule's "avoid crossing ahead" wording is there for a reason.
  • Stand-on vessel moving too early or turning to port. Many collisions involve both ships acting at once in opposite directions.
  • VHF "agreements". Arranging a port-to-port or starboard-to-starboard passing by radio wastes time. It risks talking to the wrong ship, and it can lead both vessels away from what the rules require. Many administrations and accident investigation bodies have warned against it. Follow your company SMS on VHF use.
  • Relying on AIS alone. AIS heading, course and speed data can lag or be wrong, and not every vessel transmits. Use visual bearings and radar plotting.
  • Using Rule 15 in fog. There is no stand-on vessel under Rule 19.
  • Ignoring Rule 18. A power-driven vessel crossing from your port side that turns out to be restricted in her ability to manoeuvre is not one you can expect to give way.
  • Resuming course too soon. Return only when the other vessel is finally past and clear, as Rule 8 requires.

Oral exam: what examiners ask and good answers

Q: State Rule 15 in your own words. Two power-driven vessels are crossing with risk of collision. The one with the other on her starboard side keeps out of the way and, if circumstances admit, avoids crossing ahead.

Q: Why "avoid crossing ahead"? Crossing ahead brings you closer to the other ship's path, and you have to rely on her holding course and speed. Passing astern keeps the distance opening and is predictable to the stand-on vessel.

Q: Does Rule 15 apply in restricted visibility? Only when the vessels are in sight of one another. For radar-only contacts, Rule 19 applies, and there is no give-way or stand-on vessel.

Q: You are stand-on. When can you act, and when must you act? You may act once it becomes apparent the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action (Rule 17(a)(ii)). You should also sound the doubt signal under Rule 34(d). You must act when collision cannot be avoided by her action alone (Rule 17(b)). In a crossing, you shall not alter course to port for a vessel on your own port side if the circumstances of the case admit (Rule 17(c)).

Q: A vessel approaches from 30° abaft your starboard beam and gradually draws forward. Who gives way? She is overtaking (Rule 13), so she keeps out of the way until finally past and clear, even if the geometry later looks like a crossing.

Q: Crossing situation in a narrow channel? Rule 9 says a vessel must not cross a narrow channel or fairway if that impedes a vessel that can safely navigate only within it. Examiners want to hear that Rules 9 and 15 must be read together, and that Rule 2 good seamanship governs the final decision.

Q: How do you tell the other ship what you are doing? When in sight, use Rule 34(a) sound signals: one short blast for starboard, two for port, three for astern propulsion. Light signals may supplement them.

Key points
  • Rule 15 applies only to two power-driven vessels in sight of one another, crossing with risk of collision.
  • The vessel with the other on her own starboard side gives way and, if circumstances admit, avoids crossing ahead.
  • Give-way action must be early, substantial and readily apparent, normally a bold starboard alteration or a speed reduction.
  • The stand-on vessel keeps course and speed, sounds the doubt signal if in doubt (Rule 34(d)), and may act once it is apparent the give-way vessel is not acting, avoiding a turn to port for a vessel on her port side.
  • Overtaking (Rule 13), head-on (Rule 14) and Rule 18 responsibilities take priority over a simple crossing.
  • In restricted visibility without visual contact, use Rule 19, which has no stand-on vessel.
  • Confirm risk with compass bearings and radar plotting, not AIS alone, and keep watching until finally past and clear.

Frequently asked questions

Who is the give-way vessel in a crossing situation?

The power-driven vessel that has the other on her own starboard side. She must keep out of the way and, if circumstances admit, avoid crossing ahead of the other vessel. The vessel on the other side is the stand-on vessel.

Does Rule 15 apply to sailing vessels or fishing vessels?

No. Rule 15 applies only between two power-driven vessels. Where a sailing vessel, a vessel engaged in fishing, a vessel not under command or a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre is involved, Rule 18 sets out who keeps out of the way. Between two sailing vessels, Rule 12 applies.

Can the give-way vessel alter course to port in a crossing situation?

The rules do not prohibit it outright, but it is generally poor practice. It can put you across the other ship's bow, and it can conflict with a late starboard turn by the stand-on vessel. A bold alteration to starboard to pass astern, or a reduction of speed, is the normal and expected action.

What should the stand-on vessel do if the give-way vessel doesn't act?

Keep course and speed while plotting, and sound at least five short and rapid blasts if in doubt whether she is taking sufficient action. Once it is apparent she is not taking appropriate action, you may manoeuvre, but if circumstances admit you shall not alter to port for a vessel on your port side. If collision cannot be avoided by her action alone, you must take the best action to avoid it.

Is it acceptable to agree a crossing over VHF instead of following Rule 15?

VHF can confirm intentions, but agreements that depart from the rules are risky. The causes include misidentification, delay and misunderstanding, and accident investigators have repeatedly linked them to collisions. Company SMS procedures often set limits on VHF use for collision avoidance, so follow your company's instructions and the rules.

Test yourself

Two power-driven vessels are crossing in clear visibility with a steady bearing. Which vessel must keep out of the way?

Under Rule 15 the vessel with the other on her own starboard side keeps out of the way. Size and who sighted first are irrelevant (COLREG Rule 15).

In dense fog you detect a radar contact on your starboard bow on a steady bearing, not in sight. Which rule governs your action?

Section II rules, including Rule 15, apply only to vessels in sight of one another. Restricted visibility without visual contact falls under Rule 19 (COLREG Rules 11 and 19).

As stand-on vessel in a crossing, you decide to act under Rule 17(a)(ii). What does Rule 17(c) require?

Rule 17(c) says a power-driven stand-on vessel in a crossing situation shall, if the circumstances of the case admit, not alter course to port for a vessel on her own port side (COLREG Rule 17(c)).

A power-driven vessel approaches from 30° abaft your starboard beam, then draws forward until she appears to be crossing. Who keeps out of the way?

A vessel coming up from more than 22.5° abaft the beam is overtaking. A later change in bearing does not make her a crossing vessel (COLREG Rule 13).

Which give-way action best meets Rules 8, 15 and 16 for a crossing vessel fine on your starboard bow?

The give-way vessel must take early and substantial action that is readily apparent, and should avoid crossing ahead. Small successive alterations should be avoided (COLREG Rules 8, 15 and 16).
