COLREG Rule 19 – Conduct of Vessels in Restricted Visibility

Quick answer

Rule 19 applies to vessels that are not in sight of one another when navigating in or near an area of restricted visibility. There is no stand-on or give-way vessel. Every vessel must proceed at a safe speed, have engines ready, and act in ample time on radar information, avoiding certain alterations of course. If you hear a fog signal forward of the beam, or cannot avoid a close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam, you must slow to bare steerage way unless you have established that no risk of collision exists.

What Rule 19 says, in plain terms

Rule 19 is the only rule in Section III of Part B, and it stands on its own. Here is a paraphrase of its five paragraphs:

  • 19(a) – Application. It applies to vessels not in sight of one another when navigating in or near an area of restricted visibility.
  • 19(b) – Speed and engines. Every vessel must proceed at a safe speed adapted to the conditions of restricted visibility. A power-driven vessel must have her engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  • 19(c) – Section I still applies. When complying with Rules 4–10 (look-out, safe speed, risk of collision, action to avoid collision, narrow channels, TSS), you must give due regard to the restricted visibility.
  • 19(d) – Radar-only detection. A vessel that detects another by radar alone must decide whether a close-quarters situation is developing or risk of collision exists. If either applies, she must take avoiding action in ample time. If that action is an alteration of course, she should avoid two things so far as possible:
    • altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam, unless that vessel is being overtaken
    • altering towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam
  • 19(e) – Fog signal heard ahead. In two cases you must reduce to the minimum speed at which you can keep your course. The first is when you hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam. The second is when you cannot avoid a close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam. The only exception is where you have determined that no risk of collision exists. If necessary you must take all way off. In any event you must navigate with extreme caution until the danger is over.

"Restricted visibility" is defined in Rule 3. It covers visibility reduced by fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms, sandstorms or similar causes. COLREG gives no distance figure. The visibility at which the Master must be called, or at which fog routines begin, is set by the company SMS and the Master's standing orders. Read yours on joining.

The key idea: no stand-on, no give-way

This is the point most candidates get wrong. The Section II rules apply only to vessels in sight of one another. That includes Rule 13 (overtaking), Rule 14 (head-on), Rule 15 (crossing), Rules 16 and 17 (give-way and stand-on) and Rule 18 (responsibilities between vessels). Rule 11 says so explicitly.

So when two ships are closing in fog and see each other only as radar echoes:

  • Neither is stand-on. Both are obliged to take avoiding action if risk of collision or a close-quarters situation exists.
  • The "crossing from starboard" logic does not apply. A target on your port bow does not have to keep clear of you.
  • Rule 18's hierarchy does not apply between vessels not in sight. A fishing vessel or a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre that you detect only on radar does not automatically get priority under Rule 18. Good seamanship (Rule 2) still means giving such vessels a wide berth where you can identify them.
  • Once the vessels are in sight, Rule 19 stops applying between them and Section II takes over, even if the fog persists around you.

"In sight" means visually seen. A radar echo or an AIS target does not count. Being able to hear the other vessel's whistle does not count either.

Applying Rule 19 on the bridge: when visibility drops

The rule itself is short. In practice it pulls in Rules 5, 6, 7, 8 and 35, plus the watchkeeping principles in STCW Code Section A-VIII/2. A typical sequence when visibility starts closing in looks like this. Your company SMS will have its own checklist, so follow it.

  1. Inform the Master as required by standing orders.
  2. Engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. Inform the engine room. On UMS ships, whether the engine room must be manned is decided by the SMS and the Master.
  3. Safe speed. Reduce speed as appropriate. Rule 6(b) lists the radar-related factors to consider, such as small targets, sea and rain clutter, and range-scale limitations.
  4. Sound signals under Rule 35 (see below).
  5. Look-out. Post an additional look-out, by sight and hearing, positioned where ship's noise will not drown out fog signals. Consider a look-out on the forecastle where the SMS calls for it.
  6. Radar. Use both radars on suitable ranges. Use long range for early warning and short range for small targets. Plot systematically with ARPA.
  7. Steering. Change to hand steering in good time, with a helmsman on the wheel.
  8. Navigation lights on, plus any other precautions the SMS lists, such as closing watertight doors.
  9. Log the time, visibility, actions taken and engine status.

Rule 35 signals at intervals of not more than 2 minutes include:

Situation Signal
Power-driven, making way One prolonged
Power-driven, underway but stopped Two prolonged
NUC, RAM, CBD, sailing, fishing, towing/pushing One prolonged + two short

Anchored and aground vessels use bell (and gong) signals. These are covered on the Rule 35 page.

Worked scenarios

In each case you are a power-driven vessel in fog, the target is detected by radar only, and ARPA shows a small and steady CPA. What counts as a minimum acceptable CPA is set in the Master's standing orders. COLREG does not set a figure.

Scenario 1 – Target on the starboard bow, crossing. Relative bearing about 040°, 8 nm, CPA near zero. - Avoid altering to port, because the target is forward of the beam and you are not overtaking it. - Make a bold alteration to starboard, enough to bring the target well onto the port side and pass astern of it. Alternatively reduce speed substantially, or do both. - Check by continued plotting that the action is having the intended effect (Rule 8(d)).

Scenario 2 – Target fine on the port bow, nearly reciprocal. - A substantial starboard alteration is correct. A port alteration is the one to avoid. - Remember the other ship may not have plotted you yet. Do not assume she will turn too.

Scenario 3 – Target abaft your starboard beam, closing (she is overtaking you). - Rule 13 does not apply because you are not in sight, so you are not stand-on. - Avoid altering towards her, which here means to starboard. An alteration to port, away from her, is consistent with 19(d)(ii). - Keep plotting. A small CPA from astern can still develop into a close-quarters situation.

Scenario 4 – Fog signal heard ahead, nothing on radar. You hear one prolonged blast, apparently on the starboard bow, with no echo visible. It is possibly a small wooden craft. - You cannot have determined that no risk of collision exists, because you have no information. - Under 19(e), reduce to minimum steerage speed, take all way off if necessary, and proceed with extreme caution until the danger is over. - Direction finding by ear is unreliable in fog. Do not alter course towards where you "think" the sound came from.

Common mistakes

  • Treating radar targets as crossing or overtaking under Rules 13–15. Those rules only apply in sight.
  • Small successive alterations. Rule 8(b) requires changes large enough to be readily apparent to a vessel observing by radar. A 10° nudge may never show on the other ship's ARPA.
  • Sounding manoeuvring signals in fog. One short blast for "altering to starboard" belongs to Rule 34(a), which applies only to vessels in sight. In fog it only adds confusion.
  • Acting on scanty radar information. Rule 7(c) warns against assumptions based on it, such as a single bearing or a target that has only just been acquired.
  • Relying on VHF to agree a manoeuvre. Misidentification on VHF has contributed to collisions. Any agreement made on VHF does not override the Rules.
  • Forgetting "near" in 19(a). You may be in clear weather beside a fog bank. A vessel inside it may not see you, so Rule 19 still applies to you.
  • Engines on standby but speed unchanged. Having engines ready is not the same as proceeding at a safe speed. Rule 19(b) requires both.

Oral exam: what the examiner is looking for

"You are OOW and visibility drops. What do you do?" Run through the checklist above in a logical order: Master, engines, speed, signals, look-outs, radar, hand steering, lights, log. Mention that the visibility threshold comes from standing orders, not COLREG.

"Target on your starboard bow by radar only. Which way do you alter?" Starboard, boldly, with continued plotting. Then state the reason: Rule 19(d)(i) says avoid port for a vessel forward of the beam other than one being overtaken.

"Who is stand-on in fog?" Nobody. Cite Rule 11 for why Section II does not apply.

"When can you ignore 19(e)?" Only where you have determined that risk of collision does not exist. That needs systematic radar plotting, not a hunch.

"Vessel overtaking you from your port quarter by radar. Which way should you not alter?" Towards her, which here means to port.

"Fog clears and you sight the other vessel. What now?" Section II applies from that moment. Re-assess who is give-way and stand-on, and use Rule 34 signals as appropriate.

Examiners pick up quickly on candidates who quote "stand-on" in a restricted-visibility answer. Avoid the phrase entirely unless you are explaining why it does not apply.

Key points
  • Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another in or near restricted visibility.
  • There is no stand-on or give-way vessel; every vessel must act if risk exists.
  • Proceed at safe speed with engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  • By radar only: avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam (unless overtaking it) and avoid altering towards a vessel abeam or abaft.
  • Fog signal heard forward of the beam: slow to minimum steerage speed unless no risk has been determined.
  • Manoeuvring signals under Rule 34 are for vessels in sight; in fog use Rule 35 signals.
  • COLREG sets no visibility distance; company SMS and standing orders do.

Frequently asked questions

Does the crossing rule apply in fog?

Not between vessels that cannot see each other. Rule 15 is in Section II, which Rule 11 limits to vessels in sight of one another. Two ships detected only by radar are both under Rule 19 and both must take avoiding action if risk of collision exists.

At what visibility does Rule 19 start to apply?

COLREG gives no distance. It defines restricted visibility by its causes, such as fog, mist, snow, heavy rain and sandstorms. Rule 19 applies whenever vessels are not in sight in or near such conditions. The visibility at which you call the Master and start fog routines is set by your company SMS and the Master's standing orders.

Why should I avoid altering to port for a radar target ahead?

If the other vessel makes the common choice of altering to starboard, a port alteration by you can cancel out her action and cause a collision. Rule 19(d) therefore says to avoid it so far as possible for a vessel forward of the beam, except one you are overtaking.

Do I sound one short blast when altering course in fog?

No, not for a vessel you cannot see. Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals apply to vessels in sight of one another. In restricted visibility you sound the Rule 35 fog signals appropriate to your status.

If I hear a fog signal ahead but radar shows nothing, must I slow down?

Yes. Rule 19(e) requires you to reduce to the minimum speed at which you can hold course unless you have determined that no risk of collision exists. With nothing on radar you cannot have determined that. Take all way off if necessary and proceed with extreme caution.

Test yourself

In fog, you detect by radar alone a vessel on your starboard bow with risk of collision. Which alteration should you avoid so far as possible?

For a vessel forward of the beam (other than one being overtaken), an alteration to port should be avoided so far as possible. COLREG Rule 19(d)(i).

To which vessels does Rule 19 apply?

Rule 19(a) applies to vessels not in sight of one another in or near restricted visibility. COLREG sets no distance figure. COLREG Rule 19(a) and Rule 3.

Two power-driven vessels are closing in thick fog, each detecting the other only by radar. Which is the stand-on vessel?

Section II rules, including stand-on/give-way, apply only to vessels in sight. COLREG Rule 11 and Rule 19(d).

You hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam and have not determined whether risk of collision exists. What must you do?

Unless no risk has been determined, you must reduce to minimum steerage speed and navigate with extreme caution. COLREG Rule 19(e).

In restricted visibility, a vessel detected by radar alone is overtaking you from abaft your starboard beam with a small CPA. Which action should you avoid so far as possible?

For a vessel abeam or abaft the beam, an alteration towards her should be avoided so far as possible. Rule 13 does not apply because you are not in sight. COLREG Rule 19(d)(ii) and Rule 11.
