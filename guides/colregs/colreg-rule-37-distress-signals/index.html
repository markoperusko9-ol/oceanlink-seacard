COLREG Rule 37 – Distress signals

Quick answer

Rule 37 is one sentence long. A vessel in distress that needs assistance must use or show the signals listed in Annex IV, and Annex IV bans those signals for any other purpose. For the watchkeeper, the rule matters most in two cases: recognising a distress signal from another vessel and acting on it at once, and making or cancelling a distress alert from your own ship correctly.

What Rule 37 actually says

Rule 37 is the last rule in Part D of COLREG (Sound and Light Signals, Rules 32–37). In plain terms, it says that a vessel in distress and requiring assistance shall use or exhibit the signals described in Annex IV.

The full content sits in Annex IV. Three points matter for exams and for real life:

  • The trigger is distress and need of assistance. Distress means grave and imminent danger to the vessel or persons on board. A machinery breakdown in open water with no immediate danger is normally an urgency situation (PAN PAN), not distress (MAYDAY).
  • The signals can be used together or separately. A vessel can fire red parachute flares, send a DSC alert and show orange smoke all at once. There is no required order.
  • Annex IV also has a prohibition. No vessel may use these signals for any purpose other than distress. Nor may it use any other signal that could be confused with them.

Rule 37 does not by itself oblige anyone to go and help. That duty comes from SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 33, and from long-standing maritime law. COLREG only defines the language of distress.

The Annex IV signals at a glance

Annex IV lists the following signals (paraphrased). Learn them in groups, not as a random list.

Group Signal
Sound A gun or other explosive signal fired at intervals of about a minute
Sound Continuous sounding of any fog-signalling apparatus
Pyrotechnic Rockets or shells throwing red stars, fired one at a time at short intervals
Pyrotechnic Rocket parachute flare or hand flare showing a red light
Pyrotechnic Orange smoke signal
Visual Flames on the vessel (for example a burning tar or oil barrel)
Visual Square flag with a ball, or anything resembling a ball, above or below it
Visual International Code Signal of distress, N over C
Visual Arms outstretched to each side, slowly and repeatedly raised and lowered
Any method SOS (···–––···) in Morse by any signalling method
Radiotelephony The spoken word "MAYDAY"
Radio DSC distress alert on VHF channel 70, or on the MF/HF distress frequencies (2187.5, 4207.5, 6312, 8414.5, 12577 or 16804.5 kHz)
Satellite Ship-to-shore distress alert from the ship's Inmarsat or other mobile satellite service ship earth station
Beacons Signals from emergency position-indicating radio beacons (EPIRBs)
Other radio Approved signals from radiocommunication systems, including survival craft radar transponders

Annex IV also points to the International Code of Signals and IAMSAR Manual Volume III. It mentions two aids that help aircraft find you:

  • an orange canvas with a black square and circle (or another suitable symbol)
  • a dye marker

The colour that matters is red for lights and stars and orange for smoke. White and green pyrotechnics are not distress signals under Annex IV.

Signals that are not distress, and the prohibition

Many mistakes come from confusing distress with other signals. Keep these separate:

  • Rule 34(d): at least five short, rapid blasts. This is the doubt or warning signal between vessels in sight of each other. It is not distress.
  • Rule 36: signals to attract attention. A vessel may use light or sound to attract attention, provided the signal cannot be mistaken for any signal authorised elsewhere in the rules.
  • White flares. Some vessels carry them to warn of collision danger. They are not in Annex IV.
  • PAN PAN and SECURITE. These are urgency and safety messages, not distress.

Annex IV bans two things:

  • using any of its signals for anything other than distress and need of assistance
  • using other signals that could be confused with them

In practice, the following are all breaches:

  • firing an out-of-date red flare to celebrate New Year
  • sounding the whistle continuously as a joke in port
  • testing an EPIRB in a way that transmits a live alert

National authorities may also penalise false distress signals under their own law. The penalties differ by country.

On the bridge: when you see or hear a distress signal

Under SOLAS V/33, a master who receives information that persons are in distress at sea must proceed with all speed to help, if able to do so. The master informs the persons in distress or the search and rescue service where possible. If the ship cannot assist, or the master judges it unreasonable or unnecessary, the reason must be recorded. Your company SMS will set out the exact reporting chain. A typical OOW sequence is:

  1. Note the time, bearing and type of signal immediately. For flares, take a compass bearing and estimate the elevation. Put an EBL on the radar and mark ECDIS or the chart.
  2. Call the master. Distress is always a master's call.
  3. Try to identify and contact the vessel. Check radar, AIS and visual. Listen on VHF channel 16, and check that your DSC is not showing an alert you missed.
  4. Report to the nearest RCC or coast station. Give your own position, the bearing and estimated range of the signal, and what you saw. Do not assume someone else has reported it.
  5. Prepare to assist.
    • Plot an intercept course.
    • Bring the engine to readiness.
    • Post extra lookouts.
    • Prepare the rescue boat, scrambling nets, heaving lines and lifebuoys as directed.
  6. Follow IAMSAR Volume III. It covers on-scene coordination and search patterns, and the RCC may designate an on-scene coordinator.
  7. Log everything in the deck log and GMDSS log: times, positions, communications and actions.

SOLAS V/29 requires an illustrated table of life-saving signals to be readily available to the OOW. Know where it is on your bridge.

Worked scenarios

1. Red stars at night, nothing on radar.

You are on the 0000–0400 watch in the open ocean. A lookout reports a red light rising and falling slowly fine on the starboard bow. There are no radar or AIS targets.

  • Treat it as a rocket parachute flare.
  • Take a bearing and call the master.
  • Report to the RCC with your position and the bearing.
  • Expect a small craft or a liferaft, which may be a poor radar target.
  • If a second flare follows, take a fresh bearing. A cross-bearing from another ship can help the RCC fix the position.

2. Restricted visibility, continuous fog signal.

In fog you hear a whistle sounding without a break, not the prolonged blasts of Rule 35.

  • A continuous sound from fog-signalling apparatus is a distress signal.
  • Call the master and report.
  • Approach at a safe speed under Rule 6 and Rule 19, using radar.
  • The distress does not suspend the steering and sailing rules.

3. Small fishing boat by day, crew waving arms.

A fisherman slowly raises and lowers outstretched arms. It may be distress or simply a wave. Treat it as distress until you prove otherwise:

  • Try VHF.
  • Reduce speed and close to a safe distance.
  • Inform the master.

In high-risk piracy areas, follow your company's BMP-based guidance. Do not ignore a genuine distress because of suspicion alone. Report it to the RCC either way.

4. DSC distress alert received on channel 70.

In coastal waters, a VHF DSC distress alert from another ship normally gets its acknowledgement from a coast station. Your actions are:

  • Listen on channel 16.
  • Inform the master and plot the position.
  • Acknowledge by voice if appropriate.

Only relay or acknowledge by DSC in line with current GMDSS procedures and the master's instructions. Unnecessary DSC relays add to the false-alert problem.

Your own ship: making and cancelling a distress alert

Sending a distress alert is the master's decision. Under SOLAS III/6, ships carry rocket parachute flares stowed on or near the navigating bridge. Use whatever signals will reach help fastest. A practical order on a GMDSS ship is:

  1. DSC alert or satellite alert
  2. A MAYDAY voice call on channel 16 (and the MF frequency if appropriate)
  3. Pyrotechnics when other vessels or aircraft are close enough to see them

Cancelling a false alert must happen immediately. The usual method after an inadvertent VHF DSC alert is:

  1. Reset the DSC equipment immediately. Do not switch it off, because you need it for the voice cancellation.
  2. Make a voice broadcast on channel 16 to all stations. Give the ship's name, call sign, MMSI and position, and say that you are cancelling the distress alert sent at a stated time.
  3. Inform the RCC directly.

For satellite and MF/HF alerts, follow the equipment instructions and the GMDSS procedures in your company SMS.

An inadvertent EPIRB activation should be reported to the nearest RCC straight away. Never just switch the beacon off and say nothing. Log the incident.

Common mistakes and oral-exam points

Examiners like Rule 37 because it tests recognition, not memorised text. Expect questions such as:

  • "What does Rule 37 say?" A vessel in distress and requiring assistance shall use or exhibit the Annex IV signals. Then list as many signals as you can, grouped by type.
  • "Is a green star rocket a distress signal?" No. Red stars, red parachute flares and red hand flares are.
  • "What's the difference between N over C and a square flag with a ball?"
    • N over C is the International Code Signal of distress, flown as two flags.
    • The square flag with a ball is a separate improvised signal.
    • Do not get NC upside down: N is uppermost.
  • "Who must respond?"
    • SOLAS V/33 places the obligation on the master.
    • The OOW's job is to recognise the signal, call the master, report and record.
  • "Five short blasts — distress?" No. That is Rule 34(d), the doubt or warning signal.
  • "Do local rules change this?"
    • Under Rule 1(b), harbour or inland rules may differ.
    • Some inland regimes add extra distress signals.
    • Always check local regulations and sailing directions.

Common real-life mistakes:

  • assuming another ship has already reported a flare
  • failing to take a bearing at the moment of sighting
  • using MAYDAY when PAN PAN was appropriate
  • delaying the cancellation of a false alert out of embarrassment

The last one causes real search and rescue costs and can mask a genuine case.

Key points
  • Rule 37: a vessel in distress needing assistance shall use or exhibit the Annex IV signals.
  • Annex IV signals may be used together or separately; red is the pyrotechnic colour for lights and stars, orange for smoke.
  • Continuous sounding of a fog-signal is distress; five short blasts (Rule 34(d)) is not.
  • Using distress signals for any other purpose, or using look-alike signals, is prohibited.
  • The duty to assist comes from SOLAS V/33, not from COLREG.
  • On sighting a distress signal: take a bearing, call the master, report to the RCC, prepare to assist and log it.
  • Cancel any false distress alert at once by voice and inform the RCC.

Frequently asked questions

What are the distress signals under COLREG Annex IV?

They include: a gun fired at about one-minute intervals; continuous fog-signal sounding; red star rockets; SOS; MAYDAY; NC flags; a square flag with a ball; flames on the vessel; red parachute or hand flares; orange smoke; and slowly raising and lowering outstretched arms. Radio and satellite signals are also included: DSC alerts, satellite alerts, EPIRB signals, and approved radiocommunication signals such as survival craft radar transponders.

Is MAYDAY the same as PAN PAN?

No. MAYDAY is a distress call, used for grave and imminent danger that needs immediate assistance, and it appears in Annex IV. PAN PAN is an urgency signal for a serious situation that does not amount to distress. Examples include a disabled vessel in no immediate danger or a medical case.

What should the OOW do on seeing a red flare at night?

Note the time and take a bearing immediately. Call the master, try to identify the source by radar, AIS and VHF, and report to the nearest RCC or coast station. Then prepare to assist as the master directs and record everything in the log.

Can I fire expired flares for practice or celebration?

Not as a casual act. Annex IV prohibits using distress signals for anything other than distress. Disposal and training firing should follow your company procedures and the requirements of the local or national authority, which vary between countries.

How do I cancel a false DSC distress alert?

Reset the equipment immediately; do not switch it off, because you need it for the voice cancellation. Then broadcast a cancellation by voice on the associated frequency (channel 16 for VHF) to all stations, giving the ship's name, call sign, MMSI and position, and the time of the false alert. Inform the RCC directly and log the incident.

Test yourself

Which of these is NOT a distress signal under COLREG Annex IV?

Annex IV specifies rockets or shells throwing red stars; green stars are not a distress signal. Reference: COLREG Annex IV, para 1.

What does COLREG Rule 37 require?

Rule 37 only defines how distress is signalled; the duty to assist comes from SOLAS V/33, and Annex IV signals may be used together or separately. Reference: COLREG Rule 37 and Annex IV.

In restricted visibility you hear a whistle sounding continuously without a break. What does this indicate?

Continuous sounding of any fog-signalling apparatus is a distress signal; the Rule 35 signals are intermittent blasts or bell and gong patterns. Reference: COLREG Annex IV, para 1.

On which VHF channel is a DSC distress alert listed in Annex IV transmitted?

Channel 70 is the VHF DSC channel; channel 16 is used for voice distress traffic that follows the alert. Reference: COLREG Annex IV, para 1.

A crew member fires an out-of-date red parachute flare at midnight on New Year's Eve. Which statement is correct?

Annex IV prohibits using any of its signals except to indicate distress and need of assistance, whatever the flare's expiry date. Reference: COLREG Annex IV, para 2.
