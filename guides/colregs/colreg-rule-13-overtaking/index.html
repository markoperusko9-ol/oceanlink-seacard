COLREG Rule 13 – Overtaking: What It Means on the Bridge

Quick answer

Under Rule 13, any vessel overtaking another must keep out of the way of the vessel being overtaken. This applies whatever the type of either vessel. You are overtaking when you come up from more than 22.5° abaft the other vessel's beam, which at night means you would see only her sternlight. If in doubt, assume you are overtaking, and the duty stays with you until you are finally past and clear, even if the bearing later changes.

What Rule 13 says, in plain terms

Rule 13 sits in Part B, Section II of the COLREG. That section covers the conduct of vessels in sight of one another. The rule has four parts.

  • 13(a) – Who keeps clear. A vessel overtaking any other must keep out of the way of the vessel being overtaken. This overrides everything else in Part B, Sections I and II. It does not matter whether you are bigger, faster, sailing, fishing or anything else.
  • 13(b) – What counts as overtaking. You are overtaking if you come up with another vessel from a direction more than 22.5° abaft her beam. At night, from that position, you would see her sternlight but neither of her sidelights.
  • 13(c) – Doubt. If you are unsure whether you are overtaking, you must assume you are and act on that basis.
  • 13(d) – No changing roles. A later change in bearing between the two vessels does not turn the overtaking vessel into a crossing vessel. Nor does it relieve her of the duty to keep clear until she is finally past and clear.

The overtaken vessel is the stand-on vessel. Her conduct is governed by Rule 17. The overtaking vessel is the give-way vessel and must follow Rule 16: take early and substantial action to keep well clear.

The 22.5° geometry and why it matches the lights

The overtaking sector is tied to the arcs of the navigation lights in Rule 21.

Light Arc Coverage
Sidelights 112.5° each From right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on each side
Sternlight 135° 67.5° either side of right aft

The two arcs meet at 22.5° abaft the beam. Measured from the overtaken vessel's head, that is a relative bearing of 112.5° on the starboard side and 247.5° on the port side.

  • If you are coming up with her from within her sternlight arc, you are overtaking.
  • If you are clearly forward of that boundary and steadily see a sidelight, you are not overtaking. Rule 14, 15, 12 or 18 may apply, depending on the situation and the vessel types.
  • If there is any doubt, Rule 13(c) applies and you are overtaking.

Night: you see a white sternlight and no red or green. That is the classic picture.

Day: you have no lights to go on, so judge the other vessel's aspect. Ask yourself whether you are looking at her stern and quarter, or at her side. Combine that with radar or ARPA heading and relative bearing.

The practical problem comes near the boundary. Sidelight screening is not a perfect knife-edge, and both vessels yaw. You may see the sternlight with a sidelight flickering in and out. That is exactly the situation 13(c) was written for.

How a watchkeeper applies Rule 13, step by step

When you are coming up on a vessel ahead:

  1. Establish risk of collision early (Rule 7). Take a series of compass bearings and check the ARPA CPA/TCPA. A slow closing speed can hide a steady bearing for a long time.
  2. Decide your status. Check her aspect, her lights and the relative bearing from her heading. If there is any doubt, you are the overtaking vessel.
  3. Plan the pass. Choose a side with sea room. Consider traffic ahead of both of you, any TSS or channel limits, and depth.
  4. Act early and substantially (Rule 16). In open water, a clear course alteration is normally best. A small change of a few degrees may not be apparent to the other ship.
  5. Keep a generous passing distance. In shallow or confined water, allow for interaction and suction between hulls. Interaction forces increase with speed and with decreasing separation and depth. A small speed difference also prolongs the time spent alongside. See the guides on squat and shallow-water effect.
  6. Monitor throughout. You remain the give-way vessel until you are finally past and clear. Being abeam is not enough.
  7. Do not cut across her bow when finishing. Resume your course only when you are well ahead and there will be no new close-quarters situation.

When you are being overtaken:

  • Keep your course and speed as Rule 17(a)(i) requires. Unpredictable alterations are what make overtaking dangerous.
  • Keep watch astern. It is easy to neglect a slow-closing target on the quarter.
  • If the overtaking vessel is clearly not taking appropriate action, you may act to avoid collision by your own manoeuvre (Rule 17(a)(ii)). Sound at least five short and rapid blasts if in doubt of her intentions (Rule 34(d)).
  • If collision cannot be avoided by her action alone, you must take the action that will best help avoid it (Rule 17(b)).

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Open-sea overtaking. You are on a container ship making 20 knots. A bulk carrier at 12 knots is fine on your port bow, about 6 miles ahead, steady bearing, CPA near zero. You see only her sternlight.

  • You are overtaking and must keep clear.
  • With sea room to starboard, a bold alteration to starboard that opens the CPA to a safe distance is a typical choice. Your company SMS or Master's standing orders may set a minimum CPA.
  • Hold the offset until she is well astern. Then come back to course gradually.

Scenario 2: The boundary case at night. You are roughly 2 points abaft the other vessel's starboard beam. From your bridge you see her sternlight, and now and then a glimpse of green.

  • Rule 13(c) applies: assume you are overtaking and keep clear.
  • Do not treat her as a crossing vessel on your port side and expect her to give way. That is precisely the error the rule prevents.

Scenario 3: The bearing changes during the pass. You started overtaking from her port quarter. As you draw up, you come to bear forward of 22.5° abaft her port beam, and you can now see her port sidelight. The geometry starts to look like a crossing.

  • Rule 13(d) says nothing has changed. You stay the give-way vessel until finally past and clear.
  • She stays the stand-on vessel and should keep her course and speed.

Scenario 4: Fishing vessel overtaking a merchant ship. A trawler engaged in fishing is coming up from astern on your power-driven vessel.

  • Rule 18 opens with the words "except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 otherwise require". The overtaking duty therefore overrides the usual pecking order, and the trawler must keep clear.
  • You still keep a proper look-out. Rule 2 still applies if she fails to act.

Scenario 5: Fog, radar target closing from astern. You are in restricted visibility and a radar target is closing from your quarter.

  • Rule 13 does not apply, because Section II only covers vessels in sight of one another. Rule 19 governs.
  • If a close-quarters situation is developing, both vessels must act.
  • Under Rule 19(d)(ii), avoid altering course towards a vessel that is abeam or abaft the beam.

Overtaking in narrow channels and traffic separation schemes

Rule 13 still applies in a narrow channel or fairway. Rule 9(e) adds a procedure for cases where the overtaken vessel has to take action to allow a safe pass. The sound signals in Rule 34(c) are used only when the vessels are in sight of one another:

Meaning Signal
I intend to overtake you on your starboard side Two prolonged, one short
I intend to overtake you on your port side Two prolonged, two short
Agreement by the vessel being overtaken Prolonged, short, prolonged, short
Doubt At least five short and rapid blasts

Agreement from the overtaken vessel does not transfer responsibility. Rule 9(e)(ii) confirms that the overtaking vessel's obligations under Rule 13 remain.

In practice, local regulations and VTS procedures often govern overtaking in particular channels and port approaches. Many restrict or prohibit it in certain stretches. Check the passage plan and the local regulations.

In a traffic lane, Rule 10 does not relieve any vessel of her obligations under other rules. Same-direction traffic in a lane is a common setting for overtaking. Plan a pass that keeps you within the lane where you can, and stay clear of the separation zone.

Common mistakes seen at sea and in exams

  • Thinking the slower ship should move aside. The overtaken vessel stands on. The overtaking vessel does the work.
  • Ending the duty when abeam. The duty lasts until the overtaking vessel is finally past and clear. Many close-quarters incidents happen when an overtaking ship returns to her course too early, across the other vessel's bow.
  • Applying Rule 13 in fog. In restricted visibility with no visual contact, Rule 19 applies instead.
  • Assuming size or type changes the answer. A sailing vessel, fishing vessel or small craft that is overtaking must keep clear too.
  • Passing too close in shallow water. Hydrodynamic interaction can sheer either ship. This is worse when the speed difference is small, which prolongs exposure, when the pass is close and when the under-keel clearance is small.
  • Relying on a VHF "arrangement". VHF may help confirm intentions. It cannot change the rules, and misidentifying the ship you are talking to has contributed to collisions. Many administrations and companies discourage using VHF to agree on collision avoidance.
  • Using Rule 34(c) signals in open water. Those signals belong to narrow channels and fairways under Rule 9(e). In open water, a power-driven vessel uses the Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals when altering course in sight of another vessel.

Oral exam: what the examiner is likely to ask

Q: Define an overtaking vessel. A vessel coming up with another from more than 22.5° abaft her beam. At night she would see only the sternlight. Back this up with the light arcs from Rule 21.

Q: You are not sure if you are overtaking or crossing. What do you do? Assume you are overtaking under Rule 13(c), keep clear, and take early, substantial action under Rule 16.

Q: Does Rule 13 apply in restricted visibility? No. It is in Section II, which applies to vessels in sight of one another (Rule 11). Rule 19 applies instead. Be ready to explain Rule 19(d)(ii).

Q: A vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre is being overtaken by a sailing vessel. Who keeps clear? The sailing vessel, because she is overtaking. Rule 18 is expressly subject to Rule 13.

Q: When does your obligation end? When you are finally past and clear. A change of bearing does not make you a crossing vessel (Rule 13(d)).

Q: What are the stand-on vessel's duties? Keep course and speed. She may act once it is apparent the give-way vessel is not acting appropriately. She must act when collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone (Rule 17).

Expect the examiner to put a model ship or a radar plot in front of you. Your answer should give the status of each vessel, the rule numbers, your intended action and how you will check that it is working.

Key points
  • The overtaking vessel keeps clear of the vessel being overtaken, whatever the type of either vessel.
  • Overtaking means approaching from more than 22.5° abaft the other vessel's beam; at night you see only her sternlight.
  • If in doubt whether you are overtaking, assume you are and act accordingly.
  • A later change of bearing never turns the overtaking vessel into a crossing vessel; the duty lasts until finally past and clear.
  • Rule 13 applies only to vessels in sight of one another; in restricted visibility Rule 19 governs.
  • In narrow channels, Rule 9(e) and the Rule 34(c) sound signals apply, but agreement does not remove the overtaking vessel's duty.
  • Pass with a generous CPA, allow for interaction in shallow water, and never cut across the overtaken vessel's bow.

Frequently asked questions

Who has right of way when overtaking at sea?

The vessel being overtaken is the stand-on vessel. The overtaking vessel must keep out of her way. This applies regardless of size or type, so even a sailing or fishing vessel that is overtaking must keep clear.

What angle defines overtaking in COLREG?

A vessel is overtaking when she comes up from more than 22.5° abaft the other vessel's beam. This matches the boundary between the sidelight arcs and the 135° sternlight arc, so at night the overtaking vessel would see only the sternlight.

Does Rule 13 apply in fog or restricted visibility?

No. Rule 13 is in Part B, Section II, which applies only to vessels in sight of one another. In restricted visibility, Rule 19 applies, and neither vessel is stand-on or give-way. Under Rule 19(d)(ii), avoid altering course towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam.

When does the overtaking vessel's obligation end?

Only when she is finally past and clear of the overtaken vessel. Drawing abeam or ahead is not enough. Rule 13(d) states that a change of bearing during the manoeuvre does not make her a crossing vessel.

Which sound signals are used for overtaking in a narrow channel?

Two prolonged blasts followed by one short means you intend to overtake on her starboard side. Two prolonged followed by two short means her port side. The overtaken vessel signals agreement with prolonged, short, prolonged, short, and sounds at least five short and rapid blasts if in doubt. These signals apply in narrow channels and fairways when the vessels are in sight of one another.

Rule 18 applies except where Rule 13 otherwise requires, so the overtaking fishing vessel keeps clear. Reference: COLREG Rules 13(a) and 18.
