COLREG Rule 14 – Head-on Situation

Quick answer

When two power-driven vessels in sight of one another meet on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses with risk of collision, Rule 14 requires both to alter course to starboard so they pass port to port. Neither vessel stands on. If you are in any doubt whether it is head-on, you must assume it is and act accordingly.

What Rule 14 says, in plain English

Rule 14 has three parts. In paraphrase:

  • 14(a) – the action. Two power-driven vessels meet on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses so as to involve risk of collision. Each alters course to starboard so that they pass port side to port side. Rule 14 sits alongside the other Rules. It applies only between power-driven vessels, so Rule 18 determines responsibilities where one vessel is sailing, fishing, NUC or RAM. The "not impede" duties in Rules 9 and 10 may also apply. Rule 10(a) makes clear that a TSS does not relieve vessels of their steering-rule obligations, so Rule 14 still applies inside a TSS.
  • 14(b) – how to recognise it. You see the other vessel ahead or nearly ahead. At night her masthead lights are in line or nearly in line, and/or you see both her sidelights. By day you see the matching aspect of her hull.
  • 14(c) – the doubt clause. If you are not sure whether a head-on situation exists, you assume that it does and act on that basis.

The point that catches candidates out is that there is no stand-on vessel. In a crossing or overtaking situation one vessel keeps her course and speed. In a head-on situation both vessels have a duty to act, and both act the same way: to starboard.

When Rule 14 applies: the four conditions

Before you call it head-on, run through the conditions. All four must be met.

Condition Where it comes from What it rules out
Both vessels are power-driven Rule 14(a), definitions in Rule 3 Sailing vessels, fishing vessels engaged in fishing, NUC and RAM vessels are dealt with by Rule 18
Vessels are in sight of one another Rule 11 (Section II applies only in sight) A radar-only contact in fog falls under Rule 19
Courses are reciprocal or nearly reciprocal Rule 14(a) and (b) Crossing situations (Rule 15) and overtaking (Rule 13)
Risk of collision exists Rule 14(a), assessed under Rule 7 Vessels already passing well clear

A vessel that is power-driven by build stops being treated as an ordinary power-driven vessel for these purposes when she is engaged in fishing, not under command or restricted in her ability to manoeuvre. That is Rule 18's territory, and her lights or shapes tell you so.

A vessel constrained by her draught remains a power-driven vessel. Other vessels must, if circumstances admit, avoid impeding her safe passage (Rule 18(d)). If risk of collision develops on reciprocal courses, Rule 14 applies.

Rule 2 still sits over everything. Following Rule 14 does not excuse neglect of ordinary seamanship or special circumstances.

Recognising a head-on situation, day and night

At night, look for:

  • Masthead lights in line or nearly in line. On a vessel showing two masthead lights, the forward and after lights appear one above the other.
  • Both sidelights visible. Annex I requires sidelights to cut off within a few degrees outside their sectors. Seeing red and green together therefore means you are very close to her fore-and-aft line.
  • The word "and/or". You do not need both clues. A vessel under 50 m may carry only one masthead light, so there is no "in line" check. Both sidelights alone can be enough.

By day, the equivalent is the aspect. Her bow is pointing at you, both sides of the superstructure look roughly symmetrical, and the masts are in line.

The rule gives no fixed angle for "nearly ahead" or "nearly reciprocal". You will hear figures quoted in classrooms. They are rules of thumb, not part of the regulation. Use the visual aspect, the compass bearing trend and your radar/ARPA heading and CPA data together.

Always confirm with compass bearings. A steady or nearly steady bearing with closing range is your clearest sign of risk of collision under Rule 7. Watch for a slowly changing bearing too, especially with a large vessel or at close range.

Applying Rule 14 on the bridge: step by step

  1. Detect early. Use visual look-out (Rule 5), radar and ARPA. Use AIS only as an aid, because heading and course data can be wrong or lagging.
  2. Classify the target. Is she power-driven, and not showing NUC, RAM or fishing signals? If she shows CBD signals, she is still power-driven, but you must also avoid impeding her. Is she in sight?
  3. Assess risk. Take compass bearings and check ARPA CPA/TCPA. Assume risk exists if you are unsure, as Rule 7 requires.
  4. Decide it is head-on. Check the aspect and lights. If in doubt between head-on and crossing, Rule 14(c) tells you to treat it as head-on.
  5. Check your starboard side. Look for sea room, depth, other traffic and any vessel that your turn will put you in conflict with.
  6. Act early and boldly. Rule 8 wants an alteration large enough to be readily apparent visually and on radar. Avoid a string of small alterations. Make the turn large enough that she clearly sees your aspect change and your red sidelight.
  7. Sound one short blast. Rule 34(a) requires a power-driven vessel underway, in sight of another, to signal an alteration to starboard with one short blast. The light signal is optional.
  8. Monitor. Keep checking the bearing and CPA until she is past and clear, as Rule 8(d) requires. Watch for her own alteration, or her lack of one.
  9. Resume course only when she is finally past and clear. Log the event according to your company SMS.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Textbook head-on, open sea, night. You are on a container ship and see a vessel right ahead at about 8 miles. Her two masthead lights are nearly in line, and both sidelights show as she closes. The compass bearing is steady and ARPA shows a CPA near zero.

  • Both vessels are power-driven and in sight, on nearly reciprocal courses, with risk of collision. This is Rule 14.
  • Your starboard side is clear, so make a bold alteration to starboard in good time and sound one short blast.
  • Expect her to alter to starboard too. Keep watching until she is past and clear.

Scenario 2 – Fine on the starboard bow, showing green. At night, a power-driven vessel is fine on your starboard bow on a nearly reciprocal course, showing her green sidelight. ARPA gives a small CPA, passing starboard to starboard, and the margin feels uncomfortable.

  • If you are genuinely satisfied she will pass well clear, there is no risk of collision and Rule 14 does not bite.
  • If the CPA is marginal, Rule 7(a) says to deem that risk of collision exists. If you are then unsure whether this is head-on (she is nearly ahead on a nearly reciprocal course) rather than crossing, Rule 14(c) says assume it is head-on.
  • A starboard alteration initially turns you towards her. Make it early, at long range, and large enough to put her clearly on your port bow.
  • If it is too late for that to be safe, Rule 8(e) allows you to slacken speed, stop or reverse.
  • A late, small alteration to port is a classic way to collide if she alters to starboard at the same moment.

Scenario 3 – Daytime, vessel ahead showing two cones. She is ahead, on a reciprocal course, with risk of collision. But two cones with points together mean she is engaged in fishing. Rule 14 does not apply. Rule 18 makes you, as a power-driven vessel, keep out of her way.

Scenario 4 – Fog, radar contact fine ahead. You have a contact fine ahead, closing on a reciprocal heading, and she is not in sight. This is Rule 19, not Rule 14.

  • The practical answer is still usually a starboard alteration, because Rule 19(d) tells you to avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam (other than one being overtaken).
  • Do not sound one short blast. Rule 34(a) only applies in sight. Instead sound the restricted visibility signals under Rule 35.

Common mistakes watchkeepers make

  • Treating head-on like a crossing. Holding course and speed because you think you are the stand-on vessel. Nobody stands on in Rule 14.
  • Altering to port. Usually done to "open the CPA" when the other ship is slightly on the port bow. If she follows the rule and turns to starboard, you turn into each other.
  • Small, late alterations. A few degrees is invisible to the other bridge. Rule 8 wants the action to be obvious.
  • Relying on AIS heading or VHF. The Rules contain no provision for agreeing a starboard-to-starboard passing by radio. Identification can be wrong, the language can be misunderstood, and time is lost. Marine accident investigators have repeatedly highlighted VHF-assisted collisions. Follow your company SMS on VHF use, but never let a radio conversation replace compliance with the Rules.
  • Applying Rule 14 in fog. No visual contact means Rule 19.
  • Forgetting Rule 18. A vessel ahead may be showing signals that change your obligations, so check her lights and shapes before deciding which rule applies.
    • NUC, RAM or fishing signals mean Rule 14 does not apply and Rule 18 governs.
    • CBD signals mean you must, if circumstances admit, avoid impeding her safe passage (Rule 18(d)). She remains a power-driven vessel.
  • Not checking the starboard side before turning, especially in coastal waters and near TSS boundaries.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect questions like these, and answer them in the language of the Rules.

  • "Define a head-on situation." Two power-driven vessels, in sight, meeting on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses so as to involve risk of collision. Then give the night and day recognition clues from 14(b).
  • "Who is the give-way vessel?" Both. Each alters to starboard to pass port to port.
  • "You can see only one masthead light and both sidelights. Is that head-on?" It can be. The rule says masthead lights in line and/or both sidelights, and a vessel under 50 m may carry only one masthead light.
  • "You are unsure if it is crossing or head-on. What do you do?" Rule 14(c): assume head-on, alter to starboard, sound one short blast, and keep monitoring.
  • "Does Rule 14 apply in restricted visibility?" Only if the vessels are in sight of each other. Otherwise Rule 19 governs.
  • "What sound signal?" One short blast when altering to starboard in sight (Rule 34(a)). At least five short and rapid blasts if you doubt the other vessel is taking sufficient action (Rule 34(d)).
  • "How does Rule 9 relate?" In a narrow channel you keep as near the starboard limit as is safe and practicable. Meeting traffic then naturally passes port to port, which is consistent with Rule 14.

A good structure for your answers is: rule number, conditions, action, signal. When asked how you know it is head-on, mention confirming risk with compass bearings (Rule 7(d)).

Key points
  • Rule 14 applies only to two power-driven vessels in sight of one another, on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses, with risk of collision.
  • Both vessels alter course to starboard and pass port to port; there is no stand-on vessel.
  • At night look for masthead lights in line and/or both sidelights; by day the corresponding aspect.
  • If in doubt whether it is head-on, assume it is and act accordingly (Rule 14(c)).
  • Alterations must be early and substantial, and in sight you signal one short blast under Rule 34(a).
  • In restricted visibility with no visual contact, Rule 19 governs, not Rule 14.
  • Vessels showing fishing, NUC or RAM signals are dealt with by Rule 18, not Rule 14; a CBD vessel must not be impeded but remains a power-driven vessel.

Frequently asked questions

Who is the stand-on vessel in a head-on situation?

Neither. Rule 14 places the same obligation on both power-driven vessels: each alters course to starboard so they pass port to port. This is the key difference from crossing and overtaking situations, where one vessel stands on.

Does Rule 14 apply in fog or restricted visibility?

Only if the two vessels are in sight of one another, because Rule 14 sits in Section II, which applies to vessels in sight. If you only have a radar contact, Rule 19 applies. Rule 19 tells you, among other things, to avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam other than one being overtaken.

Can two ships agree on VHF to pass starboard to starboard?

The Rules contain no provision allowing vessels to agree by radio to depart from the Rules. Misidentification and misunderstanding on VHF have contributed to collisions, and accident investigation bodies have warned about it repeatedly. Check your company SMS, and never let a VHF agreement replace early, rule-compliant action.

What angle counts as 'nearly ahead' under Rule 14?

The rule does not give a figure. It relies on the visual aspect: masthead lights in line or nearly in line and/or both sidelights seen, or the matching daytime aspect. Back this up with compass bearings and radar/ARPA data, and if you are in doubt, treat it as head-on.

Do I have to sound a whistle signal when I alter in a head-on situation?

Yes, when the vessels are in sight of one another. Rule 34(a) requires a power-driven vessel underway to sound one short blast when altering course to starboard. Rule 34(b) allows a supplementary light signal. If you doubt the other vessel's intentions, sound at least five short and rapid blasts under Rule 34(d).

Test yourself

Two power-driven vessels in sight are meeting on nearly reciprocal courses with risk of collision. What does Rule 14 require?

Rule 14(a) requires each vessel to alter to starboard so they pass port to port; neither stands on. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rule 14(a).

Rule 14 applies between which vessels?

Rule 14 covers only power-driven vessels, and Section II applies only to vessels in sight of one another under Rule 11. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rules 11 and 14.

You cannot decide whether a vessel fine on your bow is crossing or head-on. What should you do?

Rule 14(c) says that where there is doubt, the vessel shall assume a head-on situation exists and act accordingly. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rule 14(c).

In sight of the other vessel, what sound signal accompanies your alteration to starboard in a head-on situation?

Rule 34(a) requires one short blast for 'I am altering my course to starboard'. Two short blasts mean altering to port, and at least five short and rapid blasts signal doubt. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rule 34.

In dense fog you have a radar contact fine ahead on a reciprocal heading, not in sight. Which rule governs your action?

Vessels not in sight of one another in or near restricted visibility are governed by Rule 19, not the Section II steering rules. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rules 11 and 19.
