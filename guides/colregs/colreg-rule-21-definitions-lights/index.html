COLREG Rule 21 – Definitions (lights): arcs, colours and how to use them on watch

Quick answer

Rule 21 defines the six basic lights in the COLREGs by colour and horizontal arc. The masthead light covers 225°, each sidelight 112.5°, the sternlight 135°, the towing light matches the sternlight in yellow, an all-round light covers 360°, and a flashing light shows 120 or more flashes per minute. Because the arcs meet at 22.5° abaft the beam, the lights you can see tell you which part of the other vessel you are looking at.

What Rule 21 actually says (paraphrased)

Rule 21 is a definitions rule. It sits in Part C (Lights and Shapes). It does not tell any vessel what to show. Rules 23 to 31 do that. Rule 21 tells you what each named light is, so that those later rules make sense.

  • Masthead light (21(a)): a white light placed over the fore-and-aft centreline. It shows an unbroken light over an arc of 225°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on either side.
  • Sidelights (21(b)): green on the starboard side and red on the port side. Each shows an unbroken light over 112.5°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on its own side. A vessel of less than 20 m may combine both sidelights in one lantern carried on the fore-and-aft centreline.
  • Sternlight (21(c)): a white light placed as nearly as practicable at the stern. It shows an unbroken light over 135°, that is 67.5° from right aft on each side.
  • Towing light (21(d)): a yellow light with the same characteristics as the sternlight.
  • All-round light (21(e)): an unbroken light over 360°.
  • Flashing light (21(f)): a light flashing at regular intervals at 120 flashes or more per minute.

The arithmetic is the whole point. The masthead light's 225° plus the sternlight's 135° makes 360°. Two sidelights at 112.5° each make up the same 225° forward arc. So every direction around a vessel falls in either the "forward" lights or the "aft" light.

The arcs in degrees and points

Older examiners, and many senior masters, still talk in compass points. One point is 11.25°. It is worth knowing both forms.

The boundary at 22.5° (2 points) abaft the beam is the most important line in this rule. It is the same line Rule 13 uses to define an overtaking vessel. A vessel coming up from more than 22.5° abaft your beam would, at night, see only your sternlight and neither of your sidelights.

Rule 21 defines arcs, not ranges. The minimum visibility of each light depends on vessel length and is set by Rule 22. The technical details, such as colour limits, intensity and how sharply a light must cut off at its sector edges, are in Annex I.

How a watchkeeper uses Rule 21 on the bridge

At night, the lights you see tell you three things about another vessel:

  1. Which side of her you are on. Green means you are on her starboard side. Red means you are on her port side. Both means you are near right ahead of her.
  2. Whether you are forward or abaft her 22.5°-abaft-the-beam line. Masthead and sidelights mean you are forward of it. Sternlight only means you are abaft it.
  3. Her aspect and heading. On vessels of 50 m and over (Rule 23), the second, higher masthead light sits aft of the forward one. The horizontal spacing between the two masthead lights shows how she is heading relative to your line of sight. A heading change shows up as the masthead lights opening or closing before her bearing has moved much.

Rule 21 does not let you judge risk of collision on its own. You still need compass bearings and radar or ARPA, as Rule 7 requires. The lights tell you the geometry. The bearing trend tells you whether that geometry is dangerous.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Two white lights, the after one higher, plus a green light, fine on your port bow. This is a power-driven vessel, probably 50 m or more. You are on her starboard side, forward of her 22.5°-abaft-the-beam line. If her bearing is steady, she is crossing with you on her starboard side. Under Rule 15 she is the give-way vessel and you are the stand-on vessel. Keep watching and apply Rule 17. Her lights alone do not settle this. Check the bearing.

Scenario 2: Two white lights nearly in line, with red and green both visible, right ahead. She is heading almost directly at you. This matches the night description of a head-on situation in Rule 14. Each vessel alters to starboard.

Scenario 3: A single white light ahead, slowly closing, with no sidelights visible. This might be a sternlight, meaning you are overtaking her (Rule 13). It might also be:

  • the all-round anchor light of a small vessel (Rule 30);
  • the all-round white light of a small power-driven vessel (Rule 23);
  • a vessel whose sidelights are not yet within range.

Use radar and AIS to check her speed. If you are coming up on her and in any doubt about whether you are overtaking, Rule 13 says to assume that you are, and keep clear.

Scenario 4: A yellow light vertically above a white light, seen from astern. This is a towing light above a sternlight. A vessel towing astern shows it under Rule 24. Both lights cover the same 135° aft sector, so you will lose both together as you draw forward. Remember the tow lies between you and the towing vessel; look for the tow's own lights (sidelights and sternlight) before you consider passing close.

Scenario 5: A fast yellow all-round flashing light with normal steaming lights. This is an air-cushion vessel operating in the non-displacement mode (Rule 23). It is a Rule 21(f) flashing light at 120 or more flashes per minute. Expect high speed and rapid changes of aspect.

Annex I points that link to Rule 21

Rule 21 gives the nominal arcs. Annex I deals with how real lanterns behave at the edges of those arcs. These are the points worth knowing:

  • Forward cut-off of sidelights. Sidelights must show their minimum intensity in the forward direction. Their intensity must fall to a practical cut-off between 1° and 3° outside the prescribed sector. This limits how far a green light can be seen across the bow on the port side, and vice versa.
  • Tolerances at other sector edges. Annex I allows intensity to reduce near the after limits of the sidelights and the limits of masthead lights and sternlights. Near 22.5° abaft the beam, you may briefly see a sidelight and the sternlight together, or neither clearly. Treat that zone with caution and use bearings.
  • All-round lights and obstructions. All-round lights must be sited so that masts and structures do not obscure them over more than a small angular sector. Anchor lights get some relief on height.
  • Colours. Annex I sets the colour limits for white, red, green and yellow lights. A faded or wrongly filtered lantern may not comply.

On joining a ship, check the arcs of your own lanterns from the deck at the sector edges. Pay particular attention after a lantern change or when deck cargo such as containers may block the sternlight or sidelights.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking the masthead light must be on a mast. The definition only requires it to be over the fore-and-aft centreline. On many vessels it is on a post or the wheelhouse top.
  • Saying sidelights run "from the bow to the beam". They run from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam.
  • Treating the towing light as all-round. It has the sternlight's 135° arc. It is visible only from aft.
  • Confusing the Rule 21(f) flashing light with national inland signals. Rule 21(f) means 120 or more flashes per minute. The yellow "special flashing light" (50–70 per minute) used on pushed barges exists only in some national inland rules, such as the US Inland Rules. It is not part of the international COLREGs.
  • Calling the Rule 34 manoeuvring light a "flashing light". It flashes in step with the whistle signals, about one second per flash. It is not a Rule 21(f) light.
  • Assuming one white light always means overtaking. It can also mean an anchored vessel or a small craft. Confirm with radar and AIS.
  • Forgetting the under-20 m combined lantern. A single red/green lantern on the centreline is legal for vessels under 20 m. Small craft can therefore look "narrower" than you expect.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect rapid-fire definitions, then follow-ups that test whether you understand the geometry. Typical lines:

  • "Define a masthead light." It is a white light over the fore-and-aft centreline, showing an unbroken light over 225°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on either side.
  • "Why 22.5° abaft the beam?" So that the forward lights and the sternlight together cover the full horizon. It is also the same line that defines overtaking in Rule 13.
  • "Add up the arcs for me." 225° plus 135° makes 360°. Two sidelights at 112.5° each make 225°.
  • "What's the towing light?" A yellow light with the same characteristics as the sternlight. It is shown above the sternlight when towing astern.
  • "What is a flashing light?" A light flashing at regular intervals at 120 or more flashes per minute (Rule 21(f)). There is no special flashing light in the international COLREGs; it exists only in some national inland rules.
  • "Who can combine sidelights?" A vessel of less than 20 m, in one lantern on the centreline.
  • "Where do the ranges come from?" Rule 22, by length of vessel. The technical details are in Annex I.

The exact format of the oral differs between administrations. Indian, Philippine, UK and other examiners each set their own style. The definitions themselves are the same everywhere, because the COLREGs are international. Learn them word-perfect in meaning, but answer in your own words.

Key points
  • Masthead light: white, 225°, right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on both sides, over the centreline.
  • Sidelights: green starboard and red port, 112.5° each, right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on their own side.
  • Sternlight: white, 135°, 67.5° either side of right aft; the towing light is yellow with the same characteristics.
  • All-round light covers 360°; a flashing light flashes 120 or more times per minute.
  • Vessels under 20 m may combine both sidelights in one centreline lantern.
  • The 22.5°-abaft-the-beam line links Rule 21 to the overtaking definition in Rule 13.
  • Ranges are in Rule 22 and technical details in Annex I, not in Rule 21.

Frequently asked questions

What is the arc of visibility of a masthead light under COLREG Rule 21?

A masthead light shows an unbroken white light over 225° of the horizon. It shows from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on each side, and it is placed over the fore-and-aft centreline.

Why do sidelights and the masthead light end at 22.5° abaft the beam?

The 225° forward arc and the 135° sternlight arc together cover the full 360°. Any observer therefore sees either the forward lights or the sternlight. The same 22.5°-abaft-the-beam line is used in Rule 13 to define an overtaking vessel.

Is the towing light an all-round light?

No. The towing light is yellow and has the same characteristics as the sternlight: a 135° arc, 67.5° either side of right aft. It is visible only from astern. A vessel towing astern shows it vertically above her sternlight under Rule 24.

What is a flashing light under Rule 21?

A flashing light is a light flashing at regular intervals at 120 or more flashes per minute. An example is the yellow all-round flashing light of an air-cushion vessel in non-displacement mode (Rule 23). The yellow 50–70 per minute special flashing light belongs to some national inland rules, not the international COLREGs.

Does Rule 21 say how far navigation lights must be visible?

No. Rule 21 only defines colour and arc. The minimum ranges of visibility, which depend on the length of the vessel, are in Rule 22. Intensity, colour limits and sector cut-off details are in Annex I.

Test yourself

Over what horizontal arc does a masthead light show an unbroken light?

A masthead light shows from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on both sides, a total of 225°. The other figures are the arcs of a sidelight, a sternlight and an all-round light. (COLREG Rule 21(a))

How is the sector of a sternlight defined?

The sternlight shows an unbroken light over 135°, which is 67.5° from right aft on each side. (COLREG Rule 21(c))

What frequency defines a "flashing light" in Rule 21?

Rule 21(f) defines a flashing light as one flashing at regular intervals at 120 or more flashes per minute. The other options do not match any COLREG definition. (COLREG Rule 21(f))

Which description correctly defines a towing light?

The towing light is yellow and has the same characteristics as the sternlight, a 135° arc showing aft. (COLREG Rule 21(d))

Below what length may a vessel combine her sidelights in one lantern on the fore-and-aft centreline?

Rule 21(b) allows a vessel of less than 20 m to combine sidelights in one lantern carried on the fore-and-aft centreline. (COLREG Rule 21(b))
