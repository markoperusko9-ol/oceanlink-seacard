COLREG Annex III – Technical details of sound signal appliances

Quick answer

Annex III gives the technical specification for whistles, bells and gongs: frequency bands linked to ship length, minimum sound pressure levels, nominal audibility ranges, directional limits, siting, and bell and gong construction. On watch, the key points are that a large ship's whistle is pitched low and is nominally audible to about 2 nautical miles in still air, that real audibility can be far less, and that whistles more than 100 m apart must not be sounded together.

Where Annex III fits in the Rules

Sound signal equipment is covered at three levels, and it helps to keep them separate:

  • Rule 32 defines the signals: a short blast lasts about one second and a prolonged blast four to six seconds.
  • Rule 33 says what equipment a vessel must carry, based on length.
  • Annex III says what that equipment must be capable of: pitch, loudness, directivity, siting and construction.

Rule 33 requirements by length:

Length Equipment required
Less than 12 m Not obliged to carry the appliances below, but must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal
12 m or more Whistle
20 m or more Whistle and bell
100 m or more Whistle, bell and gong (the gong's tone must not be confusable with the bell's)

A bell or gong may be replaced by other equipment with the same sound characteristics, but manual sounding of the prescribed signals must always remain possible. Annex III finishes with a short paragraph stating that construction, performance and installation must satisfy the flag State's appropriate authority. In practice the yard and class or the administration deal with type approval and fitting. Your job is to know what the equipment is supposed to do and to notice when it is not doing it.

Whistles: frequency and why big ships sound deep

Annex III requires the fundamental frequency of a whistle to lie between 70 and 700 Hz. It then narrows the band by ship length so that different sizes of ship sound different.

Ship length Fundamental frequency limits
200 m or more 70–200 Hz
75 m but less than 200 m 130–350 Hz
Less than 75 m 250–700 Hz

The audibility range is set by frequencies that may include the fundamental and/or higher harmonics. These lie between 180 and 700 Hz (±1%) for vessels of 20 m or more. For vessels under 20 m the band is extended to 180–2,100 Hz (±1%).

What this means on watch:

  • A deep, low note in fog usually indicates a large ship. A high, thin note usually indicates something small.
  • The bands overlap. Pitch is a clue, not proof, of size.
  • Annex III does not state a reason, but lower frequencies suffer less atmospheric attenuation, which suits the larger audibility ranges required of big ships.

Sound pressure levels and audibility ranges

Each whistle must reach a minimum sound pressure level. This is measured 1 m from the whistle, in its direction of maximum intensity, in at least one one-third-octave band within the specified frequency range.

Ship length Minimum 1/3-octave band level at 1 m Audibility range (nautical miles)
200 m or more 143 dB 2
75 m but less than 200 m 138 dB 1.5
20 m but less than 75 m 130 dB 1
Less than 20 m 120 dB* 0.5

*For vessels under 20 m the required level depends on the measured frequency. It is 120 dB in the 180–450 Hz band, 115 dB in the 450–800 Hz band and 111 dB in the 800–2,100 Hz band.

The ranges in the table are for information only, and this is the point examiners care about most. The Annex describes them as roughly the range at which the whistle may be heard on its forward axis with 90% probability. That figure assumes still air and average background noise at the listening post. The Annex itself warns that actual range is extremely variable and depends critically on weather. Strong wind or high ambient noise can reduce it a great deal.

The practical consequences:

  • Even a VLCC's whistle is only nominally audible to about 2 nm in ideal conditions. In fog with wind, a closed wheelhouse and the engine running, you may hear nothing until the other vessel is very close.
  • Failing to hear a signal does not mean nothing is there. This is why Rule 19 relies on radar and on what you can detect, not on silence.
  • Hearing a fog signal apparently forward of the beam is a trigger under Rule 19(e). Annex III's nominal ranges are a reminder that the vessel may already be within a mile or two, and possibly much closer.

Directivity, siting, multiple whistles and combined systems

Directional properties. A directional whistle may not fall more than 4 dB below its required on-axis level anywhere within ±45° of the axis in the horizontal plane. In any other horizontal direction it may not fall more than 10 dB below the on-axis level. The aim is that its range in any direction is at least half the range on the forward axis.

Positioning.

  • If a directional whistle is the only whistle on board, it must be installed with maximum intensity directed straight ahead.
  • Whistles should be mounted as high as practicable. This reduces obstruction of the sound and the risk of hearing damage to the crew.
  • The ship's own signal at listening posts must not exceed 110 dB(A), and so far as practicable should not exceed 100 dB(A). This limits, but does not eliminate, the noise exposure of the watch at listening posts during prolonged fog signalling.

More than one whistle. Whistles fitted more than 100 m apart must be arranged so that they are not sounded simultaneously. Rule 34(f) adds that only one of them is then used for manoeuvring and warning signals. Two whistles far apart sounding together would give a confused, smeared signal and could mislead another ship about your position and heading.

Combined whistle systems. Obstructions such as stacked containers, a large funnel or a forward accommodation block can create a zone where a single whistle is heard poorly. In that case Annex III recommends a combined system. Its requirements are:

  • It counts as one whistle for the purposes of the Rules.
  • Its whistles are no more than 100 m apart.
  • They sound simultaneously.
  • Each differs in frequency from the others by at least 10 Hz.

Bell and gong

The bell and gong are covered in a few lines, but they matter at anchor and aground in restricted visibility.

  • Intensity: the bell, gong or equivalent device must give at least 110 dB at 1 m.
  • Material: corrosion-resistant, and designed to give a clear tone.
  • Size: for vessels of 20 m or more, the bell's mouth diameter must be at least 300 mm.
  • Striker: a power-driven striker is recommended where practicable, to give constant force. Manual operation must still be possible. The striker's mass must be at least 3% of the bell's mass.

Link to Rule 35. At anchor in restricted visibility, the bell is rung rapidly for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than one minute. On a vessel of 100 m or more, the bell is rung in the fore part and the gong is sounded immediately afterwards in the after part. A vessel aground adds three separate and distinct strokes on the bell before and after the rapid ringing.

On many ships the bell is an electrically struck unit linked to the whistle control panel. Check during familiarisation that it actually works and that someone knows how to ring it by hand.

Practical checks and worked scenarios

Routine checks. Most company SMS pre-departure and pre-arrival bridge checklists include a whistle test, and many include the automatic fog signal programme. The exact frequency, and whether the test is logged, depends on your company and flag. Follow your SMS rather than any general rule. Things worth noticing:

  • The whistle sounds weak, hoarse or off-pitch. This may be an air or steam supply problem, ice, a diaphragm problem or water in the line, and should be reported.
  • The automatic fog signal timer does not keep the Rule 35 intervals.
  • The manual whistle and bell controls are not available on the bridge wing or at the conning position.
  • The bell striker is seized, or the bell has been painted over and gives a dull tone.

Scenario 1: fog in the anchorage. You are 2/O on a 180 m bulker at anchor in dense fog. You hear a high-pitched prolonged blast, then another two minutes later. Pitch suggests a smaller vessel. The prolonged blast at intervals of not more than two minutes indicates a power-driven vessel making way (Rule 35(a)). The nominal audibility range of a small vessel's whistle is short, so it may already be close. Track it on radar, call the master, keep your anchor bell and gong signals going, and consider the Rule 35(g) optional signal of one short, one prolonged and one short blast to warn an approaching vessel.

Scenario 2: whistles fore and aft. A large container ship has whistles on the foremast and on the funnel, more than 100 m apart. They must not sound together, and only one is used for manoeuvring and warning signals. Know which one is selected on your ship's whistle panel before you need to give five short blasts.

Scenario 3: heavy rain and wind. You are on watch in heavy rain with a fresh gale and poor visibility. Expect hearing range to be far less than the Annex III table suggests. Raise your level of radar vigilance, post a look-out where they can actually hear, and set your speed for the conditions (Rules 5 and 6).

Oral exam: what examiners ask

Candidates are not expected to recite every decibel figure. You should, however, be able to answer the following confidently.

  • Where are the technical details of sound appliances found? In COLREG Annex III. Rule 33 sets the carriage requirements and Rule 32 defines the signals.
  • What is the audibility range of a whistle on a ship of 200 m or more? About 2 nm, for information only. It is highly variable with wind and background noise.
  • Why do large ships have low-pitched whistles? Annex III sets frequency bands by length, which gives a rough indication of size. Annex III does not state a reason, but lower frequencies suffer less atmospheric attenuation, which suits the larger audibility ranges required of big ships.
  • What if whistles are more than 100 m apart? They must not be sounded simultaneously, and Rule 34(f) restricts manoeuvring and warning signals to one whistle.
  • What is a combined whistle system? Whistles no more than 100 m apart, sounded together, differing by at least 10 Hz, and treated as a single whistle. It is fitted to overcome a zone of reduced signal caused by obstructions.
  • What is the bell requirement for a ship of 20 m or more? At least 300 mm mouth diameter, at least 110 dB at 1 m, corrosion-resistant, and capable of manual operation.
  • Who approves sound appliances? The appropriate authority of the flag State.

A weak answer recites figures. A strong answer explains why the figures matter: you may not hear another ship until she is close, so silence proves nothing.

Key points
  • Annex III specifies whistle frequency, sound level, directivity and siting, plus bell and gong construction; Rule 33 says who must carry them.
  • Whistle pitch is banded by length: 70–200 Hz for ships of 200 m or more, so large ships sound deep and small craft sound high.
  • Nominal audibility ranges run from 2 nm (200 m or more) down to 0.5 nm (under 20 m), but these are for information only and are often far less in practice.
  • Whistles more than 100 m apart must not sound together, and Rule 34(f) allows only one for manoeuvring and warning signals.
  • A combined whistle system has whistles no more than 100 m apart, sounding together, differing by at least 10 Hz, and counts as one whistle.
  • The bell must give at least 110 dB at 1 m and have a mouth of at least 300 mm on vessels of 20 m or more, with manual operation always possible.
  • The ship's own whistle must not exceed 110 dB(A) at listening posts, and so far as practicable should not exceed 100 dB(A).

Frequently asked questions

How far can a ship's whistle be heard under COLREG?

Annex III gives approximate ranges of 2 nm for ships of 200 m or more, 1.5 nm for 75–200 m, 1 nm for 20–75 m and 0.5 nm under 20 m. These are for information only and assume still air and average background noise. Wind, rain and engine noise can reduce real audibility drastically.

Why do big ships have a deeper whistle than small boats?

Annex III assigns fundamental frequency bands by length: 70–200 Hz for 200 m and over, 130–350 Hz for 75–200 m, and 250–700 Hz under 75 m. The Annex does not state a reason, but lower frequencies suffer less atmospheric attenuation, and the banding gives listeners a rough idea of a vessel's size. The bands overlap, so pitch is only a clue.

Can a ship have two whistles sounding at the same time?

Yes, if they are 100 m or less apart. A combined whistle system is the recognised arrangement for this: whistles no more than 100 m apart, sounded simultaneously, differing in frequency by at least 10 Hz, and treated as one whistle. Whistles more than 100 m apart must not be sounded together, and only one may be used for manoeuvring and warning signals.

What size must a ship's bell be under COLREG?

For vessels of 20 m or more, the bell's mouth must be at least 300 mm in diameter. It must give at least 110 dB at 1 m and be made of corrosion-resistant material with a clear tone. A powered striker is recommended, but manual ringing must always be possible.

How often must the whistle be tested on board?

Annex III does not set a testing interval. Testing is normally governed by your company SMS, typically through pre-departure and pre-arrival bridge checklists, and by any flag State requirements. Follow your SMS and report any weak or faulty signal.

Test yourself

What is the fundamental frequency range specified by Annex III for the whistle of a vessel of 200 m or more in length?

Vessels of 200 m or more have the lowest band, 70–200 Hz. The 130–350 Hz band is for 75–200 m and 250–700 Hz is for vessels under 75 m (COLREG Annex III, section 1(b)).

Whistles on a ship are fitted more than 100 m apart. Which statement is correct?

Annex III section 1(f) prohibits simultaneous sounding when whistles are more than 100 m apart, and Rule 34(f) limits manoeuvring and warning signals to one whistle.

How should the audibility ranges listed in Annex III be treated?

Annex III section 1(c) states the ranges are for information and notes that actual audibility is extremely variable, especially in strong wind or high ambient noise.

What are the features of a combined whistle system under Annex III?

Annex III section 1(g) describes a combined system as whistles no more than 100 m apart, sounded together, with frequencies differing by at least 10 Hz, regarded as a single whistle.

What is the minimum bell mouth diameter for a vessel of 20 m or more?

Annex III section 2(b) requires a bell mouth diameter of at least 300 mm for vessels of 20 m or more, in addition to the 110 dB at 1 m intensity requirement.
