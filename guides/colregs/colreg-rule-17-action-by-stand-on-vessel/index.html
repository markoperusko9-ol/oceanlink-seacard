COLREG Rule 17 – Action by Stand-on Vessel

Quick answer

Rule 17 makes the stand-on vessel keep her course and speed. She may act alone once it is apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action, and she must take whatever action best aids to avoid collision once the give-way vessel can no longer avoid it alone. A power-driven stand-on vessel acting under 17(a)(ii) in a crossing situation shall, if circumstances admit, not alter to port for a vessel on her own port side.

What Rule 17 says, in plain words

Rule 17 sits in Part B, Section II of the COLREGs. Under Rule 11, that section applies only to vessels in sight of one another. It sets out the duties of the vessel that is not required to keep out of the way. Rules 13, 15 and 18 decide which vessel that is.

Paraphrased, the four paragraphs say:

  • 17(a)(i): Where one of two vessels must keep out of the way, the other must keep her course and speed.
  • 17(a)(ii): The stand-on vessel may take action to avoid collision by her own manoeuvre alone. She may do this as soon as it becomes apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action under the Rules.
  • 17(b): If, for any reason, the stand-on vessel finds herself so close that the give-way vessel's action alone cannot avoid collision, she must take such action as will best aid to avoid it.
  • 17(c): A power-driven vessel acting under 17(a)(ii) in a crossing situation with another power-driven vessel shall, if the circumstances of the case admit, not alter course to port for a vessel on her own port side.
  • 17(d): Nothing in the Rule relieves the give-way vessel of her obligation to keep out of the way.

Note the change in wording from 'shall keep' to 'may' to 'shall take'. That progression is the heart of the Rule. Examiners expect you to explain it.

The stages of a stand-on encounter

Many instructors teach Rule 17 as a sequence of stages. The Rule does not use these labels, and it gives no ranges, CPAs or times for moving between them. Those judgements depend on ship type, speed, manoeuvrability, sea room and traffic. Many companies set CPA and calling limits in the SMS or the master's standing orders, and those vary from fleet to fleet.

Stage Situation Duty of stand-on vessel
1 No risk of collision yet Free to manoeuvre; Section II stand-on duty not yet engaged
2 Risk of collision exists Keep course and speed (17(a)(i))
3 Apparent that give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action May act by own manoeuvre alone (17(a)(ii)); 17(c) applies in power-driven crossing cases
4 Collision cannot be avoided by give-way vessel alone Must take action that best aids to avoid collision (17(b))

'Keep course and speed' does not freeze the helm. It is generally understood to mean continuing the course and speed the other vessel would reasonably expect. That includes ordinary navigational manoeuvres such as following a buoyed channel or a known bend in a fairway. What it rules out is an unexpected change that could wreck the give-way vessel's plan.

Applying it on the bridge

A practical sequence for the OOW once a target is identified as give-way to you:

  1. Confirm the situation. Take a series of compass bearings and use radar/ARPA plotting. Under Rule 7, a steady or nearly steady bearing means risk of collision may exist, and with a large ship or at close range risk may exist even with an appreciable bearing change. Confirm that the vessels are in sight of one another and identify the type of vessel, so you know Rule 17 applies at all.
  2. Hold course and speed. Keep a steady heading. Avoid casual autopilot tweaks. Do not start engine changes that are not part of your expected passage.
  3. Watch for the give-way vessel's action. Look for an alteration that is large enough to be readily apparent (Rule 8). Check that the CPA is actually opening, not just that her heading has changed.
  4. Call the master as your standing orders require. Generally call whenever you are in doubt. STCW Section A-VIII/2 expects the OOW to call the master in such circumstances.
  5. If in doubt, sound the signal. Under Rule 34(d), a vessel in sight that doubts whether the other is taking sufficient action shall sound at least five short and rapid blasts. This may be supplemented by a light signal of at least five short and rapid flashes.
  6. Act decisively when stage 3 or 4 is reached. Make one bold, readily apparent manoeuvre. Accompany course changes or astern propulsion with the Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signals. Then monitor until the other vessel is finally past and clear (Rule 8(d)).

In a power-driven crossing case, a bold alteration to starboard is usually the right choice. This may mean a large turn, or a round turn, that brings the other vessel onto your port side or astern. Reducing speed is often counter-productive, because a give-way vessel that finally alters to starboard will be aiming to pass astern of you.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Crossing, give-way vessel holding on. It is clear weather. You are on a power-driven bulk carrier. A power-driven container ship is on your port bow, crossing, on a steady bearing with a CPA near zero. She is the give-way vessel under Rule 15, and you stand on. (The ranges below are illustrative, not rule thresholds.)

  • At 8 nm: Risk of collision exists. You keep course and speed and monitor.
  • At 5 nm: There is still no visible alteration, and the bearing is still steady. You call the master.
  • At about 2–3 nm: Still no alteration. If you doubt her intentions, you sound at least five short and rapid blasts once within audible range of the whistle (Annex III ranges are 0.5–2 nm depending on vessel length), supplemented by five short and rapid flashes if useful.
  • Still nothing: It is now apparent she is not taking appropriate action, so you may act under 17(a)(ii). You make a bold alteration to starboard, sound one short blast, and keep turning until she is safely on your port side or astern. Do not turn to port. If she wakes up late and goes hard to starboard, you would be turning into her.
  • If you leave it too late: 17(b) now obliges you to take whatever action best aids, which might mean hard over combined with full astern. At that point no option is ideal.

Scenario 2 – Being overtaken. A faster vessel is overtaking you close on your starboard quarter. Under Rule 13 she must keep clear, and you are the stand-on vessel. Rule 17(c) applies only to crossing situations, so it does not restrict your options here. Still, an alteration to starboard towards the overtaking vessel would obviously be dangerous. If her CPA keeps shrinking, sound the doubt signal and call the master. If action becomes necessary, choose a manoeuvre that opens the distance from her, and allow for interaction at close range.

Scenario 3 – Stand-on in a channel. You are following a buoyed channel that bends to port, and a crossing vessel is give-way to you. Following the bend is part of your expected 'course'. Do not hold the old heading into shoal water just to 'keep course'. Rule 2 and good seamanship still apply, and so may Rule 9.

Common mistakes

  • Acting too early. Once risk of collision exists, keeping course and speed is a duty, not a suggestion. An unexpected early turn, especially to port, can cancel out a give-way vessel's correct alteration.
  • Standing on to the bitter end. Some OOWs treat 'stand-on' as 'right of way' and wait for the other ship to act. Rule 17(b) and Rule 2 make that indefensible.
  • Turning to port for a vessel on the port side in a power-driven crossing. This is the classic error that 17(c) exists to prevent.
  • Slowing down as the stand-on action when the give-way vessel is trying to pass astern.
  • Skipping the doubt signal. Rule 34(d) is mandatory when its conditions are met.
  • Small, creeping alterations that the other vessel cannot detect by eye or radar. They breach Rule 8.
  • Settling it on VHF. VHF is not part of the Rules. It risks misidentifying the vessel and wastes time. Any agreement reached over VHF cannot authorise a breach of the Rules.
  • Applying Rule 17 in fog. In restricted visibility, with vessels not in sight of one another, Rule 19 governs, and there is no stand-on or give-way vessel.

Oral exam points

Expect questions like these at OOW and master-level orals:

  • 'When may the stand-on vessel act, and when must she?' She may act once it becomes apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action (17(a)(ii)). She must act once collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone (17(b)).
  • 'Why not alter to port?' The give-way vessel's most likely action is a starboard turn to pass astern of you. Turning to port for a vessel on your port side brings you towards her track.
  • 'Does 17(c) apply when overtaken?' No. It applies only to a power-driven vessel crossing another power-driven vessel.
  • 'What signals would you sound?' At least five short and rapid blasts if in doubt (Rule 34(d)). Then the Rule 34(a) manoeuvring signal for any course alteration or astern propulsion.
  • 'Does acting under Rule 17 change who is give-way?' No. Rule 17(d) keeps the give-way vessel's obligation intact.
  • 'Does it apply in restricted visibility?' Not when vessels are detected by radar alone. That is Rule 19.

Examiners want reasoning, not recitation. Explain why each stage exists, and be ready to sketch a crossing plot showing what happens if both vessels turn the wrong way.

Key points
  • Rule 17 applies only to vessels in sight of one another (Section II).
  • Once risk of collision exists, the stand-on vessel must keep her course and speed.
  • She may act alone once it is apparent the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action.
  • She must take the action that best aids to avoid collision once the give-way vessel alone cannot avoid it.
  • In a power-driven crossing, if circumstances admit, do not alter to port for a vessel on your own port side.
  • Sound at least five short and rapid blasts when in doubt, and use Rule 34(a) signals when manoeuvring.
  • Acting under Rule 17 never relieves the give-way vessel of her duty to keep clear.

Frequently asked questions

Can the stand-on vessel alter course early to make the situation clear?

Before risk of collision exists, the Section II duties are not yet engaged, and an early, substantial alteration can prevent a close-quarters situation developing. Once risk of collision exists, the stand-on vessel must keep her course and speed. She may act only when it becomes apparent that the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action. A change made just as risk is developing is the most confusing for the other ship.

Is reducing speed an acceptable action for the stand-on vessel?

The Rule does not forbid it. Rule 17(c) restricts only an alteration to port in power-driven crossing cases, and under 17(b) any action that best aids is required. In a crossing situation, though, slowing down can put you in the path of a give-way vessel trying to pass astern. A bold alteration to starboard is usually more effective.

Does Rule 17 apply in fog when I only see the target on radar?

No. Rules 11 to 18 apply only to vessels in sight of one another. When vessels are not in sight of one another in or near restricted visibility, Rule 19 applies, and neither vessel is stand-on or give-way.

When should the OOW call the master in a stand-on situation?

Follow the master's standing orders and the company SMS. Calling limits and CPA criteria differ between companies and ships. As a general principle, STCW Section A-VIII/2 expects the OOW to call the master when in doubt or when traffic causes concern, and a give-way vessel that is not acting is exactly that.

Should I use VHF to agree the manoeuvre with the give-way vessel?

VHF is not part of the COLREGs, and an agreement made over it cannot authorise action that breaches the Rules. Misidentifying the vessel and losing time are well-known risks in collision investigations. Many company SMSs discourage relying on it for collision avoidance. If you use it at all, it must not delay the action the Rules require.

Test yourself

Under Rule 17(a)(ii), when may the stand-on vessel take action by her manoeuvre alone?

Rule 17(a)(ii) permits action once it becomes apparent the give-way vessel is not taking appropriate action. The 'cannot be avoided' test is the mandatory stage under 17(b). (COLREG Rule 17(a)(ii))

A power-driven stand-on vessel acting under 17(a)(ii) in a crossing situation with a power-driven vessel on her port side should, if circumstances admit, avoid which action?

Rule 17(c) says such a vessel shall, if the circumstances of the case admit, not alter course to port for a vessel on her own port side. (COLREG Rule 17(c))

What does Rule 17(b) require when the stand-on vessel is so close that collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone?

At this stage action is mandatory, and the test is whatever best aids to avoid collision, not a prescribed manoeuvre. (COLREG Rule 17(b))

In restricted visibility, a target is detected by radar alone and is closing on your port bow. How does Rule 17 apply?

Section II rules apply only to vessels in sight of one another. Vessels not in sight in restricted visibility are governed by Rule 19. (COLREG Rules 11 and 19)

Vessels are in sight of one another, and the stand-on vessel doubts that the give-way vessel is taking sufficient action. Which sound signal shall she give?

Rule 34(d) requires at least five short and rapid blasts in these circumstances, which may be supplemented by a light signal. (COLREG Rule 34(d))
