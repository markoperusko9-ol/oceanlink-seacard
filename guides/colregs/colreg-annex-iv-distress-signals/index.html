COLREG Annex IV – Distress Signals Explained for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Annex IV is the list of signals that Rule 37 says a vessel in distress shall use or exhibit. It has three paragraphs. The first lists the recognised visual, sound and radio distress signals. The second prohibits using them, or anything resembling them, for any other purpose. The third points you to the International Code of Signals, IAMSAR Volume III and two signals intended for spotting from the air.

Where Annex IV fits in the Rules

Rule 37 is one of the shortest rules in the book. It says that a vessel in distress and requiring assistance shall use or exhibit the signals described in Annex IV. The rule itself gives no list. Everything sits in the Annex.

Annex IV has three paragraphs:

  • Paragraph 1 lists the signals that indicate distress and need of assistance. They can be used together or separately.
  • Paragraph 2 prohibits using any of them except to indicate distress. It also prohibits any other signal that could be confused with them.
  • Paragraph 3 draws attention to the International Code of Signals, the IAMSAR Manual Volume III, and two extra signals meant to be seen from the air.

The Annex has been amended over the years to keep pace with GMDSS. Older radio entries were replaced with DSC, satellite and beacon alerts. If you learnt the list from an old textbook, check it against the current consolidated text carried on board.

The recognised distress signals in paragraph 1

The list is easier to remember if you group it by type rather than learning it as one long alphabet.

Type Signal
Sound Gun or other explosive signal fired at intervals of about a minute
Sound Continuous sounding with any fog-signalling apparatus
Pyrotechnic Rockets or shells throwing red stars, fired one at a time at short intervals
Pyrotechnic Rocket parachute flare or hand flare showing a red light
Pyrotechnic Smoke signal giving off orange-coloured smoke
Visual Flames on the vessel, such as from a burning tar or oil barrel
Visual Square flag with a ball, or anything resembling a ball, above or below it
Visual International Code Signal of distress, NC
Visual Slowly and repeatedly raising and lowering arms outstretched to each side
Radio / light A signal made by any signalling method consisting of the group SOS (· · · – – – · · ·) in the Morse Code
Radio Spoken word MAYDAY by radiotelephony
Radio DSC distress alert on VHF channel 70, or on MF/HF 2187.5, 4207.5, 6312, 8414.5, 12577 or 16804.5 kHz
Radio Ship-to-shore distress alert from the ship's satellite ship earth station (Inmarsat or another recognised mobile satellite service)
Radio Signals transmitted by an EPIRB
Radio Approved signals from radiocommunication systems, including survival craft radar transponders

Points that catch candidates out:

  • Continuous fog signal means exactly that: an unbroken sound. It is not the prolonged and short blasts of Rule 35.
  • The DSC alert goes out on channel 70 or the DSC frequencies. The follow-up MAYDAY voice call is made on the associated distress traffic frequency, such as VHF channel 16 or 2182 kHz. Your GMDSS procedures cover this.
  • Only red stars, flares and lights mean distress. Orange is for smoke. White flares and white stars are not distress signals.

Paragraph 2: no misuse, no look-alikes

Paragraph 2 is the part that gets officers into trouble. It forbids two things:

  1. Using any of the listed signals for anything other than indicating distress and need of assistance.
  2. Using any other signal that may be confused with them.

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 35 says the same thing for all ships under SOLAS. It prohibits using an international distress signal except to indicate that a person or persons are in distress, and it bans signals that may be confused with one.

In practice this covers:

  • Out-of-date pyrotechnics. Do not fire them off on deck to get rid of them. Dispose of them through an approved shore facility, as your company and the port allow.
  • Training. Do not use them for training unless the activity is properly authorised and coordinated. Arrangements for pyrotechnic training, and for testing beacons and DSC, vary by administration and company SMS. Follow those rules exactly.
  • Attracting attention. Rule 36 lets you attract another vessel's attention with light or sound signals, provided they cannot be mistaken for any signal authorised elsewhere in the Rules. A red flare to wake up a fishing boat is not acceptable.
  • Accidental transmissions. A false DSC, satellite or EPIRB alert is not a deliberate misuse, but it ties up rescue resources, so it must be cancelled promptly (see the scenarios below).

Paragraph 3: the IAMSAR link and air-search signals

Paragraph 3 does not add new distress signals. It points you to:

  • The International Code of Signals, which covers distress and emergency signalling by flags, flashing light and radio.
  • IAMSAR Manual Volume III (Mobile Facilities), the volume carried on board. It covers what to do when you are in distress, when you hear or see someone else's distress signal, and when you are part of a search.
  • Two signals intended for identification from the air:
    • a piece of orange-coloured canvas with a black square and circle, or another appropriate symbol;
    • a dye marker.

These are for survivors in boats or rafts who need to be found by aircraft. Your survival craft equipment and liferaft instructions tell you what is actually packed on your ship.

What you actually need on board

Many of the listed signals are historical. Guns, tar barrels and continuous fog signals are rarely the first choice on a modern merchant ship. What matters day to day:

  • Know your GMDSS distress buttons. Know where they are, what each transmits and how to cancel a false alert. That means VHF DSC, MF/HF DSC and the satellite terminal.
  • Know your pyrotechnics. SOLAS Chapter III requires rocket parachute flares to be stored on or near the bridge. Lifeboats and liferafts carry their own outfit of rocket parachute flares, hand flares and buoyant smoke signals as set out in the LSA Code. Check quantities and expiry dates against your flag requirements and the manufacturer's markings.
  • Know the beacons. The EPIRB float-free arrangement and HRU, plus manual activation. The SART (radar or AIS type) and how it shows on the display.
  • Know what is not a distress signal:
    • five or more short and rapid blasts under Rule 34(d) is a doubt or warning signal;
    • flag V means 'I require assistance', which is not distress;
    • flag W means 'I require medical assistance';
    • an inverted ensign is not in Annex IV.

When you see or hear a distress signal from another vessel, SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 33 obliges the master, if able, to proceed with all speed to assist. Where possible, the master informs the persons in distress or the SAR service. If the master decides not to go, the reason is entered in the log and the SAR service informed as appropriate.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Red flare sighted at night

On the 0000–0400 watch you see a single red star rise and fall about 30° on the port bow. Nothing on AIS, nothing heard on VHF.

  1. Take an immediate bearing and note the time. Try to estimate range or elevation.
  2. Call the master.
  3. Keep watching the bearing. A second signal gives you a cross-check, and red stars are meant to be fired at short intervals.
  4. Listen on channel 16 and watch DSC.
  5. Report to the nearest MRCC or coast station with your position, the bearing, the time and a description.
  6. Plan to alter towards the bearing on the master's decision, and keep SOLAS V/33 in mind.
  7. Log everything.

Do not dismiss it as fireworks without a proper assessment.

Scenario 2 – Accidental VHF DSC distress alert

A cadet presses the distress button while cleaning the console.

  1. Reset the equipment immediately to stop the alert repeating. If the set has a DSC distress self-cancel function, use it as your GMDSS manual directs. Then set the radio to VHF channel 16.
  2. Inform the master.
  3. Broadcast a voice cancellation on the associated channel, VHF channel 16. Address all stations, give the ship's name, call sign, MMSI and position, and cancel the distress alert sent at the stated date and time.
  4. Contact the relevant RCC if requested or if your procedures require it.
  5. Make a log entry.

Follow the exact wording in your GMDSS manual and company SMS. Do not simply stay silent and hope nobody noticed.

Scenario 3 – Liferaft, aircraft overhead

Keep the rocket parachute flares for when a ship or aircraft is likely to see them. Do not fire them all at once. In daylight with an aircraft close, orange smoke is usually more effective than a flare. Make sure the SART is on and raised as high as practicable.

Oral exam: what examiners ask

Typical questions at OOW and Chief Mate orals, with the points an examiner wants to hear:

  • 'Which rule refers to distress signals?' Rule 37, which refers you to Annex IV.
  • 'Give me the distress signals in Annex IV.' Group them by sound, pyrotechnic, visual and radio. Do not miss DSC, the satellite alert, the EPIRB and SART.
  • 'Is five short blasts a distress signal?' No. It is the Rule 34(d) doubt or warning signal.
  • 'What does paragraph 2 say?' No use except for distress, and no signals that may be confused with them. Link it to SOLAS V/35.
  • 'You see red flares. Your actions?' Bearing and time, call the master, report to the MRCC, assist under SOLAS V/33, log.
  • 'False DSC alert. Your actions?' Reset the equipment immediately (using the self-cancel function if fitted), inform the master, cancel by voice on the associated frequency, contact the RCC as required, log.
  • 'What does paragraph 3 refer to?' The International Code of Signals, IAMSAR Volume III, and the orange canvas with black square and circle and the dye marker for air identification.

Examiners dislike hesitation over basics. Being able to recite the DSC distress frequencies and the colour rules (red for lights and stars, orange for smoke) shows you have done the work.

Key points
  • Rule 37 requires a vessel in distress to use the signals in Annex IV; the list itself is in the Annex.
  • Paragraph 1 lists sound, pyrotechnic, visual and radio signals, including DSC, satellite alerts, EPIRBs and SARTs.
  • Red means distress for stars, flares and lights; orange means distress for smoke.
  • Paragraph 2 and SOLAS V/35 ban using distress signals, or anything resembling them, for any other purpose.
  • Paragraph 3 points to the International Code of Signals, IAMSAR Volume III, and two air-search signals.
  • Five short blasts, flag V and an inverted ensign are not Annex IV distress signals.
  • A false distress alert must be cancelled promptly using your GMDSS and company procedures.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Rule 37 and Annex IV?

Rule 37 places the obligation: a vessel in distress requiring assistance shall use or exhibit the signals described in Annex IV. Annex IV contains the actual list of signals, the prohibition on misuse and the cross-references to other publications. In an exam, quote both together.

Is a white flare a distress signal?

No. Annex IV specifies red for rocket stars, parachute flares and hand flares, and orange for smoke. White flares are commonly used to illuminate or to attract attention, and should not be read as distress.

Can I fire expired flares to get rid of them?

No. Doing so breaches Annex IV paragraph 2 and SOLAS V/35, and can trigger a real search. Dispose of expired pyrotechnics through an approved shore facility or as directed by your company and the local authority. Rules for training use vary by flag and port.

Is pressing the DSC distress button enough to call for help?

The DSC alert is a recognised distress signal in its own right. GMDSS procedure is to follow it with a MAYDAY voice call and distress message on the associated distress traffic frequency, such as VHF channel 16. Follow the sequence in your GMDSS manual and company procedures.

Does flag V or flag W mean distress?

No. Under the International Code of Signals, V means 'I require assistance' and W means 'I require medical assistance'. Neither is an Annex IV distress signal. The flag distress signal in Annex IV is NC, or a square flag with a ball or something resembling a ball above or below it.

Test yourself

Which of the following is a recognised distress signal under COLREG Annex IV?

A continuous sounding with any fog-signalling apparatus is listed in Annex IV paragraph 1. Five short blasts is the Rule 34(d) doubt signal, and the prolonged-plus-two-short signal belongs to Rule 35.

On which VHF channel is a DSC distress alert transmitted?

Annex IV paragraph 1 lists a DSC distress alert on VHF channel 70. Channel 16 is used for the following voice distress traffic, not the DSC alert itself.

What does Annex IV paragraph 2 prohibit?

Paragraph 2 bans any use of the listed signals other than to indicate distress and need of assistance, and bans signals that may be confused with them. SOLAS V/35 carries the same prohibition.

Which signal does Annex IV paragraph 3 mention for identification from the air?

Paragraph 3 draws attention to orange canvas with a black square and circle (or another appropriate symbol) and to a dye marker for air identification. It also references the International Code of Signals and IAMSAR Volume III.

Which visual signal is listed in Annex IV as a distress signal?

The arm signal is listed in Annex IV paragraph 1. The inverted ensign is not in Annex IV, and flags V and W are International Code of Signals requests for assistance and medical assistance, not distress.
