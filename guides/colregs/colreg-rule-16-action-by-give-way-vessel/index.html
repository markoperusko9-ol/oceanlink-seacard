COLREG Rule 16 – Action by Give-Way Vessel

Quick answer

Rule 16 says that a vessel required to keep out of the way of another must, so far as possible, act early and substantially so that it keeps well clear. It is one of the shortest steering rules but one of the most tested. On the bridge it means acting while there is plenty of sea room and time, and making an alteration large enough that the other vessel cannot miss it.

What Rule 16 actually says

Rule 16 is a single sentence in Part B, Section II of COLREG. In substance, any vessel that another rule directs to keep out of the way of another vessel must, so far as possible, take early and substantial action to keep well clear.

Each of those three words carries weight:

  • Early means acting while the range is still large enough for the other vessel to see your intention and for the situation to resolve calmly. It does not mean waiting until the stand-on vessel starts to worry.
  • Substantial means a change of course and/or speed that is large and obvious, visually and on radar. A few degrees on the autopilot is not substantial.
  • Well clear means a passing distance that is safe in the circumstances. It is not just "no collision".

The words "so far as possible" accept that sea room, traffic, depth or the ship's own handling may limit what you can do. They do not excuse a lack of action.

COLREG gives no figure for "early", "substantial" or "well clear". Many companies set minimum CPA and action-range guidance in their SMS or the Master's standing orders. Those figures vary between companies and between open-sea and coastal waters, so follow your own ship's instructions. The Rule still applies even when you meet the company figure.

Rule 16 works together with Rule 8 (Action to avoid collision). Rule 8 sets out how any avoiding action should be taken: positive, in ample time, large enough to be readily apparent, a single large alteration rather than a series of small ones, and checked until the other vessel is finally past and clear.

When Rule 16 applies – and when it does not

Rule 16 does not make any vessel the give-way vessel by itself. It applies only when another rule directs a vessel to keep out of the way. The main ones are:

Rule Who must keep out of the way
Rule 12 Sailing vessels relative to each other, depending on the wind
Rule 13 The overtaking vessel, until finally past and clear
Rule 15 The power-driven vessel that has the other on its own starboard side
Rule 18 Vessels lower in the order of responsibility (e.g. a power-driven vessel underway keeping clear of a vessel NUC, RAM, engaged in fishing or sailing), except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 require otherwise

Rule 16 does not apply in the following cases:

  • Restricted visibility with vessels not in sight of one another. Section II applies only to vessels in sight of one another (Rule 11). Rule 19 governs instead, and under Rule 19 there is no give-way or stand-on vessel.
  • A head-on situation (Rule 14). Neither vessel is "directed to keep out of the way". Both must alter to starboard. The spirit of early, substantial action still applies through Rule 8.
  • "Shall not impede" duties in Rules 9, 10 and 18(d). These are a different obligation. A vessel that must not impede may still have to act early to leave sea room, but that duty comes from those rules, not from Rule 16.

A vessel that is the stand-on vessel under one rule can still be the give-way vessel towards a third vessel. Multi-ship situations need each pair assessed separately.

Applying Rule 16 on the bridge: step by step

A practical sequence for the OOW once risk of collision is suspected:

  1. Establish risk of collision (Rule 7). Take compass bearings and use radar plotting or ARPA. A steady or nearly steady bearing with closing range means risk exists. Remember that risk may exist with an appreciable bearing change, especially with a very large vessel or at close range.
  2. Identify the situation and your role. Decide whether this is crossing, overtaking or a responsibility case under Rule 18. Check the target's lights, shapes, AIS status and behaviour, and do not rely on AIS alone. If you are in doubt about overtaking, Rule 13 says to assume you are overtaking.
  3. Check the surroundings. Look for other traffic, navigational dangers, depth, TSS boundaries and sea room before choosing the action.
  4. Decide early and act boldly. For a crossing vessel on your starboard side, the usual action is a clear alteration to starboard to pass astern of it. Rule 15 says to avoid crossing ahead if the circumstances admit. Reduce speed, stop or reverse if more time is needed or sea room is tight (Rule 8(e)).
  5. Signal if required. If the vessels are in sight and you are a power-driven vessel, Rule 34(a) requires the manoeuvring signal: one short blast for an alteration to starboard, two for port, three for operating astern propulsion.
  6. Check the action works. The bearing should open clearly in the direction expected and the ARPA CPA should increase. Re-plot after the alteration.
  7. Hold the action until finally past and clear. Only then resume course. Resuming too early is a classic way to recreate the close-quarters situation.
  8. Call the Master in line with standing orders and the SMS. Log the action as required.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Crossing, open sea, clear visibility. You are on a bulk carrier making 12 knots. A container ship is on your starboard bow at 9 miles. The compass bearing is steady and the ARPA shows a CPA of about 0.2 miles with the target passing ahead of you.

This is a crossing situation under Rule 15, and you are the give-way vessel. You confirm there is no other traffic to starboard. You then make one bold alteration to starboard so that you are heading to pass clearly astern of her, with her on your port bow showing her red sidelight, and the ARPA shows a generous CPA astern of it. If the vessels are in sight of one another when you alter, give one short blast as you make the alteration (Rule 34(a)). You watch the bearing draw clearly left and hold the new course until the container ship is past and clear, then come back to your track.

Scenario 2 – Overtaking. You are overtaking a slow general cargo ship from more than two points abaft her beam, and you can see only her sternlight at night. If in doubt, Rule 13(c) requires you to assume you are overtaking. You are the give-way vessel under Rule 13. You can pass on either side, but you must keep well clear until finally past and clear. Your duty does not end if the bearing later changes so that she appears to be crossing. Choose a side that leaves room for her to manoeuvre and is clear of other traffic. In a narrow channel where she would have to take action to let you pass, the Rule 9(e) signals apply.

Scenario 3 – Vessel engaged in fishing on the port bow. You are a power-driven vessel underway. A trawler is showing green-over-white and the correct sidelights, and she is on your port bow on a steady bearing. Under Rule 15 alone you might think you are the stand-on vessel. Rule 18, however, requires you to keep out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing, so Rule 16 applies to you. Act early. Allow for gear that may extend well astern or to one side, and for the irregular movements of fishing vessels. Do not cross close ahead.

Scenario 4 – Limited sea room. You are coastal, with shoal water to starboard, and a crossing vessel is on your starboard bow on a steady bearing. A large starboard alteration is not safe. "So far as possible" does not let you do nothing. Reduce speed substantially, or stop, so that she crosses well ahead. Remember that a speed change is less obvious to the other vessel than a course change. Make it early and large, and give three short blasts if you go astern.

Common mistakes watchkeepers make

  • A series of small alterations. Five degrees, then another five, then another. The stand-on vessel cannot tell what you are doing, and her radar plot may not show it. Rule 8(b) specifically warns against this.
  • Acting late. Waiting until a stand-on vessel is close leaves her in the Rule 17 zone, where she may act herself. If both vessels act at once, they may act in conflicting directions.
  • Altering to port for a vessel on the starboard bow. This risks turning across her bow, or into her path if she later takes her own action under Rule 17.
  • Relying on VHF agreements. Arrangements made by VHF often involve misidentification and confusion. Your duties under the Rules do not change because of a radio call.
  • Speed reductions nobody notices. A small speed change is often invisible to the other ship. If speed is your tool, make the change large.
  • Resuming course too early. The duty lasts until the other vessel is past and clear.
  • Treating the CPA figure as the only test. A CPA that meets the company minimum may still not be "well clear" in heavy weather, near a large tow, or with a fishing vessel's gear out.
  • Forgetting Rule 2. Following the letter of the steering rules never excuses neglecting ordinary seamanship or the special circumstances of the case.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect short, direct questions and give short, rule-referenced answers.

  • "What does Rule 16 require?" A vessel directed to keep out of the way must, so far as possible, take early and substantial action to keep well clear. Mention that Rule 8 describes how that action must be taken.
  • "Which rules make a vessel the give-way vessel?" Mainly Rules 12, 13, 15 and 18. Rule 16 itself creates no duty to give way.
  • "Does Rule 16 apply in fog?" Not to vessels that are not in sight of one another. Section II applies only to vessels in sight (Rule 11), and Rule 19 governs in restricted visibility.
  • "What is a substantial alteration?" One large enough to be readily apparent to the other vessel visually or by radar. COLREG gives no fixed number of degrees, so refer to the SMS or Master's orders for any company guidance.
  • "Can the give-way vessel reduce speed instead of altering course?" Yes. Rule 8(e) allows slackening speed, stopping or reversing. The examiner will want you to say the change must be large enough to be apparent.
  • "What happens if the give-way vessel does not act?" The stand-on vessel may act under Rule 17(a)(ii), and must act under Rule 17(b) when collision cannot be avoided by the give-way vessel alone. Also mention the five-short-blast doubt signal under Rule 34(d).
  • "When does the give-way vessel's duty end?" When the other vessel is finally past and clear. Rule 13 states this explicitly for overtaking.

The examiner may draw a scenario and ask what lights you see and what you would do. Name the situation, state your role with the rule number, then describe your action, any sound signal and how you would check that the action is working.

Key points
  • Rule 16 requires a give-way vessel to take early, substantial action to keep well clear, so far as possible.
  • It applies only when another rule (mainly 12, 13, 15 or 18) directs a vessel to keep out of the way.
  • It does not apply to vessels not in sight of one another in restricted visibility, where Rule 19 governs.
  • Rule 8 sets out how to act: positive, in ample time, readily apparent, and not a series of small alterations.
  • In a crossing situation, avoid crossing ahead of the stand-on vessel if circumstances admit; a bold starboard alteration is usual.
  • Large speed reductions, stopping or reversing are valid give-way actions, especially with limited sea room.
  • Keep the action going and keep checking it until the other vessel is finally past and clear.

Frequently asked questions

How many degrees should a give-way vessel alter course under Rule 16?

COLREG does not specify a number of degrees. The alteration must be large enough to be readily apparent to the other vessel visually or by radar (Rule 8(b)). Many companies give guidance in the SMS or Master's standing orders, and those figures vary, so follow your ship's instructions and judge each case on its circumstances.

Does Rule 16 apply in fog or restricted visibility?

Not between vessels that are not in sight of one another. Rule 16 is in Section II, which applies only to vessels in sight of one another (Rule 11). Where vessels cannot see each other, Rule 19 applies, and there is no give-way or stand-on vessel under that rule.

Is there a give-way vessel in a head-on situation?

No. Under Rule 14 both power-driven vessels must alter course to starboard so that each passes on the port side of the other. Neither is directed to keep out of the way, but both should still act early and substantially in line with Rule 8.

Can a give-way vessel slow down instead of altering course?

Yes. Rule 8(e) allows a vessel to slacken speed, stop or reverse if necessary to avoid collision or allow more time. A speed change is harder for the other vessel to detect than a course change, so it must be large and made early.

Should I agree a manoeuvre on VHF with the stand-on vessel?

Using VHF for collision avoidance carries real risks: calling the wrong ship, misunderstanding, and losing time that should be spent manoeuvring. A radio agreement does not change your obligations under the Rules. Take the action the Rules require, and follow your company's procedures on VHF use.

Test yourself

Which phrase best describes the action required of a give-way vessel under Rule 16?

Rule 16 requires early and substantial action to keep well clear, so far as possible. A CPA figure alone does not satisfy it. (COLREG Rule 16)

Two power-driven vessels in sight of each other are crossing with risk of collision. Which is the give-way vessel?

Under Rule 15, the vessel with the other on her own starboard side keeps out of the way, and Rule 16 then governs how she does it. Size and speed do not decide the roles. (COLREG Rules 15 and 16)

In dense fog, a radar target is detected closing on your starboard bow with risk of collision. The vessels are not in sight of each other. What applies?

Section II, including Rules 15 and 16, applies only to vessels in sight of one another. Rule 19 governs vessels not in sight of one another in restricted visibility. (COLREG Rules 11 and 19)

According to Rule 8, what should a give-way vessel avoid when altering course?

Rule 8(b) says alterations should be large enough to be readily apparent, and a succession of small alterations should be avoided. Rule 8(e) expressly permits speed reduction. (COLREG Rule 8)

A power-driven vessel underway sees a vessel engaged in fishing on her port bow with risk of collision. Who must keep out of the way?

Rule 18 requires a power-driven vessel underway to keep out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing, whatever the relative bearing, except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 require otherwise. Rule 16 then governs how she keeps clear. (COLREG Rules 16 and 18)
