COLREG Rule 6 – Safe speed explained for watchkeepers

Quick answer

Rule 6 requires every vessel, at all times and in any visibility, to proceed at a speed that lets her take proper and effective action to avoid collision and stop within a distance suited to the conditions. The Rule sets no figure. Instead it lists factors the watchkeeper must weigh, with extra factors for vessels using radar. Safe speed is a continuous judgement you must be able to justify, not a number on the telegraph.

What Rule 6 actually requires

Rule 6 sits in Part B, Section I, so it applies in any condition of visibility (Rule 4). Paraphrased, it says:

  • Every vessel must at all times proceed at a safe speed.
  • A safe speed lets her take proper and effective action to avoid collision.
  • A safe speed also lets her be stopped within a distance appropriate to the prevailing circumstances and conditions.
  • To decide what is safe, the Rule lists factors to take into account. One set applies to all vessels. A second set applies additionally to vessels with operational radar.

Three points examiners like to hear:

  1. No number. The Rule contains no knots figure and no formula. You will hear rules of thumb on board and in casualty reports, but none of them comes from the Rule itself.
  2. The factor lists are not exhaustive. The text says the factors shall be among those taken into account. Anything else relevant, such as a fishing fleet shooting gear or a pilot boarding ahead, counts too.
  3. It applies to every vessel, including sailing vessels, fishing vessels and the stand-on vessel. Being stand-on under Rule 17 does not excuse an unsafe speed.

Rule 1(b) preserves special rules made by an appropriate authority for roadsteads, harbours, rivers, lakes and inland waterways connected with the high seas. Port, canal, river and VTS regulations often impose fixed speed limits. Obeying a local limit does not by itself make your speed safe. If the conditions call for less, you go slower.

Factors for all vessels

Rule 6(a) lists the factors every vessel must consider:

Factor What it means on the bridge
State of visibility How far can you actually see? Haze, rain squalls, fog banks and the low sun all count.
Traffic density Includes concentrations of fishing vessels or any other vessels. Count the targets and think about the sea room you will need.
Manoeuvrability Stopping distance and turning ability in the prevailing conditions. Consider loaded or ballast, speed, wind, and any engine or steering limitations.
Background light at night Shore lights or back-scatter from your own deck lights can hide navigation lights.
Wind, sea and current, and proximity of navigational hazards These affect leeway, set, sea room and how quickly you run out of options.
Draught against available depth Covers under-keel clearance, squat and reduced manoeuvrability in shallow water.

The manoeuvrability factor is where your own ship's data matters. Know where to find the manoeuvring information (wheelhouse poster, pilot card, manoeuvring booklet). Remember that trial figures are usually for ideal conditions, not a loaded ship in a beam wind.

Additional factors for vessels using radar

Rule 6(b) adds factors for vessels with operational radar:

  • Characteristics, efficiency and limitations of the radar. This includes X-band versus S-band behaviour, blind and shadow sectors, and the performance monitor.
  • Constraints of the range scale in use. A short range scale may give late warning. A long scale may lose small echoes in clutter.
  • Sea state, weather and other interference. Sea and rain clutter, and the effect of over-aggressive clutter suppression.
  • Small vessels, ice and floating objects may not be detected at an adequate range. These include GRP yachts, wooden fishing boats, growlers and containers.
  • Number, location and movement of radar targets. Consider how many CPA/TCPA problems you are managing at once.
  • A more exact assessment of visibility. Use radar ranges of objects as they become visible or disappear to measure the visibility rather than guess it.

The key point is that radar is listed as something to take into account. It is not a licence for higher speed. Rule 7 warns against assumptions based on scanty radar information. Many collisions in fog have involved vessels running fast because the radar picture looked clear.

Applying Rule 6 on watch: a step-by-step habit

Safe speed is reassessed continuously, not set once at the start of the watch. A practical routine:

  1. At handover, note the speed, the visibility, the traffic and the next hazards. Ask yourself whether the current speed still fits.
  2. Check visibility objectively. Note the radar range at which targets come into sight. At night, step outside the wheelhouse and away from deck light glare.
  3. Count and classify traffic. Look for fishing concentrations, crossing ferries, and small craft that AIS will not show.
  4. Know your stopping and turning ability today. Consider displacement, trim, wind and how the engine is configured. Ask whether the engine is on bridge control, whether it is ready for immediate manoeuvre, and how long a crash stop or a large alteration would take.
  5. Look ahead on the passage plan. Check for narrow channels, TSS entries, shallow patches, and pilot stations where speed must come down.
  6. Decide and act early. If speed must come down, tell the engine room and the Master per the standing orders. Reduce in good time rather than as a last-minute crash stop.
  7. Record it. Log changes in speed and the reason, especially in restricted visibility.

In restricted visibility, Rule 6 works together with Rule 19(b). That rule requires a safe speed adapted to the conditions and, for power-driven vessels, engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. Rule 8(e) also requires you to slacken speed, or take all way off, if that is needed to avoid collision or allow more time to assess. Company SMS and Master's standing orders usually set specific triggers, such as a visibility threshold for calling the Master or putting the engine on standby. These triggers vary by company.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Fog bank off a busy coast. Your loaded bulk carrier is at full sea speed in clear weather with a coastal TSS ahead. Visibility drops quickly to about a mile. - Call the Master per the standing orders and get the engine ready for immediate manoeuvre. - Start fog signals (Rule 35), post an additional look-out, and switch to hand steering if your procedures require it. - Reduce speed to one at which you can deal with a close-quarters situation developing from a small target first detected late. Account for your loaded stopping distance. - Use radar ranges of targets coming into sight to confirm the actual visibility.

Scenario 2: Clear night, fishing fleet, shore lights behind. Visibility is excellent, but dozens of fishing vessels are working between you and a brightly lit coast. - Background light makes their lights hard to read, and many may not be on AIS. - Traffic density and background light both point to lower speed and more sea room, even though visibility is good.

Scenario 3: Shallow approach channel. You have a moderate under-keel clearance and a strong cross current. - Excessive speed increases squat and reduces UKC. - Going too slow loses steerage and increases drift towards the channel edge. - Here the safe speed is a band, not simply the slowest speed. Agree it with the pilot and keep a watch on UKC and cross-track error.

Scenario 4: Heavy clutter, small craft area. A high sea state is producing heavy sea clutter near a yachting area. - Small GRP craft may not show inside the clutter. - Lower speed buys time to detect them late and still act effectively.

Common mistakes

  • Treating safe speed as a restricted-visibility rule only. Rule 6 applies at all times. Clear-weather collisions in dense traffic often involve excessive speed.
  • Equating sea speed or charter speed with safe speed. Commercial pressure does not change the Rule. Under the ISM Code the Master has the overriding authority to act for safety.
  • Assuming the radar sees everything. Small craft, ice and debris can be missed, especially in clutter.
  • Relying on AIS to judge traffic density. Many small vessels and fishing boats do not transmit.
  • Forgetting that too slow can be unsafe. Loss of steerage, excessive leeway, or being unable to clear another vessel's path in time are all hazards.
  • Late reduction. Slowing down only when a close-quarters situation has already developed removes options instead of creating them.
  • Not telling the engine room. An unannounced manoeuvre from full sea speed can trip machinery or be slower than you expect.

Oral exam points

Typical examiner questions and what a good answer includes:

  • Define safe speed. Paraphrase both limbs: proper and effective action to avoid collision, and being stopped within a distance appropriate to the conditions. Stress that it applies at all times.
  • Is there a maximum speed in fog? No figure exists in the Rules. Explain the factors and refer to Rule 19(b) and any local regulations.
  • List the factors. Give all six general factors and the six radar factors. Mention that the list is not exhaustive.
  • Does radar allow higher speed? No. Radar limitations are themselves factors, and Rule 7 forbids assumptions on scanty radar information.
  • Can a vessel go too slowly? Yes. Give the examples of steerage, current and leeway, and the need to keep out of the way in time.
  • How do you know your stopping distance? Name the manoeuvring information on board, and explain that conditions on the day may differ from trial data.
  • Which Rules link to Rule 6? Rules 5, 7, 8(e) and 19(b), plus Rule 1(b) for local speed limits.

Examiners want reasoning tied to the conditions you describe. A memorised list alone will not satisfy them, so practise talking through a scenario aloud.

Key points
  • Rule 6 applies to every vessel, at all times and in any visibility.
  • Safe speed means you can act effectively to avoid collision and stop within a distance suited to the conditions.
  • The Rule gives no speed figure; you judge it from the listed factors and any other relevant ones.
  • Radar limitations are factors in themselves; radar is not a reason to go faster.
  • Too slow can also be unsafe in current, wind or narrow waters.
  • Local and VTS speed limits apply in addition to Rule 6, not instead of it.
  • Reassess speed continuously, reduce early, and log your reasons.

Frequently asked questions

What is the safe speed in fog under COLREG?

The COLREG does not give a figure. Rule 6 requires a speed at which you can take effective action to avoid collision and stop within a distance appropriate to the conditions. Rule 19(b) adds that the speed must suit restricted visibility and that power-driven vessels must have engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. Company standing orders often add specific procedures.

Does Rule 6 apply in clear visibility?

Yes. Rule 6 is in Section I of Part B, which applies in any condition of visibility. Traffic density, background lights, depth and manoeuvrability can all require a lower speed on a clear day or night.

Can a ship be going too slow under Rule 6?

Yes. Safe speed is about being able to act effectively. If a vessel is so slow that she loses steerage, sets towards danger or cannot keep out of the way in time, that speed is not safe either.

Does having radar allow a vessel to proceed faster?

Not as such. Rule 6(b) lists radar limitations as factors, including the risk that small craft, ice and floating objects may not be detected in time. Radar can help you measure visibility more accurately, but it does not replace a cautious speed.

If the port speed limit is 10 knots, is 10 knots automatically safe?

No. Local speed limits set by appropriate authorities, whose special rules Rule 1(b) preserves, are a maximum, not a guarantee. If visibility, traffic or depth call for less, Rule 6 still requires you to go slower.

Test yourself

In which conditions of visibility does Rule 6 apply?

Rule 6 is in Part B, Section I, which applies in any condition of visibility (COLREG Rule 4).

Which of these is a factor listed for ALL vessels in Rule 6(a)?

Draught against available depth is a Rule 6(a) factor; range scale constraints belong to Rule 6(b) for radar-equipped vessels. AIS and charter terms are not listed factors.

What maximum speed does Rule 6 set for restricted visibility?

Rule 6 contains no numerical limit; safe speed is judged against the listed factors (COLREG Rule 6, with Rule 19(b)).

How does Rule 6 treat a vessel's radar when determining safe speed?

Rule 6(b) lists radar characteristics, limitations and the possibility of missing small vessels and ice as factors; Rule 7 forbids assumptions on scanty radar information.

A stand-on vessel in a crossing situation in clear weather is proceeding fast through dense traffic. Which statement is correct?

Rule 6 applies to every vessel at all times; being stand-on under Rule 17 does not excuse an unsafe speed.
