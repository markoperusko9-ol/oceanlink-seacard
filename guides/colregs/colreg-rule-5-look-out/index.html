COLREG Rule 5 – Look-out: what it requires and how to apply it on watch

Quick answer

Rule 5 requires every vessel, at all times, to keep a proper look-out by sight and hearing and by all other means suited to the conditions. The purpose is to fully appraise the situation and the risk of collision. STCW watchkeeping principles set out who may act as look-out and when. Under STCW, the officer of the watch may be the sole look-out only in daylight, and only under strict conditions.

What Rule 5 actually says

Rule 5 is one sentence long, but nearly every collision inquiry comes back to it. In plain terms, it requires that:

  • every vessel keeps a proper look-out;
  • at all times, not just at night, in fog or in heavy traffic;
  • by sight and hearing, the two senses named in the rule;
  • and by all available means appropriate in the prevailing circumstances and conditions, such as radar, ARPA, AIS, VHF and ECDIS;
  • so as to make a full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of collision.

The last part is the purpose of the rule. A look-out is not someone standing on the bridge. It is the process of gathering information early enough to judge risk of collision under Rule 7 and act under Rule 8.

Rule 5 sits in Part B, Section I. That means it applies in any condition of visibility (Rule 4). It applies to all vessels on the high seas and connected navigable waters (Rule 1). A failure of look-out is also a classic case of the "neglect of any precaution" that Rule 2 warns against.

Breaking the rule into its parts

Examiners like candidates who can take the rule apart. Work through it phrase by phrase.

"Every vessel" This covers power-driven, sailing, fishing, NUC and RAM vessels alike. Being a stand-on vessel or a privileged vessel does not excuse a poor look-out.

"At all times" - It applies at anchor, drifting and waiting for orders, as well as underway. - An anchored ship still needs to detect vessels closing on her and to notice her own dragging.

"Sight" - Use your eyes and binoculars, and scan all round the horizon, not just ahead. - Move about the bridge to cover blind sectors caused by masts, cranes, deck cargo or container stacks.

"Hearing" - Fog signals, other ships' whistles, breaking surf and VHF traffic all count. - A closed wheelhouse with the air-conditioning running can make the bridge effectively deaf. - Ships with totally enclosed bridges generally carry a sound reception system under SOLAS Chapter V. Know where it is and check it is switched on.

"All available means appropriate" - This includes radar on suitable ranges, ARPA, AIS, VHF, ECDIS, NAVTEX and navigational warnings. - "Available" means that if the equipment is fitted and working, you are expected to use it sensibly. - "Appropriate" means using it in a way that fits the conditions. For example, a long-range scale for early warning and a short-range scale for small targets in clutter.

"Full appraisal" The look-out must give enough information to judge the whole situation. That includes shipping, but also navigational hazards, persons in the water, debris and changes in weather or visibility.

Who keeps the look-out: the STCW watchkeeping principles

COLREG says a proper look-out must be kept. STCW Code Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1, adds how the watch should be arranged.

Purpose of the look-out The purpose goes beyond collision avoidance. It includes:

  • continuous vigilance by sight, hearing and all other available means;
  • appraising the risk of collision, stranding and other dangers;
  • detecting ships or aircraft in distress, shipwrecked persons, wrecks and debris.

Full attention, no conflicting duties The look-out must be able to give full attention to the job. They must not be given tasks that interfere with it.

Helmsman and look-out are separate duties The helmsman is not counted as the look-out while steering. The only exception is a small ship with an unobstructed all-round view from the steering position, and no impairment of night vision or other obstacle to keeping a look-out.

OOW as sole look-out: daylight only The OOW may be the sole look-out in daylight only, and only when all three conditions are met:

  • the situation has been carefully assessed and established without doubt to be safe;
  • full account has been taken of weather, visibility, traffic density, nearby dangers and any traffic separation scheme;
  • assistance is immediately available to be summoned to the bridge when any change in the situation so requires.

Flag and company rules Many flag states and company SMS procedures are stricter than this minimum. Some forbid sole look-out entirely, or set fixed bridge manning levels for night, coastal waters and restricted visibility. Always follow the master's standing orders and your SMS. Where they differ from the STCW minimum, the stricter requirement applies on your ship.

Applying Rule 5 on the bridge: a practical routine

A good look-out is a habit, not a single act. On each watch:

  1. At handover, confirm who the look-out is, where they are posted and what they have been told. Agree how they report: relative bearing, rough distance, lights or aspect, and whether the bearing is changing.
  2. Protect night vision. Dim ECDIS, radar and console lighting. Keep the chartroom curtain closed. Do not let the look-out stare at a phone screen.
  3. Scan systematically. Sweep the horizon sector by sector, then the near field. Repeat with binoculars. Look astern and into blind sectors at regular intervals.
  4. Use radar properly. Switch between long and short range. Adjust sea and rain clutter so small targets are not suppressed. Remember that radar can miss wooden craft, small GRP boats and targets inside clutter.
  5. Treat AIS as an aid, not a look-out. Not every vessel carries AIS. Some have it switched off, and data can be wrong or delayed. Always confirm AIS targets against radar and visual sighting.
  6. Listen. In reduced visibility, open a wing door or post the look-out outside so fog signals can be heard.
  7. Re-assess as things change. Increasing traffic, a fishing fleet, falling visibility, approaching land or a TSS all call for more look-outs. Call the extra hand early, not late.
  8. Keep the bridge clear of distractions. Paperwork, phone calls and ECDIS route editing must never take priority over the look-out.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Clear night, open ocean, empty radar. The AB asks to go down and sweep the accommodation.

  • Correct action: Refuse. STCW does not allow the OOW to be sole look-out in darkness, whatever the traffic.
  • Why it matters: An empty radar screen does not prove an empty sea. Small craft without AIS may be invisible to radar in a moderate sea.

Scenario 2 – Daylight, good visibility, mid-ocean. You are sole look-out as your company SMS permits. Two hours later, several fishing vessels appear ahead and more echoes show on radar.

  • Correct action: Call the look-out to the bridge immediately and inform the master if standing orders require it.
  • Why it matters: The conditions that justified sole look-out no longer exist.

Scenario 3 – Visibility falls to about one mile in coastal waters. Typical actions include:

  1. Inform the master and comply with Rule 19.
  2. Reassess safe speed under Rule 6 and have engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  3. Post an additional look-out.
  4. Change to hand steering with a separate helmsman if standing orders or conditions require it; the helmsman does not count as the look-out.
  5. Open a wing door or post the look-out on the wing so fog signals can be heard, and start sounding your own.
  6. Exhibit navigation lights.
  7. Use radar on suitable ranges, with systematic plotting.

Scenario 4 – At anchor in a congested anchorage, night. Rule 5 still applies.

  • Keep a visual and radar watch for vessels approaching or manoeuvring nearby.
  • Monitor own position for dragging.
  • Listen on VHF to the port or VTS channel.

Scenario 5 – Container ship with a high deck stack. The view right ahead from the wheelhouse centreline is partly blocked.

  • Correct action: Use the bridge wings regularly to see past the stack. Brief the look-out to do the same. Pay extra attention to radar for the obscured sector.

Common mistakes that lead to collisions

Inquiry reports show the same failures again and again:

  • Relying on AIS or ARPA alone and not looking out of the window, especially in clear weather.
  • Giving the look-out other jobs, such as cleaning, paperwork or making tea, so they are on the bridge but not looking.
  • Ruined night vision from bright screens, chartroom lights or mobile phones.
  • A deaf bridge: closed doors in reduced visibility, sound reception system switched off, or loud music.
  • Unbriefed look-outs who see a light but do not report it, or report it too late.
  • Ignoring blind sectors behind masts, cranes or deck cargo.
  • Poorly set radar, such as too much clutter suppression or staying on one range, which hides small targets.
  • Fatigue, which reduces alertness even when everyone is in position.
  • Not increasing bridge manning as traffic, visibility or proximity to danger gets worse.
  • An OOW absorbed in other tasks, such as ECDIS route editing, emails or cargo calculations.

Oral exam: points examiners look for

Expect questions like these at OOW and Chief Mate/Master orals. Short, accurate answers win marks.

Q: State Rule 5 in your own words. Every vessel, at all times, must keep a proper look-out by sight, hearing and all appropriate available means. The aim is to fully appraise the situation and the risk of collision.

Q: Does Rule 5 apply at anchor or in clear weather? Yes, it applies "at all times" and, as a Section I rule, in any condition of visibility.

Q: Can the helmsman be the look-out? Not while steering, except on a small ship with an unobstructed all-round view from the steering position and no impairment of night vision or other impediment to keeping a proper look-out.

Q: Can you be the sole look-out? Only in daylight, under the STCW conditions described above, and only if flag and company rules permit it.

Q: What are "all available means"? Radar and ARPA, AIS, VHF, ECDIS, binoculars, sound reception and navigational warnings. Give the limits of each, especially radar on small targets and AIS on non-carrying vessels.

Q: How does Rule 5 link to other rules? - It supplies the information needed to judge risk of collision (Rule 7). - It affects safe speed (Rule 6). - It is critical in restricted visibility (Rule 19). - A poor look-out is a neglect of ordinary seamanship (Rule 2).

Q: How should a look-out report? As soon as they detect something, without waiting to be sure. They should give the object (light, vessel, buoy, debris), the relative bearing, the approximate distance and any change.

Key points
  • Rule 5 requires a proper look-out by sight, hearing and all appropriate available means, at all times and for every vessel.
  • The purpose is a full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of collision, which feeds directly into Rules 7 and 8.
  • Under STCW, the OOW may be sole look-out only in daylight, after careful assessment, with help immediately available.
  • The helmsman is not the look-out while steering, except on small ships with an unobstructed all-round view from the steering position and no impairment of night vision or other impediment to keeping a proper look-out.
  • AIS and radar support the visual and aural look-out; they never replace it.
  • In restricted visibility, make sure the bridge can actually hear fog signals.
  • Flag state rules and company SMS may be stricter than the STCW minimum; follow the stricter requirement.

Frequently asked questions

Can the officer of the watch be the sole look-out at night?

No. STCW Code Section A-VIII/2 allows the OOW to be the sole look-out only in daylight, and then only after careful assessment that it is safe. At night a separate look-out is required. Many company SMS procedures and flag administrations add stricter rules even for daylight.

Does Rule 5 apply when the ship is at anchor?

Yes. Rule 5 applies at all times to every vessel. At anchor you still need to watch for vessels approaching or manoeuvring nearby and to check your own ship is not dragging. Your anchor watch arrangements should reflect this.

Is using radar and AIS enough to meet Rule 5?

No. The rule names sight and hearing first and adds other available means on top. Radar can miss small or low targets, especially in clutter. AIS only shows vessels that carry it, have it switched on and broadcast correct data.

Can the helmsman also act as look-out?

Not while steering, except on small ships with an unobstructed all-round view from the steering position and no impairment of night vision or other impediment to keeping a proper look-out. On most merchant ships the duties are kept separate. That is why, when the steering goes into hand in fog, a separate look-out is still needed.

What should a look-out report and how?

Report every light, vessel, object, sound signal or hazard as soon as it is detected, without waiting to be certain. Give the relative bearing, approximate distance and any change in bearing or aspect. The OOW should acknowledge each report so the look-out knows it has been received.

Test yourself

Under STCW watchkeeping principles, when may the OOW act as the sole look-out?

Sole look-out is permitted only in daylight, after the situation has been carefully assessed and with assistance immediately available. Darkness or restricted visibility rule it out (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1).

When may the helmsman be considered the look-out while steering?

STCW keeps the helmsman and look-out duties separate. The only exception is small ships with an unobstructed all-round view from the steering position and no impairment of night vision or other impediment to keeping a proper look-out (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1).

A ship is lying at anchor in clear weather. Does Rule 5 apply?

Rule 5 applies to every vessel at all times. Section I rules apply in any condition of visibility (COLREG Rules 4 and 5).

Which statement best describes "all available means" in Rule 5?

Rule 5 requires sight and hearing plus all available means appropriate to the circumstances. Electronic aids add to the visual look-out and never replace it (COLREG Rule 5; see also Rule 7(b)).

In restricted visibility on a ship with an enclosed wheelhouse, how should the "hearing" part of Rule 5 be met?

Rule 5 expressly requires look-out by hearing. In restricted visibility the bridge team must be able to hear other vessels' fog signals (COLREG Rules 5, 19 and 35).
