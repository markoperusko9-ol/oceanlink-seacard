COLREG Rule 23 – Power-Driven Vessels Underway

Quick answer

Rule 23 requires a power-driven vessel underway to show a forward masthead light, sidelights and a sternlight, plus a second, higher masthead light further aft if she is 50 metres or more in length. Air-cushion vessels and WIG craft add a flashing light, and small craft under 12 m (and under 7 m) have simplified options. The lights stay on whenever she is underway, whether or not she is making way.

What Rule 23 says, in plain terms

Rule 23 sets out the basic lights for any power-driven vessel that is underway. Under Rule 3, a power-driven vessel is any vessel propelled by machinery. Underway means not at anchor, not made fast to the shore and not aground. Most of the other lighting rules build on this basic set.

Paragraph (a): the standard set

  • A masthead light forward.
  • A second masthead light abaft of and higher than the forward one. A vessel under 50 m is not obliged to carry this light but may do so.
  • Sidelights.
  • A sternlight.

Paragraph (b): air-cushion vessels. When operating in the non-displacement mode (up on the cushion), she shows the (a) lights plus an all-round flashing yellow light.

Paragraph (c): WIG craft. Only when taking off, landing and in flight near the surface, she shows the (a) lights plus a high-intensity all-round flashing red light.

Paragraph (d): small craft

  • Under 12 m: she may show an all-round white light and sidelights instead of the (a) lights.
  • Under 7 m with a maximum speed not over 7 knots: she may show just an all-round white light, and should also show sidelights if practicable.
  • Under 12 m: the masthead or all-round white light may be off the centreline if centreline fitting is not practicable. In that case the sidelights must be combined in one lantern on the centreline, or as nearly as practicable in the same fore-and-aft line as the white light.

Rule 23 prescribes no day shape for an ordinary power-driven vessel underway. By day, the absence of a shape tells you something too.

Arcs, ranges and positioning you must know

Rule 23 tells you which lights to show. Rules 21 and 22 and Annex I tell you how they look. Examiners always link them.

Arcs (Rule 21)

  • Masthead light: 225°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on each side.
  • Sidelights: 112.5° each, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam on their own side. Green is starboard, red is port.
  • Sternlight: 135°, centred right aft (67.5° each side).
  • Flashing light (air-cushion, WIG): 120 or more flashes per minute.

Minimum ranges (Rule 22)

Vessel length Masthead Sidelights Sternlight All-round
50 m or more 6 nm 3 nm 3 nm 3 nm
20 m to under 50 m 5 nm 2 nm 2 nm 2 nm
12 m to under 20 m 3 nm 2 nm 2 nm 2 nm
Under 12 m 2 nm 1 nm 2 nm 2 nm

Positioning (Annex I)

  • When two masthead lights are carried, the after one must be at least 4.5 m vertically higher than the forward one.
  • On a power-driven vessel, the horizontal distance between them must be at least half the vessel's length, but need not exceed 100 m.
  • The forward light must be no more than a quarter of the length from the stem.
  • The aim is that, in normal trim, the after light is seen above and separate from the forward light at 1,000 m from the stem when viewed from sea level. This separation is what lets you read another ship's heading at night.

Applying it on the bridge

Your own lights

  • Under Rule 20, lights are shown from sunset to sunrise in all weathers. They must also be shown by day in restricted visibility, and may be shown in any other circumstance where you judge it necessary.
  • Switch from anchor lights to navigation lights when the anchor is aweigh. At that moment you are underway, even if you are not yet making way.
  • Check the navigation light panel at every watch handover. Most ocean-going ships have duplicated lanterns and failure alarms. Follow your company SMS for changeover, reporting to the master and log entries.
  • Under Rule 20, no other light may be shown if it could be mistaken for a prescribed light, impair its visibility or character, or interfere with the look-out. Badly aimed deck floodlights, galley lights and accommodation lights at sea are common culprits.

Reading another ship's lights

  • Two masthead lights in a range: almost certainly a power-driven vessel. She is likely to be 50 m or more, though smaller vessels may also carry two.
  • The lower light is the forward light. The way the lights 'open' tells you her heading. If the lower light is to the right of the higher one, she is heading to your right.
  • Sidelights confirm aspect. Green means you are on her starboard side. Red means you are on her port side. Both sidelights together mean she is pointing at you.
  • Masthead lights in line with both sidelights: head-on or nearly so. Rule 14 applies.
  • Sternlight only: you are more than 22.5° abaft her beam. If you are coming up with her, you are the overtaking vessel and Rule 13 applies.

Take compass bearings, plot or acquire the target on radar/ARPA, and watch the aspect change. Do not decide on lights alone. Rule 7 warns against assumptions based on scanty information.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: crossing from port

  • Situation: You are on passage at night. Fine on your port bow you see two white lights, the lower one to the right of the higher, with a green light below them. The compass bearing is steady and the range is closing.
  • Reading: This is a power-driven vessel heading across from your left to your right, showing you her starboard side. You are on her starboard side, so under Rule 15 she is the give-way vessel. You are stand-on under Rule 17.
  • Action: Hold course and speed, keep monitoring, and be ready to sound at least five short and rapid blasts if you are in doubt whether she is taking sufficient action (Rule 34(d)). If she does not act, you may take action under Rule 17(a)(ii). When she is so close that collision cannot be avoided by her action alone, you must act under Rule 17(b). Do not alter to port for a vessel on your port side (Rule 17(c)).

Scenario 2: head-on

  • Situation: Right ahead you see two white lights nearly in line, with red and green visible together.
  • Reading and action: This is a head-on situation under Rule 14. Each vessel alters to starboard. Make the alteration early and large enough to be readily apparent, and sound one short blast as required by Rule 34(a).

Scenario 3: one white light and a red

  • Situation: You see a single white light with a red sidelight.
  • Reading: This could be a power-driven vessel under 50 m that has chosen not to carry an after masthead light. It could also be a larger ship with a failed or obscured after light, or a small craft under 12 m with an all-round white light.
  • Action: Treat her as a power-driven vessel showing you her port side. Confirm with radar, AIS and bearings before relying on any assumption about her size.

Scenario 4: stopped at the pilot station

  • Situation: You are drifting off the pilot station, engines stopped.
  • Reading: You are underway but not making way. Keep all your Rule 23 lights on.
  • Action: Under Rule 35, your fog signal changes from one prolonged blast to two prolonged blasts at intervals of not more than two minutes. Your lights do not change.

Common mistakes

  • Switching off sidelights when stopped. A power-driven vessel underway but not making way still shows her full Rule 23 lights. Contrast this with Rule 26 (fishing) and Rule 27 (NUC), where sidelights and sternlight are shown only when making way.
  • Leaving anchor lights on after heaving up. Once the anchor is aweigh you are underway, so show navigation lights.
  • Getting the 50 m threshold wrong. The second masthead light is mandatory from 50 m. Don't confuse this with the 20 m threshold used elsewhere in Annex I and Rule 22.
  • Motor-sailing under sailing lights. A sailing vessel using her engine is a power-driven vessel and must show Rule 23 lights. By day, Rule 25(e) requires a cone, apex downwards, shown forward where it can best be seen (optional for vessels under 12 m).
  • Assuming a missing after masthead light means a small vessel. It may have failed. Lights give you clues, not certainties.
  • Forgetting that other rules modify Rule 23. A towing vessel under Rule 24 shows two or three masthead lights in a vertical line in place of the forward or after masthead light. A vessel constrained by her draught (Rule 28) shows Rule 23 lights and may add three all-round reds.
  • Treating an all-round white with sidelights as an anchored vessel. Close in, a small motor craft's lights can be misread. Use bearings and radar.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect rapid-fire questions, often with a lights board or flash cards. Have crisp answers ready:

  • 'Lights for a 45 m power-driven vessel underway?' A forward masthead light, sidelights and sternlight. She may also show a second masthead light abaft and higher.
  • 'Same vessel, 180 m?' Two masthead lights (the after one higher), sidelights and sternlight.
  • 'Give me the arcs and ranges.' Recite Rules 21 and 22 for her length.
  • 'She stops her engines. What changes?' The lights do not change. The fog signal changes from one prolonged blast to two prolonged blasts.
  • 'What is a hovercraft's extra light?' An all-round flashing yellow, when in non-displacement mode.
  • 'And a WIG craft?' A high-intensity all-round flashing red, only when taking off, landing or in flight near the surface.
  • 'Day signal for a power-driven vessel underway?' None under Rule 23.
  • 'Your forward masthead lantern fails at 0200. What do you do?' Change over to the standby lantern or circuit. Inform the master. Increase vigilance. Log the failure and the corrective action. Report to the company and authorities as your SMS and flag require.
  • 'You see a white light over a green, and a higher white to the left. What is she doing?' She is a power-driven vessel heading to your right, showing her starboard side. Check her bearing for risk of collision.

Examiners want the reasoning, not just the list. Always link what you see to the action rule it triggers: Rule 13, 14, 15, 17 or 19.

Key points
  • A power-driven vessel underway shows a forward masthead light, sidelights and a sternlight.
  • A second masthead light, abaft and higher, is mandatory from 50 m and optional below.
  • Lights stay the same when underway but not making way; only the fog signal changes.
  • Air-cushion vessels in non-displacement mode add an all-round flashing yellow; WIG craft add a high-intensity flashing red in the specified phases.
  • Vessels under 12 m may show an all-round white plus sidelights; under 7 m and 7 knots, an all-round white alone, with sidelights if practicable.
  • The lower masthead light is the forward one, and the way the lights open shows the other ship's heading.
  • Rule 23 has no day shape; confirm what lights tell you with bearings, radar and AIS (Rule 7).

Frequently asked questions

Does a vessel under 50 metres need two masthead lights?

No. Rule 23 requires only one masthead light forward for a power-driven vessel under 50 m, but she may carry a second, higher one aft. From 50 m, the second masthead light is mandatory.

Should a power-driven vessel switch off her sidelights when stopped in the water?

No. A power-driven vessel underway but not making way keeps all her Rule 23 lights on. Only her restricted-visibility sound signal changes, to two prolonged blasts under Rule 35. Switching off sidelights when stopped applies to categories such as fishing vessels and NUC vessels, not to ordinary power-driven vessels.

Is a sailing yacht using its engine a power-driven vessel?

Yes. Under Rule 3, a sailing vessel is one under sail with propelling machinery, if fitted, not being used. Once the engine is propelling her, she must show Rule 23 lights. By day, she shows a cone, apex downwards, under Rule 25(e); a vessel under 12 m is not required to but may do so.

What lights does a small motor boat under 12 m show at night?

She may show the full Rule 23(a) set, or an all-round white light and sidelights instead. If she is under 7 m and cannot exceed 7 knots, an all-round white alone is permitted, with sidelights if practicable. Local or national rules may add requirements for inland waters.

What should the OOW do if a navigation light fails at sea?

Change over to the standby lantern or circuit immediately, inform the master and keep a sharper look-out. Record the failure and the action taken in the log. Arrange repair and any reporting according to the company SMS and flag requirements.

Test yourself

A power-driven vessel of 40 m is underway at night. Which lights must she show as a minimum?

Under 50 m, the second masthead light is optional, but the forward masthead light, sidelights and sternlight are required. COLREG Rule 23(a).

A power-driven vessel of 150 m is underway but stopped, not making way. What lights does she show?

Rule 23 applies whenever she is underway, whether or not she is making way. Only her fog signal changes, under Rule 35(b).

What additional light does an air-cushion vessel show when operating in the non-displacement mode?

Rule 23(b) adds an all-round flashing yellow. The high-intensity flashing red is for WIG craft under Rule 23(c).

What is the minimum required range of the masthead light on a vessel of 50 m or more in length?

Rule 22(a) requires 6 nm for masthead lights on vessels of 50 m or more. 5 nm applies to vessels of 20 m to under 50 m.

At night you see two white lights, the lower one to the right of the higher one, with a green light below them. What is the other vessel doing?

The lower masthead light is the forward one, so she is heading to your right, and green shows her starboard side. COLREG Rules 21 and 23 and Annex I.
