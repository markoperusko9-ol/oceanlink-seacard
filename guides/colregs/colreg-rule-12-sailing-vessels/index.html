COLREG Rule 12 – Sailing Vessels: Who Gives Way Between Two Sailing Vessels

Quick answer

Rule 12 decides which of two sailing vessels in sight of each other keeps out of the way when risk of collision exists. If they have the wind on different sides, the one with the wind on the port side gives way. If they have the wind on the same side, the windward vessel gives way. A vessel with the wind on her port side that sees a vessel to windward and cannot determine with certainty which side that vessel has the wind on keeps out of the way. Wind side is judged by where the mainsail is carried, not by the actual wind. Rule 12 applies only between two vessels under sail. Overtaking, narrow channels, traffic separation schemes and restricted visibility are governed by other rules.

What Rule 12 actually says

Rule 12 sits in Part B, Section II. Under Rule 11, it applies only to vessels in sight of one another. It covers one situation: two sailing vessels approaching each other so as to involve risk of collision. One of them must keep out of the way, decided as follows:

  • Wind on different sides: the vessel with the wind on her port side keeps out of the way of the other.
  • Wind on the same side: the vessel to windward keeps out of the way of the vessel to leeward.
  • Doubt: a vessel with the wind on her port side may see a vessel to windward and be unable to tell which side that vessel has the wind on. In that case she keeps out of the way.

Paragraph (b) defines the windward side for this rule. It is the side opposite to where the mainsail is carried. On a square-rigged vessel, it is the side opposite to where the largest fore-and-aft sail is carried.

In yachting language, port tack gives way to starboard tack, and windward gives way to leeward.

Key definitions that make or break the answer

Sailing vessel (Rule 3(c)). This is any vessel under sail, provided that propelling machinery, if fitted, is not being used. A yacht with her sails up and her engine driving is a power-driven vessel for the Rules. Rule 12 does not apply to her.

Mainsail side, not true wind. The rule uses the boom and mainsail position so that both vessels can judge the matter by eye.

  • When running dead downwind, the wind is technically astern.
  • Under Rule 12, the wind is deemed to be on the side opposite the mainsail.
  • A yacht running with her main out to starboard is therefore treated as having the wind on her port side.

Square-riggers. The test is the largest fore-and-aft sail, often the spanker. It is not a square course.

Headsails and spinnakers do not decide the tack. Only the mainsail (or the largest fore-and-aft sail on a square-rigger) counts.

Port tack meets starboard tack Port tack vessel
Both on same tack Windward vessel
Port tack, other vessel to windward, her tack unclear Port tack vessel
One sailing vessel overtaking another Overtaking vessel (Rule 13)

Where Rule 12 stops: the rules that override or replace it

Examiners like this area, because Rule 12 is narrower than candidates think.

  • Overtaking (Rule 13). Rule 13 applies notwithstanding anything in Sections I and II. A sailing vessel overtaking another sailing vessel keeps out of the way, whatever tack either is on.
  • Narrow channels (Rule 9(b)). A sailing vessel must not impede a vessel that can safely navigate only within the channel. Its right under Rule 12 against another yacht does not change this.
  • Traffic separation schemes (Rule 10(j)). A sailing vessel must not impede the safe passage of a power-driven vessel following a traffic lane.
  • Responsibilities between vessels (Rule 18). Rule 18(b) has a vessel under sail keep out of the way of:
    • a vessel not under command;
    • a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre;
    • a vessel engaged in fishing.

Under Rule 18(d), she must also, if circumstances admit, avoid impeding a vessel constrained by her draught showing the Rule 28 signals. - Restricted visibility (Rule 19). When vessels are not in sight of one another, Section II does not apply at all, so neither does Rule 12. Every vessel, sail or power, acts under Rule 19. Sailing vessels sound one prolonged followed by two short blasts under Rule 35(c), not the power-driven signals. - Power-driven vessel vs sailing vessel. This is Rule 18(a), not Rule 12. The power-driven vessel keeps out of the way, except where Rules 9, 10 and 13 require otherwise.

Once Rule 12 fixes the roles, Rule 16 applies to the give-way vessel: take early and substantial action. Rule 17 applies to the stand-on vessel. Rules 2 and 8 still apply to both.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Different tacks. - Yacht A has her mainsail out to port, so the wind is on her starboard side. - Yacht B, crossing ahead, has her mainsail out to starboard, so the wind is on her port side. - B keeps out of the way. A is the stand-on vessel and acts under Rule 17.

Scenario 2: Same tack. - Both yachts carry their mainsails to port, so both have the wind on the starboard side. - Yacht C is upwind of Yacht D, and they are converging. - C (windward) keeps out of the way of D (leeward).

Scenario 3: Doubt. - At dusk, Yacht E has the wind on her port side. - She sees Yacht F to windward but cannot make out F's mainsail against the land. - E keeps out of the way. Rule 12(a)(iii) removes the guesswork. If she cannot tell, she treats herself as give-way.

Scenario 4: Overtaking. - Yacht G, on starboard tack, comes up from more than 22.5° abaft the beam of Yacht H, which is on port tack. - G keeps out of the way under Rule 13. Being on starboard tack does not help an overtaking vessel.

Scenario 5: Motor-sailing. - Yacht J has her sails set and her engine running. - She is crossing Yacht K, which is under sail only. - J is a power-driven vessel, so Rule 18(a) applies and J keeps out of the way. This assumes no overtaking or channel/TSS situation. By day, J should be showing a cone, apex downwards forward under Rule 25(e). Many do not.

Applying it from a merchant ship's bridge

A merchant OOW is rarely one of the two vessels in a Rule 12 situation. Still, knowing the rule lets you predict what yachts around you will do. That matters near coasts, in regatta areas and at harbour approaches.

  • Anticipate alterations. Two yachts converging near your track will usually resolve it under Rule 12. The give-way yacht may tack, gybe or bear away, possibly towards you. Leave sea room for that.
  • Do not assume the yacht is under sail. At night, sidelights and a sternlight (or a masthead tricolour) look the same whether the engine is running or not. A sailing vessel shows no masthead light. A white masthead light appearing with the sidelights means she is under power.
  • Tricolour plus deck-level lights. A yacht showing both at once is showing duplicate sidelights not provided for in Rule 25, which can mislead. Treat her intentions with caution.
  • Small targets. Yachts can give weak, intermittent radar returns in sea clutter, and not all carry AIS. Rule 5 lookout by sight and hearing is essential. Rule 7 warns against assumptions from scanty radar information.
  • Your own obligations do not change. If you are power-driven and not overtaking, and not in a channel or TSS where she must not impede you, you keep out of the way of a sailing vessel. Act early, make it obvious, and follow your company SMS and the master's standing orders on minimum CPA.
  • Where she must not impede you, as in Rules 9 and 10, you are not relieved of your duties if risk of collision still develops. Watch her, remain fully obliged to comply with the Part B rules when risk of collision exists (Rule 8(f)(iii)), and be ready to act under Rules 2 and 8.

On sail training ships, cadets may stand watch where Rule 12 applies directly. The largest fore-and-aft sail test matters there.

Common mistakes

  • Using the true wind instead of the mainsail. The deemed windward side is opposite the mainsail. When running, this gives a different answer from 'wind astern'.
  • Applying Rule 12 in fog. Rule 12 is a Section II rule. In restricted visibility, it is Rule 19 for everyone.
  • Forgetting overtaking. Being on starboard tack does not make an overtaking yacht the stand-on vessel.
  • Treating a motor-sailing yacht as a sailing vessel. If the machinery is in use, she is power-driven.
  • Thinking 'sail has right of way' over everything. Under Rule 18, a sailing vessel keeps clear of vessels that are not under command, restricted in their ability to manoeuvre, or fishing. Rules 9 and 10 stop her impeding ships in channels and lanes.
  • Stand-on vessel switching off. Rule 17 still applies. The stand-on yacht may act, and in the end must act, if the give-way vessel does not.
  • Confusing racing rules with COLREG. Yacht racing rules govern competitors among themselves under the event's terms. They do not bind a merchant ship, and COLREG still governs dealings with non-racing vessels.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Q: Two sailing vessels, wind on different sides. Who gives way? The one with the wind on her port side.

Q: Same side? The windward vessel keeps out of the way of the leeward one.

Q: How is windward determined for Rule 12? It is the side opposite to where the mainsail is carried. For a square-rigger, it is the side opposite the largest fore-and-aft sail.

Q: What if the port-tack vessel cannot tell the other's tack? If the other is to windward and her tack is uncertain, the port-tack vessel keeps out of the way.

Q: Does Rule 12 apply in restricted visibility? No. It is a Section II rule for vessels in sight of one another. Rule 19 applies.

Q: Sound signal for a sailing vessel underway in restricted visibility? One prolonged followed by two short blasts, at intervals of not more than two minutes (Rule 35(c)).

Q: Lights of a sailing vessel underway? Sidelights and a sternlight. Under 20 m, these may be combined in one masthead lantern. Red over green all-round at the masthead is optional, but not together with the combined lantern (Rule 25).

Q: Shape for a vessel under sail and power? A cone, apex downwards, forward where it can best be seen (Rule 25(e)).

Expect the examiner to rotate a model or flashcard and ask you to read the mainsail side. Say your reasoning aloud: 'Main is to starboard, so wind is deemed port side, so she gives way.'

Key points
  • Rule 12 applies only between two sailing vessels in sight of one another with risk of collision.
  • Wind on different sides: the vessel with the wind on her port side keeps out of the way.
  • Wind on the same side: the windward vessel keeps out of the way of the leeward vessel.
  • If on port tack and unsure of a windward vessel's tack, keep out of the way.
  • Windward is judged by the mainsail (or the largest fore-and-aft sail on a square-rigger), not the true wind.
  • Overtaking (Rule 13) overrides Rule 12, and Rule 12 does not apply in restricted visibility when vessels are not in sight (Rule 19). Rules 9(b) and 10(j) still require a sailing vessel not to impede vessels confined to a channel or following a traffic lane, whatever her tack.
  • A yacht using her engine, even with sails set, is a power-driven vessel.

Frequently asked questions

Does starboard tack always have right of way?

No. Starboard tack only stands on against a port-tack sailing vessel in a crossing or converging situation in sight. If the starboard-tack vessel is overtaking, Rule 13 makes her keep out of the way. She must also keep clear of vessels not under command, restricted in their ability to manoeuvre, or engaged in fishing, and must not impede ships in narrow channels or traffic lanes.

How do I tell which side the wind is on when a yacht is running downwind?

Look at the mainsail. Rule 12(b) deems the windward side to be opposite to the side on which the mainsail is carried. A yacht running with her main out to port is treated as having the wind on her starboard side, even though the wind is actually astern.

Is a yacht with sails up and engine running a sailing vessel?

No. Rule 3(c) defines a sailing vessel as under sail with propelling machinery, if fitted, not being used. With the engine propelling her, she is power-driven and the Rules for power-driven vessels apply. By day she should show a cone, apex downwards, forward where it can best be seen (Rule 25(e)).

Does Rule 12 apply in fog?

No. Rule 12 is in Section II, which applies only to vessels in sight of one another. In restricted visibility, when vessels are not in sight, all vessels follow Rule 19, and there is no stand-on or give-way vessel.

As OOW of a power-driven ship, do I use Rule 12 with yachts?

Not directly. Your relationship with a sailing vessel is set by Rule 18(a), subject to Rules 9, 10 and 13. Knowing Rule 12 helps you predict how two yachts near your track will resolve their own encounter, so you can allow room for their alterations.

Test yourself

Two sailing vessels are crossing with risk of collision. Vessel A carries her mainsail to starboard; Vessel B carries hers to port. Which vessel keeps out of the way?

With her mainsail to starboard, A is deemed to have the wind on her port side, so she keeps out of the way. COLREG Rule 12(a)(i) and (b).

Two sailing vessels on the same tack are converging. Which keeps out of the way?

When both have the wind on the same side, the windward vessel keeps out of the way of the leeward vessel. COLREG Rule 12(a)(ii).

For Rule 12, how is the windward side of a fore-and-aft rigged vessel determined?

Rule 12(b) deems the windward side to be opposite to the mainsail side. For square-riggers, the largest fore-and-aft sail is used. COLREG Rule 12(b).

A sailing vessel on starboard tack is overtaking another sailing vessel on port tack. Which keeps out of the way?

Rule 13 applies notwithstanding the other rules of Sections I and II, so the overtaking vessel keeps clear whatever her tack. COLREG Rule 13(a).

In restricted visibility, two sailing vessels detect each other only by radar. What governs their conduct?

Rule 12 is in Section II and applies only to vessels in sight of one another (Rule 11). When not in sight, Rule 19 applies. COLREG Rules 11 and 19.
