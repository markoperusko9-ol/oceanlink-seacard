COLREG Rule 22 – Visibility of Lights

Quick answer

Rule 22 sets the minimum ranges at which each navigation light must be visible, depending on the vessel's length: 50 m or more, 12 m to under 50 m, or under 12 m. The ranges are design minimums calculated under Annex I Section 8 for a dark night in clear air. They do not tell you how far off a target is when you first see its lights, so a watchkeeper must never estimate distance from them.

What Rule 22 actually says

Rule 22 requires the lights prescribed in the Rules to have the intensity set out in Section 8 of Annex I. That intensity must make them visible at least at the ranges listed below. All ranges are in nautical miles. The rule groups vessels by length.

Light 50 m or more 12 m to under 50 m Under 12 m
Masthead light 6 5 (3 if under 20 m) 2
Sidelight 3 2 1
Sternlight 3 2 2
Towing light 3 2 2
All-round light (white, red, green or yellow) 3 2 2

There is also a separate category in Rule 22(d). Inconspicuous, partly submerged vessels or objects being towed must show a white all-round light visible at a minimum of 3 miles. This applies whatever the size of the object.

The rule only sets ranges. Rule 21 says what each light is (colour and arc). The rules in Part C, such as Rules 23 to 30, say which lights a vessel must show. Annex I covers positioning, intensity, sectors and approval.

Where the ranges come from: Annex I Section 8

The ranges are not a promise that you will see a light at that distance. They are a design standard. Annex I Section 8 gives a formula linking luminous intensity to range. The formula assumes two things:

  • a dark night with a defined illuminance threshold at the observer's eye
  • an atmospheric transmissivity factor of 0.8, which Annex I equates to a meteorological visibility of roughly 13 nautical miles

Annex I also includes a table of minimum luminous intensities. These rise steeply with range. A 6-mile light needs far more than twice the intensity of a 3-mile light, because the atmosphere absorbs more light over longer distances.

Other points in Annex I that go with Rule 22:

  • Horizontal sectors (Section 9): intensity must fall away at the edges of a light's arc so the arc is clear-cut. This matters most for sidelights, because their cut-off is what tells you the other ship's aspect.
  • Vertical sectors (Section 10): lights must keep their required intensity when the vessel rolls and pitches, within the angles Annex I specifies.
  • Glare: Annex I limits the maximum intensity to avoid undue glare. It does not allow this to be done by a variable intensity control.
  • Approval (Section 14): construction and installation of lights must satisfy the flag Administration.

Annex I also sets some ranges outside Rule 22. The manoeuvring light under Rule 34(b) is specified in Annex I. Annex II says the additional signals for fishing vessels fishing in close proximity are visible at a lesser range than the vessel's main fishing lights.

Applying Rule 22 on the bridge

1. Never judge range from first sighting. The minimum ranges assume clear air and a dark background. In practice many lights, especially modern LEDs, are seen well beyond the minimum. On the other hand, the following can cut detection range sharply:

  • haze, rain or salt-caked lenses
  • shore lights behind the target
  • your own deck lighting

Use radar, ARPA and compass bearings for range and risk of collision, as Rule 7 requires.

2. Use the minimums as a planning floor. If a light can lawfully be visible at only 2 or 1 mile, a small craft may be very close before you see it at all. This feeds into look-out (Rule 5) and safe speed (Rule 6), particularly in fishing areas and approaches.

3. Remember that aspect comes later than detection. On a large ship you will usually pick up the masthead lights first. The sidelights, which tell you which way she is heading, need only reach half that range. On vessels of 50 m or more, the alignment of the two masthead lights gives an early indication of heading, but only the sidelights confirm it. Treat aspect as provisional until a sidelight is seen.

4. Keep your own lights at standard. Typical routine checks include:

  • testing the navigation light panel and its failure alarms
  • confirming that spare lamps or spare lanterns are on board and correct
  • keeping lenses clean
  • making sure deck lights do not obscure or swamp the navigation lights

Replacement lamps or LED units must be of a type approved for that lantern. A cheaper unit may not give the Rule 22 intensity or the Annex I sector cut-off. Check the company SMS and the flag Administration's requirements for type approval and spares.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Two large ships head-on. Two power-driven vessels, each over 50 m, are approaching end-on at 15 knots each. The closing speed is 30 knots, or half a mile a minute.

  • If the masthead lights are only just at their 6-mile minimum, you have about 12 minutes from first sighting to collision.
  • The sidelights need only be visible at 3 miles. That leaves about 6 minutes after you can confirm red and green together.

The lesson is to act on radar information and the masthead light range and bearing. Do not wait for the sidelights to confirm the situation.

Scenario 2 – Small fishing craft at night. You are on a 180 m bulk carrier at 14 knots off a coast with small open fishing boats under 12 m.

  • A power-driven boat of that size need only show a masthead light visible at 2 miles and sidelights at 1 mile.
  • Smaller craft may show even less: under 7 m and not more than 7 knots, only an all-round white light (Rule 23(d)(ii)); under oars, possibly just a torch (Rule 25(d)(ii)).
  • If the boat is crossing slowly ahead, the sidelights may appear only about four minutes before you reach her position.
  • Wave clutter may hide a small wooden hull on radar.

Here Rules 5 and 6 come in: a dedicated look-out, reduced speed in dense small-craft areas, and the Master called early.

Scenario 3 – Tug and awkward tow. A 30 m tug is towing a partly submerged pontoon. Under Rule 22:

  • the tug's masthead lights need to reach 5 miles
  • its sidelights, sternlight and yellow towing light need to reach 2 miles
  • the white all-round light on the pontoon needs to reach 3 miles

The tow's light may therefore carry further than the tug's own sternlight. Read the whole picture, and do not pass astern of a tug without confirming where the tow is.

Scenario 4 – Anchored vessel versus underway. A 200 m ship at anchor shows all-round white anchor lights, each with a 3-mile minimum. Underway, the same ship's masthead light carries 6 miles. An anchored ship can therefore appear later than you would expect from her size. This is especially true near shore lights, so cross-check with radar, AIS and the chart.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the ranges as detection ranges. They are minimum design ranges under defined conditions, not what you will see tonight.
  • Forgetting the 20 m split. A vessel from 12 m to under 20 m needs a masthead light of only 3 miles, not 5.
  • Mixing up sternlight and sidelight ranges for small craft. Under 12 m, the sidelights need 1 mile but the sternlight needs 2.
  • Assuming yellow and red all-round lights carry less far. In each length band, Rule 22 treats all all-round lights the same, whatever their colour.
  • Sizing a towed object's light by its length. Rule 22(d) gives a flat 3 miles for inconspicuous, partly submerged towed objects.
  • Fitting unapproved replacements. Any LED or lamp swap must keep the lantern within the Annex I requirements and flag approval.

Oral exam points

Examiners often ask for the table, then test whether you understand what it means. Be ready for these:

  • Q: Minimum range of the masthead light on a 45 m vessel?
    • A: 5 miles. If she were under 20 m, it would be 3 miles.
  • Q: Are these ranges guaranteed in all weather?
    • A: No. They are minimums calculated in Annex I Section 8 for a dark night with a transmissivity of 0.8, about 13 miles meteorological visibility. Real range varies with atmosphere and background.
  • Q: Which annex section links intensity to range?
    • A: Annex I, Section 8.
  • Q: Why does sidelight cut-off matter?
    • A: The sector boundaries tell you the other vessel's aspect. Annex I Section 9 requires intensity to drop sharply just outside the prescribed arc.
  • Q: Who approves the lights fitted on board?
    • A: The Administration of the flag State, under Annex I Section 14.
  • Q: What is the range for the white light on a partly submerged towed object?
    • A: At least 3 miles, all-round, regardless of the object's size.

Give answers in nautical miles and quote the length bands precisely. Examiners mark down "about 50 metres" style answers.

Key points
  • Rule 22 sets minimum visibility ranges for navigation lights in three length bands: 50 m or more, 12 m to under 50 m, and under 12 m.
  • For 50 m or more: masthead 6 miles; sidelights, sternlight, towing light and all-round lights 3 miles.
  • For 12 m to under 50 m: masthead 5 miles (3 if under 20 m); all others 2 miles.
  • For under 12 m: masthead 2 miles, sidelights 1 mile, sternlight, towing and all-round lights 2 miles.
  • Inconspicuous, partly submerged towed objects show a white all-round light visible at 3 miles minimum.
  • Ranges are design minimums from Annex I Section 8 for a dark night in clear air, never a tool for judging distance.
  • Lights and replacements must meet Annex I and be approved by the flag Administration.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum range of a masthead light under COLREG Rule 22?

It depends on vessel length. The minimum is 6 miles for 50 m or more. It is 5 miles for 12 m to under 50 m, reduced to 3 miles if the vessel is under 20 m. It is 2 miles for vessels under 12 m.

Can I estimate the distance to a ship from when I first see her lights?

No. Rule 22 ranges are minimum design ranges under defined clear-night conditions. Actual sighting range varies widely with lantern type, haze, rain and background lighting. Use radar, ARPA and bearings to determine range and risk of collision.

Why are sidelights visible at a shorter range than masthead lights?

Rule 22 simply sets lower minimum ranges for sidelights in every length band. In practice the masthead lights are usually seen first. A ship's aspect often cannot be confirmed until she is considerably closer, so early action must be based on radar and bearing information.

Do LED navigation lights have to meet Rule 22?

Yes. Any light, LED or filament, must give at least the Annex I Section 8 intensity for its required range. It must also meet the Annex I sector and glare requirements. Construction and installation must be to the satisfaction of the flag Administration, which normally means fitting only replacement units of an approved type for that lantern. Check flag and SMS requirements.

What light range applies to a partly submerged object being towed?

Rule 22(d) requires a white all-round light visible at a minimum of 3 miles. This applies regardless of the object's size. Rule 24 sets out how and where such objects must be lit.

Test yourself

What is the minimum required visibility of the masthead light on a 60 m power-driven vessel?

For vessels of 50 m or more in length, the masthead light must be visible at a minimum of 6 miles (COLREG Rule 22(a)).

What is the minimum required visibility of the masthead light on a 15 m power-driven vessel?

In the 12 m to under 50 m band the masthead light needs 5 miles, except that vessels under 20 m need only 3 miles (COLREG Rule 22(b)).

What is the minimum required visibility of a sidelight on a vessel under 12 m?

Vessels under 12 m need sidelights visible at a minimum of 1 mile, although their sternlight needs 2 miles (COLREG Rule 22(c)).

What do the Rule 22 visibility ranges represent?

Annex I Section 8 derives the required intensity from a formula assuming a dark night and atmospheric transmissivity of 0.8, so the ranges are design minimums only (COLREG Rule 22 and Annex I Section 8).

What light must an inconspicuous, partly submerged object being towed show under Rule 22?

Rule 22(d) requires a white all-round light visible at a minimum of 3 miles, regardless of the object's size (COLREG Rule 22(d)).
