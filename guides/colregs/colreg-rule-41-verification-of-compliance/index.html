COLREG Rule 41 – Verification of compliance

Quick answer

Rule 41 requires every Contracting Party (the governments, not the ships) to undergo periodic IMO audits that check how it implements and enforces the Collision Regulations. The audits are run under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme using the III Code as the standard. Rule 41 places no direct duty on the officer of the watch, but it is examinable, and it explains why flag administrations keep tightening their oversight.

What Rule 41 says, in plain terms

Rule 41 is the last rule in the COLREGs. It sits in Part F – Verification of compliance with the provisions of the Convention. Paraphrased, it contains four points:

  • Every Contracting Party is subject to periodic audits by IMO. The audits measure the state against the audit standard and check whether it complies with and implements the Convention.
  • The IMO Secretary-General administers the Audit Scheme, based on guidelines developed by the Organization.
  • Every Contracting Party must facilitate the audit. It must also implement a programme of actions to address the findings, again following IMO guidelines.
  • Audits of all Contracting Parties follow an overall schedule drawn up by the Secretary-General and are conducted at periodic intervals, both taking IMO guidelines into account.

The word that matters is Contracting Party. Rule 41 is addressed to governments that have accepted the COLREG Convention. It is not addressed to masters, owners or watchkeepers. Nothing in it changes how you keep a lookout, assess risk of collision or show lights.

Where Part F came from and how Rules 39–41 fit together

Part F is not part of the original 1972 text. The IMO Assembly added it by amendment in Resolution A.1085(28), and it entered into force on 1 January 2016. The timing matched the date the IMO Member State Audit Scheme (IMSAS) became mandatory. Similar verification-of-compliance provisions were inserted into other mandatory IMO instruments at the same time. Examples are SOLAS (new Chapter XIII) and STCW (Regulation I/16).

The three rules work as a set:

Rule What it does
Rule 39 – Definitions Defines Audit, Audit Scheme, Code (the IMO Instruments Implementation Code, or III Code) and Audit Standard (the III Code)
Rule 40 – Application Requires Contracting Parties to use the III Code when carrying out their obligations under the Convention
Rule 41 – Verification of compliance Makes Contracting Parties subject to periodic IMO audits against that standard

Put simply, Rule 40 sets the expectation (implement COLREG using the III Code) and Rule 41 checks it (IMO audits you on it). The detailed audit procedure is not in the COLREGs. It is in IMO's Framework and Procedures for the Audit Scheme, Resolution A.1067(28).

What an IMO audit actually looks at

Auditors do not board your ship and check your sidelights. They examine the state's systems for implementing the Convention. Depending on the roles the state plays, those systems can cover its work as flag state, coastal state and port state. Under the III Code this broadly means questions such as:

  • Has the state given effect to COLREG in its national law, including the amendments?
  • Does it have the people, procedures and records to make sure ships on its register comply? Lights, shapes and sound-signalling appliances are the obvious COLREG items.
  • How does it handle exemptions under Rule 38 and equivalents under Rule 1(e)?
  • Does it investigate marine casualties, including collisions, and act on the findings?
  • Does it use its enforcement powers, and does it oversee any recognized organizations it delegates work to?
  • Does it review its own performance and correct weaknesses?

After the audit, the state must put together a corrective action plan for any findings. That plan is the programme of actions Rule 41 refers to. Follow-up arrangements are set by the IMO procedures, not by the COLREGs themselves.

How it touches the bridge in practice

Rule 41 has no direct bridge application. Say so clearly in an exam. The examiner usually wants to see that you understand who the rule binds. Even so, it reaches you indirectly:

  • Flag oversight. An administration that knows its own implementation will be audited has good reason to make its surveys and inspections thorough. Expect thorough checks of navigation lights, shapes, whistle, bell and gong arrangements against Annex I and Annex III.
  • Flag circulars and national rules. Corrective actions after an audit can show up as new or revised flag requirements or guidance. Follow your company SMS and current flag circulars. What changes, and when, depends on the individual administration.
  • Casualty reporting. If your ship is in a collision or near miss, the flag's investigation process is one of the things an audit can examine. Accurate logbook entries, preserved VDR data and honest reports support that system.
  • Records of exemptions. If your ship holds an exemption or equivalent arrangement for lights or sound signals, keep the document on board and know what it covers. A flag inspector or PSC officer may ask for it.

Rule 41 is not Port State Control, an ISM audit, a vetting inspection or a flag survey. Those inspect ships. IMSAS audits governments.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – The trap question. The examiner asks: "You are OOW in restricted visibility. A vessel is detected by radar alone forward of the beam. Which rules apply, and does Rule 41 change anything?" Answer: Rule 19 governs your conduct. Rules 5, 6, 7 and 8 apply alongside it, and Rule 35 covers your sound signals. Rule 41 changes nothing. It concerns IMO audits of Contracting Parties and imposes no duty on the vessel.

Scenario 2 – A flag inspection after a change of register. Your ship has recently changed flag. The new administration's inspector spends a long time checking the arcs and positioning of navigation lights and asks to see documentation for a non-standard masthead light arrangement. The link to Rule 41 is this. The flag is carrying out its implementation duties, and those duties are what its own IMO audit will examine. Produce the exemption or equivalence documentation if one exists. Report any defect through the SMS and get it rectified. Do not argue that the arrangement was "accepted by the old flag". The current flag is responsible now.

Scenario 3 – A new national requirement appears. The company forwards a flag circular that tightens reporting of collisions and close-quarters situations. You cannot tell from the circular alone whether it came from audit findings. Many triggers are possible. Your response is the same either way. Read it, make sure the bridge team knows the reporting route, and update the SMS familiarisation if the company instructs you to.

Common mistakes

  • "Rule 41 means the ship must verify its own compliance with COLREG." No. The ship must comply with Parts A–E and the Annexes. Rule 41 is about IMO verifying Contracting Parties.
  • Confusing it with Port State Control. PSC is a port state inspecting foreign ships. IMSAS is IMO auditing the state itself.
  • Confusing it with the ISM Code. ISM internal audits and DOC/SMC audits are company and ship audits. The III Code is a separate instrument aimed at states.
  • Assuming Part F has always been in the COLREGs. It came into force on 1 January 2016. Older textbooks end at Rule 38.
  • Mixing up Rules 38 and 41. Rule 38 gives transitional exemptions for certain ships and equipment. Rule 41 has nothing to do with exemptions for individual ships.
  • Inventing audit intervals or penalties. The COLREGs only say "periodic" and "overall schedule". The details sit in IMO's audit framework, so do not quote a figure you cannot source.

Oral exam points

Be ready to answer these in one or two sentences each:

  1. Who is bound by Rule 41? Contracting Parties, meaning the states that are party to the COLREG Convention.
  2. Who carries out the audits? IMO, under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme, which the Secretary-General administers.
  3. What is the audit standard? The IMO Instruments Implementation Code (III Code), as defined in Rule 39 and applied through Rule 40.
  4. What must the audited state do? Facilitate the audit and implement a programme of actions to address the findings.
  5. When did Part F enter into force? 1 January 2016, following Assembly Resolution A.1085(28).
  6. What does it mean for the OOW? No change to conduct, lights or signals. It strengthens the system of flag oversight that ships operate within.
  7. Name another convention with a matching provision. SOLAS Chapter XIII or STCW Regulation I/16.

A strong candidate also mentions that Part F is structurally separate from Parts A–E. Rules 39–41 sit beside the steering, lighting and signalling rules without amending them.

Key points
  • Rule 41 makes every Contracting Party subject to periodic IMO audits of its COLREG implementation.
  • It binds governments, not ships, masters or watchkeepers.
  • The audit standard is the III Code, defined in Rule 39 and applied through Rule 40.
  • The IMO Secretary-General administers the audit scheme and sets the overall schedule.
  • The audited state must facilitate the audit and implement a programme of actions on the findings.
  • Part F (Rules 39–41) entered into force on 1 January 2016 via Resolution A.1085(28).
  • Rule 41 is not PSC, ISM auditing or a flag survey, and it does not change any conduct, light or signal rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is COLREG Rule 41 about?

Rule 41 makes every state party to the COLREG Convention subject to periodic audits by IMO. The audits check how well the state complies with and implements the Collision Regulations. It is part of Part F, which was added to the COLREGs with effect from 1 January 2016.

Does Rule 41 apply to my ship or to me as officer of the watch?

Not directly. Rule 41 is addressed to Contracting Parties, which are governments. Your obligations remain those in Parts A to E and the Annexes. You may notice its effect indirectly through stricter flag inspections or new flag circulars.

What is the III Code and how does it relate to the COLREGs?

The III Code is the IMO Instruments Implementation Code, adopted by Resolution A.1070(28). Rule 39 defines it as the audit standard, and Rule 40 requires Contracting Parties to use it when carrying out their COLREG obligations. Rule 41 then subjects them to audit against it.

Is a Rule 41 audit the same as a Port State Control inspection?

No. Port State Control is a port state inspecting foreign ships in its ports. A Rule 41 audit is IMO examining a state's own systems for implementing the Convention, such as its legislation, survey arrangements, casualty investigation and enforcement.

Why do older COLREG books stop at Rule 38?

Part F (Rules 39 to 41) was adopted by IMO Assembly Resolution A.1085(28) and only entered into force on 1 January 2016. Books printed before that date end at Rule 38 and the Annexes. Always study from a current consolidated edition.

Test yourself

Under COLREG Rule 41, who is subject to periodic audits?

Rule 41 makes every Contracting Party, meaning each state, subject to periodic IMO audits. Ships and companies are inspected or audited under other regimes (COLREG Rule 41).

Which instrument is the audit standard referred to in Part F of the COLREGs?

Rule 39 defines the audit standard as the III Code, and Rule 40 requires Contracting Parties to use it (COLREG Rules 39 and 40).

Who has responsibility for administering the audit scheme under Rule 41?

Rule 41 gives the IMO Secretary-General responsibility for administering the Audit Scheme, based on IMO guidelines (COLREG Rule 41).

What effect does Rule 41 have on an officer of the watch in restricted visibility?

Rule 41 concerns audits of Contracting Parties and places no duty on the vessel. Conduct in restricted visibility remains under Rule 19 (COLREG Rules 19 and 41).

When did Part F (Rules 39–41) of the COLREGs enter into force?

Part F was adopted by IMO Assembly Resolution A.1085(28) and entered into force on 1 January 2016, in line with the mandatory IMO Member State Audit Scheme.
