COLREG Rule 7 – Risk of Collision: How to Judge It on the Bridge

Quick answer

Rule 7 requires every vessel to use all available means suited to the circumstances to decide whether risk of collision exists, and to treat any doubt as risk. Radar, if fitted and operational, must be used properly, including long-range scanning and systematic plotting. A steady compass bearing means risk exists, but risk can still exist when the bearing is changing, especially with very large ships, tows or targets at close range.

What Rule 7 actually says (paraphrased)

Rule 7 sits in Part B, Section I of the COLREGs, so under Rule 4 it applies in any condition of visibility: clear weather, fog, day or night. It has four parts:

  • 7(a) – All available means. Every vessel must use all means available and appropriate to the circumstances and conditions to decide whether risk of collision exists. The rule then adds the line every examiner expects you to know: If there is any doubt such risk shall be deemed to exist.
  • 7(b) – Proper use of radar. If radar is fitted and working, it must be used properly. That includes long-range scanning to get early warning, and radar plotting or an equivalent systematic observation of detected objects.
  • 7(c) – No assumptions on scanty information. You must not draw conclusions from scanty information, and the rule singles out scanty radar information.
  • 7(d) – Bearings. Among the things to consider: risk is deemed to exist if the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change; and risk may sometimes exist even when the bearing is changing appreciably, particularly with a very large vessel, a tow, or a vessel at close range.

Note what Rule 7 does not do. It does not tell you what action to take. It is the trigger. Once risk exists, the steering and sailing rules take over: Rule 8 for the manner of action, Rules 11 to 18 for vessels in sight of one another (notably Rules 13 to 17), and Rule 19 in restricted visibility. Nor does Rule 7 give a numerical CPA threshold. Any figure you work to comes from the master's standing orders or the company SMS, not from the COLREGs.

Applying 'all available means' on a real bridge

Under Rule 7(a), you use everything that is appropriate. On a modern bridge that typically means:

  • Visual compass bearings taken with the azimuth ring on a gyro repeater, or on the magnetic compass, at regular intervals. This is still the most direct check you have.
  • Radar / ARPA: EBL and VRM readings, plotted or tracked targets, CPA/TCPA, the true and relative vectors, and the trial manoeuvre.
  • AIS as a supplementary source. It gives identity, heading and intentions, but it is not a sensor on your own ship. The other vessel's data may be wrong, delayed or missing, and some craft do not carry AIS at all.
  • Sight and hearing: aspect, lights and shapes, sound signals, and how the other vessel's aspect changes over time.
  • ECDIS overlays, which can help, but they are only as good as the radar and AIS data feeding them.

"Appropriate to the prevailing circumstances" cuts both ways. In fog, visual bearings are not available, so radar plotting becomes the main tool, and, for vessels not in sight of one another, Rule 19 governs your action. In a crowded anchorage approach at night, visual bearings and aspect may tell you more than a cluttered radar picture. The skill lies in using several sources together and cross-checking them. When they disagree, Rule 7(a) settles it: you have doubt, so you treat it as risk.

Compass bearing, not relative bearing – and why the exceptions matter

Rule 7(d)(i) refers to the compass bearing. A relative bearing ("two points on the starboard bow") shifts every time your own ship yaws or alters course. It can appear to change while the true geometry stays dangerous. Read the bearing off the compass repeater, and aim at the same point on the other ship each time, such as the masthead light or the bridge front.

The bearing check works only when the range is closing. A steady bearing at a steady range is just a vessel keeping station on you.

Rule 7(d)(ii) warns that a changing bearing does not always mean safety:

Situation Why a changing bearing can mislead
Very large vessel Her bow may draw ahead while her stern, 300 m or more away, still lies on a collision bearing. Take bearings of both ends.
Tow The tug's lights may draw clear while the tow, perhaps several cables astern on a long wire, stays on a steady bearing.
Close range At close range even a near-miss produces a rapid bearing change, so "it's moving" proves very little.

Also watch for the slow overtaking or slow crossing case. When relative speed is low, the bearing changes so slowly that a few minutes of observation tell you almost nothing. You need a longer series of bearings, or a properly settled ARPA plot.

Radar, ARPA and scanty information

Rule 7(b) and 7(c) are where many accident investigations end up. Proper use of radar means:

  • Long-range scanning. Switch up periodically to a longer range, even if you normally watch on a shorter scale. This gives early detection, more time to plot, and room for early, substantial action under Rule 8.
  • Systematic observation. Acquire targets early and let the ARPA settle. The IMO performance standards allow a period after acquisition before the vectors reach full accuracy. Do not treat a freshly acquired target's CPA as reliable.
  • Know your inputs. ARPA is only as good as its heading and speed input. A gyro error, a wrong log input or the wrong stabilisation mode will corrupt every vector. For judging the other vessel's aspect and heading, sea (water-track) stabilisation is generally preferred. Ground stabilisation in a strong tidal stream can show an aspect that does not match what you see out of the window.
  • Watch for target swap and lost targets, especially near land, other ships or clutter.

"Scanty information" covers more than a single radar echo. Examples include one bearing with no follow-up, a target picked up only at short range, a CPA read straight after your own alteration before the plot has settled, an AIS CPA you have not checked against radar, and a small craft fading in and out of sea clutter. In each case you do not assume the situation is clear. You keep observing, and if doubt remains, you treat it as risk.

Worked scenarios

1. The classic steady bearing. Open sea, clear night. You see her masthead lights fine on the starboard bow at about 6 nm, and later her red sidelight. Over successive observations the compass bearing stays essentially the same while the range closes steadily. ARPA confirms a CPA close to zero.

  • Under Rule 7(d)(i), risk of collision is deemed to exist.
  • She is crossing from your starboard side, so you are the give-way vessel under Rule 15 and must act under Rules 16 and 8: early, substantial, and readily apparent action. A bold alteration to starboard, passing astern of her, is the usual answer.
  • Then check the result under Rule 8(d) by watching the bearing open and the CPA increase until she is finally past and clear.

2. The VLCC that is "drawing ahead". A large tanker is crossing ahead from port to starboard at about 3 nm. Her masthead lights are drawing slowly right, so it looks as if she is passing ahead. A bearing of her stern shows almost no change. Rule 7(d)(ii) applies: risk may exist despite the change. Plot the whole ship, not one light, and apply the steering and sailing rules accordingly.

3. The tug and the unseen tow. At night you see towing lights (two or three masthead lights in a vertical line, plus the yellow towing light) on the port bow, drawing aft nicely. You check the length of tow from the lights and AIS, and search for the tow's own sidelights and sternlight. The tow is well astern of the tug and its bearing is steady. Risk exists with the tow even though the tug will pass clear.

4. AIS and ARPA disagree. AIS shows a CPA of well over a mile. Your ARPA, settled for several minutes, shows a few cables. Visual bearings are changing only slightly. You do not pick the comfortable answer. The disagreement is doubt, so risk is deemed to exist. Act in good time, and investigate the discrepancy later. Common causes are the other ship's wrong heading input or a lag in her AIS data.

5. Your own alteration fools you. You have altered 10° to starboard for another target. A vessel on the port bow now appears to have "moved" relative to your bow. The relative bearing changed because of your turn, but her compass bearing has not. Reassess her from scratch once you are steady on the new course.

Common mistakes watchkeepers make

  • Taking relative bearings, or eyeballing the window frame, instead of compass bearings.
  • Taking one or two bearings and calling it a trend. Rule 7(c) exists for this reason.
  • Treating ARPA CPA/TCPA as the truth without checking sensor inputs, stabilisation or settling time.
  • Relying on AIS alone, or using it as a substitute for radar plotting and lookout.
  • Watching only the short range scale and detecting targets too late for early action.
  • Thinking risk exists only when the CPA is zero. A small CPA in open water, or a CPA inside your standing-order limit, still calls for action.
  • Ignoring the far end of a large ship or the tow behind a tug.
  • Waiting to "see what she does" while the bearing stays steady. Doubt means risk now, not later.
  • Overlooking small craft, fishing boats and yachts that give weak radar echoes and may have no AIS.
  • Making VHF "arrangements" instead of applying the rules. Talking to the other ship does not change whether risk exists.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect Rule 7 to come up in any OOW or Chief Mate/Master oral, often combined with Rules 5, 8 and 19. Typical exchanges:

  • "How do you determine risk of collision?" Use all available means appropriate to the conditions: compass bearings, radar plotting/ARPA, AIS as a supplement, and visual and aural observation. If in doubt, risk is deemed to exist. Then quote the steady-bearing test and its exceptions.
  • "Why compass bearing and not relative?" Relative bearings change with own-ship yaw and course alterations. They can show change when the true geometry has not changed.
  • "Bearing is changing. Are you safe?" Not necessarily. Think of a very large vessel, a tow, or close range. Also check that the range is closing and confirm with a plot.
  • "What is scanty radar information?" Give examples: a single observation, a target just acquired, a plot during or just after your own alteration, a weak or intermittent echo, or unverified AIS data.
  • "What does proper use of radar mean?" Long-range scanning for early warning, plus plotting or equivalent systematic observation. Know your ARPA limitations and sensor inputs.
  • "Does Rule 7 apply in clear weather?" Yes. It is in Section I, which applies in any visibility.
  • "What CPA do you work to?" The COLREGs set no figure. State that you follow the master's standing orders and the circumstances (traffic density, sea room, visibility, manoeuvrability), and that you would increase it when in doubt.

Examiners like candidates who link the rules together. Rule 5 lookout feeds Rule 7 assessment. Rule 7 triggers action under Rule 8 and the relevant steering rule. Rule 8(d) checks that the action worked.

Key points
  • Rule 7 applies in any visibility and requires all available means appropriate to the conditions to determine risk of collision.
  • If there is any doubt, risk of collision is deemed to exist, so act on it.
  • A compass bearing that does not appreciably change, with range closing, means risk exists; relative bearings can mislead.
  • Risk may exist even with a changing bearing, especially with very large ships, tows and at close range.
  • Proper radar use means long-range scanning plus plotting or equivalent systematic observation.
  • Never assume safety from scanty information: single observations, unsettled ARPA data or unchecked AIS.
  • Rule 7 decides whether risk exists; Rules 8, 11 to 18 and 19 decide what you do about it.

Frequently asked questions

What does COLREG Rule 7 say in simple terms?

Every vessel must use all appropriate means to decide whether risk of collision exists, and treat any doubt as risk. Radar, if fitted and operational, must be used properly, including long-range scanning and systematic plotting, and no assumptions may be made on scanty information. A steady compass bearing means risk exists, but risk can exist even when the bearing changes.

Can I rely on AIS to determine risk of collision?

AIS is one of the available means and is useful for identification and cross-checking, but it should not be your only source. The data comes from the other ship and may be inaccurate, delayed or missing, and some vessels carry no AIS. Confirm with compass bearings, radar plotting and lookout, and treat any disagreement between sources as doubt.

Why must the bearing be a compass bearing?

A compass bearing is fixed to north, so it reflects the true geometry between the two ships. A relative bearing changes whenever your own ship yaws or alters course. It can therefore show a change when the other vessel is still on a collision course.

Does Rule 7 set a minimum CPA?

No. The COLREGs give no numerical CPA. Minimum passing distances come from the master's standing orders and the company SMS, and should be increased according to traffic, sea room, visibility and the vessels involved.

Does Rule 7 apply in restricted visibility?

Yes. Rule 7 is in Section I of Part B, which applies in any condition of visibility. In restricted visibility you will rely more on radar plotting; if the vessels are not in sight of one another, your action is governed by Rule 19, but once they are in sight of one another, Rules 11 to 18 apply.

Test yourself

Under Rule 7, what must you do if you are unsure whether risk of collision exists?

Rule 7(a) states that if there is any doubt, risk of collision shall be deemed to exist. COLREG 1972, Rule 7(a).

Which bearing does Rule 7(d)(i) require you to monitor?

Rule 7(d)(i) refers to the compass bearing, because relative bearings change with own-ship yaw and course alterations. COLREG 1972, Rule 7(d)(i).

A tug's lights are drawing clearly aft on your bow at night. What does Rule 7(d)(ii) warn you about?

Rule 7(d)(ii) says risk may exist despite an appreciable bearing change, particularly when approaching a tow. COLREG 1972, Rule 7(d)(ii).

What does Rule 7(b) require of a vessel with operational radar?

Rule 7(b) requires proper use of radar, including long-range scanning for early warning and plotting or equivalent systematic observation. COLREG 1972, Rule 7(b).

Which is an example of the 'scanty information' that Rule 7(c) warns against?

A freshly acquired ARPA target has not settled, so relying on its CPA is an assumption based on scanty radar information. COLREG 1972, Rule 7(c).
