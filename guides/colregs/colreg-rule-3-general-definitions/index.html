COLREG Rule 3 – General Definitions Explained for Watchkeepers

Quick answer

Rule 3 sets the meaning of the key terms used throughout the Collision Regulations: vessel, power-driven vessel, sailing vessel, vessel engaged in fishing, NUC, RAM, CBD, underway, length, in sight, restricted visibility, seaplane and WIG craft. These definitions decide which rule applies, which lights and signals you show, and who keeps out of whose way. If you get a definition wrong, you will apply the wrong rule.

Why Rule 3 matters more than it looks

Cadets often skip Rule 3 because it has no manoeuvring instructions. That is a mistake. Every steering and sailing rule, and every light, shape and sound signal, depends on a status defined here:

  • Rule 18 ranks vessels by their status.
  • Rule 19 depends on whether vessels are in sight.
  • Rule 35 depends on whether a vessel is underway and making way.
  • Light requirements depend on length.

In practice, applying the Rules on the bridge means asking two questions in order:

  1. What is the other vessel, in Rule 3 terms? Is she power-driven, sailing, fishing, NUC, RAM or CBD? Is she underway?
  2. Are we in sight of one another? If yes, Section II (Rules 11–18) applies. If not, and either vessel is in or near restricted visibility, Rule 19 applies.

Only after you have answered both questions can you pick the correct rule.

Rule 3 applies everywhere the COLREG applies. Special rules made under Rule 1(b) for roadsteads, harbours, rivers, lakes or inland waterways take precedence there and may differ from the COLREG, so always check the local regulations.

The definitions, paraphrased

Para Term What it means in practice
(a) Vessel Any water craft used or capable of being used as transport on water. This includes non-displacement craft such as hovercraft, WIG craft and seaplanes.
(b) Power-driven vessel Any vessel propelled by machinery.
(c) Sailing vessel A vessel under sail, provided any propelling machinery fitted is not being used.
(d) Vessel engaged in fishing Fishing with nets, lines, trawls or other apparatus that restrict manoeuvrability. Trolling lines, or other gear that does not restrict manoeuvrability, do not count.
(e) Seaplane Any aircraft designed to manoeuvre on the water.
(f) Not under command (NUC) Through some exceptional circumstance, she cannot manoeuvre as the Rules require, so she cannot keep out of the way.
(g) Restricted in ability to manoeuvre (RAM) Because of the nature of her work, she cannot manoeuvre as the Rules require, so she cannot keep out of the way. The rule gives a non-exhaustive list of examples.
(h) Constrained by draught (CBD) A power-driven vessel severely restricted in her ability to deviate from her course because of her draught in relation to the available depth and width of navigable water.
(i) Underway Not at anchor, not made fast to the shore and not aground.
(j) Length and breadth Length overall and greatest breadth.
(k) In sight Vessels count as in sight of one another only when one can be observed visually from the other.
(l) Restricted visibility Visibility restricted by fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms, sandstorms or similar causes.
(m) WIG craft A multimodal craft which, in its main operational mode, flies close to the surface using surface-effect action.

The RAM examples listed in Rule 3(g) are:

  • laying, servicing or picking up a navigation mark, submarine cable or pipeline
  • dredging, surveying or underwater operations
  • replenishment, or transferring persons, provisions or cargo, while underway
  • launching or recovering aircraft
  • mine clearance
  • a towing operation that severely restricts the towing vessel and her tow in their ability to deviate from their course

Applying the definitions on the bridge

Underway vs making way. Rule 3 defines underway only. Making way is not defined in Rule 3. It is used elsewhere, notably in Rule 35, and means moving through the water under her own means.

A vessel drifting with engines stopped is still underway, provided she is not anchored, not made fast and not aground. She shows her underway lights. In restricted visibility she sounds two prolonged blasts at the required interval instead of one.

Power-driven or sailing. The test is whether the machinery is being used, not whether it is fitted. A yacht with sails up and her engine running is a power-driven vessel. Rule 25(e) requires her to show a cone, apex downwards, by day. A vessel of less than 12 m is not required to show the cone, but may do so.

Fishing status depends on the gear, not the hull. A trawler steaming to the grounds with her nets on deck is simply a power-driven vessel. She should not show fishing lights. A vessel trolling lines is not a vessel engaged in fishing. She keeps her ordinary status (power-driven or sailing).

NUC needs an exceptional circumstance. Typical examples are a main engine failure, a steering gear failure or a fouled propeller. A ship that is merely large, slow to answer the helm or awkward in a seaway is not NUC. Your company SMS will normally set out who decides to display NUC signals and how this is reported. Follow it.

RAM comes from the work, not the ship type. A dredger on passage with no dredging in progress is a power-driven vessel. A tug towing is RAM only if the tow severely restricts her ability to deviate. Ordinary towing is covered by Rule 24 lights alone.

CBD is only for power-driven vessels. It depends on her draught in relation to the available depth and width of water at that place. A VLCC in the open ocean is not CBD.

The lights and shape for CBD under Rule 28 are permissive ("may"). Rule 18(d) asks other vessels, apart from NUC and RAM vessels, to avoid impeding the safe passage of a CBD vessel exhibiting the signals in Rule 28, if the circumstances of the case admit.

In sight and restricted visibility – the most tested pair

Under Rule 3(k), in sight means visual observation. A radar echo, an AIS target or a VHF call does not put you in sight of another vessel.

This is why the crossing, head-on and overtaking rules (13, 14 and 15) do not apply to a radar-only target in fog. Those rules sit in Section II, which by Rule 11 applies to vessels in sight of one another. For a target detected by radar alone, you apply Rule 19.

Rule 19 contains no stand-on or give-way roles. Instead it requires:

  • safe speed, with engines ready for immediate manoeuvre
  • early determination of risk of collision
  • avoiding action in ample time, without certain alterations
  • reducing speed to the minimum on hearing a fog signal forward of the beam

Rule 3(l) gives no numerical figure for restricted visibility. No visibility distance automatically triggers the definition. Decide on the circumstances, your ship's manoeuvring characteristics and the traffic. Follow your master's standing orders and the company SMS, which often set a figure for calling the master and posting extra lookouts.

The two conditions can exist together. You may be in a fog bank generally, yet suddenly see a fishing boat at close range. Between you and that vessel, you are now in sight. Section II applies to that encounter, while Rule 19 still governs your conduct towards other vessels you cannot see.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Drifting off port. Your ship is waiting for a berth, engines stopped, drifting about 5 miles off, not anchored. Fog comes down. - Status: underway but not making way. - Lights: underway lights (Rule 23). - Sound signal: two prolonged blasts, about 2 seconds apart, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes (Rule 35(b)).

Scenario 2 – Radar target in fog. At night in thick fog, ARPA shows a target fine on your starboard bow at 6 nm, closing, CPA 0.3 nm. Nothing is visible. - Wrong reasoning: "It is a crossing situation from starboard, so I am give-way." - Correct reasoning: You are not in sight (Rule 3(k)), so apply Rule 19. Make an early, substantial alteration. Avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken.

Scenario 3 – Yacht under sail and engine. By day you see a yacht with sails set. You can see exhaust at her stern and a cone, apex down, forward. - Status: power-driven vessel. - Rule 18 privileges of a sailing vessel: do not apply. - Applicable rules: crossing, head-on and overtaking rules for power-driven vessels.

Scenario 4 – Tug and tow in a channel. A tug with a long barge tow shows towing lights. During the passage she adds red-white-red all-round lights. - Meaning: she is signalling that the tow severely restricts her ability to deviate. - Status: she is now RAM (Rule 3(g)(vi), lights under Rule 27(c)) and ranks accordingly under Rule 18.

Common mistakes

  • Treating radar contact as "in sight". This is the classic error behind many fog collisions. Radar is not visual.
  • Thinking "stopped" means "not underway". Only being anchored, made fast or aground takes you out of underway.
  • Showing NUC lights for convenience. NUC needs an exceptional circumstance. Misuse misleads other vessels and will not be accepted by an inquiry.
  • Assuming all towing is RAM. It is RAM only when the tow severely restricts ability to deviate.
  • Declaring CBD on a sailing vessel or in open water. CBD applies only to power-driven vessels, and only where depth and width actually constrain them.
  • Showing fishing lights on passage, or reading them as fishing on passage. Fishing status needs restricting gear in use.
  • Using registered length for lights and sound appliances. Rule 3(j) means length overall. Length thresholds matter throughout. For example, Rule 33 requires more sound appliances at 12 m, 20 m and 100 m.

Oral exam points

Examiners like short, precise answers that cite the paragraph. Expect questions like these:

  • "Define underway." Not at anchor, not made fast to the shore, not aground (Rule 3(i)). Then add the making-way distinction and its effect on Rule 35 signals.
  • "Is a vessel engaged in fishing a sailing vessel?" Status depends on the activity. A sailing vessel fishing with restricting gear is a vessel engaged in fishing and shows fishing lights.
  • "What is the difference between NUC and RAM?"
    • NUC comes from an exceptional circumstance, usually a failure.
    • RAM comes from the nature of the work.
    • Both are unable to keep out of the way.
  • "Can a vessel be NUC at anchor?" NUC lights are for a vessel underway. An anchored vessel shows anchor lights (Rule 30). Explain the logic: the NUC concept concerns a vessel's ability to manoeuvre while underway.
  • "When do Rules 13–15 apply?" Only when vessels are in sight of one another (Rules 11 and 3(k)).
  • "What visibility distance is restricted visibility?" Rule 3(l) specifies none. It depends on circumstances and standing orders.
  • "What is a WIG craft, and how does she behave on the water?" Give the Rule 3(m) definition. Then add that under Rule 18(f), when operating on the water surface she complies as a power-driven vessel.

Always state the paragraph letter. It shows you know where the definition lives, and it is what the examiner listens for.

Key points
  • Rule 3 statuses decide which rules, lights and signals apply, so identify status before choosing a rule.
  • Underway means not anchored, not made fast to the shore and not aground; a drifting ship is underway.
  • "In sight" means visual only; radar or AIS contact alone means Rule 19, not Rules 13–15.
  • A sailing vessel using her engine is a power-driven vessel.
  • NUC needs an exceptional circumstance; RAM arises from the nature of the work.
  • CBD applies only to power-driven vessels constrained by depth and width of water.
  • Length means length overall, and restricted visibility has no numerical definition.

Frequently asked questions

Is a ship drifting with engines stopped considered underway under COLREG?

Yes. Under Rule 3(i), a vessel is underway unless she is at anchor, made fast to the shore or aground. A drifting ship is underway but not making way. She shows underway lights, and in restricted visibility she sounds two prolonged blasts under Rule 35(b).

Does seeing a ship on radar mean we are in sight of one another?

No. Rule 3(k) says vessels are in sight only when one can be observed visually from the other. A radar-only target in fog is handled under Rule 19, and the crossing and head-on rules do not apply.

What visibility counts as restricted visibility under COLREG?

Rule 3(l) lists causes such as fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms and sandstorms but gives no distance. Whether visibility is restricted is a judgement based on circumstances. Master's standing orders and company SMS usually set a figure for calling the master and taking extra precautions.

Is every tug that is towing a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre?

No. Under Rule 3(g)(vi), a towing vessel is RAM only when the tow severely restricts her and her tow in their ability to deviate from their course. Ordinary towing is shown with Rule 24 lights. RAM lights under Rule 27 are added only when that condition exists.

Can a sailing vessel be constrained by her draught?

No. Rule 3(h) limits CBD status to power-driven vessels that are severely restricted by their draught in relation to the available depth and width of navigable water. A sailing vessel cannot claim CBD status.

Test yourself

A vessel has her engines stopped and is drifting. She is not anchored, not made fast and not aground. What is her status under Rule 3?

Rule 3(i) excludes only vessels at anchor, made fast to the shore or aground. Being stopped by choice is not an exceptional circumstance for NUC (COLREG Rule 3(i), Rule 35(b)).

In dense fog you detect a vessel by radar only, crossing from starboard with risk of collision. Which rule governs your action?

Under Rule 3(k), "in sight" means visual observation. Section II rules apply only to vessels in sight, so Rule 19 governs (COLREG Rules 3(k), 11 and 19).

A vessel is fishing with trolling lines that do not restrict her manoeuvrability. What is her status?

Rule 3(d) expressly excludes trolling lines and other gear that does not restrict manoeuvrability. Such a vessel keeps her ordinary status (COLREG Rule 3(b) and 3(d)).

A yacht has her sails set and her engine running ahead. How should you treat her under the Rules?

Rule 3(c) defines a sailing vessel only where propelling machinery, if fitted, is not being used. By day she should show a cone, apex downwards, though a vessel of less than 12 m is not required to show it but may do so (COLREG Rules 3(b), 3(c), 25(e)).

Which vessel may be a vessel constrained by her draught under Rule 3(h)?

CBD status applies only to power-driven vessels, and only in relation to the available depth and width of navigable water; Rule 3(h) sets no fixed draught figure. A vessel aground is not underway (COLREG Rules 3(h), 3(i), 28).
