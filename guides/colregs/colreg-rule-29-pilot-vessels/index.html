COLREG Rule 29 – Pilot Vessels: Lights, Signals and Bridge Practice

Quick answer

Under COLREG Rule 29, a pilot vessel engaged on pilotage duty shows two all-round lights in a vertical line at or near the masthead, white over red. Underway it adds sidelights and a sternlight, and at anchor it adds the Rule 30 anchor light(s) or shape. Off duty it shows the lights of an ordinary vessel of its length, and it gets no special right of way under Rule 18.

What Rule 29 says (paraphrased)

Rule 29 is short and has two paragraphs.

(a) A vessel engaged on pilotage duty shows:

  • At or near the masthead, two all-round lights in a vertical line, the upper white and the lower red.
  • When underway, sidelights and a sternlight as well.
  • When at anchor, the anchor light, lights or shape prescribed in Rule 30, in addition to the white-over-red.

(b) A pilot vessel not engaged on pilotage duty shows the lights or shapes of a similar vessel of her length. In practice that usually means an ordinary power-driven vessel under Rule 23.

Points that catch people out:

  • No masthead light when underway on duty. The white all-round light at the top takes its place. Do not add a Rule 23 masthead light.
  • No day shape is prescribed by Rule 29 itself. The only shape involved is the anchor ball from Rule 30 when the pilot vessel is at anchor.
  • The word "underway" applies even when the boat is stopped. A pilot boat lying stopped on station is still underway if she is not at anchor, made fast to the shore or aground. She keeps her sidelights and sternlight on.
  • COLREG does not define "engaged on pilotage duty". It is generally understood to mean when the vessel is actually working as a pilot vessel, for example on station or embarking and landing pilots. Local pilotage authority practice can affect how this is applied.

Reading the lights at night: the white-over-red picture

The usual memory aid is "White over red, pilot ahead." Learn it together with the other two-light vertical combinations, because examiners like to mix them up.

All-round lights (upper over lower) Meaning Rule
White over red Pilot vessel on duty 29
Red over white Fishing vessel, not trawling 26
Green over white Vessel trawling 26
Red over red Not under command 27

The sidelights and sternlight then give you the aspect:

  • White over red with a green sidelight. You are looking at her starboard side.
  • White over red with both sidelights. She is end-on, heading towards you.
  • White over red with the sternlight only. You are astern of her.
  • White over red with no sidelights or sternlight, plus an all-round white light (or two at different heights). She is at anchor.

The range at which these lights must be visible depends on the vessel's length and is set out in Rule 22. Positioning and vertical spacing are covered in Annex I. Most pilot boats are small, so expect the lights to be closer together and to show up later than those of a large ship. Some pilot stations use larger station vessels, so do not assume the size from the lights alone.

By day there is no COLREG identification. Pilot boats are usually recognisable by their "PILOT" marking and are commonly seen flying International Code of Signals flag H. Neither of these is a COLREG requirement.

Sound signals and restricted visibility

Rule 29 covers lights only. The sound signal is in Rule 35.

A pilot vessel on pilotage duty in or near an area of restricted visibility first gives the normal signal for her condition:

  • Underway and making way: one prolonged blast at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • Underway but stopped: two prolonged blasts at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • At anchor: the Rule 35 anchor signal (bell, and gong if required by her length).

She may, in addition, sound an identity signal of four short blasts. Note the word "may". The identity signal is optional and is given on top of the normal signal, not instead of it.

Do not confuse four short blasts with five or more short blasts. Under Rule 34(d), five or more short blasts is the signal used when a vessel doubts the intentions or actions of another vessel in sight.

Applying Rule 29 on the bridge

A pilot vessel has no special privilege. Rule 18 lists the vessels that others keep out of the way of, and a pilot vessel is not on that list. She is not restricted in her ability to manoeuvre just because she is on duty.

A pilot boat underway is normally a power-driven vessel. The steering and sailing rules apply to her exactly as they would to any other power-driven vessel.

What Rule 29 does give you is information. White over red tells you that the boat near the pilot station may:

  • stop,
  • turn sharply,
  • run up alongside other ships, or
  • lie stopped waiting for traffic.

Good practice when approaching a pilot station:

  1. Brief the boarding arrangements in the passage plan. Know the boarding position, speed and side requested, and the VHF working channel.
  2. Reduce speed early. Have the engine on standby and the steering in hand control, in line with your SMS.
  3. Maintain a proper look-out (Rule 5) by sight, radar and AIS. Pilot boats are small and fast, and their radar and AIS picture can change quickly.
  4. Make early contact on VHF and agree the arrangement. A VHF agreement does not suspend the rules, so keep assessing risk of collision under Rule 7.
  5. Make a lee as requested, and keep other traffic in mind. There is often more than one ship converging on the boarding ground.
  6. Remember Rule 2. Good seamanship and the special circumstances of a busy pilot station can call for action beyond the strict letter of the steering rules.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: crossing at night.

You are inbound to a pilot station. On your starboard bow you see all-round white over red, with a red sidelight, bearing steady and the range closing.

  • Reading: She is a pilot vessel on duty, underway. You are seeing her port side, so she is crossing from your starboard.
  • Action: Treat her as a power-driven vessel in a crossing situation (Rule 15). You are the give-way vessel and should take early and substantial action (Rule 16). In practice you will also call her on VHF, but the call does not replace the action.

Scenario 2: fog off the pilot station.

In restricted visibility you hear one prolonged blast followed by four short blasts, apparently forward of the beam.

  • Reading: A pilot vessel on duty, underway and making way, using the optional identity signal.
  • Action: Rule 19(e) applies. Unless you have determined that risk of collision does not exist, reduce speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course, and if necessary take all way off. Navigate with extreme caution until the danger of collision is over.

Scenario 3: the cutter at anchor.

You see white over red at the top, a single all-round white light lower down, and no sidelights.

  • Reading: A pilot vessel on duty at anchor. She is showing the Rule 30 anchor light for a vessel of her size.
  • Action: Treat her as an anchored vessel. Give her a wide berth and watch for a launch or boat leaving her.

Scenario 4: the off-duty pilot boat.

A pilot boat heads back to harbour after her shift, showing only a masthead light, sidelights and sternlight.

  • Reading: This is correct under Rule 29(b). She is now simply a power-driven vessel.
  • What it means for you: You have no way of knowing from her lights that she is a pilot boat, and you do not need to know.

Common mistakes

  • Showing a masthead light as well as white over red. On duty and underway, the white all-round light replaces the masthead light. It is sidelights and sternlight only in addition.
  • Switching off sidelights when stopped on station. If she is not anchored, made fast or aground, she is underway and the sidelights and sternlight stay on.
  • Assuming the pilot boat is stand-on because she is "working". Rule 18 gives her no priority. Apply the ordinary steering rules.
  • Thinking a ship with a pilot on board shows white over red. It does not. Rule 29 is for the pilot vessel, not the piloted ship. By day, a ship with a pilot embarked customarily flies flag H under the International Code of Signals. Some local regulations may require further signals.
  • Mixing up red over white and white over red. Red over white is a fishing vessel that is not trawling. Getting it upside down in an orals exam is an easy fail.
  • Treating four short blasts as mandatory. The identity signal in Rule 35 is optional and is given in addition to the normal fog signal.

Oral exam style questions

Typical questions an examiner might ask at OOW level, with the answer they expect:

  • "Show me a pilot vessel on duty, underway, heading towards you." White over red all-round at or near the masthead, both sidelights, no masthead light.
  • "Same vessel, stopped. Anything change?" No. She is still underway, so the lights stay the same. In fog her signal changes to two prolonged blasts, and she may add four short.
  • "And at anchor?" White over red, plus the Rule 30 anchor light or lights. By day, the anchor ball. The sidelights and sternlight go off.
  • "Off duty?" The lights of a similar vessel of her length, usually Rule 23 lights.
  • "Who gives way, you or the pilot boat?" It depends on the encounter under Rules 13 to 15. She has no special status under Rule 18.
  • "What's her day signal?" None under COLREG, apart from the anchor ball when she is at anchor. Flag H is an International Code of Signals signal, not a COLREG one.

Examiners will often follow up by flipping the lights to red over white or red over red to check that you are not guessing. Answer slowly and name the rule each time.

Key points
  • On duty, a pilot vessel shows all-round white over red at or near the masthead.
  • Underway on duty, she adds sidelights and a sternlight, with no masthead light.
  • At anchor on duty, she adds the Rule 30 anchor light(s), or the ball by day.
  • Off duty, she shows the lights of a similar vessel of her length.
  • In restricted visibility she may add four short blasts to her normal Rule 35 signal.
  • Rule 18 gives a pilot vessel no priority, so apply the ordinary steering rules.
  • Remember: white over red, pilot ahead; red over white, fishing at night.

Frequently asked questions

What lights does a pilot boat show at night?

When on pilotage duty, a pilot boat shows two all-round lights in a vertical line at or near the masthead, white over red. Underway she adds sidelights and a sternlight. At anchor she adds the anchor light or lights of Rule 30 instead.

Does a pilot vessel on duty show a masthead light?

No. Under Rule 29, the additional lights underway are sidelights and a sternlight only. The all-round white light at the top replaces the masthead light. When off duty she reverts to the normal lights for her length, which will include masthead light(s) if she is power-driven.

Does a pilot vessel have right of way over other ships?

No. A pilot vessel is not included in the Rule 18 list of vessels that others must keep out of the way of. She follows the ordinary steering and sailing rules as a power-driven vessel. Good seamanship near a pilot station still calls for early speed reduction and caution.

What is the sound signal for a pilot vessel in fog?

She gives the normal Rule 35 signal for her condition: one prolonged blast if making way, two prolonged if stopped, or the anchor signal if anchored. She may also sound an identity signal of four short blasts. The identity signal is optional and does not replace the normal signal.

Does a ship with a pilot on board show any special lights?

No. COLREG prescribes no light or shape for a ship with a pilot embarked. White over red is only for the pilot vessel itself. By day such a ship customarily flies flag H of the International Code of Signals, and local port rules may add requirements.

Test yourself

Which lights does a pilot vessel engaged on pilotage duty show when underway at night?

Rule 29(a) requires white over red plus sidelights and a sternlight when underway, with no masthead light. COLREG Rule 29(a).

What does a pilot vessel on pilotage duty show when at anchor at night?

At anchor she keeps the white over red and adds the Rule 30 anchor light(s). Sidelights and sternlight are for underway only. COLREG Rules 29(a) and 30.

How does Rule 18 treat a pilot vessel on duty?

Pilot vessels are not listed in the Rule 18 responsibilities, so the ordinary encounter rules apply. COLREG Rule 18.

In restricted visibility, what identity signal may a pilot vessel on duty sound?

Rule 35 allows a pilot vessel on duty to add an identity signal of four short blasts to her normal fog signal. COLREG Rule 35.

By day, how does a merchant ship indicate she has a pilot on board?

Rule 29 applies only to the pilot vessel. The ball-diamond-ball is the restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre shape under Rule 27, and flag H comes from the International Code of Signals. COLREG Rules 27 and 29.
