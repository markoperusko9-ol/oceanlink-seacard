COLREG Rule 8 – Action to Avoid Collision

Quick answer

Rule 8 sets the standard for any collision-avoidance manoeuvre. The action must follow the steering and sailing rules and be positive, taken in ample time and large enough for the other ship to see it by eye or radar. It must produce a safe passing distance and be checked until the other vessel is finally past and clear. If needed, you must slow down or stop. The rule also explains how the 'not to impede' duty works alongside the ordinary give-way and stand-on rules.

What Rule 8 says, paragraph by paragraph

Rule 8 sits in Part B, Section I of the COLREG. Under Rule 4, Section I applies in any condition of visibility. Rule 8 therefore governs your manoeuvre in clear weather and in fog alike.

The Rule does not tell you who gives way. Rules 9, 10 and 13 to 18 decide who keeps out of the way or must not impede. Rule 19 sets conduct in restricted visibility. Rule 8 tells you how any avoiding action must be carried out.

Para In plain terms
8(a) Action must comply with the Part B rules. If the circumstances allow, it must be positive, made in ample time and in line with good seamanship.
8(b) Course and/or speed changes must be large enough for another vessel to notice them visually or on radar. Avoid a series of small alterations.
8(c) With enough sea-room, a course alteration alone may be the most effective way to avoid a close-quarters situation. It must be made in good time and be substantial, and it must not create a new close-quarters situation.
8(d) The action must result in passing at a safe distance. Keep checking its effect until the other vessel is finally past and clear.
8(e) If necessary to avoid collision, or to gain time to assess the situation, slacken speed or take all way off by stopping or reversing.
8(f) Sets out how the duty 'not to impede' (Rules 9, 10 and 18) works alongside the normal rules once risk of collision exists.

Two phrases recur: 'if the circumstances of the case admit' and 'in good time / ample time'. Neither carries a number. The Rule deliberately leaves the judgement to the watchkeeper. Courts and examiners judge that judgement against what a competent seafarer would have done.

Positive, ample time, good seamanship – what they mean on the bridge

Positive means decisive and unambiguous. Do not nudge the autopilot a few degrees and hope. The other ship's OOW should be in no doubt that you have acted.

Ample time means acting while you still have options. Assess early using radar plotting or ARPA, AIS as a supplement and visual bearings (Rule 7). Early action needs smaller changes and leaves room to correct. Late action forces large, risky manoeuvres and may push the stand-on vessel into acting under Rule 17.

Good seamanship covers everything the Rules cannot list. It includes:

  • your ship's manoeuvring characteristics
  • traffic beyond the target you are dealing with
  • navigational hazards
  • squat and shallow water
  • weather
  • the state of your engine (for example, whether it is on manoeuvring or sea speed)

'In accordance with the Rules of this Part' links Rule 8 to the rest of Part B. The 'how' must not breach the 'who'. Some examples:

  • A give-way vessel in a crossing situation should, if the circumstances admit, avoid crossing ahead of the other (Rule 15).
  • A power-driven stand-on vessel taking action under Rule 17(a)(ii) in a crossing situation with another power-driven vessel should, if the circumstances admit, not alter to port for a vessel on her own port side (Rule 17(c)).
  • In restricted visibility, Rule 19(d) restricts which way you turn.

A bold manoeuvre that breaks one of these rules does not satisfy Rule 8.

Course, speed or both – Rules 8(b), 8(c) and 8(e)

Readily apparent (8b). The test is what the other ship sees. On a radar display or ARPA, a small course change can take a while to register clearly in the target's vector or trail. Visually, a few degrees is often invisible, especially at night when you are judging by lights. Speed changes are harder still to detect, because a large ship loses way slowly.

The Rule gives no minimum figure in degrees or knots. Training establishments and company SMS often give guidance figures. Treat those as guidance and do not quote them in an exam as 'what Rule 8 says'.

Course alone (8c). A course alteration alone may be the most effective action, provided three conditions hold:

  • there is sufficient sea-room
  • the alteration is made in good time
  • it is substantial and does not create another close-quarters situation

In open water a ship answers her helm much faster than her engine can take off way. That is why course is usually the first tool.

Speed (8e). Reduce speed or stop when:

  • sea-room is limited, for example in a TSS, a narrow channel or near shoals
  • a course change would only shift the problem to another vessel
  • you need time to understand a confusing situation

In restricted visibility, Rule 19(e) imposes a separate duty. It applies except where it has been determined that risk of collision does not exist. If you hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam, you must reduce speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course. The same applies if you cannot avoid a close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam. If necessary you must take all way off. In any event you must navigate with extreme caution until danger of collision is over.

Combining a course alteration with a speed reduction is often the most effective option. Know your stopping and turning data from the wheelhouse poster and manoeuvring booklet. That data tells you how quickly a speed change actually takes effect.

Checking the action until 'finally past and clear' – Rule 8(d)

Taking action is half the job. Rule 8(d) requires two further things:

  • a safe passing distance
  • careful checking of the action until the other vessel is finally past and clear

The Rule does not define a safe distance. It depends on:

  • ship size and type
  • speed
  • sea-room and traffic
  • visibility and sea state
  • the reliability of your information

Most companies set a minimum CPA in their SMS or Master's standing orders, and these figures vary between companies. If the Master's orders set a figure, you work to it.

Practical checking routine:

  1. Before you act, run a trial manoeuvre on ARPA, if fitted, to see the predicted CPA. Check that the alteration does not close another target.
  2. Execute the alteration. Hand steering may be the better choice for a large or critical turn; follow company procedure.
  3. Confirm visually that the compass bearing is changing appreciably and the aspect or lights are opening.
  4. Confirm on radar that the new CPA is developing as predicted. Remember that ARPA vectors lag after your own manoeuvre.
  5. Watch for the other vessel manoeuvring as well. A new course or speed on her part resets your assessment.
  6. Hold the new course until she is past and clear, then return to your track. Watch other traffic as you come back.

'Not to impede' and Rule 8(f)

Several rules require a vessel 'not to impede' another:

  • Rule 9: vessels of less than 20 m in length, sailing vessels, fishing vessels and crossing vessels in a narrow channel
  • Rule 10: fishing vessels, vessels under 20 m and sailing vessels in a TSS
  • Rule 18(d): any vessel, other than one not under command or restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, towards a vessel constrained by her draught

Rule 8(f) makes three points:

  1. Early action. The vessel required not to impede must act early, when the circumstances require. Her aim is to leave enough sea-room for the other vessel's safe passage. This duty starts before risk of collision exists.
  2. The duty does not end. If the two vessels get into risk of collision anyway, the vessel required not to impede is still bound by that duty. When she acts, she must have full regard to the actions the Part B rules may require.
  3. Both sides remain bound. The vessel that is not to be impeded is not a privileged ship. Once risk of collision exists, she must still comply fully with Part B. That may make her the give-way vessel under Rule 15, for example.

Exam trap: 'not to impede' is not the same as 'give way'. It means acting early so that a collision situation never develops.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – open-sea crossing, clear weather.

  • You are a power-driven vessel. A power-driven vessel is fine on your starboard bow, with a steady bearing and closing range. Rule 15 makes you give-way, and Rule 16 requires early and substantial action.
  • You have plenty of sea-room. A trial manoeuvre shows a bold alteration to starboard gives your company's minimum CPA, passing astern of her. Other traffic is unaffected.
  • You alter early and by a clearly visible amount. Then watch the bearing draw left and her aspect broaden, with her masthead lights opening out as you pass astern of her.
  • You hold the course until she is past and clear, then return to track.
  • That satisfies 8(a), (b), (c) and (d).

Scenario 2 – the 'creeping' alteration (what not to do).

  • Same situation, but the OOW alters 5° to starboard, waits, then alters another 5°, and so on.
  • The other ship sees no clear change. She may conclude you are standing on and act herself, possibly turning to port.
  • This is the succession of small alterations that 8(b) tells you to avoid.

Scenario 3 – restricted visibility, target on the port bow.

  • You detect a target by radar alone, forward of your beam on the port bow, with a close CPA.
  • Rule 19(d) says to avoid, so far as possible, an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam (other than one being overtaken). An alteration to starboard, combined with a speed reduction if needed, normally fits both Rules 8 and 19.
  • Rule 19(e) applies except where it has been determined that risk of collision does not exist. Reduce speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course if you hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam or cannot avoid a close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam. If necessary take all way off, and in any event navigate with extreme caution until danger of collision is over.
  • Plot the target continuously afterwards. Radar-only information is less certain.

Scenario 4 – fishing vessel in a TSS lane.

  • A vessel engaged in fishing is ahead in a traffic lane you are following.
  • Under Rule 10(i) she must not impede you, and Rule 8(f)(i) expects her to act early.
  • If she does not and risk of collision develops, the normal Part B rules apply. If you are overtaking her, Rule 13 requires you to keep out of the way. Otherwise, Rule 18(a) generally requires your power-driven vessel to keep out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing.
  • With limited sea-room in the lane, a substantial speed reduction under 8(e) may be your best action.

Common mistakes watchkeepers make

  • Acting too late. Waiting for the 'last minute' turns a routine manoeuvre into an emergency.
  • Small or slow alterations. These are invisible to the other ship and breach 8(b).
  • Relying on VHF or AIS. Agreeing a manoeuvre by VHF can cause confusion through misidentification. Action must still comply with the Rules. AIS data supplements radar and visual observation and does not replace them.
  • Forgetting the third ship. An alteration that clears one target but creates a new close-quarters situation fails 8(c).
  • Returning to course too early. The other vessel is not 'finally past and clear' just because the CPA has been reached on the display.
  • Treating speed as a last resort. Rule 8(e) makes slowing or stopping an expected option, not a failure.
  • Assuming the 'not to impede' vessel has priority. Under 8(f)(iii), she still follows Part B once risk of collision exists.

Oral exam points

Examiners for OOW and Chief Mate/Master orals commonly probe the following areas. Model answers are given in brief.

  • 'Does Rule 8 apply in fog?' Yes. Section I applies in any visibility under Rule 4, so Rules 8 and 19 work together.
  • 'How much should you alter?' Enough to be readily apparent visually or by radar and to give a safe passing distance. The Rule sets no fixed figure. Mention your company or Master's CPA requirement.
  • 'When would you use speed instead of course?' When sea-room is limited, when a course change would create another close-quarters situation, or when you need time to assess.
  • 'What does good seamanship add?' It covers the ship's handling characteristics, hazards, other traffic and local conditions that the Rules do not list.
  • 'Explain 8(f).' Give the three-part answer: act early, the duty continues once risk of collision exists, and both vessels remain bound by Part B.
  • 'When can you resume course?' When the other vessel is finally past and clear, and only after checking that the return does not create a new risk.

Answer in your own words and link each point to the paragraph number. Examiners want understanding, not recitation.

Key points
  • Rule 8 governs how you take avoiding action; other Part B rules (notably 9, 10 and 13–18, and 19 in restricted visibility) decide who acts and in what manner.
  • Action must be positive, in ample time, follow good seamanship and comply with the rest of Part B.
  • Alterations must be large enough to be readily apparent visually or by radar; avoid a series of small changes.
  • With sufficient sea-room, a timely and substantial course alteration alone may be most effective, if it creates no new close-quarters situation.
  • Slow down, stop or reverse when necessary to avoid collision or to gain time to assess.
  • Check the effect of your action until the other vessel is finally past and clear.
  • 'Not to impede' means act early; once risk of collision exists, both vessels remain bound by Part B.

Frequently asked questions

How many degrees should I alter course under COLREG Rule 8?

Rule 8 gives no figure. It requires the alteration to be large enough to be readily apparent to another vessel observing visually or by radar, and to produce a safe passing distance. Training establishments and company SMS often give guidance figures, but those are not part of the Rule.

Does Rule 8 apply in restricted visibility?

Yes. Rule 8 is in Part B, Section I, which applies in any condition of visibility under Rule 4. In restricted visibility you must also comply with Rule 19, which sets out which alterations of course to avoid and when speed must be reduced.

Is reducing speed an acceptable action to avoid collision?

Yes. Rule 8(e) requires a vessel to slacken speed, stop or reverse if necessary to avoid collision or to allow more time to assess the situation. A speed reduction is slow to show on a large ship, so make it substantial. It is often combined with a course alteration.

What does 'finally past and clear' mean in Rule 8(d)?

It means the risk of collision with that vessel has completely gone. She is no longer closing, and resuming your course will not bring you back into a close-quarters situation. Until then you must keep checking the effectiveness of your action by visual bearings and radar.

If a fishing vessel is required not to impede me, do I still have to give way?

Possibly. Rule 8(f) requires the fishing vessel to act early so that risk of collision does not develop. If risk of collision does develop, both vessels must comply with Part B. Under Rule 18, a power-driven vessel underway generally keeps out of the way of a vessel engaged in fishing.

Test yourself

Under Rule 8(b), what is the test for an alteration of course or speed?

Rule 8(b) sets no numerical minimum and discourages a succession of small alterations. The test is whether the alteration is readily apparent to an observer. (COLREG Rule 8(b))

In which conditions of visibility does Rule 8 apply?

Rule 8 is in Part B, Section I, which applies in any condition of visibility. (COLREG Rule 4)

According to Rule 8(c), when may alteration of course alone be the most effective action?

Rule 8(c) makes course-alone action conditional on sea-room, good time, substantial size and not creating another close-quarters situation. (COLREG Rule 8(c))

A vessel required not to impede another finds that risk of collision has developed. What does Rule 8(f)(ii) say?

The vessel is not relieved of her duty not to impede when risk of collision develops, and must consider the Part B rules when acting. (COLREG Rule 8(f)(ii))

Rule 8(e) requires a vessel to slacken speed or take all way off in which circumstances?

Rule 8(e) applies in any visibility, whenever slowing or stopping is needed to avoid collision or gain time to assess. (COLREG Rule 8(e))
