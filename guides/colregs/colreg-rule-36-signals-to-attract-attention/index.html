COLREG Rule 36 – Signals to attract attention

Quick answer

Rule 36 allows any vessel to make light or sound signals to attract another vessel's attention, or to direct a searchlight towards a danger. Three conditions apply. The signal must not be mistakable for any other COLREG signal or for an aid to navigation, it must not embarrass any vessel, and high-intensity intermittent or revolving lights such as strobes should be avoided. It is permissive and never replaces the action the steering and sailing rules require.

What Rule 36 actually says

Rule 36 is short and is often skimmed in exam preparation. In paraphrase, it says:

  • Any vessel, if it is necessary to attract another vessel's attention, may make light or sound signals. These must not be capable of being mistaken for any signal authorised elsewhere in the Rules.
  • Alternatively, she may direct the beam of her searchlight in the direction of the danger. She must do this in such a way as not to embarrass any vessel.
  • Any light used to attract attention must not be capable of being mistaken for an aid to navigation.
  • The use of high-intensity intermittent or revolving lights, such as strobe lights, shall be avoided for this purpose.

That is the whole Rule. It does not create a new signal. It gives you freedom to improvise, within limits, when the prescribed signals do not fit the situation.

The key words, unpacked

"Any vessel": the Rule applies to every vessel, whatever her size, type or status. It applies whether she is underway, at anchor, give-way or stand-on.

"If necessary": Rule 36 is permissive ("may"), not mandatory. Nobody is required to use it. However, failing to use an obvious means of warning may still be judged under Rule 2, the ordinary practice of seamen.

"Cannot be mistaken for any signal authorised elsewhere": your improvised signal must not resemble any of these:

  • manoeuvring signals of one, two or three short blasts or flashes (Rule 34(a) and (b))
  • narrow-channel overtaking signals (Rule 34(c))
  • the doubt or warning signal of five or more short, rapid blasts or flashes (Rule 34(d))
  • the bend signal (Rule 34(e))
  • the restricted-visibility signals of Rule 35
  • any distress signal under Rule 37 and Annex IV

"Direction of the danger": the searchlight is aimed at the hazard, not into the other vessel's wheelhouse. Examples are the wreck, the unlit buoy, the small craft or your own anchored hull.

"Not to embarrass any vessel": in practice this means do not dazzle a watchkeeper, do not destroy someone's night vision and do not create confusion.

"Not mistaken for an aid to navigation": a regular flashing pattern can look like a light buoy or beacon. This is why the strobe prohibition exists.

Rule 36 versus the other signal rules

Examiners like to test whether you can tell Rule 36 apart from its neighbours.

Situation Rule Signal
Vessels in sight, one in doubt about the other's intentions or action 34(d) At least five short and rapid blasts; may be supplemented by at least five short and rapid flashes
Altering course or going astern in sight of another vessel 34(a), (b) One, two or three short blasts / flashes
Anchored in or near restricted visibility, warning an approaching vessel 35(g) Optional one short, one prolonged, one short blast, in addition to the bell (and gong where required)
Need to attract attention for any other reason 36 Any light or sound signal not mistakable for the above, or a searchlight on the danger
Distress and need of assistance 37 / Annex IV Prescribed distress signals only

If a prescribed signal fits the situation, use it. Rule 36 is for situations the prescribed signals do not cover. It is not a substitute for Rule 34(d) when you are in doubt about a vessel in sight.

Remember also that Annex IV forbids using distress signals, or anything that could be confused with them, except to indicate distress. Firing a parachute flare to wake up a fishing boat is wrong.

Applying it on the bridge: a step-by-step approach

When you think another vessel has not seen you, or has not seen a danger, work through this order:

  1. Confirm the threat. Use radar/ARPA, AIS and visual bearings, as Rules 5 and 7 require. Is there really a risk, and how much time do you have?
  2. Use the prescribed signal if one applies. If you are in sight and in doubt, use Rule 34(d). If it is restricted visibility and you are at anchor, consider Rule 35(g).
  3. Add a Rule 36 signal if needed. Options include:
    • the signalling lamp (Aldis/daylight signalling light), for example Morse U (·· −), which the International Code of Signals defines as "You are running into danger"
    • the searchlight directed at the danger, such as your own hull, an obstruction or a small craft
    • whistle signals that cannot be confused with Rules 34 and 35
  4. Call on VHF if practicable. This is a communication, not a COLREG signal. Make sure you are certain which vessel you are talking to.
  5. Take whatever action the Rules require of you. Signalling never relieves you of your obligations under Rules 8, 16, 17 or 2.
  6. Call the Master in line with the standing orders and the company SMS, and record the signals made in the logbook.

Company standing orders and the SMS may add specific requirements, such as when to call the Master or mandatory log entries. Follow your own ship's instructions.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – At anchor, clear night, a vessel closing. You are anchored in an anchorage. A coaster is heading towards you with CPA near zero, and her bearing has been steady for several minutes. No action is apparent.

  • She is in sight and you are in doubt whether she is taking sufficient action to avoid collision, so sound at least five short and rapid blasts (Rule 34(d)), optionally supplemented by at least five short and rapid flashes. Then add Rule 36 measures as below.
  • Switch on the available working or equivalent lights to illuminate your decks. Rule 30(c) permits this for any anchored vessel and requires it for vessels of 100 m or more in length.
  • Use the signalling lamp with Morse U towards her.
  • Use the searchlight on your own hull, which is the danger, not into her wheelhouse.
  • Call her on VHF, put the engine on standby, inform the Master, and log the events.

Scenario 2 – A small craft heading for an unlit hazard. Underway at night, you see a semi-submerged drifting container ahead. A small fishing boat is steering straight for it.

  • Direct your searchlight onto the container so the boat sees what is in its path.
  • Take care that the beam does not sweep across the boat's crew or other traffic.
  • Report the hazard as a navigational danger in accordance with SOLAS Chapter V and your company procedures.

Scenario 3 – Daytime, crossing vessel not reacting. You are the stand-on vessel and the give-way vessel is not altering course.

  • Rule 34(d) is the right signal here: five or more short, rapid blasts, optionally with flashes. Rule 36 is not the first tool.
  • Then follow Rule 17. You may act alone once it is apparent the other vessel is not taking appropriate action. You must act when collision cannot be avoided by her action alone.

Scenario 4 – What not to do. A cadet suggests switching on the strobe on the mast to wake up a yacht. Strobes are specifically to be avoided under Rule 36. They can be mistaken for an aid to navigation and they degrade night vision.

Common mistakes

  • Shining the searchlight into the other ship's bridge. This blinds her watchkeeper at the very moment you need them to see. Aim at the danger instead.
  • Using strobes or rotating beacons. These are specifically to be avoided, and could be mistaken for a buoy, a beacon or a special-purpose light.
  • Improvised whistle patterns that mimic prescribed signals. For example, three short blasts tell the other vessel you are going astern.
  • Using distress signals to attract attention. Annex IV prohibits this. It also triggers unnecessary SAR action.
  • Treating Rule 36 as a substitute for action. A signal does not discharge give-way, stand-on or safe-speed duties.
  • Using Rule 36 when Rule 34(d) applies. If you are in sight and in doubt, the doubt signal is the correct one.
  • Relying on VHF alone. You may misidentify the vessel, or agree something that conflicts with the Rules.
  • Not logging it. If there is an incident, the record of signals made matters.

Oral exam points

Q: State the essence of Rule 36. A: Any vessel may use light or sound signals, or direct her searchlight at a danger, to attract attention. Three conditions apply: the signals must not be mistakable for other COLREG signals or aids to navigation, they must not embarrass other vessels, and strobes and similar lights are to be avoided.

Q: Is Rule 36 mandatory? A: No, it is permissive. However, Rule 2 can still hold you responsible for neglecting an obvious precaution.

Q: Where do you point the searchlight? A: In the direction of the danger, without dazzling or embarrassing any vessel.

Q: Give an acceptable Rule 36 light signal. A: Morse U by signalling lamp, meaning "You are running into danger" in the International Code of Signals. Make sure it cannot be confused with Rule 34 flashes.

Q: Can you fire a red flare to attract attention? A: No. Distress signals may only be used to indicate distress (Rule 37 and Annex IV).

Q: Difference between Rule 34(d) and Rule 36? A: Rule 34(d) is a prescribed signal for vessels in sight of one another when one is in doubt. Rule 36 is a general permission for any situation where attention must be attracted, using non-prescribed signals.

Examiners often follow up with "and then what?". Always finish by stating the action you would take under the steering and sailing rules.

Key points
  • Rule 36 is permissive: any vessel may use light or sound signals, or a searchlight, to attract attention.
  • The signal must not be mistakable for any other COLREG signal or for an aid to navigation.
  • Direct the searchlight at the danger, never in a way that dazzles or embarrasses another vessel.
  • High-intensity intermittent or revolving lights such as strobes are to be avoided.
  • If Rule 34(d) or Rule 35(g) fits the situation, use the prescribed signal first.
  • Distress signals must never be used just to attract attention.
  • Signalling never replaces the action required by the steering and sailing rules.

Frequently asked questions

What is COLREG Rule 36 in simple terms?

It permits any vessel to make light or sound signals, or to shine a searchlight towards a danger, when she needs to attract another vessel's attention. The signals must not resemble any other COLREG signal or an aid to navigation, and must not embarrass other vessels. Strobe-type lights should be avoided.

Can I use the Aldis lamp to flash Morse U under Rule 36?

Yes, this is a widely used and acceptable practice. U means "You are running into danger" in the International Code of Signals. Make sure the flashing cannot be confused with the manoeuvring or doubt signals of Rule 34, and aim the lamp so it does not dazzle the other bridge.

Is it allowed to shine a searchlight at another ship's bridge?

Rule 36 allows the searchlight to be directed towards the danger, in a way that does not embarrass any vessel. Shining it into a wheelhouse blinds the watchkeeper and is contrary to the spirit of the Rule. Illuminate the hazard, or your own hull, instead.

What is the difference between Rule 36 and the five short blasts signal?

The five or more short, rapid blasts are a prescribed signal under Rule 34(d). They are used when vessels in sight of one another approach and one is in doubt about the other's intentions or actions. Rule 36 is a general permission to attract attention using non-prescribed signals in situations the other rules do not cover.

Why should strobe lights be avoided under Rule 36?

The Rule says high-intensity intermittent or revolving lights, such as strobes, should be avoided. They can be confused with aids to navigation or other special lights. They can also impair the night vision of watchkeepers on other vessels.

Test yourself

Under Rule 36, in which direction should a searchlight be directed?

Rule 36 permits the searchlight to be directed towards the danger, in such a way as not to embarrass any vessel. (COLREG Rule 36)

Which type of light does Rule 36 say should be avoided when attracting attention?

Rule 36 specifically states that high-intensity intermittent or revolving lights, such as strobe lights, shall be avoided. (COLREG Rule 36)

Two power-driven vessels in sight of each other are approaching, and you are in doubt whether the other is taking sufficient action. Which signal is correct?

When in sight and in doubt, Rule 34(d) prescribes at least five short and rapid blasts, optionally supplemented by flashes. Rule 36 does not replace it. (COLREG Rule 34(d))

Is a vessel obliged to make a signal under Rule 36?

Rule 36 uses "may", so it is permissive. Neglecting an obvious precaution can still be judged under Rule 2. (COLREG Rules 2 and 36)

Your ship is not in distress, but a yacht is not responding to your signals. Which of these is NOT permitted?

Distress signals may only be used to indicate distress and need of assistance. Using them to attract attention is prohibited. (COLREG Rule 37 and Annex IV)
