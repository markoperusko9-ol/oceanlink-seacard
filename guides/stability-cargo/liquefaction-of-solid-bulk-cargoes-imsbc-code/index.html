Liquefaction of solid bulk cargoes under the IMSBC Code

Quick answer

A Group A bulk cargo such as nickel ore, iron ore fines or mineral concentrate can turn from a firm stow into a fluid slurry at sea if its moisture content is too high. The cargo can then shift and capsize the ship. The IMSBC Code controls this risk by requiring the moisture content (MC) at loading to stay below the Transportable Moisture Limit (TML), backed by the shipper's certificates, recent tests and the master's own checks.

What liquefaction is and why it sinks ships

Liquefaction affects cargoes made of fine particles with some moisture between the grains. In port the stow looks dry and firm. At sea, engine vibration and the ship's motion compact the cargo. The spaces between particles shrink, and the water in them comes under pressure. Friction between the grains drops sharply, and the cargo starts to behave like a liquid. The Code calls this a flow state.

A liquefied cargo does not act like a normal free surface that sloshes back and forth. It flows to the low side on a roll and then partly re-solidifies there. The next roll moves more cargo across, so the ship builds a list step by step. This can happen within hours, and a vessel with good intact GM can still capsize.

Typical casualties have involved:

  • Supramax and Handysize bulkers loading nickel ore in Indonesia and the Philippines during the wet season
  • iron ore fines
  • the loss of Bulk Jupiter with bauxite from Malaysia in 2015

The Bulk Jupiter case drew attention to dynamic separation. In that process, water and fines separate from the solid cargo during the voyage and form a free slurry on top. It is a related but different mechanism from classic liquefaction.

Cargo groups and the key definitions

The IMSBC Code is mandatory under SOLAS chapter VI. Dangerous goods in solid form in bulk are also covered by chapter VII. The Code sorts cargoes into three groups:

Group Hazard Examples
A May liquefy if shipped above its TML Mineral concentrates, nickel ore, iron ore fines, bauxite fines
B Chemical hazard Direct reduced iron, some coals, sulphur
C Neither liquefies nor has a chemical hazard Many coarse ores, bauxite (coarse grades meeting the BAUXITE schedule)

Some cargoes are both A and B. Always check the individual schedule in Appendix 1. Also note that the same commodity name can fall into a different group depending on particle size and moisture.

Key definitions, paraphrased:

  • Moisture content (MC): the share of a representative sample that is water, ice or other liquid. It is expressed as a percentage of the total wet mass.
  • Flow Moisture Point (FMP): the moisture content at which a flow state develops under the prescribed laboratory test.
  • Transportable Moisture Limit (TML): the maximum moisture content considered safe for carriage in ships that are not specially constructed or fitted. It is derived from laboratory tests in Appendix 2. For the flow table and penetration tests, TML is 90% of the FMP. Other methods, such as the Proctor/Fagerberg variants, derive TML differently. Some cargoes, such as iron ore fines, have their own specified test method.

The basic rule: on an ordinary bulk carrier, a Group A cargo may be loaded only if its MC is below its TML.

Formulas and a worked example

Moisture content (wet basis):

MC % = (wet mass − dry mass) ÷ wet mass × 100

TML from a flow table test:

TML = 0.9 × FMP

Worked example 1: a laboratory sample

  • Wet sample mass = 500 g
  • Mass after drying = 455 g
  • Water = 45 g
  • MC = 45 ÷ 500 × 100 = 9.0%
  • Flow table test gives FMP = 10.5%, so TML = 0.9 × 10.5 = 9.45%
  • MC 9.0% is below TML 9.45%, so the cargo is acceptable on paper. The margin is only 0.45 percentage points.

A common trap is calculating on a dry basis: 45 ÷ 455 × 100 = 9.89%. That figure is wrong for IMSBC purposes. If you use it, you would wrongly conclude the cargo is above TML. Always divide by wet mass.

Worked example 2: rain on the stockpile

  • Assume the stockpile's certified TML is 9.45% (as in example 1).
  • Stockpile = 50,000 t at MC 8.5%
  • Water content = 0.085 × 50,000 = 4,250 t
  • Overnight rain is absorbed: +500 t of water
  • New MC = (4,250 + 500) ÷ (50,000 + 500) × 100 = 4,750 ÷ 50,500 × 100 ≈ 9.41%

That is almost at the 9.45% TML from a modest amount of rain. Moisture is also never evenly spread through a stockpile, so wet pockets will be well above the average. This is why a certificate issued before a downpour is not reassuring.

Documents and time limits before loading

Under Section 4 of the Code, the shipper must give the master the cargo information in advance. For a Group A cargo this includes:

  • A certificate of the TML. The TML test must have been done within six months before loading starts. It must be repeated sooner if the cargo's composition or characteristics have changed.
  • A certificate or declaration of the MC. Sampling and testing must be done as close as practicable to loading, and never more than seven days before loading starts.
  • Fresh checks after significant rain or snow between testing and loading, to confirm the MC is still below the TML.

The Code also expects sampling and testing at the loading port to follow procedures overseen by the port's competent authority. How strictly this is applied varies widely between countries and between ports. Company SMS and P&I club guidance often add requirements, for example appointing an independent surveyor for high-risk cargoes or loading ports.

Things to look for in the paperwork:

  • The certificate actually names the cargo you are loading.
  • The dates fall within the limits above.
  • MC and TML are expressed on the same basis.
  • The test method is the one required by the individual schedule.

On-board procedure: before, during and after loading

Before loading

  1. Read the individual schedule for the cargo and check its group.
  2. Check the TML and MC certificates against the time limits.
  3. Prepare the holds. Bilge wells should be clean, dry and covered so water can drain while cargo is kept out. Test the bilge suctions.
  4. Look at the stockpiles. Watch for standing water, uncovered heaps, recent rain, or cargo that looks muddy or glistens.

During loading

  • Watch each grab or conveyor discharge. Signs of trouble include wet cargo, splashing, cargo spreading flat instead of forming a cone, and water appearing at the edges.
  • Do the can test (Section 8) at intervals:
    1. Half-fill a cylindrical can of about 0.5 to 1 litre with a sample.
    2. Strike it sharply on a hard surface from about 0.2 m, 25 times at 1 to 2 second intervals.
    3. If free moisture or a fluid surface appears, stop and arrange further laboratory testing before loading continues.
  • Close hatches during rain when the cargo is liable to absorb water.
  • Trim the cargo reasonably level as the Code requires, to reduce the chance of a shift.

At sea

  • Sound the bilges regularly and log the results. Water draining out of the cargo is an early warning.
  • Inspect the cargo surface where it is safe to do so. Look for free water, slumping or flattening, and cracks.
  • Watch for an unexplained, slowly increasing list.

The can test only screens a sample. A sample that passes does not prove the whole cargo is safe. A sample that fails, however, is a strong reason to refuse or stop.

If the cargo starts to liquefy at sea

If free water or a fluid surface appears, or the ship takes on a progressive list, good practice (company SMS and P&I guidance) is for the master to act to prevent a cargo shift and capsize. In practice:

  • Reduce motions. Alter course and speed to cut rolling and pitching, and avoid heading into heavier weather.
  • Do not pump ballast in haste to correct the list. If the cargo moves back, the ship can be thrown over to the other side. Assess the stability with the chief officer first.
  • Keep pumping the bilges if water is draining into them.
  • Seek shelter or a place of refuge. Inform the company, the coastal state and the nearest RCC early, while the ship can still be handled.
  • Prepare for the worst. Send an urgency message early. Transmit a DSC distress alert and activate the EPIRB as soon as the ship is in grave and imminent danger. Muster the crew and prepare to launch survival craft on the lee side.

Speed of decision is critical. Recorded losses suggest that the time from the first list to capsize can be very short.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Common mistakes

  • Treating the shipper's certificate as the end of the matter. The master can and should refuse cargo that looks or tests unsafe.
  • Calculating MC on a dry basis.
  • Accepting an MC certificate that is more than seven days old, or one issued before heavy rain.
  • Assuming high GM protects the ship. Liquefaction defeats normal stability margins.
  • Assuming a Group C entry is safe without checking that the cargo matches the schedule description, for example treating bauxite fines as Group C bauxite.

Typical oral exam questions

  • Define TML and FMP and state their relationship for the flow table test. TML is the safe moisture limit. For the flow table test it is 90% of the FMP.
  • How recent must the MC test be? No more than seven days before loading starts, and rechecked after significant rain.
  • Describe the can test and what you do if it fails. Give the method from Section 8. If it fails, stop loading and require laboratory tests.
  • What are the warning signs at sea? Water in the bilges, free water on the cargo surface, slumping, and a progressive list.
  • Can a cargo above its TML ever be carried? Only in a ship specially constructed or specially fitted for it, with Administration approval.
Key points
  • Group A cargoes can turn into a flow state at sea and shift, even when the ship has a healthy GM.
  • On an ordinary bulk carrier, the moisture content at loading must be below the TML.
  • For flow table and penetration tests, TML is 90% of the Flow Moisture Point.
  • Always calculate moisture content on a wet-mass basis.
  • The TML test must be within six months of loading and the MC test within seven days, with rechecks after significant rain.
  • The can test is a screening check: a fail means stop and test further, but a pass does not prove the cargo is safe.
  • Bilge water, free surface water, cargo slumping or a creeping list at sea call for immediate action and early reporting.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between TML and FMP?

The Flow Moisture Point is the moisture content at which the cargo actually starts to flow in the laboratory test. The Transportable Moisture Limit is a lower, safe figure derived from it. For the flow table and penetration tests, the TML is 90% of the FMP.

Can the master refuse to load a Group A cargo even if the shipper's certificate is in order?

Yes. The master is responsible for the safety of the ship and is not bound to accept cargo that appears or tests unsafe. Examples include a failed can test, visibly wet cargo or heavy rain since sampling. Inform the company and P&I club, and record the reasons in writing.

How old can the moisture content certificate be?

Sampling and testing for moisture content must be done as close as practicable to loading. The interval must never exceed seven days before loading starts. If there has been significant rain or snow since the test, further checks are needed to confirm the cargo is still below its TML.

Is the can test enough to prove a cargo is safe to load?

No. The can test is a simple screening check done by the ship's staff. A failure is a clear warning to stop and demand laboratory testing. A pass only means that particular sample showed no free moisture, so it does not replace the shipper's certified tests.

Is bauxite a liquefaction risk?

Bauxite meeting the BAUXITE schedule criteria remains Group C. Since Amendment 05-19 (mandatory 1 January 2021), BAUXITE FINES has its own Group A schedule because of the risk of dynamic separation. That process forms a slurry of water and fines on top of the cargo, as in the Bulk Jupiter loss. Check the shipper's particle-size data and moisture data against the schedules to decide which applies.

Test yourself

Under the IMSBC Code, how is moisture content expressed?

The Code defines moisture content on a wet-mass basis. Using dry mass overstates the result. Reference: IMSBC Code, Section 1 definitions.

A flow table test gives an FMP of 12.0%. What is the TML?

For the flow table test, TML is 90% of FMP: 0.9 × 12.0 = 10.8%. Reference: IMSBC Code, Appendix 2.

What is the maximum interval between moisture content sampling and testing and the start of loading?

MC testing must be as close as practicable to loading and never more than seven days before it. Six months applies to the TML test. Reference: IMSBC Code, Section 4.

During loading of nickel ore, a can test shows free moisture on the sample surface. What should be done?

A failed can test indicates the cargo may be above its TML, so further laboratory testing is needed before loading continues. Reference: IMSBC Code, Section 8.

A Group A cargo is above its TML. On which ship may it be carried?

Cargo above TML may only be carried in specially constructed or specially fitted ships approved by the Administration. Reference: IMSBC Code, Section 7.
