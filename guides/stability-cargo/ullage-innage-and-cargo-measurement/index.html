Ullage, innage and cargo measurement: a practical guide for tanker officers

Quick answer

Ullage is the distance from a fixed reference point at the top of a tank down to the liquid surface. Innage (sounding) is the depth of liquid measured up from the datum at the bottom, and the two add up to the tank's reference height. Cargo quantity is found by correcting the reading for trim and list, entering the calibration tables for volume, deducting free water, and applying the volume correction factor and density to get standard volume and mass.

Ullage, innage and reference height: the basic geometry

Every gauged tank has two fixed points, and both are shown in the tank calibration tables:

  • Reference point (gauge point) – a marked point at the top, usually on the ullage hatch, vapour lock valve or sounding pipe. All ullages are measured from here.
  • Datum point (datum plate or striking plate) – the point at the bottom where a sounding tape or bob lands. All innages are measured from here.

The vertical distance between the two is the reference height, also called the reference gauge height. The relationship you must know cold for the oral:

Reference height = Ullage + Innage

Term Measured from Measured to Typical use
Ullage Reference point (top) Liquid surface Cargo tanks, especially when full or nearly full
Innage / sounding Datum (bottom) Liquid surface Ballast, bunker and fresh water tanks, small quantities, free water

On tankers, ullage is preferred for cargo. The tape does not have to reach the bottom, so viscous or waxy cargo, sediment and a damaged striking plate do not affect the reading. Innage is the natural choice for ballast and service tanks with sounding pipes, and for measuring free water at the bottom of a cargo tank.

If an ullage and an innage taken at the same point do not add up to the tabulated reference height, something is wrong. The tape may be damaged, the datum plate may be fouled, or the sounding pipe may be bent. Investigate before you sign anything.

Gauging equipment and the IBC/IGC categories

You will meet several types of equipment:

  • Fixed gauges – radar, float or pressure-type. These are fitted for continuous monitoring during cargo operations.
  • Portable UTI tapes (ullage, temperature, interface) – these read ullage, temperature and the oil/water interface in one run. They are normally lowered through vapour lock valves so the tank stays closed.
  • Steel tape with bob, and water-finding paste – the traditional method. It is still used where the cargo and the ship's procedures allow.

The IBC Code (Chapter 13) sorts gauging devices for chemical tankers by how much they open the tank:

  • Open – uses an opening in the tank and may expose the gauge user to cargo or vapour. An ullage hatch is an example.
  • Restricted – penetrates the tank but releases only a small amount of vapour or liquid when in use. A sounding tube is an example.
  • Closed – penetrates the tank but stays part of a closed system and keeps the contents in.
  • Indirect – does not penetrate the tank shell at all.

Gas carriers under the IGC Code (Chapter 13) do not use open gauging. Level gauging must be by indirect devices or by closed devices, either non-penetrating or penetrating but forming part of a closed system.

The product's entry in the IBC Code list sets the minimum gauging type allowed for that cargo. Inerted tanks are normally gauged by closed methods so the inert atmosphere is maintained. Any open or restricted gauging of an inerted tank must follow the precautions in ISGOTT and the company SMS.

Two safety points always apply:

  • Static electricity: bond portable metallic equipment to the ship's structure before it goes into the tank. Respect the settling time after loading that ISGOTT and your SMS require before dipping non-inerted tanks.
  • Calibration: fixed gauges drift. Many companies require regular cross-checks of fixed gauges against a calibrated portable UTI. The frequency and acceptable difference are set by the company SMS and terminal requirements, not by one international figure.

Trim, list and why the tables need correcting

Calibration tables are normally drawn up for the ship on even keel and upright. The reference point is rarely at the geometric centre of the tank. When the ship is trimmed or listed, the gauge point sits higher or lower relative to the level liquid surface than it would on even keel. The raw reading therefore does not match the tabulated volume.

To deal with this:

  • Apply the trim correction and list correction from the tables. They are usually tabulated against trim (or list) and ullage, and may be added to the ullage or applied to the volume, depending on how the tables are laid out.
  • Take the sign from the table. Do not reason it out under pressure at 0300. Read the table heading to check whether trim is defined by the stern or by the head and whether the correction applies to ullage or volume.
  • Use the trim and list at the moment of gauging, from draughts or the loading computer, not the departure figures.

When the quantity is very small, as with remaining on board (ROB) or on-board quantity (OBQ), the liquid may not touch every bulkhead. It lies as a wedge against the lower end of the tank. Normal trim corrections then give a wrong answer, and the wedge formula or wedge tables in the calibration book are used instead. This matters in disputes over ROB after discharge.

From tape reading to tonnes: the calculation chain

The standard petroleum sequence, using terms from the industry measurement standards, runs as follows:

  1. Observed ullage → apply trim/list corrections → corrected ullage.
  2. Enter the calibration table and interpolate → Total Observed Volume (TOV).
  3. Deduct free water measured by interface probe or paste → Gross Observed Volume (GOV).
  4. Multiply GOV by the Volume Correction Factor (VCF) for the observed temperature and the density at 15 °C → Gross Standard Volume (GSV) at 15 °C. Use ASTM/API Table 54A for crude and 54B for products. Imperial-unit cargoes use the 60 °F tables.
  5. Deduct sediment and water held in suspension, from the shore laboratory analysis → Net Standard Volume (NSV). This applies mainly to crude.
  6. Multiply by the Weight Correction Factor (WCF) → metric tonnes in air. In metric practice, WCF is the density at 15 °C in vacuum minus 0.0011 (Table 56). Multiplying by density at 15 °C instead gives mass in vacuum.

Temperature is normally the average of readings at several levels, typically upper, middle and lower when depth allows. The number of readings follows the terminal, cargo inspector and company procedure.

The ship's total is compared with the shore figure. Over time, the ratio of ship to shore figures is tracked as the Vessel Experience Factor (VEF). It is a commercial tool and does not replace measurement.

Worked example: one product tank

Tank 3 Port, gas oil. Reference height 18.50 m. Trimmed 1.2 m by the stern, no list.

  • Observed ullage: 1.420 m
  • Trim correction from the table for 1.2 m by stern: +0.012 m
  • Corrected ullage: 1.420 + 0.012 = 1.432 m
  • Cargo innage check: 18.50 − 1.432 = 17.068 m

Interpolating the calibration table:

Ullage (m) Volume (m³)
1.42 4,985.6
1.44 4,977.2

The difference is 8.4 m³ per 0.02 m, which is 0.42 m³ per mm. The corrected ullage is 12 mm beyond 1.42 m, so 12 × 0.42 = 5.04 m³.

Volume falls as ullage rises, so TOV = 4,985.6 − 5.04 = 4,980.56 m³.

Free water: the interface reading gives 3.50 m³ from the tables. GOV = 4,980.56 − 3.50 = 4,977.06 m³

Volume correction: density at 15 °C is 0.8450 and average temperature is 32.0 °C. Table 54B gives a VCF of approximately 0.9857. GSV = 4,977.06 × 0.9857 = 4,905.89 m³

Mass:

  • WCF = 0.8450 − 0.0011 = 0.8439
  • Mass in air = 4,905.89 × 0.8439 = 4,140.08 t
  • For comparison, mass in vacuum = 4,905.89 × 0.8450 = 4,145.48 t

Always take the VCF from the table edition your company and the charter party specify. Different editions and rounding rules can change the fourth decimal.

Common mistakes that cause cargo claims

  • Gauging before the cargo has settled. Turbulence, foam and entrained vapour give false ullages.
  • Wrong reference height. Using the figure for the wrong gauge point, or ignoring a modified hatch or a new vapour lock valve.
  • Wrong sign on the trim correction. Misreading 'by head' and 'by stern' columns.
  • Interpolating in the wrong direction. Forgetting that volume decreases as ullage increases.
  • Stale trim. Correcting with the trim from an hour earlier, after ballast or cargo has moved.
  • Single-point temperature in a stratified or heated cargo.
  • Mixing tables. Using 54A for a product, using 60 °F tables with 15 °C density, or applying the VCF to TOV instead of GOV.
  • Unrecorded free water. Missing water is later claimed as a cargo loss or contamination.
  • Tape damage. Kinked or stretched tapes, or a UTI not checked against a certified reference.

Good practice is to gauge jointly with the surveyor, record every raw reading before any correction, and keep the ullage report consistent with the Oil Record Book or Cargo Record Book entries.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

  • Define ullage and innage. What is the relationship between them? Ullage is measured from the reference point down to the liquid, and innage from the datum up to the liquid. Their sum is the reference height.
  • Why measure ullage rather than sound a cargo tank? The tape does not have to reach the bottom, so sediment, a fouled striking plate and viscous cargo do not affect the reading. It is also more accurate near full.
  • Why do we apply trim corrections? The tables are for even keel and the gauge point is off the tank centre.
  • When is the wedge formula used? For small quantities that do not cover the whole tank bottom when trimmed, typically ROB and OBQ.
  • Explain TOV, GOV, GSV and NSV. See the calculation chain above.
  • What is a VCF and how do you get it? It corrects observed volume to standard temperature, using the appropriate ASTM/API table with density at 15 °C and the observed temperature.
  • Why is WCF less than density? Air buoyancy. Commercial cargo is usually traded by weight in air.
  • What precautions apply to manual gauging? Bonding, settling time, closed gauging for inerted or toxic cargo as normally required, a gas-free or protected position, and correct PPE.
  • The ship figure is 0.5% short of shore. What do you do? Re-check readings, trim, tables, temperatures and free water. Compare with the VEF. Issue a letter of protest according to company procedure.
Key points
  • Ullage is measured down from the reference point; innage is measured up from the datum; together they equal the reference height.
  • Calibration tables assume even keel, so trim and list corrections from the tables must be applied using the trim at the time of gauging.
  • Small quantities such as ROB and OBQ that do not cover the tank bottom need the wedge formula, not normal trim corrections.
  • The chain runs TOV, minus free water to GOV, times VCF to GSV, minus S&W to NSV, times WCF to tonnes in air.
  • Use Table 54A for crude and 54B for products; WCF equals density at 15 °C minus 0.0011.
  • Bond portable gauging equipment, respect settling times and normally gauge inerted or toxic cargo by closed methods; gas carriers never use open gauging.
  • Record raw readings first and gauge jointly with the surveyor to protect against cargo claims.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between ullage and sounding?

Ullage is the empty space measured from the reference point at the top of the tank down to the liquid surface. Sounding (innage) is the depth of liquid measured from the datum plate at the bottom up to the surface. Cargo tanks are usually ullaged, while ballast and bunker tanks are usually sounded.

What is reference height in tank gauging?

It is the vertical distance between the gauge reference point at the top and the datum point at the bottom, as given in the calibration tables. Ullage plus innage at the same point should equal it. A mismatch points to a damaged tape, a fouled datum plate or the wrong reference figure.

Why is free water deducted before applying the VCF?

Free water is not cargo, and water does not expand with temperature in the same way as oil. It is deducted from TOV to give GOV, and only GOV is corrected to standard temperature. Applying the VCF to the total volume overstates the cargo.

What is the difference between weight in air and weight in vacuum?

Weight in vacuum is standard volume multiplied by density at 15 °C. Weight in air allows for air buoyancy and uses the weight correction factor, which in metric practice is density minus 0.0011. Most cargo documents use weight in air, but check the charter party.

How often should fixed tank gauges be checked against a portable UTI?

No single international figure applies. The interval and acceptable tolerance are set by the company SMS, the gauge maker's guidance and sometimes the terminal or charterer. Record each cross-check, and report persistent differences so the gauge can be recalibrated.

Test yourself

A tank has a reference height of 16.20 m and a corrected ullage of 2.35 m. What is the innage?

Innage equals reference height minus ullage: 16.20 − 2.35 = 13.85 m. Adding the two figures is a common slip.

Why are trim corrections applied to observed ullages?

Tank calibration tables are prepared for even keel, so the off-centre gauge point needs correcting when the ship is trimmed. Trim has no effect on density or VCF.

Which volume is the VCF applied to?

Free water is removed from TOV to give GOV, and GOV × VCF gives GSV at standard temperature, per the ASTM/API petroleum measurement tables.

A small ROB in a trimmed tank does not touch the forward bulkhead. How should its volume be found?

When liquid lies as a wedge and does not cover the tank bottom, standard trim corrections do not apply and the wedge method is used.

In metric practice, how is the Weight Correction Factor obtained?

Table 56 gives WCF as density in vacuum at 15 °C minus 0.0011, which allows for air buoyancy to give weight in air.
