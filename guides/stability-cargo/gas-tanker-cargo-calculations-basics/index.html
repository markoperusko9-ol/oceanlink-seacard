Gas tanker cargo calculations basics

Quick answer

On a gas tanker the cargo in each tank is two quantities: the liquid, worked out from corrected level, tank tables and liquid density, and the vapour above it, worked out from vapour volume, absolute pressure and temperature. LPG is normally accounted for in metric tonnes. LNG is normally sold on energy content, but it still starts from the same corrected liquid volume. Separately, the IGC Code loading limit caps how much liquid volume you may load in the first place.

Why gas cargo calculations are different from oil

On an oil tanker you can largely ignore the vapour space. On a gas carrier you cannot. The tank holds a boiling liquid in equilibrium with its own vapour, and both are cargo.

The main differences an examiner expects you to know:

  • Two phases. Total cargo = liquid mass + vapour mass. The vapour is usually a small fraction on a fully refrigerated ship. On pressurised or semi-refrigerated ships, and in nearly empty tanks, it can be significant.
  • Temperature and tank shape. Cargo tanks are calibrated at ambient temperature. At −42 °C (propane) or around −160 °C (LNG) the tank material contracts. Where the calibration tables provide a shrinkage or thermal correction, it must be applied.
  • Density basis. Liquid density is taken at the observed liquid temperature. The source may be the cargo documents, recognised LPG density/volume correction tables, or a calculation from the analysed composition. The method is set by the charter party or sale contract, not by the ship.
  • Closed gauging. Levels come from closed gauges: float, radar, capacitance or other approved types. The gauge's own corrections matter as much as trim and list.
  • Commercial unit. LPG and chemical gases are usually traded in tonnes, sometimes weight in air rather than mass in vacuo. LNG is usually traded on energy, typically in MMBtu, under the sale and purchase agreement.

Step-by-step: from gauge reading to cargo quantity

This is the sequence used at a custody transfer survey. Your ship's cargo manual and calibration tables are the authority for the exact corrections.

  1. Record conditions. Note draughts forward and aft (for trim), list from the inclinometer, and the time.
  2. Read the level in each tank. Use the primary gauge and compare it with the secondary, if fitted. Take several readings and average them if the surface is moving.
  3. Apply gauge corrections. Float gauges can need corrections for tape contraction at low temperature and for float immersion, as given in the gauge tables. Radar gauges may have their own calibration offsets.
  4. Apply trim and list corrections from the tank tables to get the corrected level (innage or ullage, as the tables are arranged).
  5. Read the liquid volume from the calibration table against the corrected level. Interpolate properly.
  6. Apply the tank shrinkage/thermal correction factor for the observed tank temperature, if the tables provide one.
  7. Record temperatures. Average only the sensors immersed in liquid for the liquid temperature. Average the sensors in the vapour space for the vapour temperature.
  8. Find liquid density at the observed liquid temperature by the agreed method.
  9. Liquid mass = corrected liquid volume × liquid density.
  10. Vapour volume = corrected total tank volume − corrected liquid volume.
  11. Vapour density from absolute pressure, vapour temperature and molecular mass (see next section).
  12. Vapour mass = vapour volume × vapour density.
  13. Total = liquid mass + vapour mass. Convert to weight in air if the contract requires it.

The difference between the before and after figures (opening and closing gauge) is the quantity loaded or discharged.

Formulas you must be able to write down

Liquid mass

m(liquid) = V(liquid, corrected) × ρ(liquid at observed temperature)

Vapour density (ideal gas approximation)

ρ(vapour) [kg/m³] = (M ÷ 22.414) × (273.15 ÷ T) × (P(abs) ÷ 1.01325)

Where:

  • M is the molecular mass of the vapour in kg/kmol. For propane this is about 44.1. For mixtures, use the molecular mass of the vapour phase as determined by the agreed method (vapour analysis or a calculated equilibrium vapour composition). It is usually lower than that of the liquid.
  • 22.414 is the molar volume in m³/kmol at 0 °C and 1.01325 bar.
  • T is the vapour temperature in kelvin (°C + 273.15).
  • P(abs) is the absolute pressure in bar: gauge pressure + atmospheric pressure.

Some contracts apply a compressibility factor for vapour at higher pressures. Follow the agreed standard.

Weight in air (common convention)

Weight in air ≈ V × (ρ in vacuo − 0.0011), with ρ in t/m³. The 0.0011 deduction accounts for air buoyancy. This is a simplified form of the petroleum convention. The exact buoyancy correction and density basis are defined by the measurement standard named in the contract. Whether and how this is applied to the vapour is also set by the contract and the surveyor's standard. Confirm it before you start.

IGC Code loading limit

LL = FL × ρR ÷ ρL

  • LL is the loading limit: the maximum liquid volume you may load, as a percentage of tank volume.
  • FL is the filling limit, normally 98%.
  • ρR is the relative density of the cargo at the reference temperature. That is the temperature at which the cargo's vapour pressure equals the relief valve set pressure.
  • ρL is the relative density at the loading temperature.

The Code allows a higher filling limit only under specified conditions accepted by the Administration.

Worked example: fully refrigerated propane

The figures below are illustrative. Your real densities, shrinkage factors and table values come from the ship's calibration tables and the cargo documents.

Data for one tank after loading:

Item Value
Total tank volume (100%, table) 11,000.000 m³
Liquid volume from table (after gauge, trim and list corrections) 10,500.000 m³
Tank shrinkage factor at observed temperature 0.99800
Liquid temperature −42 °C
Liquid density at −42 °C (in vacuo) 0.5810 t/m³
Vapour temperature −40 °C
Tank pressure 0.150 bar gauge
Atmospheric pressure 1.01325 bar
Molecular mass (propane) 44.10

Liquid

  • Corrected volume = 10,500.000 × 0.99800 = 10,479.000 m³
  • Mass = 10,479.000 × 0.5810 = 6,088.299 t

Vapour

  • Corrected total volume = 11,000.000 × 0.99800 = 10,978.000 m³
  • Vapour volume = 10,978.000 − 10,479.000 = 499.000 m³
  • P(abs) = 0.150 + 1.01325 = 1.16325 bar
  • T = −40 + 273.15 = 233.15 K
  • ρ(vapour) = (44.10 ÷ 22.414) × (273.15 ÷ 233.15) × (1.16325 ÷ 1.01325)
  • That is 1.9675 × 1.1716 × 1.1480 ≈ 2.646 kg/m³ (0.002646 t/m³)
  • Vapour mass = 499.000 × 0.002646 ≈ 1.320 t

Total in this tank (in vacuo): 6,088.299 + 1.320 = 6,089.619 t

If the contract uses weight in air with the simplified convention above, the liquid works out as 10,479.000 × (0.5810 − 0.0011) = 10,479.000 × 0.5799 = 6,076.772 t. The vapour is then treated according to the agreed method.

Note how small the vapour is here. With gauge pressure used instead of absolute, the vapour density would be badly wrong. An examiner will check exactly that.

LNG: same start, different finish

LNG measurement starts the same way. The custody transfer measurement system (CTMS) gives corrected liquid volumes, temperatures and pressures at opening and closing gauge.

The commercial result is then usually energy, under the terms of the sale and purchase agreement. In outline:

  • Energy transferred = volume transferred × LNG density × gross calorific value (mass basis).
  • Gas displaced through the vapour return line (ship to shore when loading, shore to ship when discharging) is deducted.
  • Gas consumed as fuel in the engine room during the operation is adjusted for, if the contract requires.
  • Density and calorific value are calculated from the composition analysed by the terminal. The ship does not use a single table value.

The ship's officers verify the CTMS readings, the trim and list, and the gauge and sensor condition, and they witness the survey. The energy calculation itself is normally performed by the terminal or independent surveyor using the agreed standards.

Common mistakes that cause cargo disputes

  • Using gauge pressure in the vapour formula instead of absolute pressure.
  • Leaving temperature in °C in the 273.15/T term instead of converting to kelvin.
  • Averaging all temperature sensors together. Liquid and vapour averages must be kept separate, and exposed sensors must not be counted as liquid.
  • Forgetting the vapour entirely, especially on semi-refrigerated or pressurised ships and when tanks are nearly empty after discharge.
  • Skipping the shrinkage factor, or applying it twice when the tables already include it.
  • Applying trim corrections when the tables assume even keel, or failing to apply them when they don't.
  • Mixing ullage and innage tables.
  • Mixing density bases. Using density at 15 °C with an observed volume, or in-air density with in-vacuo volumes.
  • Failing to cross-check primary and secondary gauges. Then a faulty float or radar is noticed only after the bill of lading is issued.
  • Not stating units on the ullage report (tonnes in air or in vacuo, m³ at observed or standard conditions).

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: What is the cargo in a gas tank made of, for calculation purposes? The liquid mass plus the mass of vapour in the space above it.

Q: Why do you need absolute pressure for vapour density? The gas laws relate density to absolute pressure. Gauge pressure ignores the atmosphere and understates density.

Q: What is a shrinkage factor? A correction for the contraction of the tank at cargo temperature. The tank was calibrated warm, so its actual volume at service temperature is smaller.

Q: State the IGC loading limit formula and explain each term. LL = FL × ρR/ρL. Explain each term as in the formulas section above, and explain why the reference temperature is linked to the relief valve set pressure. If the cargo warms, the liquid expands, and the tank must not become liquid-full before the valves lift.

Q: Why must you not fill to 98% when loading cold cargo? Cold cargo is denser. As it warms towards the reference temperature, it expands. The loading limit is therefore below the filling limit when the loading temperature is lower than the reference temperature.

Q: How is LNG normally invoiced? On energy. That is volume × density × calorific value, adjusted for displaced vapour and, where agreed, gas consumed. Density and calorific value come from composition analysis.

Q: Who decides whether weight in air or in vacuo is used? The contract and the agreed measurement standard. The ship records which basis was used.

Key points
  • Total gas cargo = liquid mass + vapour mass; never ignore the vapour.
  • Correct the level for gauge errors, trim and list before entering the calibration tables.
  • Apply the tank shrinkage factor where the tables require it, and only once.
  • Vapour density uses absolute pressure and kelvin, and the vapour's molecular mass.
  • IGC loading limit: LL = FL × ρR/ρL, with FL normally 98%.
  • LNG is usually invoiced on energy, while LPG is normally accounted for in tonnes.
  • Weight in air versus in vacuo, and the density basis, are set by the contract. State them on every report.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate vapour weight in a gas tanker cargo tank?

Take the vapour volume (corrected tank volume minus corrected liquid volume) and multiply it by vapour density. Vapour density is found from molecular mass ÷ 22.414, corrected by 273.15/T and by absolute pressure ÷ 1.01325. Use absolute pressure in bar and temperature in kelvin.

What is the difference between filling limit and loading limit under the IGC Code?

The filling limit is the maximum liquid volume, normally 98%, that the tank may contain at the reference temperature. The loading limit is the maximum you may load at the actual loading temperature. It is calculated as FL × ρR/ρL so that the liquid will not exceed the filling limit if it warms to the reference temperature.

Why do gas tank tables have a shrinkage factor?

Tanks are calibrated at ambient temperature but carry cargo at low temperatures, so the tank structure contracts. The shrinkage factor corrects the tabulated volume to the actual volume at the observed tank temperature. Whether it is a separate factor or already built into the tables depends on the ship's calibration documents.

Is LPG cargo calculated in weight in air or in vacuo?

Either may be used. The basis is set by the charter party, sale contract and agreed measurement standard. A common simplified conversion to weight in air deducts 0.0011 t/m³ from the in-vacuo density, but the exact buoyancy correction is defined by the agreed standard. Confirm the method with the surveyor and record the basis on the documents.

Do ship's officers calculate LNG energy for custody transfer?

Officers verify the CTMS readings, trim, list and temperatures and witness the opening and closing gauge. The energy figure normally needs the terminal's composition analysis for density and calorific value. It is usually calculated by the terminal or independent surveyor under the sale and purchase agreement.

Test yourself

A tank shows 0.20 bar on the pressure gauge. What pressure do you use in the vapour density formula?

Vapour density follows the gas laws and needs absolute pressure: gauge plus atmospheric (0.20 + 1.01325). This is standard practice in gas cargo calculation, as covered in STCW Code Section A-V/1-2 competences.

Which expression gives the IGC Code loading limit?

The IGC Code (Chapter 15) sets the loading limit as the filling limit multiplied by the ratio of density at reference temperature to density at loading temperature.

Which temperature sensors should be averaged for the liquid temperature?

Liquid temperature must come only from sensors submerged in liquid. Exposed sensors read vapour temperature and are averaged separately; this is standard custody transfer practice.

Why is a shrinkage factor applied to cargo tank volumes on refrigerated gas carriers?

Tanks are calibrated warm and contract at cargo temperature, so the tabulated volume is corrected using the factor in the ship's calibration tables.

On a typical LNG cargo, what is the commercial quantity usually expressed in?

LNG is normally sold on energy under the sale and purchase agreement, using volume, density and calorific value. Liquid volume alone is not the commercial quantity.
