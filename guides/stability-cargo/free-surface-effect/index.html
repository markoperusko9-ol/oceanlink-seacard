Free Surface Effect: Principles, Calculation and Control on Board

Quick answer

Free surface effect is the loss of stability caused by liquid shifting in a slack tank as the ship heels. It is treated as a virtual rise of the centre of gravity: the free surface moment (second moment of area of the liquid surface × density of the liquid) divided by displacement. It depends on the breadth of the free surface, not on how much liquid is in the tank. It is reduced sharply by longitudinal subdivision and by keeping the number of slack tanks to a minimum.

What free surface effect actually is

When a tank is completely full (pressed up) or completely empty, its contents behave like a solid weight. When the tank is slack, the liquid surface stays level as the ship heels. Liquid therefore flows towards the low side, and the centre of gravity of the liquid moves the same way.

That transverse shift of weight works against the righting lever. The ship behaves as if its centre of gravity has risen. This is why we speak of a virtual rise of G (written GGv, FSC or GG₁ depending on your textbook) and a fluid GM (GM corrected for free surface).

Key ideas examiners expect you to state clearly:

  • The real G does not rise. The effect is virtual. It reduces the righting lever exactly as a real rise of G would.
  • The effect exists at every angle of heel. At small angles it is handled as a simple correction to GM.
  • Every slack tank on board contributes. The effects are added together.
  • A ship with a healthy solid GM can end up with a small, or even negative, fluid GM if enough wide tanks are slack. A negative fluid GM will cause the ship to take an angle of loll if GZ becomes positive at a larger angle of heel. If it does not, the ship may capsize.

The formulas

For small angles of heel, the standard treatment is:

Quantity Expression Units
Second moment of area of free surface (rectangular tank) i = l × b³ / 12 m⁴
Free surface moment FSM = i × ρ (density of liquid in the tank) tonne-metres
Virtual rise of G GGv = ΣFSM / Δ metres
Fluid GM GM fluid = GM solid − GGv metres

Here l is the length of the free surface (fore and aft) and b is its breadth (athwartships). i is taken about the centreline of the free surface, which runs fore and aft. Δ is the ship's displacement including the liquid in the tank.

The breadth is cubed. That single fact explains almost everything practical about free surface:

  • Doubling the breadth of a slack tank multiplies its effect by eight.
  • Doubling the length only doubles it.
  • Dividing a tank into n equal compartments with longitudinal (fore-and-aft) bulkheads reduces the total effect to 1/n² of the undivided value.

For non-rectangular tanks you do not calculate i by hand. The ship's stability booklet gives free surface moments (or i values) for every tank in the tank tables. On many ships these are given as the maximum value or against sounding. Use whatever the approved booklet specifies.

At large angles, free surface may be handled as an actual liquid heeling moment at each angle of heel. Loading computers apply whichever method the approved stability booklet specifies, either actual moments or a free surface correction to KG. The small-angle formula is the one you are examined on and the one you should be able to do on paper.

Worked example

Data: Displacement 12,000 t (including tank contents). KM 8.20 m, KG 7.40 m. One rectangular double bottom tank is slack. Its free surface is 15 m long and 12 m wide, and it holds fuel oil of density 0.95 t/m³.

Step 1 – Solid GM GM = KM − KG = 8.20 − 7.40 = 0.80 m

Step 2 – Second moment of area i = l × b³ / 12 = 15 × 12³ / 12 = 15 × 1,728 / 12 = 2,160 m⁴

Step 3 – Free surface moment FSM = i × ρ = 2,160 × 0.95 = 2,052 t-m

Step 4 – Virtual rise of G GGv = 2,052 / 12,000 = 0.171 m

Step 5 – Fluid GM GM fluid = 0.80 − 0.171 = 0.629 m (≈ 0.63 m)

Same tank, divided by a centreline longitudinal bulkhead

Each half is 15 m × 6 m.

  • Each half: i = 15 × 6³ / 12 = 15 × 216 / 12 = 270 m⁴
  • Both halves: 540 m⁴
  • FSM = 540 × 0.95 = 513 t-m
  • GGv = 513 / 12,000 = 0.043 m
  • GM fluid = 0.80 − 0.043 = 0.757 m (≈ 0.76 m)

The bulkhead cut the loss from 0.171 m to 0.043 m, which is one quarter (1/2²). Notice that the quantity of oil in the tank never entered the calculation.

What makes it better or worse

Breadth of free surface. This is the dominant factor because it is cubed. Wide double bottom tanks, deep tanks and cargo tanks without longitudinal bulkheads are the worst offenders.

Density of the liquid in the tank. Use the density of the tank contents, not the water the ship floats in. A slack fresh water tank and a slack ballast tank of the same size do not give the same moment.

Number of slack tanks. The effects add. Six tanks each costing a few centimetres can together remove a significant part of a modest GM.

Displacement. The same FSM matters more on a light ship. GGv is FSM divided by Δ, so a slack tank that is trivial at full load can be significant in ballast.

Things that do NOT change it (in the standard calculation):

  • Quantity of liquid. For a wall-sided tank, the surface area is the same at any level, so the FSM is the same. The weight of liquid does change the solid KG, but that is a separate correction.
  • Height of the tank above the keel. A slack tank high up has the same free surface correction as an identical slack tank in the double bottom.

Pocketing. When a tank is nearly empty or nearly full, the liquid surface can touch the tank bottom or top at larger angles of heel. This reduces the actual effect below the calculated value. Do not rely on it. The calculated value is the conservative one.

Free communication with the sea. A damaged compartment open to the sea gives a further loss beyond simple free surface. This belongs to damage stability, not routine loading.

Managing slack tanks on board: practical procedure

The Chief Officer normally prepares and monitors the stability condition. Overall responsibility rests with the Master. In practice the duty officer and engineers create or remove slack tanks every day. A workable routine:

  1. Plan the condition. For departure, arrival and critical intermediate stages, run the loading computer or a hand calculation using the approved booklet. Include free surface for every tank expected to be slack.
  2. Follow the booklet's rule for consumables. Under the IS Code approach, the booklet usually assumes the tank (or combination) with the greatest free surface for each type of consumable is slack. Small tanks may be disregarded only where the booklet's criteria allow it. Do not invent your own allowance.
  3. Minimise slack tanks. Press up ballast tanks or empty them fully. Draw fuel from as few tanks at a time as the engine department can manage. Agree this with the Chief Engineer.
  4. Coordinate transfers. Fuel transfers, ballast exchange and fresh water production all create slack tanks. The engine room should tell the bridge or Chief Officer before starting transfers that affect stability.
  5. Check the result against criteria. Compare the corrected (fluid) GM and the GZ curve with the booklet. The general intact criteria in the IS Code require the initial GM, corrected for free surface, to be at least 0.15 m. Ship-type criteria, grain cargoes and company SMS limits may be stricter.
  6. Tankers. For oil tankers of 5,000 tonnes deadweight and above, MARPOL Annex I Regulation 27 requires intact stability to be maintained, under worst-case conditions of maximum free surface, throughout cargo and ballast operations. Follow the loading manual's limits on how many cargo and ballast tanks may be slack at the same time.
  7. Watch for unplanned free surface. Firefighting water on decks or in holds, flooding, and water trapped on vehicle decks can all create free surface. During boundary cooling and firefighting, monitor heel and drain or pump out where you can.
  8. Record it. Keep stability calculations and tank soundings as required by your SMS.

If the ship takes an unexpected list with a soft, slow roll, suspect low or negative fluid GM before you start correcting with ballast. See the angle of loll guidance. Filling the wrong tank can make things worse before they get better.

Common mistakes (in exams and on board)

  • Using seawater density for every tank. FSM uses the density of the liquid in the tank: fuel, fresh water, ballast or cargo.
  • Thinking a half-full tank is worst. For a wall-sided tank, the effect is the same at any level where the surface spans the tank.
  • Using the wrong breadth. b is the athwartships breadth and is cubed. Swapping l and b gives a hugely wrong answer.
  • Forgetting to add up all slack tanks. Each slack tank needs its own FSM. Sum them, then divide by displacement once.
  • Using the wrong displacement. Use the final displacement, including the liquid just loaded.
  • Applying the 1/n² rule to transverse bulkheads. Only longitudinal (fore-and-aft) subdivision narrows the free surface and gives the n² reduction for transverse stability.
  • Mixing up solid KG and free surface correction. Loading liquid changes KG through its weight and centroid. Being slack adds the virtual rise on top of that.
  • Ignoring the engine room. A daily fuel transfer can leave several wide tanks slack without anyone on the bridge knowing.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Define free surface effect. It is the reduction in effective stability caused by liquid moving within a slack tank as the ship heels. It is expressed as a virtual rise of G equal to the free surface moment divided by displacement.

Q: Why does the quantity of liquid not matter? The moment depends on the shape and area of the free surface, not the volume below it. In a wall-sided tank, the surface is the same at any level.

Q: How does a longitudinal bulkhead help? It halves the breadth of each free surface. Because breadth is cubed, two halves give one quarter of the original effect. Generally, n equal divisions give 1/n².

Q: Does a slack tank in the double bottom have less free surface effect than one at upper deck level? No. The virtual rise is independent of the tank's height. The double bottom tank is better for solid KG, not for free surface.

Q: Where do you get free surface values on board? From the approved stability booklet's tank tables and the class-approved loading computer, which apply the booklet's method.

Q: Your ship lists in calm water and rolls sluggishly about the listed position. Your thoughts? I would suspect an angle of loll from negative fluid GM, possibly due to slack tanks. I would first check whether it is list or loll, then reduce free surface and lower G. I would not start by transferring weight to the high side.

Q: What is GM corrected used for? Compliance with stability criteria. Criteria are always assessed with free surface taken into account.

Key points
  • Free surface effect is a virtual rise of G: GGv = ΣFSM / Δ, where FSM = i × density of liquid in the tank.
  • For a rectangular tank, i = l × b³ / 12, so breadth is the dominant factor.
  • Dividing a tank with n equal longitudinal compartments reduces the effect to 1/n².
  • The effect does not depend on the quantity of liquid or the tank's height above the keel.
  • Stability criteria are assessed on fluid (corrected) GM and corrected GZ, never solid GM alone.
  • Keep slack tanks to a minimum and coordinate fuel and ballast transfers between deck and engine departments.
  • A sluggish list in calm water may be loll from negative fluid GM, so diagnose before you ballast.

Frequently asked questions

Is free surface effect greatest when a tank is half full?

No. For a wall-sided tank, the free surface area and its second moment are the same at any level, so the effect is the same. Near empty or near full, pocketing can reduce the real effect at larger heel angles, but the standard calculation ignores this and stays conservative.

What density do I use when calculating free surface moment?

Use the density of the liquid inside the tank, such as fuel oil, fresh water, ballast or cargo. The density of the dock or sea water affects displacement and draught, not the free surface moment of the tank.

Why do longitudinal bulkheads reduce free surface effect so much?

The second moment of area varies with the cube of the free surface breadth. Splitting a tank into n equal compartments across its breadth reduces the total effect to 1/n² of the original. A single centreline bulkhead therefore cuts it to a quarter.

Does the loading computer already include free surface?

An approved loading computer applies free surface according to the method in the ship's stability booklet. That is only correct if you enter the actual tank states. A tank shown as full or empty when it is really slack will give a wrong, optimistic result.

Can I ignore small slack tanks?

Only where the approved stability booklet allows it under the IS Code provisions for small tanks. Otherwise include them. Several small slack tanks can add up to a meaningful loss of GM, especially in light conditions.

Test yourself

A slack tank is divided by a single centreline longitudinal bulkhead into two equal tanks, both slack. What happens to the free surface effect?

Breadth is cubed in i = lb³/12, so each half has 1/8 of the original i. Together the two halves give 2/8 = 1/4 (1/2²) of the original effect.

Which density is used to calculate the free surface moment of a slack fuel oil tank?

FSM = i × density of the liquid in the tank, here the fuel oil (method used in the ship's approved stability booklet).

Displacement 10,000 t. A rectangular ballast tank has a free surface 10 m long and 10 m wide and contains sea water of density 1.025 t/m³. What is the virtual rise of G?

i = 10 × 10³/12 = 833.3 m⁴. FSM = 833.3 × 1.025 = 854.2 t-m. GGv = 854.2/10,000 = 0.085 m.

For a rectangular wall-sided tank, how does the calculated free surface effect vary with the liquid level?

In a wall-sided tank the free surface has the same length and breadth at every level, so i = lb³/12 is unchanged. The quantity of liquid does not appear in the FSM formula.

Two identical slack tanks hold the same liquid. One is a double bottom tank and the other is a deep tank high in the ship. How do their free surface corrections compare?

GGv = FSM/Δ, and neither term depends on the tank's vertical position. Height affects solid KG only.
