Hogging and Sagging: Longitudinal Stress Explained for Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

A ship hogs when midships rises relative to the ends, which puts the deck in tension and the bottom in compression. It sags when midships drops, which puts the deck in compression and the bottom in tension. You detect either condition by comparing the midships mean draft with the mean of the forward and aft drafts, and you control it by keeping still-water shear forces and bending moments within the approved loading manual limits.

What hogging and sagging actually are

Think of the hull as a long box girder. Weight acts downward along its length and buoyancy acts upward. The two are never distributed identically, so the girder bends.

  • Hogging happens when buoyancy is concentrated amidships, or weight is concentrated at the ends. The midships section rises relative to the bow and stern, and the keel line becomes convex upward, like a hog's back.
  • Sagging happens when weight is concentrated amidships, or buoyancy is concentrated at the ends. The midships section drops relative to the ends, and the keel line becomes concave upward.

The stresses always come in pairs:

Condition Deck Bottom / keel
Hogging Tension Compression
Sagging Compression Tension

Somewhere between deck and keel lies the neutral axis, where bending stress is zero. The deck and bottom plating sit furthest from it, which is why they carry the highest bending stress. It is also why classification societies pay particular attention to the sheer strake, deck stringer, deck plating and bottom shell.

Causes: still water, waves and temperature

Still-water loading is the part you control. Typical examples:

  • A ship in ballast with the engine room aft, peak tanks full and empty holds amidships tends to hog.
  • A ship with heavy cargo concentrated in the midship holds or tanks and light ends tends to sag.
  • Alternate-hold loading on bulk carriers produces large local shear forces at the bulkheads between loaded and empty holds, even when the overall bending moment looks acceptable.

Wave loading is added on top at sea:

  • A wave crest amidships with troughs at the ends supports the middle and leaves the ends unsupported. The ship hogs.
  • Crests at the bow and stern with a trough amidships leave the middle unsupported. The ship sags.
  • The worst bending occurs when the wavelength is roughly equal to the ship's length. As the waves pass, the hull alternates between hog and sag, and that cycling is where fatigue cracking comes from.

Temperature has a smaller effect. When strong sun heats the deck while the bottom is cooled by seawater, the deck expands more than the bottom. The ship then tends to hog slightly. Bear this in mind when reading drafts in the afternoon in the tropics.

Because a seagoing ship adds wave bending to its still-water bending, loading manuals normally give separate permissible limits for harbour and seagoing conditions. A condition that is acceptable alongside may not be acceptable for sailing.

Detecting hog and sag from the draft marks

You need six draft readings: forward, midships and aft, each on both port and starboard.

  1. Read all six marks, ideally from a boat or the quay, in calm water and allowing for wave motion.
  2. Average port and starboard at each position. This removes the effect of any list.
  3. Correct the forward and aft drafts to the perpendiculars, and the midship draft to amidships, using the ship's draft mark corrections. The marks are rarely exactly on the perpendiculars, and on a trimmed ship the error matters.
  4. Calculate the mean of the forward and aft drafts.
  5. Compare that figure with the midships mean draft:
    • Midships mean greater than the mean of forward and aft: the ship is sagging.
    • Midships mean less than the mean of forward and aft: the ship is hogging.
    • Equal: no measurable deflection.

The difference between the two figures is the deflection.

If you skip step 2, a list can masquerade as deflection. If you skip step 3, trim can do the same. Examiners like to probe both points.

Worked example: deflection and the quarter mean draft

A bulk carrier's drafts, already averaged port and starboard and corrected to the perpendiculars and amidships, are:

  • Forward (F): 8.20 m
  • Aft (A): 9.00 m
  • Midships (M): 8.70 m

Step 1: mean of forward and aft (8.20 + 9.00) / 2 = 8.60 m

Step 2: deflection 8.70 − 8.60 = 0.10 m. The midships draft is greater, so the ship is sagging by 10 cm.

Step 3: mean of means (MoM) MoM = (F + A + 2M) / 4 = (8.20 + 9.00 + 17.40) / 4 = 34.60 / 4 = 8.65 m

Step 4: quarter mean (mean of mean of means) QM = (F + A + 6M) / 8 = (8.20 + 9.00 + 52.20) / 8 = 69.40 / 8 = 8.675 m

Check: (MoM + M) / 2 = (8.65 + 8.70) / 2 = 8.675 m. The two methods agree.

The quarter mean assumes the hull deflects in a roughly parabolic curve. It is the draft used to enter the hydrostatic tables for displacement in a draft survey, before the trim, density and other corrections are applied.

Why it matters: suppose you had used the simple mean of 8.60 m and the TPC is 60 t/cm. Your draft would be wrong by 7.5 cm, which works out to 7.5 × 60 = 450 tonnes of displacement error. In a sagged condition the simple mean understates displacement. In a hogged condition it overstates it.

Controlling longitudinal stress on board

Draft-mark deflection tells you what has already happened. Stress control is about preventing it. Each ship's approved loading manual and loading instrument give the permissible still-water shear forces and bending moments at specified frames or bulkheads. On bulk carriers they also give limits on cargo mass per hold and on double bottom loading.

A practical routine looks like this:

  1. Plan the full sequence, not just the final condition. Run every intermediate stage through the loading instrument. Large peaks often appear part-way through loading, not at the end.
  2. Match deballasting to the cargo rate. On bulk carriers the terminal and ship agree a loading and unloading plan before operations begin, as required by SOLAS VI/7 and set out in the BLU Code. If the ship cannot deballast as fast as the shore loads, the stresses run ahead of the plan.
  3. Use multiple pours. Pouring a hold to its full tonnage in one run before moving on can overstress the hull locally and globally.
  4. Monitor and record. Compare actual figures against planned figures at agreed stages. Check drafts against the calculated values. Stop cargo operations if the figures diverge or the limits are approached.
  5. Check the seagoing condition before departure. Confirm the departure condition is within the seagoing limits, not just the harbour limits.
  6. Watch ballast operations at sea. Sequential ballast water exchange can take the ship through high shear force and bending moment conditions. Follow the approved ballast water management plan sequence.
  7. Verify the instrument. Test the loading instrument against the approved test conditions as required by your SMS and class.

The exact percentage at which your company wants you to stop and consult varies by company SMS and by ship, so follow your own procedures. Never treat 100% of the permissible value as a target.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the stress directions. In a hog the deck is in tension. In a sag the deck is in compression. Draw the bent girder if you are unsure.
  • Using the mean of means instead of the quarter mean in a draft survey. The mean of means only partially allows for deflection.
  • Ignoring list and trim corrections before judging deflection.
  • Checking only the final condition when the intermediate stages are where the limits get breached.
  • Applying harbour limits at sea. Seagoing limits are normally tighter because wave bending is added.
  • Trusting the loading computer blindly. If the tank soundings or cargo figures entered are wrong, the output is wrong too. Cross-check with actual drafts.
  • Forgetting shear force. A condition can be within its bending moment limits and still exceed the shear force limits at a bulkhead, particularly in alternate-hold loading.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Define hogging and sagging and state the stresses on deck and bottom. In hogging, midships rises relative to the ends, with the deck in tension and the bottom in compression. In sagging, midships drops, with the deck in compression and the bottom in tension.

Q: How would you find out whether your ship is hogged or sagged? Read the forward, midships and aft drafts on both sides. Average port and starboard, then correct to the perpendiculars and amidships. Compare the midships mean with the mean of the forward and aft drafts. If midships is greater, she is sagging. If it is less, she is hogging.

Q: Which wave condition produces hogging? A crest amidships with troughs at the ends. The worst case is a wavelength about equal to the ship's length.

Q: Why is the quarter mean used in a draft survey? It allows for deflection by giving extra weight to the midship draft, assuming a parabolic deflection curve. That gives a more accurate draft for entering the hydrostatics.

Q: What information is the master given to avoid excessive hull stress? The approved loading manual and, where required, a loading instrument. These give the permissible shear forces and bending moments, typically for both harbour and seagoing conditions. The Load Line Convention requires the master to be supplied with information enabling loading and ballasting to be arranged so as to avoid unacceptable stresses.

Q: Where on the hull are bending stresses greatest, and why? At the deck and bottom plating amidships, because those are the parts furthest from the neutral axis and the bending moment is generally highest around midships.

Q: During loading the instrument shows shear force approaching the limit. What do you do? Stop or slow the cargo, review the sequence, and adjust ballast or the pour order. Inform the chief officer and the terminal, and resume only when the revised plan keeps the figures within limits.

Key points
  • Hogging means midships is up, with the deck in tension and the bottom in compression.
  • Sagging means midships is down, with the deck in compression and the bottom in tension.
  • If the midships mean draft is greater than the mean of the forward and aft drafts, the ship is sagging. If it is less, she is hogging.
  • Quarter mean draft = (F + A + 6M) / 8, using drafts corrected for list and to the perpendiculars.
  • A wave crest amidships causes hogging and crests at the ends cause sagging. The worst case is a wavelength close to the ship's length.
  • Check every intermediate loading stage against the loading instrument, not just the final condition.
  • Harbour and seagoing limits usually differ, so the departure condition must meet the seagoing limits.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my ship is hogging or sagging?

Read the forward, midships and aft drafts on both sides and average port and starboard. Then compare the midships mean with the mean of the forward and aft drafts. If midships is deeper, she is sagging. If midships is shallower, she is hogging.

Which is more dangerous, hogging or sagging?

Both can damage the hull if the permissible limits are exceeded, so neither is 'safe'. Sagging puts the deck in compression, which risks buckling of deck plating. Hogging puts the deck in tension, which risks cracking or fracture. The governing limits for your ship are set out in its approved loading manual.

What is the quarter mean draft and why do surveyors use it?

The quarter mean is (F + A + 6M) / 8, or equivalently the average of the mean of means and the midship draft. It corrects for hull deflection by weighting the midship draft heavily, which gives a more accurate draft for reading displacement from the hydrostatic tables.

Does a ship in ballast hog or sag?

Many cargo ships in ballast tend to hog, because the holds amidships are empty while the machinery aft and the ballast in the peaks put weight at the ends. The actual condition depends on the ship's design and on which ballast tanks are filled, so always check the loading instrument.

Can the loading computer be wrong about hull stresses?

Yes, if the input data is wrong. Incorrect soundings, cargo weights or tank assignments will give misleading shear force and bending moment figures. Cross-check the computed drafts against the actual draft readings, and test the instrument against its approved test conditions as your SMS requires.
