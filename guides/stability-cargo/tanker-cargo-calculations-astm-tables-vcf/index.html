Tanker cargo calculations: ASTM tables, VCF and the worked method

Quick answer

Tanker cargo is measured as observed volume and corrected to a standard temperature (15°C or 60°F) with a Volume Correction Factor from the ASTM/API/IP Petroleum Measurement Tables. The chain runs TOV → GOV → GSV → weight: subtract free water, multiply by the VCF from the correct table, then multiply by the weight conversion factor. Common errors are using the wrong table, the wrong density or forgetting the free water.

Why oil cargo has to be corrected at all

Petroleum expands as it warms and contracts as it cools, by far more than steel or water. The same tonnes of gasoil can take up noticeably more cubic metres at 40°C than at 15°C. Buyers and sellers therefore trade on volume at a standard temperature, or on weight, never on the raw volume you find in the tank.

The standard temperatures you will meet:

  • 15°C: the metric standard used in most international trade.
  • 60°F: used where barrels and API gravity are used, notably US-linked trade.
  • 20°C: used in a few national markets, with its own set of tables.

The factor that converts an observed volume to standard volume is the Volume Correction Factor (VCF), also called CTL (correction for the effect of temperature on the liquid). It comes from the Petroleum Measurement Tables, published jointly as ASTM D1250, API MPMS Chapter 11.1 and IP 200, with ISO 91 as the related international standard. Whichever edition the charter party or sales contract names is the one you use. Do not assume.

The volume terms and the tables you will actually use

Learn the volume hierarchy in order. Surveyors, terminals and examiners all use these abbreviations.

Term Meaning
TOV: Total Observed Volume Everything in the tank at observed temperature (oil, free water, sediment), read from the calibration (ullage) tables after trim and list correction
FW: Free Water Water lying as a separate layer under the oil, measured by water-finding paste or the interface probe
GOV: Gross Observed Volume TOV minus free water, still at observed temperature
GSV: Gross Standard Volume GOV × VCF, the oil volume at 15°C (or 60°F)
NSV: Net Standard Volume GSV minus sediment and water (S&W) found by laboratory analysis
TCV: Total Calculated Volume GSV plus free water

The common tables:

  • Table 53 converts observed density to density at 15°C. You only need it if you measured density yourself with a hydrometer.
  • Table 54 gives VCF to 15°C, entered with density at 15°C and observed temperature. The suffix matters:
    • 54A for generalised crude oils.
    • 54B for generalised products (gasoline, jet, kerosene, gasoil, fuel oils).
    • 54C for individual and special products, entered with a thermal expansion coefficient rather than density.
    • 54D for lubricating oils.
  • Table 56 gives weight per cubic metre at 15°C. In practice, the weight conversion factor (WCF) for metric tonnes in air is density at 15°C (t/m³) minus 0.0011.
  • Tables 5 and 6 (A/B/C/D) are the 60°F equivalents, worked with API gravity. Tables 52 and 57 handle conversions to barrels and to long tons.

Liquefied gases are not done with Table 54. They use their own density and vapour calculations, covered separately.

The formulas

Keep these on one line in your notebook:

  • GOV = TOV − FW
  • GSV = GOV × VCF
  • NSV = GSV − S&W volume, where S&W comes from the lab certificate, not from the tank
  • Metric tonnes in air = GSV × WCF, where WCF = density@15°C − 0.0011
  • Metric tonnes in vacuum = GSV × density@15°C

Points that catch people:

  • Enter Table 54 with density at 15°C, not observed density. The cargo documents (certificate of quality or shore figures) normally give it already corrected.
  • Weight in air is what is normally traded and shown on the bill of lading. Check which figure the documents use before you compare.
  • Rounding and decimals are set by the table edition. The older 1980-based tables give VCF to four decimal places. The 2004 revision of API MPMS 11.1 gives it to five and calculates rather than looks up. On a large parcel, the two editions can give slightly different answers, which is why the contract names one.
  • For a ship, the VCF is normally the only temperature correction applied to the cargo volume. The shore-tank shell-temperature correction (CTSh) is not normally applied to ship's tanks.

Worked example: loading gasoil

Given: a product tanker has finished loading gasoil into 3 Port.

  • Corrected ullage gives a TOV of 12,450.250 m³. This is after trim and list corrections from the tank calibration tables.
  • Interface probe shows free water equal to 2.250 m³.
  • Average cargo temperature is 35.0°C, from upper, middle and lower readings.
  • Shore certificate of quality gives density at 15°C as 0.8500 t/m³ (850.0 kg/m³).
  • Product, so use Table 54B.

Step 1: GOV 12,450.250 − 2.250 = 12,448.000 m³

Step 2: VCF Enter Table 54B with 850.0 kg/m³ and 35.0°C. The factor is approximately 0.9833. Always read the actual table or approved software for the edition specified. This illustrative value is calculated from the 54B product coefficients.

Step 3: GSV 12,448.000 × 0.9833 = 12,240.118 m³ at 15°C

Step 4: WCF 0.8500 − 0.0011 = 0.8489

Step 5: weight in air 12,240.118 × 0.8489 = 10,390.636 t

Step 6: weight in vacuum (for comparison) 12,240.118 × 0.8500 = 10,404.100 t

Notice what the VCF did. About 208 m³ of the observed volume was simply thermal expansion. If you forget to apply it, the ship appears to have loaded about 176 t more (≈207.9 m³ × 0.8489) than it did. In the oral, the examiner will expect you to say which way the correction goes: above 15°C the VCF is below 1, and below 15°C it is above 1.

On-board procedure, step by step

The exact sequence is set by your company SMS, the terminal and the cargo surveyor. The usual pattern is:

  1. Before loading: measure OBQ (on-board quantity), meaning any oil, water and sludge already in the tanks and lines. After discharge, the equivalent is ROB (remaining on board). If small quantities do not touch all bulkheads, a wedge calculation may be needed rather than the normal tables.
  2. Record draught, trim and list at the moment of gauging. Calibration table corrections depend on them. Gauging a ship with a list and applying no list correction is a classic dispute.
  3. Take ullages with a checked, in-date closed gauging tape or the fixed system as the procedure allows. On closed operations (inerted or toxic cargoes), use closed or restricted gauging only.
  4. Take temperatures at the levels your procedure specifies, often upper, middle and lower, and average them.
  5. Find free water with paste or the interface probe.
  6. Calculate per tank TOV, FW, GOV, VCF, GSV, then total for the ship.
  7. Obtain the shore figure and density. If your procedure uses a VEF (Vessel Experience Factor, the ship's historical ship/shore ratio calculated per API MPMS Chapter 17.9), divide the ship's figure (normally TCV) by the VEF before comparing it with shore.
  8. Compare ship and shore. If the difference is outside the tolerance in your company instructions or the charter party, inform the master. A letter of protest is normally issued as instructed by the company.

Use the same density and the same table edition as the shore. Otherwise you are comparing two different calculations, not two measurements.

Common mistakes (and how examiners spot them)

  • Wrong table letter. Using 54A for a refined product, or 54B for crude. The VCFs differ and the error compounds over a full cargo.
  • Observed density entered in Table 54. Table 54 needs density at 15°C. If you only have a hydrometer reading, convert it with Table 53 first.
  • Free water forgotten or double-counted. Free water is subtracted before the VCF. Lab S&W is a separate deduction from GSV to get NSV.
  • °F and °C mixed. This is especially common when a terminal reports in Fahrenheit and the ship works in Celsius.
  • No trim and list correction to the ullage, or the wrong sign on it.
  • Air and vacuum figures compared against each other. On a full cargo, the 0.0011 difference is many tonnes.
  • Rounding at the wrong stage. Follow the rounding rules of the table edition. Round only where the standard says so, not at every line.
  • Interpolation errors when reading printed tables between temperature or density steps.
  • Faulty gauging equipment. A tape that is out of calibration, or an uncalibrated temperature sensor, ruins the calculation however carefully you do the arithmetic.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Typical questions at the cargo-handling part of an oral examination. The exact scope depends on your administration's syllabus.

  • What is the difference between GOV and GSV? GOV is at observed temperature with free water removed. GSV is GOV corrected to standard temperature by the VCF.
  • Which table would you use for a crude oil at 15°C standard? Table 54A, entered with density at 15°C and observed temperature.
  • The cargo is at 8°C. Is the VCF more or less than 1? More than 1, because cold cargo is contracted and its standard volume is larger than its observed volume.
  • What is the difference between weight in air and weight in vacuum? Air buoyancy. Weight in air is lower and is normally used commercially. The WCF of density@15 minus 0.0011 reflects this.
  • What is a VEF and why use it? It is the ship's historical ship-to-shore ratio, used to compare like with like. It accounts for the systematic differences in that ship's measurement.
  • Ship's figure is short against the bill of lading beyond tolerance. What do you do? Re-check the gauging and calculation, and tell the master. Then follow company instructions, which usually means issuing a letter of protest and keeping full records.
  • Why must you record trim and list at the time of gauging? Because the calibration-table corrections depend on them, and the figures may be disputed later.
Key points
  • The chain is TOV − free water = GOV, GOV × VCF = GSV, GSV × WCF = tonnes in air.
  • Enter Table 54 with density at 15°C, not observed density; use Table 53 to convert a hydrometer reading.
  • Use 54A for crude, 54B for products, 54C for special applications (by expansion coefficient) and 54D for lube oils.
  • Above 15°C the VCF is less than 1; below 15°C it is greater than 1.
  • WCF for metric tonnes in air is density at 15°C minus 0.0011; weight in vacuum uses density directly.
  • Use the table edition named in the contract; the 1980-based and 2004 versions round differently.
  • Record trim, list, OBQ/ROB and temperatures at gauging, and match the shore's density and table edition before comparing figures.

Frequently asked questions

What is VCF in tanker cargo calculation?

VCF (Volume Correction Factor) converts the oil volume at observed temperature to volume at standard temperature, usually 15°C or 60°F. You take it from the Petroleum Measurement Tables, normally Table 54A or 54B, using density at 15°C and the observed cargo temperature. Multiplying GOV by VCF gives GSV.

What is the difference between Table 54A and 54B?

Table 54A is for generalised crude oils and Table 54B is for generalised refined products such as gasoline, jet fuel, gasoil and fuel oils. They use different thermal expansion relationships, so the same density and temperature give slightly different VCFs. Choose the table that matches the cargo, and check the cargo documents or charter party if in doubt.

Why do we subtract 0.0011 from density?

Density at 15°C multiplied by volume gives the mass in vacuum. Commercial cargo figures are normally weight in air, which is slightly less because of air buoyancy. Table 56 practice gives a weight conversion factor of density at 15°C minus 0.0011 t/m³ for metric tonnes in air.

What is the difference between GSV and NSV?

GSV is the standard-temperature volume of the oil after free water has been removed. NSV is GSV minus the sediment and water (S&W) suspended in the oil, as found by laboratory analysis. Free water is measured in the tank; S&W comes from the lab certificate.

Should the ship use the 1980 tables or the 2004 tables?

Use whichever edition the charter party, sales contract or terminal procedure specifies. The 2004 revision of API MPMS Chapter 11.1 calculates VCF rather than looking it up and rounds to five decimal places, so results can differ slightly from the older tables. Match the edition the shore is using before comparing ship and shore figures.

Test yourself

Which quantity do you multiply by the VCF to obtain GSV?

Free water is removed from TOV to give GOV, and GOV × VCF = GSV. This is the standard volume hierarchy under API MPMS Chapter 17 practice.

A cargo of gasoil is at 30°C with a 15°C standard. What will the VCF be?

Warm oil occupies more volume than it would at 15°C, so the factor reduces it and is below 1. This follows from Table 54B, ASTM D1250 / API MPMS 11.1.

What do you enter Table 54B with?

Table 54B uses density at 15°C and observed temperature. Table 54C is entered with an expansion coefficient, and Table 6 works with API gravity.

GSV is 10,000.000 m³ and density at 15°C is 0.8600. What is the weight in metric tonnes in air?

WCF = 0.8600 − 0.0011 = 0.8589, and 10,000 × 0.8589 = 8,589.000 t in air; 8,600 t is the vacuum weight. This applies Table 56 practice.

Where does the S&W deducted to obtain NSV normally come from?

Suspended sediment and water is determined by laboratory analysis; paste and interface readings measure free water, which is removed earlier. This is defined in API MPMS Chapter 17 terminology.
