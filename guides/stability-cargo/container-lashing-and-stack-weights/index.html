Container lashing and stack weights: a practical exam-level guide

Quick answer

Every container stack has a maximum permissible weight set in the ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual and loading/lashing software, based on hatch cover, tank top and foundation strength. Separately, container corner post strength and lashing/twistlock forces must also be within limits. Officers check stack weight, weight distribution (heavy at the bottom) and lashing forces before sailing, secure stacks with twistlocks, lashing rods and turnbuckles as the manual specifies, and keep checking them throughout the voyage.

Why stack weights and lashing matter

A container stack is a tall, narrow column held together only at its corners. In a seaway it is pushed and pulled by:

  • Rolling, which produces the largest transverse accelerations. These grow with height above the roll centre and with shorter roll periods, as on a stiff ship with large GM.
  • Pitching and heaving, which add vertical and longitudinal accelerations. Pitch-induced accelerations are largest at the ends of the ship.
  • Wind on the outboard and exposed stacks.
  • Green seas on the forward bays and lower outboard tiers.

These forces try to rack each container, distorting its end frame sideways. They also try to tip the stack, which lifts the corners on one side and loads the opposite corner posts in compression. Finally, they try to slide the stack off its foundations.

When the limits are exceeded, you can get crushed corner posts, broken twistlocks, a collapsed stack and boxes lost overboard. Overloaded stacks, heavy-over-light stowage, misdeclared weights and slack or missing lashings come up again and again in casualty investigations. Severe rolling, including parametric rolling, makes all of these worse.

The limits: where they come from and where to find them

Several separate limits apply to the same stack. The lowest one governs.

Limit What it protects Where you find it
Maximum permissible stack weight (deck or hold) Hatch cover, tank top, container foundations Cargo Securing Manual, stowage/lashing software, class-approved drawings
Allowable stacking load on the container Corner posts of the lowest box CSC Safety Approval Plate on the container
Racking strength Container end frames CSC plate (transverse racking test load)
Lashing and securing forces Twistlocks, rods, turnbuckles, lashing points Cargo Securing Manual and lashing calculation module
Visibility and stability Bridge line of sight, GM, wind heel Stability booklet, loading computer, SOLAS bridge visibility requirements

Key points examiners expect you to know:

  • Stack weight is the total mass of all containers in one stack, from the bottom tier to the top. It is given separately for 20 ft and 40 ft stacks and for hold and deck. It may also differ between bays.
  • The CSC plate carries, among other things, the maximum operating gross mass. It also gives the allowable stacking load for 1.8 g, meaning the superimposed load the box was tested to carry on its corner posts, and the racking test load. Always read the actual plate rather than assuming a standard value, because ratings vary between container types and ages.
  • Lashing software combines stack weight, tier-by-tier weight distribution, the ship's GM (which affects roll accelerations), wind exposure and the lashing pattern. It then checks forces in the lashings, twistlocks and corner posts against the limits. Class rules and methods differ between societies, and some allow route-specific or weather-dependent criteria. Use what your ship's approved manual and software say.
  • The declared weight that goes into all of this must be the verified gross mass (VGM). SOLAS requires the shipper to provide it before loading. A container without a VGM should not be loaded.

Lashing gear and how a deck stack is secured

The usual equipment on a cellular container ship:

  • Twistlocks connect the corner castings between tiers and to the hatch cover or deck foundations.
    • Manual twistlocks are locked with a handle.
    • Semi-automatic twistlocks (SATL) lock on landing and are unlocked by pole or handle before discharge.
    • Fully automatic twistlocks (FAT) release during lifting by a specific lift motion set by the manufacturer, so no one has to go up to unlock them.
    • Different types are not interchangeable unless the manual allows it. Some ships may only use certain types in certain positions.
  • Stacking cones locate boxes in positions where no vertical locking is required, mainly in cell-guided holds (including the mid-position where two 20 ft boxes share a 40 ft cell), as specified by the Cargo Securing Manual.
  • Midlocks are used where 20 ft boxes meet in a 40 ft bay.
  • Lashing rods and turnbuckles (bottle screws) run from the lashing bridge or deck to the corner castings of the lower tiers. The rods give racking and tipping restraint. The pattern (which tiers, internal or external, crossed or parallel) is fixed by the Cargo Securing Manual for each bay.
  • Bridge fittings and buttresses are used on some ships and in non-cellular stowage.

In holds with cell guides, the guides provide the transverse and longitudinal restraint, so twistlocks are normally not needed between tiers. Exceptions are areas such as hatchless designs or partly guided positions, where the manual applies.

General principles of tensioning: turnbuckles are tightened evenly so the lashing is taut without being over-tensioned. Over-tightening adds load to the corner castings before the ship even starts to roll. Lock the turnbuckle so it cannot unwind with vibration. Follow the method and any locking device described in the manual.

Worked example: checking a deck stack

This example uses illustrative figures only. Your ship's limits come from its own approved manual.

A 40 ft deck stack has a maximum permissible stack weight of 90 t in the Cargo Securing Manual. The planner shows five tiers (VGM):

Tier Mass (t)
82 (bottom) 28
84 25
86 18
88 14
90 (top) 8

Step 1 – Stack weight. 28 + 25 + 18 + 14 + 8 = 93 t. This exceeds the 90 t limit by 3 t. It is not acceptable, however well the stack is lashed.

Step 2 – Remedy. Shift the top 8 t box to another stack that has spare capacity. The stack becomes 85 t, which is within limits. Re-run the lashing calculation, because removing a tier changes the forces on the remaining lashings and twistlocks.

Step 3 – Why the order matters. Take standard 8 ft 6 in boxes (2.591 m high). Assume each container's centre of gravity is at mid-height, and ignore the twistlock gap. Measure heights from the hatch cover top. Mid-heights are 1.30, 3.89, 6.48, 9.07 and 11.66 m.

  • As planned (heavy at bottom), using the original 93 t:
    • Moments: 28×1.30 + 25×3.89 + 18×6.48 + 14×9.07 + 8×11.66 = 470.6 t·m
    • Stack VCG = 470.6 ÷ 93 = 5.06 m
  • Reversed (heavy on top):
    • Moments: 8×1.30 + 14×3.89 + 18×6.48 + 25×9.07 + 28×11.66 = 734.7 t·m
    • Stack VCG = 734.7 ÷ 93 = 7.90 m

The total weight is the same, but the stack VCG rises by about 2.8 m. The tipping moment and the transverse forces from rolling act through a higher centre. That loads the lashings, twistlocks and bottom corner posts much harder. This is why the lashing software can fail a stack that is within its weight limit, and why heavy-over-light is flagged.

Step 4 – Corner post sanity check. The bottom box carries the static mass of everything above it: 25 + 18 + 14 + 8 = 65 t before the remedy. That value, combined with the ship's accelerations, is what the software compares against the container's rated stacking strength from its CSC plate.

On-board procedure: from stowage plan to sea

Who does what varies by company SMS. The usual sequence is:

  1. Receive the stowage plan (typically BAPLIE) from the planner. Load it into the loading computer and lashing module.
  2. Check every bay for:
    • stack weights
    • lashing force results
    • heavy-over-light warnings
    • visibility, stability (GM, wind heel) and longitudinal strength
    • IMDG segregation and reefer positions
  3. Query anything red or marginal before loading starts. The Chief Officer, with the Master's agreement, can refuse a stow that does not comply.
  4. During loading, watch for:
    • last-minute changes and restows
    • wrong twistlock types
    • cones left in deck positions
    • damaged boxes or corner castings
    • containers landed but not locked
  5. Re-run the calculation with the final as-loaded data. Do not sail on the pre-stow plan.
  6. Before departure, inspect the lashings:
    • every rod is fitted to the correct tier
    • turnbuckles are taut and locked
    • SATL handles are in the locked position
    • top-tier and outboard stacks are secured Record the inspection as your SMS requires.
  7. At sea:
    • check and re-tighten lashings after leaving port and before forecast heavy weather, where it is safe to go on deck
    • consider course and speed changes to reduce rolling, pitching and the risk of parametric rolling
    • inspect after heavy weather and log any losses or damage
  8. Maintain the lashing gear. The Cargo Securing Manual requires inspection and maintenance of securing devices. Remove damaged items from use and keep the records it specifies.

Common mistakes

  • Checking total ship stability but not individual stacks. The ship can have healthy GM while one stack is 10 t over its limit.
  • Treating a high GM as safe. A stiff ship rolls quickly, and short roll periods produce larger accelerations on the top tiers.
  • Not re-running the lashing calculation after restows or after the final load list changes.
  • Taking declared weights on trust. Discrepancies between VGM and actual weight are a known cause of stack failures. Report suspicious boxes as your company procedure requires.
  • Leaving top-tier or outboard stacks lightly secured. These carry the largest wind and acceleration loads.
  • Over-tightening turnbuckles, or tightening them unevenly.
  • Mixing twistlock types, or leaving cones in positions that need locking.
  • Stowing 20 ft boxes in 40 ft positions without the support and fittings the manual requires.
  • Ignoring the forecast. Heavy weather and synchronous or parametric rolling conditions should change the passage plan, not just the lashing check.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Which document governs how containers are secured on your ship, and who approves it? The Cargo Securing Manual, approved by the Administration (often through class). SOLAS requires cargo units, including containers, to be stowed and secured in accordance with it.
  • What is on a CSC plate? The approval reference and date of manufacture, among other details. It also gives the maximum operating gross mass, the allowable stacking load for 1.8 g and the transverse racking test load.
  • Your stack is within its weight limit but the lashing software shows red. Can you sail? No. Each limit is independent. Restow, change the weight distribution or alter the lashing pattern as the manual allows, then recalculate.
  • Why do we put heavy containers at the bottom? It lowers the stack VCG, which reduces the tipping moment and the lashing and corner post loads. It also protects the lighter boxes from crushing.
  • Explain racking. Racking is the sideways shear distortion of the container frame under transverse acceleration. Lashing rods resist it on deck, and cell guides resist it in the hold.
  • What do you do before heavy weather? Check and tighten the lashings, secure the deck and review course and speed. Watch for parametric rolling conditions, follow the SMS heavy weather checklist and record everything.
  • What happens if a container has no verified gross mass? It should not be loaded.
Key points
  • Stack weight limits come from the approved Cargo Securing Manual and the loading/lashing software, not from rules of thumb.
  • Every stack must pass stack weight, lashing force and corner post checks independently; passing one does not cover the others.
  • Heavy-over-light raises the stack VCG and can fail the lashing calculation even when total weight is within limits.
  • The CSC plate gives each container's maximum gross mass, allowable stacking load for 1.8 g and racking test load.
  • SOLAS requires a verified gross mass before loading, and the final calculation must use as-loaded data.
  • Turnbuckles should be taut and locked but not over-tensioned, with lashing rods fitted to the tiers the manual specifies.
  • A high GM gives a short, jerky roll with high accelerations on the upper tiers, so check lashings and adjust course and speed before heavy weather.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I find the maximum stack weight for a bay?

In the ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual and the stowage/lashing module of the loading computer. Limits are given separately for hold and deck, and for 20 ft and 40 ft stacks, and can vary between bays. If the software and the manual disagree, raise it with the Chief Officer and the company before loading.

What does 'allowable stacking load for 1.8 g' on the CSC plate mean?

It is the superimposed load the container's corner posts were tested to carry, with an acceleration factor of 1.8 g built into the test. It limits how much mass can be stacked on top of that box. Read the value from the plate on the box itself, because it varies between container types.

Do containers in the hold need twistlocks?

In a fully cellular hold, the cell guides provide horizontal restraint, so stacking cones or no fittings are normally used between tiers. Exceptions include partly guided positions, hatchless holds and non-cellular ships. Follow the bay-by-bay requirements in your Cargo Securing Manual.

How tight should container lashings be?

They should be taut and evenly tightened, then locked so they cannot unwind. Over-tensioning pre-loads the corner castings and lashing points and reduces the margin available in a seaway. Follow the tensioning method in your ship's manual and re-check after departure and before heavy weather.

Why can the lashing software fail a stack that is under its weight limit?

The software also checks the weight distribution, stack height, wind exposure and the ship's GM, which drives roll accelerations. A heavy box near the top, an exposed outboard position or a stiff ship can push lashing or corner post forces beyond their limits even when total weight is acceptable. The fix is a restow, a different distribution or a permitted change of lashing pattern.

Test yourself

A deck stack totals 85 t against a 90 t limit, but the lashing module shows twistlock forces exceeded. What should the Chief Officer do?

Stack weight, lashing forces and corner post loads are independent limits, and all must be met. Cargo must be secured in accordance with the approved Cargo Securing Manual (SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5).

Which information is shown on a container's CSC Safety Approval Plate?

The CSC plate gives the container's own ratings, including maximum operating gross mass, allowable stacking load and racking test load. Ship limits and VGM come from other documents (CSC 1972 as amended).

Why does a large GM increase the risk to deck container stacks?

A stiff ship rolls quickly, and the short roll period produces higher accelerations, especially high above the roll centre. The lashing calculation therefore depends on GM (Cargo Securing Manual principles, IMO CSS Code).

A container arrives at the terminal without a verified gross mass. What is the correct position?

SOLAS requires a verified gross mass to be provided before a packed container is loaded. Without one, it should not be loaded (SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 2).

Two stacks have the same total weight. Stack A has its heaviest boxes at the bottom; Stack B has them at the top. Which statement is correct?

Putting heavy boxes on top raises the stack centre of gravity, which increases the tipping moment and the loads on lashings, twistlocks and corner posts. Cargo Securing Manual and lashing software checks reflect this (IMO CSS Code principles).
