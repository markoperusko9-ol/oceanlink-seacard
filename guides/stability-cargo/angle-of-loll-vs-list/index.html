Angle of Loll vs List: How to Tell Them Apart and Correct Them Safely

Quick answer

A list is caused by the centre of gravity (G) moving off the centreline while GM is still positive. An angle of loll is caused by G rising above the initial metacentre (negative GM) while G stays on the centreline. They look the same from the bridge wing but need opposite thinking to correct, and treating loll as a list can capsize the ship.

The core difference in one paragraph

Both conditions leave the ship sitting at an angle in still water. The cause is what separates them.

  • List: G has moved off the centreline, for example from an off-centre weight, uneven bunkers or a slack wing tank. The ship heels until the centre of buoyancy (B) is vertically under the new G. GM is positive, so the ship is stable. It just has a permanent angle to one side.
  • Loll: G is on the centreline but sits above the initial metacentre (M), so the initial GM is negative. Upright, the ship is unstable. It heels until B has moved out far enough that GZ becomes zero again. It finds a new position of equilibrium at the angle of loll. It can lie at that angle to either side.

A third term, heel, describes an inclination caused by an external force such as wind pressure, a turn or a tug's pull. The angle disappears when the force is removed. Do not mix the three up in an oral exam.

How the stability works at each angle

List. With G shifted transversely to G1, the ship heels until B is under G1. For small angles, inside the range where M can be treated as fixed, the angle of list is:

tan θ = GG1 / GM

Here GG1 is the transverse shift of G, found from (weight × distance moved) / displacement.

Loll. When initial GM is negative, the ship does not stay upright. As it heels, B moves out to the low side. For a box-shaped or wall-sided form, the metacentre also effectively rises. At some angle, B comes vertically under G and GZ = 0. That is the angle of loll.

For a wall-sided ship, the GZ formula is:

GZ = sin θ (GM + ½ BM tan²θ)

Setting GZ = 0 at an angle other than zero gives:

tan θ(loll) = √(2 × |GM| / BM)

In that formula, |GM| is the size of the initial negative GM and BM is the initial BM. The formula is only valid while the sides stay wall-sided, so the deck edge must not immerse and the bilge must not emerge.

At the angle of loll the ship has a small effective positive GM, so it resists further heel. However, its range of stability and reserve of dynamic stability are much reduced. A gust, a wave or careless ballasting can push it past the point of no return.

Worked examples

Example 1: List. A ship of 10,000 t displacement has GM 0.80 m. A 50 t lift is shifted 8 m to starboard.

  • GG1 = (50 × 8) / 10,000 = 0.04 m
  • tan θ = 0.04 / 0.80 = 0.05
  • θ = 2.86° to starboard

GM is still positive, so the cure is simply to restore G to the centreline. You can shift the weight back, or ballast/transfer an equivalent moment to port.

Example 2: Loll. A box-shaped vessel has initial GM of −0.08 m and BM of 4.0 m.

  • tan θ = √(2 × 0.08 / 4.0) = √0.04 = 0.20
  • θ = 11.3°, to port or to starboard

Shifting weight across will not cure this. The ship will simply flop over to the other side, possibly at a larger angle. If wind or a wave helps it, it can do so violently. The only cure is to lower G until GM is positive again.

Examiners like to check that you can say why the second answer has no side attached to it. Loll has no preferred direction unless there is also an off-centre weight.

Recognising loll on board

Suspect loll rather than list when:

  • There is no obvious off-centre weight to explain the inclination. Soundings, cargo plan and bunker distribution are all symmetrical.
  • The ship flops from one side to the other, for example when altering course or in a swell. It then hangs at roughly the same angle on the new side.
  • The roll is long, slow and sluggish, and the ship seems to hang at the end of each roll. Roll period lengthens as GM decreases. A sudden increase in roll period is an early warning that GM is falling.
  • The angle has developed during the voyage without any cargo movement.

Typical ways G rises during a passage:

  • Fuel, fresh water or ballast is consumed from double bottom tanks.
  • Too many slack tanks create free surface (a virtual rise of G).
  • Deck cargo absorbs water, as with timber deck cargo, or ice builds up on decks and superstructure.
  • Fire-fighting water collects on upper decks or in holds.
  • High stowage of heavy cargo is made worse by the above.

List and loll can also exist together. If a ship has negative GM and a small off-centre weight, it will loll to that side. This is easy to misread as a plain list.

Always confirm with a stability calculation in the loading computer or by hand, using actual soundings. Do not act on a guess.

Correcting loll: step-by-step

Your company SMS and the ship's stability booklet take precedence. The generally taught sequence is:

  1. Confirm it is loll. Check soundings, cargo, free surface and recent consumption, and calculate GM. Inform the master.
  2. Reduce wind and sea effects if you can, for example by altering course or speed to ease rolling. Avoid heavy wheel orders, which can make the ship flop over.
  3. Do not shift weight transversely to the high side. With negative GM, this can send the ship over to the other side, possibly at a larger angle and with dangerous momentum.
  4. Remove free surface where practicable. Press up or empty slack tanks, especially high ones. Remember that emptying a tank low down can raise G.
  5. Remove or lower top weight if it is possible and safe, for example ice or water on deck.
  6. Ballast low down, one tank at a time. Start with a small, subdivided double bottom tank on the low side. Filling it adds free surface at first, so the angle may briefly increase. As the weight builds, G drops. Because the weight is on the low side, the ship will not suddenly flop to the other side.
  7. When that tank is pressed up, fill the corresponding tank on the high side. Then continue with other low tanks as needed until the ship is upright with positive GM.
  8. Never start with a large, undivided tank or several tanks at once. The free surface effect can outweigh the lowering of G and make the situation worse before it gets better.
  9. Recalculate, log the actions, and record the event as your SMS requires.

Correcting a list is simpler, because GM is positive. Move G back to the centreline by transferring ballast or fuel, or shifting cargo, to the high side. Check that the list is not really loll first.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every angle as a list. Pumping ballast to the high side of a lolling ship is the classic error, and it can cause a capsize.
  • Using tan θ = GG1/GM with negative GM. The list formula assumes positive GM and small angles. It gives nonsense for loll.
  • Forgetting free surface. Solid GM may look acceptable while the fluid GM, after free surface correction, is negative.
  • Ignoring consumption. A departure condition that was fine may not be fine on arrival. Check the worst condition of the voyage, not just departure.
  • Over-trusting the wall-sided formula. It is an approximation for wall-sided forms at moderate angles, not a general answer for every hull.
  • Confusing angle of loll with angle of heel from wind or turning. Heel goes away when the force goes away. Loll does not.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Q: Define angle of loll. The angle at which a ship with negative initial GM comes to rest because B has moved under G, making GZ zero. G remains on the centreline.

Q: How do you tell list from loll on board? Look for an off-centre weight (list), or no off-centre weight with flopping between sides and a long, sluggish roll (loll). Then confirm by calculating GM.

Q: Why not shift weight to the high side to correct loll? G is the problem, not its transverse position. Moving weight across makes the ship flop to the other side, possibly violently.

Q: Which tank would you fill first and why? A small, subdivided double bottom tank on the low side. It keeps free surface small, lowers G, and avoids a sudden flop to the other side.

Q: Is a lolling ship stable? At the angle of loll it has a small effective GM and can resist small further heel. Its range and reserve of stability are reduced, so it is in a dangerous condition.

Q: Name causes of rising G at sea. Consumption from double bottoms, slack tanks, deck cargo absorbing water, ice accretion, and fire-fighting water.

Q: What regulatory minimum GM applies? The 2008 IS Code, Part A, sets general intact stability criteria, including a minimum initial GM after free surface correction. Specific ship types such as timber carriers have their own provisions. Always state that the ship's approved stability booklet governs.

Key points
  • List: G is off the centreline and GM is positive. The ship lies to one side only.
  • Loll: GM is negative and G is on the centreline. The ship can lie to either side and flops between them.
  • Angle of list (small angles): tan θ = GG1 / GM.
  • Angle of loll (wall-sided): tan θ = √(2 × |GM| / BM).
  • Never correct loll by shifting weight to the high side. Lower G instead.
  • Ballast a small divided double bottom tank on the low side first, then the high side, one tank at a time.
  • A lengthening, sluggish roll is an early warning that GM is falling.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between list and angle of loll?

List is caused by G moving off the centreline while GM stays positive, so the ship heels to one side only. Loll is caused by negative initial GM with G still on the centreline. The ship heels until GZ becomes zero and can lie at that angle to either side.

How do you correct an angle of loll?

Lower G and reduce free surface. Remove top weight if possible, then ballast low tanks one at a time. Start with a small subdivided double bottom tank on the low side, then fill the matching tank on the high side. Do not shift weight to the high side, and do not fill large undivided tanks first.

Why is the low side tank filled first when correcting loll?

Weight added on the low side lowers G without pushing the ship to flop over to the opposite side. Filling the high side first could make a ship with negative GM swing suddenly to the other side, possibly at a larger angle. A small tank keeps the temporary free surface effect limited.

What causes angle of loll during a voyage?

Anything that raises the effective G. Common causes are consuming fuel or water from double bottom tanks, free surface from slack tanks, deck cargo absorbing water, ice accretion, and water from fire-fighting collecting high in the ship.

How do you calculate the angle of loll?

For a wall-sided vessel, tan θ = √(2 × |GM| / BM), where |GM| is the size of the negative initial GM and BM is the initial BM. For example, GM of −0.08 m and BM of 4.0 m give tan θ = 0.20, so about 11.3°. The formula is only valid while the sides remain wall-sided.

Test yourself

Which statement correctly describes a ship lying at its angle of loll?

At the angle of loll, B has moved under G, so GZ is zero, while G stays on the centreline. An off-centre G with positive GM describes a list (basic ship stability theory).

For small angles, which formula gives the angle of list?

List is found from tan θ = GG1 / GM with positive GM. The square-root expression is the wall-sided formula for angle of loll.

A ship is lolling to port. What is the correct first ballasting action?

Lower G with a small low tank on the low side first, to limit free surface and avoid a sudden flop to the other side. Transverse transfer does not cure negative GM.

A wall-sided vessel has initial GM of −0.08 m and BM of 4.0 m. What is the approximate angle of loll?

tan θ = √(2 × 0.08 / 4.0) = √0.04 = 0.20, so θ ≈ 11.3°. This uses the wall-sided GZ formula with GZ set to zero.

Which observation most strongly suggests loll rather than list?

Flopping between sides with a long, sluggish roll indicates negative GM. A heel that disappears after the turn is heel, and an off-centre lift causes a list.
