Grain Stability and the International Grain Code

Quick answer

Bulk grain can shift when the ship rolls, so the International Grain Code requires the ship to survive an assumed grain shift. Throughout the voyage, the heel caused by that shift must not exceed 12° (or deck edge immersion if that is less for newer ships). The residual area must be at least 0.075 metre-radians, and corrected GM must be at least 0.30 m. You prove this using the ship's approved grain loading manual, volumetric heeling moments and the stowage factor.

Why grain is treated differently

Grain looks like a stable dry bulk cargo, but it behaves partly like a liquid. It settles during the voyage and leaves a void under the deck and hatch covers. When the ship rolls heavily, the grain surface can slide to one side and stay there. The ship is then left with a permanent list and a reduced righting lever, and the next heavy roll can be fatal. Several total losses of grain ships led to the present rules.

The mandatory framework is:

  • SOLAS Chapter VI, Part C (carriage of grain). This makes the Grain Code mandatory for cargo ships carrying grain in bulk.
  • International Code for the Safe Carriage of Grain in Bulk (International Grain Code, adopted by Resolution MSC.23(59)). Part A contains the requirements. Part B explains how assumed heeling moments are calculated.

For the Code, "grain" covers wheat, maize (corn), oats, rye, barley, rice, pulses and seeds. It also covers processed forms of these that behave like grain in its natural state.

Documents you must have on board

  • Document of authorization. It is issued by or on behalf of the Administration. It confirms that the ship is capable of loading grain in compliance with the Code. Without it, a ship may only load grain under the restrictive conditions the Code sets for ships without a document, if the Administration allows this.
  • Approved grain loading information (grain stability booklet). It contains hold capacities, volumetric heeling moment (VHM) tables for filled and partly filled conditions, KG data for each hold, and the curves or tables needed for the stability check. Most manuals also include a table of maximum permissible heeling moments.
  • Ship's approved stability information. You need this for the KN or cross curves, KM, the deadweight scale and free-surface data.

At many loading ports a surveyor or government authority checks the grain stability calculation before the ship sails. Which authority does this, and what forms they use, depends on the country. Check with the agent well before completion of loading.

The stability criteria and the assumed shift

The Code requires the ship's intact stability, throughout the voyage, to meet at least these criteria:

Criterion Requirement
Angle of heel due to assumed grain shift Not more than 12°. For ships constructed on or after 1 January 1994, not more than the angle at which the deck edge immerses, if that is less than 12°
Residual (net) area between heeling arm and GZ curves At least 0.075 m-rad. The area is measured up to the least of: the angle of maximum difference between the curves, 40°, or the angle of flooding (θf)
Initial GM, corrected for free surface of liquids At least 0.30 m

The assumed shift (Part B). The Code assumes the following movements of the grain surface:

  • In a filled compartment, the surface of the void moves to 15° from the horizontal.
  • In a partly filled compartment, the free grain surface moves to 25° from the horizontal.

These surface shifts produce a transverse volumetric heeling moment in m⁴. The Code also allows for the rise in the centre of gravity as grain shifts. It does this by multiplying the transverse moment by 1.06 for filled compartments and 1.12 for partly filled compartments. Most approved manuals already include these factors in the tabulated VHMs. Read the manual's notes so you do not apply them twice.

"Filled, trimmed" means grain is trimmed to fill all the spaces under the deck and hatch covers as far as possible. The VHM for a "filled, untrimmed" condition is larger. A partly filled hold has by far the largest VHM, and its value depends on the ullage or grain depth.

The formulas

Convert each hold's VHM to a weight moment by dividing by the stowage factor (SF, in m³/t):

  • Grain heeling moment (t·m) = Σ (VHM ÷ SF)

Then turn it into a heeling arm:

  • λ0 = Grain heeling moment ÷ Displacement (metres, at 0°)
  • λ40 = 0.8 × λ0 (at 40°)

Draw the heeling arm on the GZ curve as a straight line from λ0 at 0° to λ40 at 40°.

  • The angle of heel is where this line crosses the GZ curve.
  • The residual area is the area between the GZ curve and the heeling arm line. Measure it from that crossing up to the limiting angle defined above.

The ship's GZ curve must use the KG corrected for free surface (fluid KG) for the condition you are checking. In practice most officers use the table of maximum permissible heeling moments, which the manual provides against displacement and KG. If the actual grain heeling moment is less than the permissible value, the heel and residual area criteria are met for that condition. Still confirm separately that GM corrected for free surface is at least 0.30 m, unless the manual's notes state that the table already accounts for it.

Worked example

A bulk carrier completes loading wheat with the following figures. All figures are illustrative.

  • Displacement: 40,000 t
  • KM: 9.60 m
  • Fluid KG (after free surface correction): 8.20 m
  • Stowage factor: 1.35 m³/t
  • Hold 1 partly filled. VHM from the manual at the measured ullage: 4,200 m⁴
  • Holds 2–5 filled, trimmed. Total VHM: 6,600 m⁴
  • Both values already include the Code's vertical shift factors

Step 1 – Grain heeling moment Total VHM = 4,200 + 6,600 = 10,800 m⁴ Heeling moment = 10,800 ÷ 1.35 = 8,000 t·m

Step 2 – Heeling arms λ0 = 8,000 ÷ 40,000 = 0.20 m λ40 = 0.8 × 0.20 = 0.16 m

Step 3 – GM check GM fluid = 9.60 − 8.20 = 1.40 m. This is at least 0.30 m, so it passes.

Step 4 – Approximate heel For small angles, tan θ ≈ λ0 ÷ GM = 0.20 ÷ 1.40 = 0.143, so θ ≈ 8.1°. This is a sanity check only. The real angle is read where the heeling arm line crosses the GZ curve. Compare it with 12° and with the deck edge immersion angle from the manual (assume 14° here, so 12° governs). It passes.

Step 5 – Permissible moment table Enter at 40,000 t and KG 8.20 m. Suppose the table gives 11,500 t·m. The actual heeling moment of 8,000 t·m is below this, so the condition complies.

Step 6 – Arrival condition Repeat the check for the arrival condition. Fuel and water consumption change the displacement and KG, and may change free surface. The Code requires compliance throughout the voyage, not just on departure.

If the check had failed, these are the usual remedies:

  • Top up the partly filled hold so it becomes filled.
  • Secure the partly filled surface by overstowing or strapping/lashing, as the Code permits.
  • Lower KG with ballast, remembering the free surface of any slack tank.
  • Re-plan the distribution so fewer holds are slack.

On-board procedure and common mistakes

Practical sequence:

  1. Pre-plan the distribution using the shipper's declared stowage factor. Aim for every hold filled except, ideally, one partly filled hold.
  2. Confirm that holds are clean, dry and passed by the surveyor, if required at the port.
  3. During loading, monitor drafts and ullages. Make sure filled holds are properly trimmed into the hatch coaming and wing spaces.
  4. Take final ullages or grain depths in any slack hold, and read the VHM for that exact level.
  5. Complete the grain stability calculation for departure and arrival. Present it to the master, and to any port authority that requires it.
  6. Secure partly filled surfaces if the calculation relies on securing.

Mistakes examiners and surveyors see:

  • Inverting the stowage factor. SF is volume per tonne. Dividing VHM by a density, or multiplying by SF, gives a wrong heeling moment.
  • Mixing units. Some shippers declare SF in ft³/long ton. Convert it before use.
  • Applying the 1.06 or 1.12 factor twice, when the manual's VHMs already include it.
  • Using a filled-hold VHM for a hold that is not actually trimmed full.
  • Ignoring free surface in ballast, fuel or fresh water tanks when finding KG.
  • Checking departure only and forgetting the arrival condition.
  • Assuming a general stability programme checks grain criteria. Confirm that the loading computer is approved for grain calculations under your flag and class.

Oral exam questions

Typical questions for CoC orals, with the points an examiner wants to hear:

  • Why can a grain ship capsize in good stability conditions on departure? Grain settles, a void forms, the surface shifts in heavy rolling, and a permanent list develops that erodes the remaining righting energy.
  • State the Grain Code intact stability criteria. Heel not more than 12° or deck edge immersion (whichever is less, for newer ships). Residual area at least 0.075 m-rad up to the limiting angle. GM after free surface correction at least 0.30 m.
  • What are the assumed grain surface shift angles? 15° for filled and 25° for partly filled compartments.
  • How is the heeling arm curve drawn? λ0 equals the heeling moment divided by displacement, at 0°. It falls in a straight line to 0.8 λ0 at 40°.
  • What is a document of authorization, and who issues it? It is issued by or on behalf of the Administration. It confirms the ship can load grain in compliance with the Code, based on approved loading information.
  • How do you reduce the grain heeling moment? Fill and trim the holds, reduce the number of slack holds, and secure partly filled surfaces by overstowing or strapping/lashing.
  • Why is a partly filled hold worse than a filled one? It has a much larger free surface able to shift, and a greater assumed shift angle, so its VHM is far larger.
Key points
  • The International Grain Code is made mandatory by SOLAS Chapter VI, Part C.
  • Heel from the assumed shift must not exceed 12°, or deck edge immersion if less (ships built on or after 1 January 1994).
  • Residual area must be at least 0.075 m-rad and corrected GM at least 0.30 m, throughout the voyage.
  • Heeling moment = Σ(VHM ÷ stowage factor). λ0 = heeling moment ÷ displacement, and λ40 = 0.8 λ0.
  • The assumed shift is 15° in filled and 25° in partly filled compartments.
  • Partly filled holds dominate the heeling moment, so keep them to a minimum or secure them.
  • Always check the arrival condition as well as departure.

Frequently asked questions

What are the stability requirements of the International Grain Code?

Throughout the voyage, heel due to the assumed grain shift must not exceed 12°, or the deck edge immersion angle if less for ships built on or after 1 January 1994. The residual area between the heeling arm and GZ curves must be at least 0.075 metre-radians. GM corrected for free surface must be at least 0.30 m.

How do I calculate the grain heeling moment?

Take the volumetric heeling moment (m⁴) for each hold from the approved grain loading manual, using the correct filled or partly filled value. Divide each by the stowage factor in m³/t, then add them up to get the heeling moment in tonne-metres. Compare this with the manual's maximum permissible heeling moment, or convert it to a heeling arm by dividing by displacement.

What is a document of authorization for grain?

It is a document issued by or on behalf of the flag Administration. It confirms that the ship can load grain in compliance with the Grain Code. It is supported by the approved grain loading information, and loading ports normally expect to see it.

Why is a partly filled hold so dangerous with grain?

A slack hold has a large free grain surface that the Code assumes can shift to 25° from the horizontal. This produces a far larger heeling moment than a trimmed, filled hold. Most remedies aim to fill the hold or secure the surface by overstowing or strapping/lashing.

Does the grain calculation need to be checked for arrival as well as departure?

Yes. The Code requires the criteria to be met throughout the voyage. Fuel and water consumption change the displacement, KG and free surface, so the arrival condition must also be calculated and must comply.

Test yourself

Under the Grain Code, what is the minimum initial GM after free surface correction for a ship carrying bulk grain?

The Grain Code requires initial GM, corrected for free surface of liquids in tanks, of not less than 0.30 m. The 0.15 m value is the general intact stability minimum, not the grain figure. (International Grain Code, Part A)

How is the grain heeling moment in tonne-metres obtained from the volumetric heeling moment?

Stowage factor is m³ per tonne, so VHM (m⁴) divided by SF gives tonne-metres. Dividing the heeling moment by displacement then gives the heeling arm. (International Grain Code, Part A)

What angle of grain surface shift does the Code assume in a partly filled compartment?

Part B assumes a 25° shift of the free grain surface in partly filled compartments, and 15° in filled compartments. The 12° figure is the heel limit, not a shift angle. (International Grain Code, Part B)

If λ0 is 0.25 m, what heeling arm is plotted at 40° heel?

The heeling arm line runs from λ0 at 0° to 0.8 λ0 at 40°, so 0.8 × 0.25 = 0.20 m. (International Grain Code, Part A)

What is the minimum residual area between the heeling arm and righting arm curves required by the Grain Code?

The residual area must be at least 0.075 m-rad. It is measured up to the least of the angle of maximum difference between the curves, 40°, or the flooding angle. The other values come from general intact stability criteria. (International Grain Code, Part A)
