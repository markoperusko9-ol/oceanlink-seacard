Hazardous Cargo: How to Read a Safety Data Sheet on Board

Quick answer

A Safety Data Sheet (SDS) is the supplier's standard 16-section document describing a product's hazards, properties and emergency measures. On board you use it to set PPE, gas-testing limits, firefighting media, segregation and first aid before the cargo or fuel comes aboard. For oil cargoes and oil fuel, SOLAS requires one before loading or bunkering; for other cargoes, the IBC, IGC, IMSBC and IMDG Codes set their own cargo-information requirements.

What an SDS is and why the ship must have one

A Safety Data Sheet (SDS) is prepared by the manufacturer or supplier of a chemical product. The older name, still used in SOLAS and on many ships, is Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS).

The modern format follows the UN Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (GHS). Under GHS the 16 sections always appear in the same order, so you can find information quickly in an emergency.

Where the requirement comes from on board:

  • Oil cargo and oil fuel: SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5-1 requires ships carrying MARPOL Annex I oil to be given an MSDS before loading oil as cargo in bulk or bunkering oil fuel. The content follows IMO Resolution MSC.286(86).
  • Chemical tankers: the IBC Code's operational requirements (Chapter 16) require cargo information on board for safe carriage. Chapter 17 then gives the product's carriage requirements.
  • Gas carriers: the IGC Code's operating requirements (Chapter 18) require cargo information to be available on board.
  • Solid bulk: the shipper must provide cargo information under Section 4 of the IMSBC Code.
  • Packaged dangerous goods: the IMDG Code relies on the dangerous goods transport document. Emergency response is based on the EmS Guide and MFAG. An SDS is useful backup but does not replace those documents.

Company SMS procedures, terminals and charterers often add their own SDS requirements. Follow your SMS and the charter party.

The 16 sections and what the officer actually reads for

No. Section What you look for on board
1 Identification Exact product name, supplier, 24-hour emergency telephone
2 Hazard identification GHS class, pictograms, signal word, H- and P-statements
3 Composition Components, CAS numbers, hidden hazards such as benzene or H2S
4 First-aid measures Treatment by exposure route; delayed effects
5 Fire-fighting measures Suitable and unsuitable media, hazardous combustion products
6 Accidental release Spill containment, ignition source control, PPE for clean-up
7 Handling and storage Temperature limits, static precautions, inerting, incompatible storage
8 Exposure controls / PPE Occupational exposure limits, respiratory, glove and eye protection
9 Physical and chemical properties Flash point, flammable limits, vapour pressure, vapour density, relative density, solubility
10 Stability and reactivity Incompatible materials, polymerisation, decomposition
11 Toxicological information Acute and chronic effects, carcinogenicity
12 Ecological information Aquatic toxicity, persistence
13 Disposal considerations Residue and washing disposal
14 Transport information UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group, marine pollutant, bulk carriage under IMO instruments
15 Regulatory information Product-specific regulations
16 Other information Revision date, abbreviations, changes

In an emergency, go to sections 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8 first. When planning the cargo, sections 7, 9, 10 and 14 matter most.

Decoding Section 2: pictograms, signal words, H and P codes

Signal words:

  • 'Danger' is used for the more severe hazard categories.
  • 'Warning' is used for the less severe ones.
  • If a product carries 'Danger', treat the whole operation with that level of caution.

Hazard statements (H-codes):

  • H2xx are physical hazards, such as flammability or gases under pressure.
  • H3xx are health hazards.
  • H4xx are environmental hazards.
  • One product can carry several codes. A crude oil SDS, for example, may list flammability, aspiration toxicity, carcinogenicity and aquatic toxicity together.

Precautionary statements (P-codes):

  • P1xx are general.
  • P2xx cover prevention.
  • P3xx cover response.
  • P4xx cover storage.
  • P5xx cover disposal.
  • The P3xx statements are effectively a short emergency checklist.

GHS pictograms versus transport labels:

  • GHS pictograms are red-bordered diamonds with black symbols.
  • IMDG transport labels use the class colour scheme.
  • The two systems overlap but are not identical. Do not use a GHS pictogram to decide segregation; use the IMDG class from Section 14 and the transport document.

Section 9 and Section 8 numbers, with a worked example

Section 9 properties that drive ship decisions:

  • Flash point: the main flammability indicator. Note the test method, usually closed cup. SOLAS Chapter II-2 generally prohibits oil fuel with a flash point below 60°C, with limited exceptions. Check the bunker SDS and the bunker delivery note against this limit.
  • Lower and upper flammable limits (LFL/LEL and UFL/UEL): given in % by volume in air.
  • Vapour density (air = 1): above 1 means the vapour sinks and collects in pump rooms, bilges, duct keels and the bottom of tanks.
  • Relative density and water solubility: tell you whether a spill floats, sinks or dissolves. This matters for spill response and for Section 12.
  • Vapour pressure and boiling point: indicate how readily the product gives off vapour at cargo temperature.
  • Auto-ignition temperature: relevant to hot surfaces, such as heating coils or steam lines.

Section 8 gives occupational exposure limits (OELs). These are typically a time-weighted average (TWA) and a short-term exposure limit (STEL). Limit values differ between jurisdictions and bodies, so use the figure on the SDS and your company procedures.

Worked example 1: converting ppm to mg/m³

At 25°C and 1 atm, use mg/m³ = ppm × molecular weight ÷ 24.45.

Suppose the SDS lists an OEL of 50 ppm for a vapour with molecular weight 92.14 (toluene):

  • 50 × 92.14 ÷ 24.45 ≈ 188 mg/m³.
  • Some references convert at 20°C (24.0 instead of 24.45), giving about 192 mg/m³, so small differences between sources are normal.
  • Use this when one reference quotes ppm and another quotes mg/m³.

Worked example 2: why 'low %LEL' does not mean safe to breathe

Section 9 gives an LFL of 1.2% by volume. Your flammable gas detector reads 5% LEL in a tank being ventilated.

  • Volume concentration = 0.05 × 1.2% = 0.06% by volume.
  • 1% by volume = 10,000 ppm, so 0.06% = 600 ppm.
  • If Section 8 gives an OEL of 20 ppm, the atmosphere is 30 times over the limit, even though it is well clear of flammability.

The lesson for enclosed space entry is that toxicity must be measured separately, with a toxic sensor or detector tubes.

Also check the detector's calibration gas and the manufacturer's response or correction factor. A catalytic sensor calibrated on one gas reads differently on another.

On-board procedure: using the SDS from pre-loading to emergency

Before loading or bunkering

  1. Get the SDS for the exact product and grade. A generic 'fuel oil' or 'crude' sheet is not good enough. Check the supplier and revision date.
  2. Cross-check the product name, UN number and class in Section 14 against the shipping documents, the cargo nomination and, for chemicals, the IBC Code Chapter 17 entry. Raise any discrepancy before loading starts.
  3. Check Section 10 for incompatibilities against:
    • the previous cargo,
    • adjacent tanks and holds,
    • tank coatings,
    • line and valve materials.
  4. Check Section 3 for H2S, benzene or other components that need special gas testing and PPE.
  5. Note the flash point, vapour density, OELs and LFL. Set detector alarm points and decide the gas-testing regime.
  6. Brief the crew in the pre-loading or toolbox meeting on:
    • hazards,
    • PPE from Section 8, including the correct glove material,
    • first aid from Section 4,
    • firefighting media from Section 5.
  7. Post the SDS where it will be used: the cargo control room, the manifold and the ship's hospital. For bunkers, keep it in the engine control room.

During operations

  • Monitor the temperature limits in Section 7, especially for heated or inhibited cargoes.
  • Follow static precautions. Low-conductivity products are static accumulators.
  • Watch for vapour collecting in low spaces if vapour density is above 1.

Emergency

  • Fire: check Section 5 for suitable and unsuitable media. Polar, water-miscible solvents need alcohol-resistant foam, because ordinary foam breaks down on them.
  • Exposure: follow Section 4, then consult MFAG or the ship's medical guide and get radio medical advice.
  • Spill: follow Section 6 together with the SOPEP or SMPEP.
  • Use the emergency telephone in Section 1 for product-specific advice.

Common mistakes officers make

  • Using an outdated or generic SDS. Formulation, additives and H2S content vary. Always ask for the current, product-specific sheet.
  • Treating the SDS as the transport document. For packaged dangerous goods, the dangerous goods declaration and IMDG requirements govern carriage. Section 14 is information only.
  • Reading only Section 2. Incompatibilities in Section 10 and the 'unsuitable extinguishing media' in Section 5 are where incidents start.
  • Confusing %LEL with toxic safety, as shown in worked example 2.
  • Wrong gloves. Section 8 often specifies a glove material and breakthrough time. A general-purpose rubber glove may give little protection.
  • Ignoring units. Check whether the flash point is closed or open cup and whether figures are in °C or °F. Check whether exposure limits are in ppm or mg/m³.
  • Leaving the SDS in a folder in the office. The people at the manifold or bunker station need it there.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: Where do you find the flash point, and why does it matter?

Section 9. It is the lowest temperature at which the product gives off enough vapour to form a flammable mixture with air, so it indicates the fire risk and drives gas-freeing and hot work precautions. For fuel, it is checked against the SOLAS 60°C limit.

Q: What SOLAS requirement covers MSDS?

Chapter VI, Regulation 5-1. It applies to MARPOL Annex I oil cargo and oil fuel, and the MSDS must be on board before loading or bunkering. The content is based on IMO recommendations.

Q: The product's vapour density is 2.5. What does that tell you?

The vapour is heavier than air. It will collect in low spaces, so gas tests should be taken at several levels, including the bottom.

Q: Your explosimeter shows 2% LEL. Can a man enter?

Not on that reading alone. Oxygen and the relevant toxic gases must also be within the limits in the SMS and the entry permit. A low %LEL can still mean a toxic concentration.

Q: How would you choose firefighting media for an unfamiliar chemical?

Use SDS Section 5, the IBC Code product requirements for bulk chemicals, or the EmS Guide for packaged goods. Watch for water-reactive products and polar solvents.

Q: What would you do if the SDS and the shipping documents give different UN numbers?

Stop. Do not load. Inform the master, the terminal and the shipper, and get the discrepancy resolved in writing.

Key points
  • An SDS follows the GHS 16-section format; sections 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8 are your emergency pages.
  • SOLAS VI/5-1 requires an MSDS for MARPOL Annex I oil cargo and oil fuel before loading or bunkering.
  • Section 9 gives flash point, flammable limits and vapour density, which drive gas testing and fire precautions.
  • 1% by volume equals 10,000 ppm, so a low %LEL reading can still be far above the toxic exposure limit.
  • Section 10 incompatibilities and Section 5 unsuitable media are often overlooked.
  • The SDS supports, but does not replace, the IMDG transport document, IBC/IGC requirements or IMSBC shipper's information.
  • Always use a current, product-specific SDS and resolve any discrepancy with the shipping documents before loading.

Frequently asked questions

Is an MSDS the same as an SDS?

Yes, in practice. SDS is the current GHS term and MSDS is the older name, which SOLAS still uses. Both should carry the same core information, though older MSDS formats may not follow the 16-section order.

Is an SDS mandatory for every cargo?

SOLAS VI/5-1 specifically requires an MSDS for MARPOL Annex I oil cargo and oil fuel. Other cargoes fall under the cargo-information requirements of the IBC, IGC, IMSBC or IMDG Codes. Many terminals, charterers and company SMS procedures also demand an SDS, so check your own requirements.

Do bunker suppliers have to give an SDS?

Yes. Under SOLAS VI/5-1 the ship must be provided with an MSDS before bunkering oil fuel. The chief engineer should check it against the bunker delivery note, particularly the flash point and any H2S information.

Which SDS section tells me what PPE to wear?

Section 8, Exposure controls/personal protection. It gives exposure limits and the recommended respiratory, eye, skin and glove protection, often including glove material. Combine it with your company PPE matrix and the permit-to-work conditions.

What if the SDS on board is old or for a different grade?

Do not rely on it. Ask the shipper, terminal or supplier, through the agent if necessary, for the current product-specific SDS before operations begin. Record the request, and inform the master and the company if it is not provided.

Test yourself

In a GHS-format SDS, which section gives the flash point and flammable limits?

Physical properties such as flash point, LFL/UFL and vapour density are in Section 9. Section 2 gives classification, not measured values (GHS SDS format).

An SDS lists vapour density (air = 1) as 3.2. What is the correct practical conclusion?

A vapour density above 1 means the vapour is heavier than air and settles in low areas. Gas testing must therefore cover the lowest parts of the space.

A vapour has an LFL of 1.0% by volume. Your detector reads 10% LEL. What is the approximate concentration?

10% of 1.0% is 0.1% by volume, and since 1% = 10,000 ppm this gives 1,000 ppm. That can be far above the Section 8 exposure limit for many products, even though it is well below the flammable range.

SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5-1 requires material safety data sheets for which of these?

SOLAS VI/5-1 applies to ships carrying MARPOL Annex I oil. The MSDS must be provided before loading oil cargo or bunkering oil fuel.

What is the difference between the GHS signal words 'Danger' and 'Warning'?

Under GHS, 'Danger' is used for the more severe hazard categories and 'Warning' for the less severe ones.
