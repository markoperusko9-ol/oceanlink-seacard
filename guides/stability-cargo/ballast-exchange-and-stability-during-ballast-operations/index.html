Ballast exchange and stability during ballast operations

Quick answer

Ballast water exchange under the BWM Convention must achieve at least 95% volumetric exchange, using the sequential, flow-through or dilution method, normally at least 200 nm from land in water at least 200 m deep. The main risks are loss of GM through free surface effect, shear forces and bending moments beyond seagoing limits, and draught or trim problems during intermediate stages. Every step of the sequence must be checked in advance, not just the final condition.

Where ballast exchange fits today

The Ballast Water Management Convention sets two standards:

  • D-1 (exchange standard): ships exchange ballast at sea with at least 95% volumetric exchange.
  • D-2 (performance standard): discharged ballast must meet limits on viable organisms and indicator microbes. In practice this usually means an approved ballast water treatment system. Discharge to reception facilities or other methods accepted by the Administration are also possible.

The implementation schedule was tied to IOPP renewal surveys and was completed on 8 September 2024. Ships to which the Convention applies must now meet D-2 unless they are exempted or covered by specific arrangements. Exchange alone is therefore no longer the everyday answer it was. It still matters for several reasons:

  • Some ships remain on exchange under exemptions, specific arrangements or their flag's application of the Convention.
  • Some port states require or recommend exchange combined with treatment, or have their own reporting forms.
  • Exchange can be used as a contingency measure if the treatment system fails, but only as agreed with the port state concerned.
  • The stability and strength principles are the same for any large ballast transfer at sea, treated or not.

Check your ship's approved Ballast Water Management Plan (BWMP) and the requirements of the next port. These differ by flag and by port state, and the plan is the document that binds you.

The three exchange methods and the location rules

Method How it works Main hazard
Sequential Empty a tank (or pair) as far as possible, then refill with ocean water Large free surface, stress and trim changes at intermediate stages
Flow-through Pump ocean water in and let it overflow through vents or overflow arrangements Over-pressurising the tank; water on deck; icing in cold weather
Dilution Fill from the top while discharging from the bottom at the same rate, keeping level constant Pump rates must be matched or the tank level drifts

The 95% figure is volumetric. For flow-through or dilution, pumping three times the tank volume is deemed to meet the standard. Pumping less is acceptable only if the ship can demonstrate at least 95% volumetric exchange.

Location (Regulation B-4):

  • Normally at least 200 nm from the nearest land, in water at least 200 m deep.
  • If that is not possible, as far from land as possible, but at least 50 nm from the nearest land and in water at least 200 m deep.
  • A port state may designate exchange areas where these distances cannot be met.
  • The ship is not required to deviate from or delay its voyage just to exchange.
  • If the master reasonably decides exchange would threaten the ship's safety or stability, its crew or passengers (for example, in adverse weather), the exchange need not be done. The reasons must be entered in the Ballast Water Record Book.

That last point is the safety override. Use it honestly and record it properly. Port state inspectors will read the entry against the weather and your position.

Stability: what actually changes

Ballast exchange moves large weights low in the ship and creates slack tanks. Three effects act together.

1. Rise of G when bottom ballast is discharged. Removing weight below G raises G and reduces GM. The ship also rises, so KM changes. Read the new KM from the hydrostatics for the new draught. Do not assume it stays the same.

2. Free surface effect. A slack tank creates a virtual rise of G. For a rectangular tank:

  • Free surface moment (FSM, t·m) = (l × b³ / 12) × ρ of liquid in the tank
  • Virtual rise of G (GG₁) = Σ FSM / displacement
  • Fluid GM = solid GM − GG₁

Breadth is cubed, so wide tanks are the dangerous ones. Examples are double-bottom tanks without a centreline division, or any other tank extending the full breadth of the ship, such as some deep tanks. In a wall-sided tank, FSM stays roughly constant from nearly empty to nearly full. A tank at 90% is not 'safer' than one at 50%.

3. Trim and draught. Emptying forward tanks can lift the forepart. This increases slamming risk and can breach the ship's minimum forward draught. Emptying aft tanks can reduce propeller immersion. Large trim changes also affect bridge visibility. For ships to which SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 22 applies, the visibility requirements must still be met, and the exchange plan should take this into account.

On a ship with modest GM in ballast, two or three wide tanks slack at once can take a large bite out of the margin. This is why the BWMP sequence limits how many tanks are worked at the same time.

Worked example: free surface during sequential exchange

Data: Displacement 30,000 t. Solid GM (after allowing for the weight already discharged) 1.20 m. A double-bottom tank 20 m long and 15 m wide is being emptied for sequential exchange. Ballast density 1.025 t/m³.

Case A: tank has no centreline division

  • FSM = (20 × 15³ / 12) × 1.025
  • 15³ = 3,375, so 20 × 3,375 / 12 = 5,625 m⁴
  • FSM = 5,625 × 1.025 = 5,765.6 t·m
  • GG₁ = 5,765.6 / 30,000 = 0.192 m
  • Fluid GM = 1.20 − 0.19 = 1.01 m

Case B: same space divided by a centreline bulkhead (two tanks, each 7.5 m wide, both slack)

  • FSM each = (20 × 7.5³ / 12) × 1.025 = (20 × 421.875 / 12) × 1.025 = 703.1 × 1.025 = 720.7 t·m
  • Total for both = 1,441.4 t·m
  • GG₁ = 1,441.4 / 30,000 = 0.048 m
  • Fluid GM = 1.20 − 0.05 = 1.15 m

Lesson: Dividing a tank into n equal longitudinal compartments reduces the total free surface effect by a factor of n². Here the factor is 4. This is also why you should work one side tank at a time only if the plan allows the resulting list. Otherwise work pairs symmetrically, accepting the free surface of both.

In practice the loading computer applies FSM from the tank tables, often as maximum FSM for slack tanks. Use the computer's figures for the actual decision. Use the hand method to check that the computer's answer makes sense.

Hull strength: the risk that catches people out

Sequential exchange can push shear forces and bending moments past seagoing limits at an intermediate stage, even when the start and end conditions are both fine. This danger is greatest on large bulk carriers and tankers in ballast.

Common problem patterns:

  • Emptying a midship tank while the ends stay full adds hogging moment.
  • Emptying end tanks with midships full adds sagging moment.
  • Uneven emptying of adjacent tanks creates sharp shear force peaks at transverse bulkheads.

The BWMP should contain an approved sequence, with each step pre-checked for stability and strength. Before you start, do the following:

  1. Run every step on the approved loading computer with actual tank soundings, not the plan's assumed figures.
  2. Confirm each step stays within seagoing SF/BM limits, required GM and minimum draughts.
  3. If actual conditions differ from the plan (different cargo remains, consumables, fuel distribution), re-plan rather than force the plan's sequence.
  4. Check the weather forecast for the whole exchange period. A sequential exchange can take many hours.

In heavy weather, wave-induced bending adds to still-water bending. A condition close to the still-water limit is not acceptable in a seaway.

On-board procedure, step by step

Exact responsibilities are set by the company SMS and the BWMP. A typical sequence looks like this:

  1. Plan. The chief officer prepares the exchange plan from the BWMP sequence. The master approves it, including the position window (distance from land, depth) and the weather.
  2. Brief. Hold a joint deck and engine briefing covering pumps, valves, tank order, communication and stop criteria.
  3. Check stability and strength for every step on the loading computer.
  4. Prepare tanks. Confirm air pipes and vents are clear and their closing devices are open where required. For flow-through, confirm the overflow path is free so the tank cannot be over-pressurised. Never run flow-through with a blocked vent.
  5. Start and monitor. Take regular soundings or remote gauge readings. Record times, positions and volumes. Watch list, trim and pump pressures.
  6. Bridge awareness. The OOW knows exchange is in progress and watches for changes in ship motion, weather and visibility over the bow.
  7. Deck safety. Flow-through puts water on deck. In cold weather it can ice up. Restrict access to deck and avoid exchange-related deck work at night where possible.
  8. Stop criteria. Suspend the exchange if the weather deteriorates, limits are approached, or equipment misbehaves. Return the ship to a safe seagoing condition first.
  9. Record. Make Ballast Water Record Book entries with start and end positions, times, volumes and method. Complete any port-state ballast reporting form, as formats vary by country.
  10. Report non-completion and the reasons, as required by the Convention and the port state.

Common mistakes

  • Checking only the final condition instead of every intermediate step.
  • Using plan figures instead of actual soundings, especially after a cargo voyage with different remains on board.
  • Treating fluid GM and solid GM as the same, or forgetting that KM changes with draught.
  • Too many slack tanks at once to save time.
  • Flow-through with vents obstructed, for example with closing devices shut or ice in the vents. This risks structural damage from over-pressure.
  • Ignoring forward draught. This leads to slamming damage in a head sea.
  • Starting late in the voyage and running out of distance or deep water before completion.
  • Poor record keeping. Mismatched positions or times in the record book are a frequent PSC finding.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: What are the methods of ballast water exchange and which is most dangerous to stability? A: Sequential, flow-through and dilution. Sequential is generally most critical, because tanks are emptied, causing large free surface, rise of G, trim change and stress changes at intermediate stages.

Q: What is the exchange standard? A: At least 95% volumetric exchange. For flow-through or dilution, pumping three times the tank volume is deemed to meet it. Less is acceptable only if 95% is demonstrated.

Q: Where may exchange be carried out? A: Normally at least 200 nm from the nearest land in at least 200 m depth. Failing that, at least 50 nm and 200 m depth, or in an area designated by the port state.

Q: Can the master refuse to exchange? A: Yes, if the master reasonably decides it would threaten the safety or stability of the ship, crew or passengers. The reasons are recorded in the Ballast Water Record Book.

Q: How does a centreline bulkhead affect free surface? A: FSM varies with breadth cubed. Dividing a tank into n equal longitudinal parts reduces the total effect by n², so a single centreline bulkhead cuts it to a quarter.

Q: Why check every step and not just the end condition? A: Shear forces, bending moments, GM and draughts can exceed limits part-way through even when start and finish are within limits.

Key points
  • The D-1 exchange standard is at least 95% volumetric exchange; for flow-through or dilution, pumping three tank volumes is deemed compliant.
  • Exchange is normally done at least 200 nm from land in 200 m depth, or at least 50 nm and 200 m depth if that is not possible.
  • Since 8 September 2024, ships to which the Convention applies must meet D-2 unless exempted, but exchange remains relevant for exemptions, contingency and some port requirements.
  • Free surface effect = Σ FSM / displacement, and FSM varies with tank breadth cubed.
  • Check every intermediate step for GM, shear force, bending moment, draught and trim, not just the final condition.
  • Flow-through needs clear vents and overflows to avoid over-pressurising tanks.
  • The master may decline exchange for safety reasons, but must record why in the Ballast Water Record Book.

Frequently asked questions

Is ballast water exchange still required if my ship has a treatment system?

A ship meeting the D-2 standard with an approved treatment system generally manages ballast by treatment, not exchange. Some port states still require or recommend exchange as well, or accept exchange as a contingency measure if the system fails. Check the BWMP and the specific port's requirements before arrival.

What is the difference between the sequential and flow-through methods?

In sequential exchange, each tank is emptied and then refilled with ocean water. In flow-through, ocean water is pumped in and allowed to overflow until enough volume has passed through, normally three times the tank capacity. Sequential creates bigger stability and stress changes. Flow-through brings over-pressure and water-on-deck risks.

Why does free surface effect matter so much during ballast exchange?

Sequential exchange leaves tanks slack, and every slack tank raises the effective centre of gravity. The effect depends on tank breadth cubed, so wide double-bottom or full-width tanks can remove a significant part of the GM. This is why the BWMP limits how many tanks are slack at the same time.

What should I do if the weather deteriorates during exchange?

Stop the exchange and bring the ship back to a safe seagoing condition, following the BWMP and company procedures. The Convention allows the master not to carry out exchange where it would threaten safety or stability. Record the reasons and any partial exchange in the Ballast Water Record Book, and report as the next port state requires.

What entries go in the Ballast Water Record Book for an exchange?

Typically the date, start and end positions and times, tanks involved, volumes, method used and the officer in charge's signature. The exact format follows the Convention's record book layout and your flag's requirements. Port states also commonly ask for a separate ballast water reporting form, which varies by country.

Test yourself

What volumetric exchange does the D-1 standard require?

The D-1 standard requires at least 95% volumetric exchange. Reference: BWM Convention, Regulation D-1.

For the flow-through method, which pumped volume is deemed to meet the exchange standard?

Pumping three times the tank volume is deemed to meet the standard; less is acceptable only if 95% is demonstrated. Reference: BWM Convention, Regulation D-1.

A slack rectangular tank is split into two equal tanks by a centreline bulkhead. How does the total free surface effect change?

FSM varies with breadth cubed, so two tanks of half breadth give 2 × (1/8) = 1/4 of the original. Reference: standard ship stability principles; IMO Intact Stability Code on free surface allowance.

If exchange cannot be done 200 nm from land, what is the minimum alternative under Regulation B-4?

The fallback is as far as possible from land, but at least 50 nm from the nearest land in water at least 200 m deep. Reference: BWM Convention, Regulation B-4.

During sequential exchange, which check gives the best assurance of safety?

Limits can be exceeded part-way through even when start and end conditions are acceptable, so each step must be checked. Reference: BWM Convention, Regulation B-1 (BWMP) and IMO Guidelines for ballast water exchange (G6).
