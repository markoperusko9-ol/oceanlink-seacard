Draft Survey Basics: Procedure, Corrections and a Worked Example

Quick answer

A draft survey finds the weight of cargo loaded or discharged by measuring the ship's displacement before and after the operation and subtracting everything on board that is not cargo. You read six drafts, correct them to the perpendiculars and for hull deflection, enter the hydrostatics, apply trim and density corrections, then subtract ballast, fuel, fresh water and other deductibles. Each step has a sign convention that examiners will test, and getting a sign wrong is how most survey errors happen.

What a draft survey is and when it is used

A draft (draught) survey uses the ship as a weighing scale. Archimedes gives you the principle: the ship displaces her own weight of water. If you know the displacement accurately before and after loading, the difference, less any change in ballast, fuel and stores, is the cargo.

It is used mainly for dry bulk cargoes such as coal, ore, grain, fertiliser and concentrates, where there is no reliable shore meter. Tankers normally rely on ullages and shore figures, although a draft check is sometimes done.

There are normally two surveys:

  • Initial survey before loading or discharge. Before loading (light ship) it gives a net displacement of lightship plus the ship's constant. Before discharge it gives lightship + constant + cargo.
  • Final survey after completion. The difference in net displacement between the two surveys is the cargo loaded or discharged.

The Chief Officer usually does the ship's survey, often alongside an independent surveyor acting for shippers, receivers or charterers. Company SMS and charter party terms decide whose figure goes on the bill of lading and how disputes are handled. The UNECE code on draught surveys of coal cargoes is the most widely referenced published procedure, and its method is widely applied to other dry bulk cargoes.

What you need before you start

Have these ready before you go down the gangway:

  • Approved hydrostatic tables or the loading computer's hydrostatics. Note the density the tables are based on. It is usually 1.025 t/m³, but check.
  • Draft mark positions: the distance of the forward, midship and aft marks from the forward perpendicular (FP), midships and aft perpendicular (AP). These figures are in the stability booklet or on the draft mark plan.
  • LBP and the LCF sign convention used in the tables. Some tables measure LCF from midships and some from the AP.
  • Tank calibration tables with trim and list corrections for every ballast, fuel, lube and fresh water tank.
  • A draft survey hydrometer in good order, a sampling can and line, and sounding tapes.
  • Lightship weight from the stability booklet.

Arrange with the engine room to stop all ballast and fresh water transfers during the survey. Agree with the shore that no cargo operation, crane movement or bunkering takes place while you read drafts and sound tanks.

On-board procedure, step by step

  1. Read six drafts: forward, midship and aft, both port and starboard. Read the offshore side from a boat or ladder, not by guessing. With metric marks the numerals are 10 cm high, and the draft is at the bottom edge of the figure.
  2. Average out the waves. In swell or wash, watch several cycles and take the mean of the highs and lows. Note the reading time and the conditions.
  3. Sample the water. Take samples at about half-draft depth, preferably at more than one position along the ship, because river outflow and discharges vary density. Read the hydrometer while it floats freely, at eye level.
  4. Sound every tank that can change weight: ballast, fuel, diesel, lube, fresh water, bilges and slops. Record trim and list at the time of sounding.
  5. Correct the soundings for trim and list from the tank tables. Convert volumes to weight using the correct density for each tank. Ballast taken from a different port may not be the same density as dock water, so sample it.
  6. Account for other variables the company procedure lists, such as stores, anchors and cables if not in lightship, and swimming pool or bilge water.

Do the drafts and soundings as close together in time as possible. A ballast pump left running between the two invalidates the survey.

The calculation: corrections and formulas

1. Perpendicular corrections. Draft marks are rarely on the perpendiculars. Each correction is found from:

Correction = distance of mark from perpendicular × apparent trim ÷ LBM

LBM is the length between the forward and aft marks. To get the sign right, picture the waterline. With the ship trimmed by the stern:

  • forward marks aft of the FP need a negative correction, because the bow is shallower;
  • aft marks forward of the AP need a positive correction.

Midship marks off midships take the same logic.

2. Hog/sag and the quarter mean. Compare the corrected midship draft with the mean of the corrected forward and aft drafts.

  • If the midship draft is less than that mean, the ship is hogged.
  • If it is more, she is sagged.

The displacement is then entered at the mean of mean of means (quarter mean):

QM = (F + A + 6M) ÷ 8

This weights the midship draft to allow for a roughly parabolic deflection.

3. First trim correction (layer correction). The tables assume an even keel. When trimmed, the ship rotates about the LCF, not midships.

FTC (t) = true trim (m) × LCF from midships (m) × TPC × 100 ÷ LBP (m)

  • The correction is positive when the LCF is on the same side of midships as the deeper end.
  • It is negative when the LCF is on the opposite side.

4. Second trim correction (Nemoto). This allows for the LCF shifting with trim.

STC (t) = trim² × 50 × dM/dZ ÷ LBP

dM/dZ is the MTC at QM + 0.5 m minus the MTC at QM − 0.5 m. The STC is always positive.

5. Density correction.

Corrected displacement = displacement × measured density ÷ table density

6. Deductibles. Subtract the total of ballast, fuel, fresh water and other deductibles to get the net displacement.

  • At the initial survey, net displacement − lightship = constant.
  • At the final survey, cargo = final net displacement − initial net displacement (for loading).

Worked example: initial survey before loading

The figures below are illustrative, not from a real ship.

Ship data

  • LBP 180.00 m
  • Forward marks 2.50 m aft of the FP
  • Aft marks 6.00 m forward of the AP
  • Midship marks at midships
  • Tables at 1.025 t/m³
  • Lightship 8,520 t

Observed drafts

Position Port Stbd Mean
Forward 5.62 5.58 5.600
Midship 6.47 6.43 6.450
Aft 7.33 7.27 7.300

Step 1: Perpendicular corrections.

  • Apparent trim = 7.300 − 5.600 = 1.700 m by the stern.
  • LBM = 180.00 − 2.50 − 6.00 = 171.50 m.
  • Forward correction = 2.50 × 1.700 ÷ 171.50 = −0.025, so F = 5.575 m.
  • Aft correction = 6.00 × 1.700 ÷ 171.50 = +0.059, so A = 7.359 m.
  • Midship correction is zero, so M = 6.450 m.
  • True trim = 7.359 − 5.575 = 1.784 m.

Step 2: Hog/sag and quarter mean.

  • Mean of F and A = 6.467 m. M is 6.450 m, so the ship is hogged 17 mm.
  • QM = (5.575 + 7.359 + 6 × 6.450) ÷ 8 = 6.454 m.

Step 3: Hydrostatics at 6.454 m. Interpolating between 6.40 m (20,708 t) and 6.50 m (20,992 t) gives:

  • Displacement = 20,708 + 0.54 × 284 = 20,861.4 t
  • TPC = 28.40
  • LCF = 2.10 m aft of midships
  • MTC at 6.954 m = 236.5; MTC at 5.954 m = 224.0; so dM/dZ = 12.5

Step 4: Trim corrections.

  • FTC = 1.784 × 2.10 × 28.40 × 100 ÷ 180 = +59.1 t. It is positive because the ship is trimmed by the stern and the LCF is aft.
  • STC = 1.784² × 50 × 12.5 ÷ 180 = +11.1 t.
  • Corrected displacement = 20,861.4 + 59.1 + 11.1 = 20,931.6 t.

Step 5: Density. Dock water is 1.018.

  • 20,931.6 × 1.018 ÷ 1.025 = 20,788.7 t.

Step 6: Deductibles.

Item t
Ballast 10,800.0
Fresh water 210.0
HFO 820.0
MGO 95.0
Lube oil 40.0
Total 11,965.0
  • Net displacement = 20,788.7 − 11,965.0 = 8,823.7 t.
  • Constant = 8,823.7 − 8,520 = 303.7 t.

Final survey. Suppose the final survey gives a net displacement of 38,410.2 t. The cargo loaded is then 38,410.2 − 8,823.7 = 29,586.5 t.

Common mistakes that cost tonnes

  • Wrong sign on a perpendicular correction. Always sketch the trimmed waterline against the marks before applying it.
  • Ignoring the LCF reference. Tables that give LCF from the AP must be converted to a distance from midships before the FTC. Using the raw figure gives a large error.
  • Using apparent trim in the FTC. Use the true trim, between the perpendiculars.
  • Single density sample taken at the surface near a discharge outlet or river mouth.
  • Assuming ballast density equals dock water density. Sample the tanks.
  • Not applying trim and list corrections to soundings, especially in wide double-bottom tanks.
  • Uncorrected list. Six-draft averaging copes with a small list, but a significant list should be removed before reading.
  • Changes between surveys. Grabs, stores, or ballast moved between initial and final surveys and not recorded will show up in the cargo figure.
  • A large unexplained constant. Compare it with previous voyages. A sudden jump usually means an unsounded tank, mud in ballast tanks or an arithmetic slip, not a real change.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

Q: Why read six drafts instead of forward and aft only? The port and starboard readings average out list. The midship readings reveal hog or sag, which the forward and aft drafts alone cannot show.

Q: Why 6M in the quarter-mean formula? It weights the midship draft so the result approximates the mean draft of a hull deflected in a parabolic curve.

Q: When is the first trim correction negative? When the LCF lies on the opposite side of midships to the deeper end. An example is a ship trimmed by the head with the LCF aft.

Q: Can the second trim correction be negative? No. Trim is squared, and dM/dZ is normally positive, so the correction is always additive.

Q: What is the constant and why does it change? It is the unknown weight on board beyond lightship and the measured deductibles. Examples are stores, spares, sediment in tanks, paint and unpumpable liquids. It changes as stores are consumed and mud builds up.

Q: Conditions are poor with heavy swell and current. What do you do? Report it to the Master. Take multiple readings and record the conditions. Consider delaying until conditions improve, and note any reservations in the survey report as company procedure requires.

Key points
  • Cargo quantity is the difference in net displacement between the initial and final surveys.
  • Correct the forward and aft drafts to the perpendiculars using apparent trim over the length between marks.
  • The quarter mean (F + A + 6M) ÷ 8 allows for hog or sag.
  • The first trim correction's sign depends on the LCF position relative to the deeper end; the second trim correction is always positive.
  • Correct displacement for measured dock water density against the tables' density.
  • Deductibles must use trim- and list-corrected soundings and the actual density of each tank's contents.
  • An unexplained change in the constant usually means an error, not a real weight.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is a draft survey?

Accuracy depends heavily on sea conditions, the quality of the hydrostatic and tank tables, density sampling and the care taken in sounding. In good conditions a careful survey is generally reliable for commercial purposes. Swell, current, ice or poor tank data can introduce significant error. Charter parties and contracts often state an acceptable tolerance, so check those rather than assuming a figure.

What is the ship's constant in a draft survey?

The constant is the net displacement found at the light survey minus the lightship weight. It covers everything you cannot measure directly, such as stores, spares, sediment and unpumpable residues. Track it voyage to voyage, because a sudden change usually points to an error.

Do I correct the hydrometer reading for temperature?

A draft survey hydrometer reads the density of the sample at its actual temperature, which is what you need. You do not apply the temperature corrections used for petroleum cargoes. Make sure the hydrometer is clean, certified and floating freely when you read it.

Why do we need both first and second trim corrections?

The first trim correction moves the displacement from the mean draft to the true draft at the LCF, about which the ship trims. The second correction allows for the LCF itself moving as the ship trims. At small trims the second correction is tiny, but at larger trims it becomes significant.

Who signs the draft survey figure, the Chief Officer or the surveyor?

Usually both the ship and the independent surveyor calculate a figure and compare them. Whose figure is used on the bill of lading is decided by the charter party and the parties' instructions, not by any single rule. If the figures disagree significantly, inform the Master and follow company procedure. That may include a note of protest or reservation.

Test yourself

What is the sign of the second trim (Nemoto) correction?

Trim is squared and dM/dZ is positive, so the correction is always added (standard method, UNECE draught survey code).

Which formula gives the quarter mean (mean of mean of means) draft?

Taking the mean of F and A, averaging it with M, then averaging again with M gives (F + A + 6M) ÷ 8. This allows for hull deflection (UNECE draught survey code).

A ship is trimmed by the stern. Her forward marks are 3 m aft of the FP. How is the forward perpendicular correction applied?

With stern trim the waterline rises towards the bow, so the draft at the FP is shallower than at marks aft of it. The correction is therefore negative.

A ship is trimmed by the stern with the LCF 2 m aft of midships. What is the sign of the first trim correction?

When the LCF is on the same side of midships as the deeper end, the true mean draft at the LCF is greater than the mid-length draft. The correction is therefore positive.

After perpendicular corrections, the midship draft is greater than the mean of the forward and aft drafts. What is the ship's condition?

A sagged ship drops amidships, so the midship draft exceeds the mean of the ends. Hogging is the reverse.
