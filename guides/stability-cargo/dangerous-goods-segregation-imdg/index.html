Dangerous goods segregation under the IMDG Code: a practical guide for officers and cadets

Quick answer

IMDG segregation keeps incompatible dangerous goods far enough apart that a leak or fire in one cannot set off a dangerous reaction in another. You find the requirement by reading the segregation table in Chapter 7.2 for every primary and subsidiary hazard. You then check column 16b of the Dangerous Goods List for special provisions, apply the strictest result, and turn it into physical distance using the stowage rules for your ship type.

What segregation is and where it sits in the Code

The IMDG Code is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII, Part A. Segregation is dealt with in Part 7:

  • Chapter 7.2: general segregation provisions, the segregation terms, the segregation table and the segregation groups.
  • Chapter 7.3: consigning operations concerning the packing and use of cargo transport units (CTUs). Segregation of packages inside a CTU, such as a container or road vehicle, is at 7.3.4.
  • Chapter 7.4: stowage and segregation on container ships.
  • Chapter 7.5: ro-ro ships.
  • Chapter 7.6: general cargo ships.

The Code is amended on a two-year cycle, and column layouts and provision numbers have changed in past amendments. Always work from the edition currently in force and carried on board, not from old notes.

Segregation is a separate requirement from stowage. Stowage covers stowage category, on deck or under deck, and away from heat or living quarters. A package can be correctly stowed and still wrongly segregated, and the reverse is also possible.

The four segregation terms

The table gives a number. Each number corresponds to a defined term. For packages in conventional (break-bulk) stowage, Chapter 7.2 describes them roughly as follows:

No. Term Practical meaning (conventional stowage)
1 Away from May share the same hold or deck area, with a minimum horizontal separation of 3 m projected vertically
2 Separated from Different holds or compartments under deck. A fire- and liquid-resistant deck between them may count as vertical separation. On deck, at least 6 m horizontally
3 Separated by a complete compartment or hold from A whole compartment or hold must lie between them, vertically or horizontally. On deck, at least 12 m
4 Separated longitudinally by an intervening complete compartment or hold from Vertical separation alone is not enough. A full compartment or hold must lie between them fore-and-aft. On deck, at least 24 m

Two further entries appear in the table:

  • X: no general segregation is required between those classes. You must still check the Dangerous Goods List (DGL) for specific provisions.
  • Class 1 (explosives) against Class 1: handled by compatibility groups, not by the numbers above.

Container ships and ro-ro ships have their own tables in Chapters 7.4 and 7.5. These convert each term into container spaces, bulkheads and vertical rules, and they distinguish closed from open CTUs and on deck from under deck. Do not apply the 3/6/12/24 m figures blindly to a container bay. Use the container ship table.

Step-by-step: finding the requirement for two consignments

Use the same routine every time, whether you are checking a stowage plan or answering an examiner.

  1. Identify each consignment fully. You need the UN number, proper shipping name, class, any subsidiary hazard(s) and packing group. Take these from the DG declaration or manifest, not from a booking summary.
  2. Read the segregation table (Chapter 7.2) for every combination: primary against primary, and each subsidiary hazard against the other goods' hazards.
  3. Take the most stringent result. Where a subsidiary hazard demands more than the primary class, the stricter requirement applies.
  4. Check column 16b of the DGL for both entries. It may contain:
    • SG codes, which are special segregation provisions. These can be stricter or more relaxed than the table.
    • SGG codes, which show membership of a segregation group (acids, alkalis, cyanides, hypochlorites, heavy metals and so on).
  5. Check the relevant exemptions in 7.2.6. One example is goods of the same class that do not react dangerously with each other.
  6. Convert to physical stowage using the chapter for your ship type (7.4 container, 7.5 ro-ro, 7.6 general cargo).
  7. Check limited quantity and excepted quantity status. Many segregation provisions are relaxed or not applied for these, but there are exceptions. Read Chapters 3.4 and 3.5 rather than assuming.

Worked examples

The excerpt below shows a few cells of the segregation table, for teaching only. Verify against the edition on board before relying on it.

3 4.1 5.1 6.1 8
3 X X 2 X X
4.1 X X 1 X 1
5.1 2 1 X 1 2
6.1 X X 1 X X
8 X 1 2 X X

Example 1: flammable liquid and oxidiser

  • Consignment A is UN 1203 motor spirit, Class 3, no subsidiary hazard.
  • Consignment B is a Class 5.1 oxidiser.
  • The table gives 2, separated from.
  • In conventional stowage this means different holds under deck, or at least 6 m apart on deck.
  • Next, check column 16b of both entries for SG codes before finalising.

Example 2: the subsidiary hazard drives the answer

  • Consignment A is Class 6.1 with a Class 3 subsidiary hazard. Consignment B is Class 5.1.
  • 6.1 against 5.1 gives 1 (away from).
  • 3 (subsidiary) against 5.1 gives 2 (separated from).
  • The most stringent result is 2, separated from.
  • A candidate who reads only the primary class answers "away from" and is wrong.

Example 3: an X that is not really an X

  • An acid in Class 8 and an alkali in Class 8 give X in the table, because they are the same class.
  • Both entries carry SGG codes showing they belong to the acid and alkali segregation groups.
  • The DGL entries typically contain an SG code requiring them to be segregated from each other.
  • An acid–alkali reaction can generate heat and spatter. The X only means go and look in the DGL.

Segregation inside containers and on container ships

Inside one CTU (7.3.4). As a general rule, goods that require segregation from each other must not be packed in the same CTU. The main exception concerns goods needing only "away from". These may be carried in the same unit, but only with the approval of the competent authority. The Code also has provisions on segregating certain hazards, such as toxic and corrosive goods, from foodstuffs.

The person packing the container signs the container or vehicle packing certificate. This confirms the packing complies with these rules. The ship normally cannot see inside, so the paperwork is your evidence.

Between containers on board (Chapter 7.4). The container ship segregation table sets the requirement by several factors:

  • whether each container is closed or open
  • whether stowage is on deck or under deck
  • the direction of separation: fore-and-aft, athwartships or vertical

In practice, the stricter terms quickly push you into different bays or holds. On hatch-covered ships, the hatch cover's fire and liquid resistance matters when deciding whether a deck counts as vertical separation. On hatchcoverless holds it may not count at all.

On most container ships, the planner's loading computer flags conflicts. The chief officer still has to check the final DG stowage plan, because software is only as good as the data entered. A wrong class or a missing subsidiary hazard in the booking means the computer will clear a wrong stow.

On-board procedure and documents

Exact responsibilities follow your company SMS, but the usual sequence for the chief officer and duty officers is:

  • Before loading. Check the ship's Document of Compliance for the carriage of dangerous goods (SOLAS II-2/19 applies to ships within its scope). Confirm that each class and each hold or deck area offered is permitted.
  • Pre-stow plan. Cross-check every DG unit's class, subsidiary hazards and UN number against the DGL. Pay particular attention to column 16a (stowage and handling) and column 16b (segregation).
  • During loading.
    • Confirm each DG unit is loaded where planned.
    • Check the placards and marks match the manifest.
    • Inspect for leakage or damage. Refuse or report damaged units according to company procedure.
  • After loading. Ensure the DG manifest or detailed stowage plan required under SOLAS Chapter VII is on board, and a copy goes ashore as required.
  • Emergency readiness. Have the EmS Guide schedules for fire and spillage, and the MFAG, readily available for each UN number.

Late changes are where errors creep in. Examples are re-stows, rolled cargo and short-shipped units that free a slot for something else. Recheck segregation after every change, not just against the first plan.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring subsidiary hazards. This is the commonest error in exams and in real stowage plans.
  • Reading X as "no restriction". Always check column 16b for SG codes and segregation groups.
  • Applying conventional metre distances to container bays. Container ships have their own table in Chapter 7.4.
  • Assuming a deck always separates. It only counts where it is fire- and liquid-resistant, and some terms exclude vertical separation entirely.
  • Treating marine pollutants as a segregation class. Marine pollutant is a mark with stowage implications, not a class in the segregation table.
  • Forgetting Class 1 and Class 7 specifics.
    • Class 1 within itself uses compatibility groups.
    • Class 7 also has separation distances from persons and from undeveloped film, not just from other goods.
  • Working from an out-of-date edition. Column numbers and provisions have moved between amendments.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Define "separated from". Under deck, the goods go in different compartments or holds. A fire- and liquid-resistant deck between them may count as vertical separation. On deck in conventional stowage, the goods must be at least 6 m apart horizontally.

Q: A Class 6.1 cargo has a Class 3 subsidiary hazard. How do you segregate it? Check the table for both the primary and the subsidiary hazard against the other cargo's hazards, and apply the most stringent result. Then check column 16b.

Q: What does X mean in the segregation table? No general segregation is required between those classes. However, the DGL may impose specific segregation through SG codes, for example for acids and alkalis.

Q: Can "away from" goods go in the same container? Only with the approval of the competent authority. Otherwise, goods needing segregation are not packed in the same CTU.

Q: Which document confirms the ship may carry a given class in a given space? The Document of Compliance for the carriage of dangerous goods, where SOLAS II-2/19 applies.

Q: How is Class 1 segregated from other Class 1 goods? By compatibility groups, using the Class 1 provisions in Chapter 7.2.

Examiners usually follow up with "show me where that is in the Code". Know your way around Part 3 (the DGL) and Part 7, not just the answers.

Key points
  • Segregation is in IMDG Code Part 7: general rules in 7.2, segregation inside CTUs at 7.3.4, and ship-type rules in 7.4 to 7.6.
  • The four terms are away from, separated from, separated by a complete compartment or hold, and separated longitudinally by an intervening complete compartment or hold.
  • Check primary and subsidiary hazards against the table and apply the most stringent result.
  • X does not mean free: always read column 16b of the DGL for SG codes and segregation groups.
  • Container ships use their own segregation table in 7.4; do not apply break-bulk metre distances to container bays.
  • Goods requiring segregation are generally not packed in the same container; "away from" needs competent authority approval.
  • Recheck segregation after every late change and always use the current edition of the Code.

Frequently asked questions

What are the four segregation terms in the IMDG Code?

They are: 1, away from; 2, separated from; 3, separated by a complete compartment or hold from; and 4, separated longitudinally by an intervening complete compartment or hold from. Each is defined in Chapter 7.2. The container ship and ro-ro chapters translate each term into physical stowage for those ship types.

What does X mean in the IMDG segregation table?

X means the table requires no general segregation between those classes. You must still check column 16b of the Dangerous Goods List, which may impose specific segregation through SG codes. Acids and alkalis are a classic case: they are both Class 8 but must normally be segregated.

Do subsidiary hazards count for segregation?

Yes. Check the segregation for each subsidiary hazard as well as the primary class, and apply the most stringent result. Missing a subsidiary hazard is one of the commonest causes of wrong stowage.

Can two incompatible dangerous goods be packed in the same container?

As a general rule, goods that must be segregated from each other cannot be packed in the same cargo transport unit. Goods requiring only "away from" may share a unit with the approval of the competent authority. The packer certifies compliance on the container or vehicle packing certificate.

Do segregation rules apply to limited quantities?

Segregation provisions are largely relaxed for dangerous goods in limited and excepted quantities, but there are exceptions. Check Chapters 3.4 and 3.5 of the edition in force rather than assuming everything is exempt.

Test yourself

In conventional stowage on deck, what minimum horizontal distance applies to "separated from"?

"Separated from" on deck requires at least 6 m horizontally; 3, 12 and 24 m belong to the other segregation terms. IMDG Code, Chapter 7.2.

A Class 6.1 cargo with a Class 3 subsidiary hazard is to be stowed near a Class 5.1 cargo. What segregation applies before checking the DGL?

6.1 against 5.1 gives away from, but 3 against 5.1 gives separated from, and the most stringent result applies. IMDG Code, Chapter 7.2.

What does an X in the IMDG segregation table require you to do?

X means no general segregation from the table, but column 16b of the DGL may still impose SG provisions. IMDG Code, Chapter 7.2 and Chapter 3.2.

For which segregation term is vertical separation alone never acceptable?

Term 4 specifically requires fore-and-aft separation with a complete compartment or hold in between. IMDG Code, Chapter 7.2.

How is Class 1 explosive cargo segregated from other Class 1 cargo?

Segregation within Class 1 is governed by compatibility groups, not the general table numbers. IMDG Code, Chapter 7.2, Class 1 provisions.
