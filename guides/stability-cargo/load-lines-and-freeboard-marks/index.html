Load Lines and Freeboard Marks: A Practical Guide for Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

Load line marks show the maximum depth to which a ship may be loaded for the zone, season and water density she is in, measured as freeboard from the deck line amidships. Winter and tropical freeboards are derived from summer freeboard using 1/48 of the summer draft. Fresh water and dock water allowances let you load deeper in less dense water so that the correct mark comes up to the waterline when the ship reaches sea water.

Why load lines exist and where the rules come from

The purpose of a load line is simple: keep enough reserve buoyancy and enough freeboard that the ship stays safe in the weather she can expect. The governing instrument is the International Convention on Load Lines, 1966, as modified by its 1988 Protocol. Most flags apply the Protocol version, but your flag's rules decide the detail.

Key points of application:

  • It applies to ships on international voyages. Excluded are new ships of less than 24 m in length, existing ships of less than 150 GT, ships of war, fishing vessels and pleasure yachts not engaged in trade.
  • A ship that complies is issued an International Load Line Certificate by or on behalf of the flag. The certificate is valid for up to five years. Under the 1988 Protocol's harmonized system it is endorsed at annual surveys held within three months either side of the anniversary date.
  • At the annual survey, the surveyor checks the conditions of assignment as well as the marks. These include hatch covers, doors, ventilators, air pipes, freeing ports, guard rails and side scuttles.
  • The core operating rule (Article 12) is this: the load line for the relevant season and zone must not be submerged when the ship puts to sea, during the voyage, or on arrival.

That last point catches people out. Being on the mark at the berth is not enough. You must also be correct for every zone you will pass through, all the way to arrival.

Layout of the marks: deck line, ring and load lines

The marks are cut, centre-punched or welded into the shell on both sides amidships. They are painted white or yellow on a dark ground, or black on a light ground. Dimensions under the Convention:

Mark Detail
Deck line Horizontal line 300 mm long, 25 mm wide. Its upper edge marks where the upper surface of the freeboard deck, continued outward, meets the outer shell.
Load line ring (Plimsoll mark) Ring of 300 mm outside diameter, 25 mm wide. A horizontal line 450 mm long passes through it, with its upper edge through the ring's centre.
Ring position Centre of the ring is amidships, below the upper edge of the deck line by the assigned summer freeboard.
Load lines Horizontal lines 230 mm long, 25 mm wide. They run from a vertical line 25 mm wide placed 540 mm forward of the ring's centre.
Assigning authority Letters about 115 mm high and 75 mm wide beside the ring, above the horizontal line (e.g. the classification society's initials).

The lines are:

  • S: Summer (level with the centre of the ring)
  • W: Winter
  • WNA: Winter North Atlantic
  • T: Tropical
  • F: Fresh water (summer)
  • TF: Tropical fresh

Ships assigned timber freeboards carry a second set of lines prefixed L (LS, LW, LWNA, LT, LF, LTF). These lines extend aft from the vertical line, so they sit between the vertical line and the ring. The ordinary lines extend forward from the vertical line. Timber lines may only be used when the ship carries a timber deck cargo stowed and secured to the Convention's timber requirements.

Measurement rule: freeboard is measured vertically down amidships, from the upper edge of the deck line to the upper edge of the relevant load line. Always read the upper edge.

How seasonal freeboards and the FWA are derived

Summer freeboard is set at assignment from the Convention's freeboard tables. The ship's type matters: Type A ships carry only liquid cargo in bulk, and Type B covers all others, including reduced B-60 and B-100 assignments. The tabular value is then corrected for block coefficient, depth, superstructures, sheer and bow height. Officers are not expected to calculate the assigned freeboard. You are expected to know how the other lines relate to it.

In the table below, summer draft means the moulded draft from the top of the keel to the centre of the ring.

Line Derivation
Tropical (T) Summer freeboard minus 1/48 of summer draft
Winter (W) Summer freeboard plus 1/48 of summer draft
Winter North Atlantic (WNA) Winter freeboard plus 50 mm, for ships not over 100 m in length. Not marked on longer ships.
Fresh (F) Summer freeboard minus the FWA
Tropical fresh (TF) Tropical freeboard minus the FWA

Fresh Water Allowance:

  • FWA (mm) = Δ ÷ (4 × TPC)
  • Δ is the summer displacement in salt water (tonnes). TPC is tonnes per centimetre immersion at the summer waterline in salt water.
  • The Convention states this as Δ/40T in centimetres, which is the same thing.
  • If the displacement cannot be determined, the deduction is 1/48 of the summer draft.

The FWA is how much the ship rises (or sinks) between water of density 1.000 and 1.025 t/m³. That is why the F line sits above S by exactly the FWA.

Dock Water Allowance for intermediate densities:

  • DWA = FWA × (1025 − ρ) ÷ 25, where ρ is the dock water density in kg/m³.

Worked example: building the marks and loading in a river berth

Ship particulars:

  • Summer draft 9.60 m
  • Summer freeboard 2,800 mm
  • Summer displacement 31,500 t
  • TPC 42.0
  • Length over 100 m, so no WNA line

Step 1: Seasonal lines

  • 1/48 × 9,600 mm = 200 mm
  • Winter freeboard = 2,800 + 200 = 3,000 mm (W line 200 mm below S)
  • Tropical freeboard = 2,800 − 200 = 2,600 mm (T line 200 mm above S)

Step 2: FWA

  • FWA = 31,500 ÷ (4 × 42) = 31,500 ÷ 168 = 187.5 mm, say 188 mm
  • Fresh freeboard = 2,800 − 188 = 2,612 mm
  • Tropical fresh freeboard = 2,600 − 188 = 2,412 mm

Step 3: DWA at the berth

  • You are loading in a summer zone. A hydrometer reading gives dock water density of 1,010 kg/m³.
  • DWA = 188 × (1,025 − 1,010) ÷ 25 = 188 × 0.6 = 112.8 mm, say 113 mm
  • So you may load until the upper edge of the S line is 113 mm below the waterline, measured amidships on both sides and averaged.

Step 4: Consumption allowance on the river

Article 12 allows a port on a river or inland waters to load deeper by the weight of fuel and other consumables used between the berth and the sea.

  • Suppose 60 t will be used on the river passage.
  • Extra sinkage = 60 ÷ 42 ≈ 1.4 cm, say 14 mm.
  • Maximum sinkage at the berth = 113 + 14 = 127 mm below the upper edge of S.

Check how your flag and company want this allowance documented before you rely on it.

Worked example: loading for a zone change during the voyage

The same ship loads in sea water (1.025) in a summer zone. Her route enters a winter seasonal zone during its winter period after 6 days at sea.

Figures:

  • Daily consumption of fuel, fresh water and stores: 35 t
  • TPC near the winter draft: 41.5

Working:

  • Winter draft = 9.60 − 0.20 = 9.40 m
  • Consumption before entering the winter zone = 6 × 35 = 210 t
  • Sinkage equivalent = 210 ÷ 41.5 ≈ 5.06 cm, say 5 cm
  • Maximum departure draft = 9.40 + 0.05 = 9.45 m

The ship must not depart at her summer draft of 9.60 m. The winter zone is the limiting condition, not the loading port.

Practical cautions:

  • Use the zone and seasonal period boundaries from the chart in Annex II of the Convention. Reference charts derived from it are carried on most bridges.
  • Seasonal dates differ by area, so check the actual date you will cross the boundary, not your departure date.
  • Add a margin for delays and weather routing. A storm diversion can put you into a zone earlier or by a different route.
  • Arrival matters too. If the discharge port is in a more restrictive zone than the passage, that limit applies on arrival.

Reading the marks and common mistakes on board

Good practice when checking the marks:

  • Read both sides amidships. Average them to remove the effect of list. Use a boat, the offshore gangway or a measured tape from the deck line when the mark is hard to see.
  • Take density properly. Use a hydrometer on a sample drawn from about mid-draft, away from discharges, rain runoff and the propeller wash. Take several readings during loading, as river density changes with tide.
  • Allow for waves and swell. Watch the mark over several cycles and use the mean, not a single glimpse.

Common mistakes examiners and surveyors see:

  • Using the mean of the forward and aft drafts instead of the amidships reading. On a hogged or sagged hull this can be significantly wrong. The load line is judged amidships.
  • Confusing FWA with DWA. Applying the full FWA in brackish water overloads the ship.
  • Reading the bottom edge of the line instead of the upper edge.
  • Loading to the port's zone and forgetting a more restrictive zone later on passage or at arrival.
  • Using timber lines without a compliant timber deck cargo.
  • Marks that are faded, painted over or smothered in growth. This is a classic port state control deficiency.
  • Load-line items left defective: wasted air pipe heads, missing ventilator closures, seized freeing port flaps, damaged hatch cover rubbers. These undermine the assignment as much as the marks do.

Record the drafts, freeboard, density and allowances in the log book in the format your flag and company require.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners at OOW and Chief Mate level typically probe the following. Practise answering out loud with a sketch.

  1. Draw the load line marks with dimensions. Include the 300 mm ring, the 450 mm horizontal line, the 540 mm offset to the vertical line, 230 mm lines and 25 mm widths, and the assigning authority letters.
  2. How are W, T and F derived from S? Winter and tropical use 1/48 of summer draft. Fresh uses the FWA.
  3. Why is there no WNA line on your ship? It is only marked on ships not over 100 m in length.
  4. What is the FWA and how do you prove the formula? Explain that the extra volume needed in fresh water equals Δ/1.000 − Δ/1.025, and convert it to sinkage using TPC.
  5. You are loading at 1,012 kg/m³. How deep can you go? Show the DWA calculation and state which edge of which line you load to.
  6. What does a load line surveyor check at an annual survey? The conditions of assignment and the marks themselves.
  7. Your ship will enter a winter zone on day 4. How do you calculate departure draft?
  8. What is the reserve buoyancy and why does freeboard matter for stability at large angles? Link this to deck edge immersion. Examiners like candidates who connect load lines to stability, not just to paperwork.

Be precise with units. Mixing millimetres and centimetres in the FWA formula is the most common arithmetic slip in orals.

Key points
  • Load lines for the relevant zone and season must not be submerged on departure, during the voyage or on arrival.
  • Freeboard is measured amidships from the upper edge of the deck line to the upper edge of the relevant load line.
  • Winter and tropical freeboards differ from summer freeboard by 1/48 of the summer draft.
  • FWA (mm) = Δ ÷ (4 × TPC); DWA = FWA × (1025 − ρ) ÷ 25.
  • WNA is only marked on ships not over 100 m in length, at 50 mm below the winter line.
  • A more restrictive zone later in the voyage can limit your departure draft more than the loading port does.
  • Conditions of assignment (closures, air pipes, freeing ports, rails) are surveyed annually alongside the marks.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between FWA and DWA?

The FWA is the change in draft between sea water of 1.025 t/m³ and fresh water of 1.000 t/m³. It is calculated as Δ ÷ (4 × TPC) in millimetres. The DWA is the proportion of the FWA that applies at an intermediate density: FWA × (1025 − ρ) ÷ 25. In brackish dock water you use the DWA, never the full FWA.

Which load line do I load to if I am in a summer zone but will enter a winter zone?

The load line for each zone must not be submerged while you are in that zone. Work out the consumption between departure and entering the winter zone. Convert it to sinkage with TPC and add it to the winter draft. The result is your maximum departure draft, capped at the load line for the zone in which you are loading (here, the summer mark).

Why do some ships have no WNA mark?

The Winter North Atlantic load line is only required for ships not exceeding 100 m in length. It is set 50 mm below the winter line. Longer ships use the winter line when in the Winter North Atlantic area during its seasonal period.

How long is a Load Line Certificate valid?

The International Load Line Certificate is valid for up to five years. Under the 1988 Protocol's harmonized system, it is endorsed at annual surveys held within three months either side of each anniversary date. Exact administrative arrangements, such as extensions, depend on the flag state.

Can I use the timber load lines whenever I carry timber?

No. Timber freeboards are only usable when the ship has been assigned them and carries a timber deck cargo. That cargo must be stowed, secured and protected in accordance with the Convention's timber requirements and the flag's rules. Otherwise the ordinary load lines apply.

Test yourself

How is the winter freeboard obtained?

Winter freeboard is summer freeboard plus 1/48 of the summer draft; subtracting it gives tropical. Load Line Convention 1966 (1988 Protocol), Annex I, Regulation 40.

A ship has summer displacement 24,000 t and TPC 30. What is her FWA?

FWA (mm) = Δ ÷ (4 × TPC) = 24,000 ÷ 120 = 200 mm. Load Line Convention, Annex I, Regulation 40.

On which ships is the Winter North Atlantic (WNA) load line marked?

WNA, 50 mm below the winter line, applies only to ships not over 100 m in length. Load Line Convention, Annex I, Regulations 6 and 40.

How far forward of the centre of the ring is the vertical line carrying the load lines placed?

The vertical line is 540 mm forward of the ring's centre; 450 mm is the horizontal line through the ring and 230 mm is the length of each load line. Load Line Convention, Annex I, Regulation 6.

FWA is 200 mm and dock water density is 1,015 kg/m³. By how much may the summer mark be submerged at the berth, ignoring consumption allowance?

DWA = 200 × (1025 − 1015) ÷ 25 = 80 mm; the ship rises to the summer mark on reaching sea water. Load Line Convention, Article 12 and Annex I, Regulation 40.
