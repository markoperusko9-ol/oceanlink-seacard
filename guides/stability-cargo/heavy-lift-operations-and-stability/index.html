Heavy Lift Operations and Stability: Principles, Calculation and Precautions

Quick answer

When ship's gear lifts a heavy weight, the weight acts at the derrick head or crane jib head as soon as it leaves the deck, quay or hold. G rises and GM reduces. The off-centreline outreach then lists the ship, and the list is greatest when the load is at maximum outreach. Calculate the condition with the load at the head, prepare the ship to keep the list within safe limits, and control the lift so the ship never runs short of stability.

The key principle: the load acts at the point of suspension

A freely suspended weight acts at the point it hangs from. On a derrick that point is the head block. On a crane it is the jib head sheave. The load's own height is not the point of action.

So once the load is clear of the quay or tank top, the ship behaves as if that weight had been placed at the derrick head. This holds whether the load is 30 cm or 15 m above the deck. Hoisting it higher does not change anything further. Topping the boom, luffing the jib or slewing does change things, because those move the head.

Two effects follow:

  • Vertical: G rises towards the head. GM reduces, and at the same time the range and size of righting levers reduce.
  • Transverse: if the head is outboard of the centreline, G moves off the centreline and the ship lists towards the load.

On a ship with only modest GM, a single lift can take the ship from comfortable to dangerous. This is why examiners ask about it so often.

Formulas you need

The usual approach treats the lift in two steps: first the vertical rise of G, then the transverse shift. Which formulas you use depends on where the load comes from.

Load coming from outside the ship (loading from the quay, a barge or overside): the weight is added to the ship as soon as it lifts off, so the displacement becomes Δ + w.

Quantity Formula
New KG (load at head) KG1 = (Δ × KG + w × Kh) / (Δ + w)
New GM (solid) G1M = KM(new Δ) − KG1 − free surface correction
Transverse shift of G GG2 = (w × d) / (Δ + w)
Angle of list tan θ = GG2 / G1M

Load already on board (lifting from the hold, restowing or discharging): treat it as a shift of weight from its stowed position to the head. Δ already includes the load and is unchanged until the load is landed ashore. Do not add w to the displacement or add w × Kh as a new moment, because that counts the weight twice.

Quantity Formula
Rise of G GG1 = w × (Kh − Kg) / Δ
New GM (solid) G1M = KM − KG − GG1 − free surface correction
Transverse shift of G GG2 = (w × d) / Δ
Angle of list tan θ = GG2 / G1M

Where:

  • w = weight of the load plus the hook block, spreader, slings and shackles
  • Kh = height of the derrick or jib head above the keel
  • Kg = height of the load's centre of gravity in its stowed position (load already on board)
  • d = horizontal transverse distance moved by the weight. For a load from shore, this is the distance from the centreline to the head. For a load already on board, it is the distance from its stowed position to the head, which equals the head's outreach if the load was stowed on the centreline.
  • Δ = displacement before the lift (for a load already on board, this already includes the load)

Keep these points in mind:

  • When loading from shore, take KM from the hydrostatic tables at the new displacement, not the old one.
  • Apply the free surface correction from the stability booklet.
  • The tan θ formula assumes small angles and a wall-sided ship. It gives a good answer for the few degrees you should be aiming for. At larger angles, use the ship's GZ curve or the loading computer.
  • On large derricks and cranes, the gear's own weight also shifts when it is topped or slewed. The approved stability information or the gear manufacturer's data usually covers this. Include it where it is given.

Worked example: loading a 150 t unit from the quay

Ship condition:

  • Displacement 12,000 t
  • KG 7.50 m
  • KM 8.80 m. For this example, take KM as still 8.80 m at 12,150 t. In practice, read it from the hydrostatics.
  • No free surface
  • Initial GM = 1.30 m

The lift:

  • 150 t, including the lifting gear, lifted from the quay by the ship's heavy derrick
  • Derrick head 22.0 m above the keel, 12.0 m outboard of the centreline
  • Final stowage on the centreline in the hold, Kg 4.0 m

Step 1: KG with the load at the derrick head

  • Moments: 12,000 × 7.50 = 90,000 t·m, plus 150 × 22.0 = 3,300 t·m, giving 93,300 t·m
  • New displacement = 12,150 t
  • KG1 = 93,300 / 12,150 = 7.679 m
  • G1M = 8.80 − 7.679 = 1.121 m. GM has dropped by about 0.18 m the moment the load lifts off.

Step 2: list at full outreach

  • GG2 = 150 × 12.0 / 12,150 = 1,800 / 12,150 = 0.148 m
  • tan θ = 0.148 / 1.121 = 0.132
  • θ ≈ 7.5° towards the quay

Step 3: after landing in the hold

  • KG = (90,000 + 150 × 4.0) / 12,150 = 90,600 / 12,150 = 7.457 m
  • GM = 8.80 − 7.457 = 1.343 m, which is slightly better than at the start

The examiner's point: the final condition is fine. The critical moment is during the lift, and the critical position is at full outreach.

Typical mistake: using the final stowage position (Kg 4.0 m) for the calculation during the lift. That gives a GM of 1.343 m and hides the real reduction during the lift.

Reducing the list: the heeling moment is 1,800 t·m. Roughly 200 t of ballast with its centroid 9 m off the centreline on the offshore side would balance it. That ballast also changes displacement, KG and free surface, so re-run the full condition. Whether you ballast beforehand, use an anti-heeling system, or pre-list the ship slightly away from the load is decided by the ship's lifting procedure and the master.

Practical on-board procedure

Procedures differ between companies and ship types. Dedicated heavy-lift ships, for example, use anti-heeling tanks and have their own lift manuals. The broad sequence below is common to most.

  1. Plan: confirm the following.
    • Weight, dimensions and lifting points of the load
    • Weight of the rigging
    • SWL of the gear and of each item in the rigging
    • Required outreach
    • Stowage position and deck or tank top strength
    • Lashing plan
  2. Stability check: calculate the condition with the load at the head at maximum outreach, both when lifting it and when landing it. Check the following against the approved stability information and any lifting criteria in it:
    • GM
    • List
    • GZ
    • Shear force and bending moment
  3. Prepare the ship:
    • Press up or empty tanks to remove free surface
    • Avoid transferring liquids during the lift unless that transfer is part of the plan
    • Set ballast or anti-heeling as planned
  4. Gear: check the following.
    • Certificates and the register of lifting appliances
    • Brakes and limit switches
    • Guys or slewing gear
    • Condition of the wires
    • Hook block and shackles
  5. Alongside:
    • Tend the moorings, because the ship will list towards the quay and then come back
    • Lift the gangway or clear it from the quay
    • Check fenders and clearances from cranes on the quay
    • Allow for UKC under the bilge on the low side
  6. Other cargo and shipboard items: secure any loose cargo, deck stores and boats on davits against the list.
  7. Communication: use one signaller and an agreed set of signals. Clear the area under and around the load. Use tag lines to control swing.
  8. During the lift:
    • Lift a short distance first and hold. Check the brakes and the list.
    • Slew and luff slowly.
    • Watch the clinometer and the moorings throughout.
  9. Landing: expect the ship to come upright quickly once the load is landed and the weight comes off the hook. Watch the slack in the wires and the moorings.

Common mistakes and how they bite

  • Using the load's actual height instead of the head height. This overstates GM during the lift.
  • Forgetting the weight of the rigging. On big lifts, the spreader, slings and block add a real amount of weight at a great height.
  • Ignoring free surface. A slack tank you forgot about takes away GM exactly when you have least to spare.
  • Checking only the lifting stage. When discharging, the worst case is often landing the load ashore at full outreach, after other cargo has already been removed.
  • Using tan θ at large angles. Once the list reaches double figures, the simple formula is no longer reliable, and you are likely beyond the list limits in most ships' lifting procedures. Check the approved stability information.
  • Ignoring the effect of heel on outreach. As the ship heels, the outreach of the head increases. That increases the heeling moment and can start the load swinging.
  • Not watching the moorings. A ship that lists and then rights itself can jerk on tight lines or lift a gangway off the quay.
  • Moving ballast during the lift without a plan. The transfer adds a moving moment and free surface while the stability is at its lowest.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Where does a suspended load act, and why does it matter? At the point of suspension, which is the derrick head or jib head. G rises towards the head as soon as the load leaves the deck, so GM reduces during the lift, not only after it.

Q: When is the list greatest? When the load is suspended at maximum outreach from the centreline. That is when lifting from the quay or overside, or when landing on the quay or into a barge.

Q: How would you reduce the list? By one of these, according to the ship's procedure:

  • Counter-ballasting on the opposite side before the lift
  • Using the anti-heeling system
  • Reducing the outreach where possible

Always re-check the condition and keep free surface to a minimum.

Q: Does hoisting the load higher reduce GM further? No. Its effective position is already at the head. Topping the boom or luffing the jib raises the head and so can affect GM. Slewing changes the outreach and therefore the list.

Q: What would you check before a heavy lift? - The stability calculation with the load at the head - Free surface and ballast - Gear SWL and certificates - The rigging - Moorings, gangway and UKC - That the deck is clear - Communications - The landing area and lashings

Q: What happens when the load is landed? The heeling moment disappears and the ship returns towards upright, sometimes quickly. Moorings and wires must be tended, and the crew kept clear.

Key points
  • A suspended weight acts at the derrick or jib head, not at the load's actual height.
  • Calculate KG and GM with the load at the head. When loading from shore, use Δ + w. When the load is already aboard, treat it as a weight shift from its Kg to the head with Δ unchanged.
  • List is greatest at maximum outreach, whether lifting or landing.
  • Small-angle list: tan θ = GG2 ÷ GM with the load at the head, where GG2 = w × d ÷ displacement.
  • Eliminate free surface and avoid unplanned liquid transfers during the lift.
  • Include rigging weight, and when loading from shore use KM for the new displacement.
  • Tend moorings and gangway: the ship lists towards the load and returns when it is landed.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a heavy lift reduce GM even before it is hoisted high?

As soon as the load is freely suspended, its weight acts at the point of suspension, which is the derrick head or jib head. G therefore rises towards that point immediately. Hoisting it higher on the same gear makes no further difference to G.

How do I calculate the list during a heavy lift?

First find the new KG and GM with the load placed at the head height. Then find the transverse shift of G: weight × transverse distance moved ÷ displacement. Use Δ + w if the load comes from shore, or the unchanged Δ if the load is already on board. For small angles, tan θ equals that shift divided by the new GM, and for larger angles you should use the GZ curve or the loading computer.

What weight counts as a heavy lift?

There is no single universal figure. The threshold depends on the ship's gear, the port, the charter party and the company's procedures. In practice, any lift that significantly affects stability, or comes near the SWL of the gear, should be planned and calculated as a heavy lift.

Is it more dangerous to load or discharge a heavy lift?

Both can be critical, and both must be calculated. When discharging, the worst moment is usually landing the load at full outreach, and the ship's condition may already have changed from removing other cargo. When loading, the worst moment is just after lifting the load from the quay at full outreach.

Should ballast be shifted during the lift to keep the ship upright?

Only where the ship's procedure or anti-heeling system is designed for it, and as part of the lift plan. Unplanned transfers add free surface and a moving moment at the time stability is lowest. The safer approach is to set up the ballast before the lift and confirm the condition by calculation.

Test yourself

A 100 t load is lifted from the hold by a ship's crane. While suspended, its weight acts at:

A freely suspended weight acts at its point of suspension, here the jib head. This is the standard principle applied in ship stability information for lifting operations.

During a heavy lift, when is the angle of list normally greatest?

The heeling moment is the weight × horizontal distance of the head from the centreline, so it is largest at maximum outreach. Hoisting higher does not change the effective position.

Displacement 10,000 t, KG 8.0 m. A 200 t load (including rigging) is lifted from the quay with the head 20.0 m above the keel. What is the new KG?

The load comes from the quay, so it is added to the displacement: KG = (10,000 × 8.0 + 200 × 20.0) / 10,200 = 84,000 / 10,200 = 8.24 m. This is the standard addition-of-weight method used with the ship's stability information.

Which precaution most directly protects GM during a heavy lift?

Removing slack tanks eliminates the free surface correction, which would otherwise reduce GM further. Unplanned transfers during the lift add free surface instead of removing it.

A cadet calculates GM for a lift using the load's final stowage Kg in the hold. What is the error?

While suspended, the load acts at the derrick or jib head, which is far above its stowage position. Using the stowage Kg hides the real loss of GM during the lift.
