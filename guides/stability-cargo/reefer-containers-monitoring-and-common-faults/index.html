Reefer Containers: Monitoring and Common Faults

Quick answer

A reefer container is a refrigeration unit that maintains a set temperature. It is not designed to bring warm cargo down to temperature. At sea, the ship's job is to keep each unit powered, check that its setpoint matches the reefer manifest, log supply and return air temperatures, and act quickly on alarms. Most problems come from a short list of causes: power supply, poor airflow, condenser heat rejection, icing and sensor faults.

How a reefer container actually works

An integral reefer is an insulated box with its own refrigeration unit at the front end. The unit has a compressor, condenser, expansion device, evaporator, fans and an electronic controller. Common makes include Carrier, Thermo King, Daikin and Star Cool. Each has its own controller menus and alarm codes, so the manufacturer's manual is the reference for any specific fault.

Key principles every officer should be able to explain:

  • Bottom-air delivery. Cold air leaves the evaporator and travels along the T-section floor. It rises up through the cargo and returns to the unit across the top. Anything that blocks the floor channels or fills the gap above the cargo will create hot spots.
  • The red load line. Cargo must not be stowed above the load line marked inside the box, because the return air needs that space.
  • It maintains, it does not pull down. Shippers are expected to pre-cool cargo to carrying temperature. Warm cargo loaded into a reefer will take a long time to come down and may never reach setpoint evenly.
  • Chilled vs frozen control. In chilled (perishable) mode, the controller normally regulates on supply air temperature, which protects the cargo from being chilled too far. In frozen mode it normally regulates on return air. The switch-over setpoint is typically around −10 °C, but it depends on the manufacturer and controller software.
  • Fresh air vent. Respiring produce such as fruit and vegetables may need a fresh air exchange setting to remove CO₂ and ethylene. Frozen cargo is normally carried with the vent closed. The vent setting is the shipper's instruction, not the ship's choice.
  • Defrost. Moisture freezes on the evaporator coil, so the unit defrosts periodically. During defrost, the displayed temperatures rise briefly. This is normal and should not be logged as a cargo temperature excursion.

Power supply and the one formula you should know

Reefers are supplied from the ship's reefer sockets through a standard 3-phase plug and socket. Voltage and frequency must suit the unit's nameplate. Most units accept a range covering typical ship supplies, and many controllers correct phase rotation automatically. The ship's reefer distribution is part of the main electrical installation. As on most ships, it is usually an insulated (unearthed) system, so a single earth fault normally does not trip the supply. Some installations do have earth-leakage protection on reefer sockets or feeders. The fault does show on the switchboard earth-fault indication.

3-phase power: P = √3 × V × I × cos φ

Worked example (illustrative figures): a reefer draws 15 A per phase at 440 V with a power factor of 0.85.

  • √3 × 440 = 762.1
  • 762.1 × 15 = 11,431.5 W apparent (about 11.4 kVA)
  • 11,431.5 × 0.85 = 9,717 W, about 9.7 kW

The electrical officer and chief engineer use this kind of figure to plan how many generators must run. Actual draw varies a lot. A frozen unit pulling down, or a unit in hot ambient air, draws far more than one idling at setpoint in mild weather. Plan load from measured switchboard readings and the manufacturer's data, not a single rule of thumb.

After a blackout: many controllers have a built-in staggered start. Even so, restore reefer feeders in a controlled way, after essential services, following the ship's blackout recovery procedure.

Monitoring routine on board

The frequency of manual reefer checks is set by company procedures and charterer or shipper instructions. Many ships also have remote monitoring through power-line modems or a dedicated system. This reduces walking rounds but does not replace them, because sensors fail and remote systems lose communication.

On loading:

  1. Get the reefer manifest or list, showing each container's setpoint, mode, vent setting and any humidity or controlled atmosphere (CA) requirement.
  2. Plug in and switch on promptly. Record the plug-in time.
  3. Check the controller display: setpoint, supply and return air, and active alarms.
  4. Compare the setpoint and vent with the manifest. If they differ, report to the chief officer. Do not change the setting without written instruction from the carrier or shipper.
  5. Note any unit arriving with alarms or out of range, and report it before departure so responsibility is clear.

Each round, record:

  • Container number and position
  • Setpoint, supply air and return air temperatures
  • Active or stored alarms
  • Whether the unit is in defrost
  • Condition of the cable and plug, and that the condenser fan is running

Interpreting readings:

  • In chilled mode, return air is normally slightly warmer than supply, because the cargo gives off heat.
  • A supply air temperature well off setpoint, outside defrost, is an alarm condition.
  • A sudden change in the gap between supply and return, compared with earlier readings, may point to a sensor fault, blocked airflow or icing. Investigate rather than just logging the number.

Under-deck reefers: air-cooled condensers reject heat into the hold. The hold ventilation fans must be running as designed. If they are not, the hold air heats up, condenser pressures rise and units trip on high pressure. Some ships use water-cooled units in holds, and these need their cooling water connections in service.

Common faults and what to check first

Symptom Likely causes First checks
Unit dead, no display Plug not fully engaged, socket breaker tripped, damaged cable, feeder off Socket breaker, plug seating, cable condition, feeder at the switchboard
Earth fault indication on switchboard Damaged cable, water in a plug, unit insulation breakdown Isolate sockets one at a time, as per procedure, to find the unit; electrical staff test it

Only trained and authorised personnel should open control boxes or refrigerant circuits. Many ships carry a dedicated reefer technician or electrician. On others, the ETO or an engineer deals with faults under the ship's permit-to-work and lock-out arrangements.

Reporting, cargo claims and safety hazards

Reefer cargo claims are expensive, and the ship's records are the defence. Good practice:

  • Report out-of-range temperatures and persistent alarms to the master. The master informs the operator or charterer as instructed.
  • Record every action and timing: when the alarm was found, what was done, and when the temperature recovered.
  • Do not open doors at sea to look at cargo unless instructed. Opening disturbs the atmosphere, and in CA boxes it is a direct hazard.
  • Preserve the unit's data logger record. It downloads the full temperature history, and claims surveyors will compare it with your log.
  • Some cargoes travel under in-transit cold treatment with dedicated pulp probes. Treat these as regulated shipments and follow the shipper's instructions exactly.

Safety:

  • Controlled atmosphere containers may hold very low oxygen and high CO₂.
  • Fumigated cargo can give off toxic gas.
  • Treat any of these as an enclosed space. Never put your head inside an opened CA box.
  • Damaged reefer cables are an electric shock risk, especially in wet weather.
  • Refrigerant circuits must only be opened, recharged or recovered by certified technicians. They must follow the manufacturer's instructions and applicable national or regional refrigerant (F-gas) rules. Deliberate venting of refrigerant is not permitted. Most modern reefer refrigerants are HFCs, not ozone-depleting substances.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Why does a reefer control on supply air in chilled mode? To stop air colder than setpoint reaching the cargo and causing chilling injury or freezing.
  • Can a reefer cool down warm cargo? It is designed to maintain temperature. Cargo should be pre-cooled by the shipper.
  • The setpoint differs from the manifest. What do you do? Report it and follow the carrier's written instruction. Never change it on your own.
  • Several under-deck reefers trip on high pressure. Cause? Most likely hold ventilation failure or high hold ambient temperature. Check the fans first.
  • The switchboard shows an earth fault with reefers connected. How do you find it? Follow the ship's procedure. Isolate reefer sockets or feeders one at a time until the indication clears, and electrical staff then test the faulty unit.
  • Temperature rises every few hours, then recovers. Fault? Probably normal defrost cycles. Confirm on the controller before logging an excursion.
  • What hazard does a CA container present? A low-oxygen atmosphere. Treat it as an enclosed space.
Key points
  • A reefer maintains temperature; it is not designed to pull warm cargo down.
  • Chilled mode normally controls on supply air and frozen mode on return air; the switch-over point depends on the manufacturer.
  • Always check setpoint, mode and vent against the reefer manifest; never change them without written instruction.
  • Under-deck reefers depend on hold ventilation; failed fans cause high-pressure trips.
  • Temperature rises during defrost are normal and not cargo excursions.
  • Earth faults from reefer cables show on the switchboard; trace them by isolating sockets per procedure.
  • Treat controlled atmosphere and fumigated containers as enclosed spaces.

Frequently asked questions

How often should reefer containers be checked at sea?

The interval is set by company procedures and the charterer's or shipper's instructions, so it varies between ships and trades. Remote monitoring may reduce physical rounds, but it does not replace them. Check more often when a unit is alarming or recovering from a fault.

What is the difference between supply air and return air?

Supply air is the air leaving the evaporator into the box. Return air is the air coming back from the cargo to the unit. In chilled mode the controller usually regulates on supply air, and in frozen mode on return air. Comparing the two over time helps reveal airflow problems, icing and sensor faults.

Can the ship change a reefer setpoint if it looks wrong?

Not on its own initiative. Report the discrepancy to the chief officer and master, who will get written instructions from the carrier or shipper. Changing a setpoint without authority can make the ship liable for cargo damage.

Why do reefers in the hold trip more than those on deck?

Air-cooled condensers reject heat into the hold. If hold ventilation is inadequate or has failed, the hold temperature rises and condensing pressure climbs until the high-pressure safety trips. Water-cooled units need their cooling water connected and flowing.

Is a temperature rise during defrost a problem?

Normally no. The displayed temperature rises briefly while the evaporator coil is defrosted, then recovers. If defrost happens too often or the temperature fails to recover, investigate for icing or a defrost fault.

Test yourself

In chilled (perishable) mode, a typical reefer controller regulates on which temperature?

Controlling on supply air stops air colder than setpoint reaching chilled cargo. Exact logic is in the manufacturer's controller manual.

On loading, a reefer's setpoint differs from the reefer manifest. What should the officer do?

The ship must not change carriage instructions on its own authority. Company procedures and the carrier's instructions govern any change.

Several under-deck reefers trip on high pressure on a hot day. What is the most likely first thing to check?

Air-cooled condensers reject heat into the hold, so ventilation failure raises condensing pressure. The ship's reefer and hold ventilation instructions cover this.

A unit draws 15 A at 440 V, 3-phase, with power factor 0.85. What is its approximate real power?

P = √3 × 440 × 15 × 0.85 ≈ 9.7 kW. 11.4 is the apparent power in kVA, and 5.6 kW leaves out √3.

Why must a controlled atmosphere reefer be treated with caution when opened?

CA containers are run at reduced oxygen to slow ripening, so they are an enclosed-space hazard. SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 and the related enclosed space entry guidance apply on board.
