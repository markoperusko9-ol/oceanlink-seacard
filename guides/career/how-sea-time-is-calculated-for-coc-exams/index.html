How sea time is calculated for CoC exams

Quick answer

Your sea time is the total of the days between sign-on and sign-off on seagoing ships, taken from your CDC/SIRB and sea service testimonials. STCW counts a month as a calendar month or 30 days made up from shorter periods. Your administration then decides which of those days are approved for the certificate you want. The rules on ship size, engine power, trading area, rank and dry-dock or standby time differ by flag, so check them against your own administration's current rules before you apply.

What 'sea time' actually means to the examiner

When the Mercantile Marine Department (MMD) in India or the MARINA STCW office in the Philippines checks your eligibility, they are not interested in how long you were away from home. They are counting approved seagoing service.

Under STCW Regulation I/1, seagoing service is service on board a ship that is relevant to the certificate you want. A seagoing ship is any ship other than one that only operates in inland waters, sheltered waters or areas covered by port regulations. That gives you three filters before a single day is counted:

  • Was the ship seagoing? Harbour craft, inland vessels and some port-limited units may not count, or may count only towards limited (e.g. near-coastal or NCV) certificates.
  • Was the service relevant? It has to be in the right department and at the right level. Deck time does not count towards an engine CoC, and time as a rating may be treated differently from time as an officer.
  • Is it approved? The administration has to accept the ship, the dates and the documents.

If any one of these fails, those days drop out of your total, however long the contract was.

The STCW minimums you are counting towards

STCW sets the floor, and administrations can add to it. Your national rules may demand more or attach conditions to particular routes. In India these are the Merchant Shipping (STCW) Rules and DG Shipping circulars. In the Philippines they are MARINA circulars.

Certificate STCW minimum seagoing service (summary)
OICNW, 500 GT or more (II/1) 12 months within an approved training programme with onboard training recorded in a TRB, or 36 months otherwise. Either way, at least 6 months of bridge watchkeeping under supervision
OICEW (III/1) Workshop skill training plus 12 months within an approved programme with a TRB, or workshop training plus 36 months, of which at least 30 months in the engine department. Either way, at least 6 months of engine-room watchkeeping under supervision
Chief mate, 3000 GT or more (II/2) At least 12 months as OICNW
Master, 3000 GT or more (II/2) At least 36 months as OICNW, cut to 24 months if at least 12 of them were as chief mate
Second engineer, 3000 kW or more (III/2) At least 12 months as assistant engineer officer or engineer officer
Chief engineer, 3000 kW or more (III/2) At least 36 months of approved seagoing service, of which at least 12 months as an engineer officer in a position of responsibility while qualified to serve as second engineer officer

Two points catch people out:

  • The 6 months of watchkeeping for OICNW and OICEW is a separate requirement inside the total. Twelve months on board does not help you if your TRB does not show supervised watchkeeping.
  • The GT and kW limits matter. Service on smaller ships or lower-powered plant may only qualify you for a limited certificate, depending on your administration's rules.

How the days are added up: step by step

STCW Regulation I/1 defines a month as a calendar month, or 30 days made up of periods of less than one month. In practice most candidates add up their days and divide by 30. Your administration's own method is the one that counts.

  1. List every contract from your CDC/SIRB: ship name, IMO number, flag, GT, kW (for engineers), rank, sign-on date and sign-off date.
  2. Count the days in each contract. Check whether your administration counts both the sign-on and sign-off days (inclusive) or not. A difference of one day per contract adds up over six contracts.
  3. Mark days that may not qualify. These can include dry-dock or lay-up periods, time on a non-seagoing vessel, time in a rank or department that does not match the certificate, and any gap the CDC does not cover.
  4. Add up the qualifying days and convert them to months and days.
  5. Check the sub-requirements, for example 6 months of watchkeeping, or 12 months in a specific rank (e.g. as chief mate for the reduced master route, or as a qualified second engineer for chief engineer).
  6. Match every date against your documents. The CDC/SIRB, the company sea service testimonial and (for cadets) the TRB must all show the same dates and rank.

Never round up. If you are 3 days short, you are short.

Worked scenario: a deck cadet heading for OICNW

A cadet on an approved training programme has three contracts in the CDC, counted inclusively:

Contract Sign-on Sign-off Days
Bulk carrier, 38,000 GT 12 Jan 2023 28 Jul 2023 198
Product tanker, 29,000 GT 03 Oct 2023 19 Mar 2024 169
Container ship, 41,000 GT 20 May 2024 06 Aug 2024 79
Total 446

446 ÷ 30 = 14 months 26 days. That is more than the 12-month STCW minimum for the TRB route.

Now apply the filters:

  • Suppose the tanker spent 25 days in dry dock. If the administration excludes or limits yard time, the total may drop to about 14 months 1 day. That is still enough, but a candidate sitting right on 12 months would now be short.
  • The examiner will also check that the TRB shows at least 6 months of supervised bridge watchkeeping and that the TRB tasks are signed off.
  • If the testimonial for contract 2 gives the sign-off as 21 Mar 2024 but the CDC says 19 Mar, expect a query. Get it corrected before you submit, not after.

The gaps between contracts (Aug–Oct 2023 and Mar–May 2024) are not on board. Leave, travel and time ashore between contracts do not count as seagoing service. Any credit for approved shore training is a separate concession that only some administrations give.

Documents that prove your sea time

The usual evidence is listed below. Each administration sets the exact list, format and verification route, so download the current checklist from the official portal.

  • CDC (India) or SIRB (Philippines) with sign-on/sign-off entries that are clear, stamped and signed.
  • Sea service testimonial or certificate from the company or master. It should give the ship's particulars (name, IMO number, flag, GT, kW), your rank, exact dates and watchkeeping duties. It should be on company letterhead with a signature and stamp. In the Philippines, MARINA requires a notarized company sea service certificate; check the current documentary requirements on stcw.marina.gov.ph.
  • Training Record Book (TRB) for cadets. It must be the approved version, fully completed and countersigned by the master and the shipboard training officer.
  • Watchkeeping certificates or entries, where your administration asks for them separately.
  • INDoS and e-Samudra records for Indian candidates. Applications go through the DG Shipping e-Samudra portal, which includes the Sea Going Service Assessment module.

Indian seafarers apply through the DG Shipping e-Samudra portal (including the Sea Going Service Assessment module), and the MMD checks the service. Check current DGS circulars for the latest portal link. Filipino seafarers deal with the MARINA STCW office. The DMW handles overseas employment and deployment, not CoC sea service assessment. Procedures and online systems change, so confirm the current process on the official website before you apply.

Common reasons sea time is rejected or reduced

From the examining side, these come up again and again:

  • Dates don't match between the CDC/SIRB, the testimonial and the TRB.
  • Missing sign-off entry, or a sign-on/sign-off stamped in a foreign port with no matching record.
  • Wrong rank or department, such as deck rating time put forward towards an engine CoC, or a cadet listed as OS.
  • Ship doesn't meet the GT/kW or trading-area condition for the certificate applied for.
  • Non-seagoing or harbour service offered as foreign-going time.
  • Dry-dock, lay-up or standby periods that the administration excludes or caps.
  • TRB incomplete: tasks unsigned, watchkeeping not recorded, or a version that isn't approved.
  • Testimonials from an unverifiable source, including companies that have closed or agencies that aren't licensed. In India, check that an RPSL agency is genuine before you sail with it.
  • Overlapping contracts, which usually point to a document error and sometimes to forgery. Forged sea service can lead to the certificate being refused, cancelled and worse.

Tips:

  • Keep your own spreadsheet of every contract from your first ship onwards.
  • Ask for a testimonial before you sign off, while the master who knows you is still on board.
  • Scan and back up every page of your CDC/SIRB.

Oral exam angle: what examiners ask about sea time

Sea time comes up at the eligibility stage, and examiners sometimes test it at the orals too:

  • "How does STCW define a month?" A calendar month, or 30 days made up of periods of less than one month.
  • "What watchkeeping is required for OICNW?" At least 6 months of bridge watchkeeping under the supervision of the master or a qualified officer.
  • "How can a master's sea time be reduced from 36 to 24 months?" By serving at least 12 months as chief mate.
  • "How do you revalidate?" Under Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 of the STCW Code, the usual route is at least one year of relevant seagoing service in total over the previous five years. The alternatives are:
    • an approved test
    • an approved course
    • at least three months of service as supernumerary or in a lower rank immediately before taking up the rank

Check which alternatives your administration accepts.

The examiner wants to see that you know your sea time is checked against documents, not taken on trust.

Key points
  • Sea time counts only approved seagoing service relevant to the certificate, not all time away from home.
  • STCW defines a month as a calendar month or 30 days made up of shorter periods.
  • OICNW and OICEW need at least 6 months of supervised watchkeeping inside the total.
  • GT, kW and trading area can limit which certificate your service qualifies for.
  • Dry-dock, lay-up and standby time are treated differently by different administrations, so check yours.
  • Dates on the CDC/SIRB, testimonial and TRB must match exactly.
  • Leave, travel and time ashore between contracts do not count as seagoing service.

Frequently asked questions

Is sea time counted from sign-on to sign-off date?

Yes, the dates in your CDC/SIRB are the basis of the count. Whether both the sign-on and sign-off days are included depends on your administration's method. Periods inside a contract, such as dry dock, may be excluded or limited.

Does dry dock time count as sea time?

It depends on the administration. Some accept it in full, some cap it and some exclude it for certain certificates. Check the current rules or circulars from DG Shipping, MARINA or your flag administration before relying on it.

How many days make a month of sea time?

STCW Regulation I/1 treats a month as a calendar month, or 30 days made up of periods of less than one month. Most candidates add up their days and divide by 30, but your administration's method is the one that counts.

Can sea time as a rating count towards an officer CoC?

It can count towards the longer non-TRB routes, such as the 36-month route for OICNW or OICEW, if it is in the relevant department and approved by your administration. How rating service is credited varies by country, so check the national rules for the ratings-to-officer route.

What if my CDC and company testimonial show different dates?

Expect your application to be queried or held. Ask the company to issue a corrected testimonial that matches the official CDC/SIRB entries before you submit, and keep copies of all correspondence.

Test yourself

Under STCW Regulation I/1, how is a 'month' of seagoing service defined?

STCW Regulation I/1 defines a month as a calendar month or 30 days made up of periods of less than one month.

A cadet on an approved TRB programme has 13 months on board but only 4 months of supervised bridge watchkeeping. Under STCW, are they eligible for OICNW?

STCW Regulation II/1 requires at least six months of bridge watchkeeping under supervision during the required seagoing service, on either route.

How can the 36-month sea time requirement for a master of ships of 3000 GT or more be reduced to 24 months?

STCW Regulation II/2 allows the reduction to not less than 24 months if at least 12 months were served as chief mate.

Which of these periods is NOT counted as seagoing service?

Under STCW Regulation I/1, seagoing service is service on board a ship. Leave between contracts is not on board, so it does not count.

Under STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 of the STCW Code, what is the usual sea service route to revalidating a CoC?

Under STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 of the STCW Code, approved seagoing service performing the certificate's functions for at least one year in total during the preceding five years is accepted.
