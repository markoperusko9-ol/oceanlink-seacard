Approved TAR Book (Training Record Book) for Cadets: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

Your TAR book (also called a Training Record Book or Cadet Record Book) is the official proof that your onboard training was structured and assessed. Under STCW, the shorter sea time route to a watchkeeping CoC depends on an approved training programme documented in an approved record book. In India, your institute will tell you how the completed book is assessed, so treat it like your CDC: keep it current, get it signed properly and never lose it.

What the TAR book is and why STCW cares about it

The Training and Assessment Record (TAR) book, also called the Training Record Book (TRB) or Cadet Record Book (CRB), lists the practical tasks you must do and be assessed on during your shipboard training. When an officer signs a task, they are confirming that you did it and showed you were competent, not just that you watched.

STCW links the shorter route to an approved training programme documented in an approved training record book:

  • Deck (Regulation II/1): at least 12 months of approved seagoing service as part of an approved training programme, documented in an approved training record book. Without that, you need at least 36 months of approved seagoing service.
  • Engine (Regulation III/1): at least 12 months of combined workshop skills training and approved seagoing service within an approved programme, documented in an approved training record book. Without that, you need a much longer combined period, most of which must be seagoing service in the engine department.
  • ETO (Regulation III/6): combined workshop skills training and approved seagoing service of at least 12 months, of which at least 6 months must be seagoing service, within an approved programme documented in an approved training record book.

So the book is not paperwork for its own sake. If it is incomplete or doubtful, the sea time it supports can be questioned. STCW Code Sections B-II/1 and B-III/1 give guidance on how onboard training should be organised, including the roles of the company training officer and the shipboard training officer.

Which book counts as 'approved'

The flag administration that will issue your CoC approves the book. You do not get to choose any record book you like. Ask your institute or sponsoring company which book applies to you before you join.

India. DG Shipping (Directorate General of Shipping) controls shipboard training through training circulars. DGS Training Circular 18 of 2020, 'Shipboard Training for Pre-Sea Courses Leading to CoC/CoP' (dated 24 April 2020), covers shipboard training as part of pre-sea courses that lead to a CoC or CoP. Your institute will tell you how and when your completed TAR book or CRB is assessed. Check the current training circulars on the official DG Shipping website, because the requirements can change. Examples of approved books in use:

  • Institute or company-issued DGS-approved TAR books for deck and engine cadets
  • IMU's DGS-approved Deck Cadet Record Book, with its Activity Work Book and supplements for IMU DNS cadets
  • The NCV deck cadet Cadet Record Book under the META Manual

Philippines. MARINA is the STCW administration. Cadets in maritime degree programmes keep the shipboard training record specified under current MARINA rules and their school's programme. Check the latest STCW circulars on the official MARINA website and confirm with your school and manning agency which book edition is accepted.

Other flags and company schemes. Many companies use a published industry record book that the administration has approved, or their own approved version. If your company hands you a book, ask: approved by which administration, and does it count for the CoC I will apply for?

Who signs what on board

Titles vary by company SMS, but the structure normally looks like this:

Role Usual holder What they do in your book
Company Training Officer (CTO) Shore-based, in the company or manager's office Oversees the programme, reviews progress reports, countersigns where required
Shipboard Training Officer (STO) Often the Chief Officer (deck) or Second Engineer (engine); the company decides Plans your training, checks the book regularly, signs off completed sections
Assessing officer The officer who supervised the task Signs, dates and adds name, rank and (where the book asks) CoC number
Master Master Countersigns sea service and review pages, stamps the book
Institute (India) Your MTI Reviews or assesses the completed book as your approved programme requires (check the current DGS training circulars)

Under IMU's guidelines, cadets give the CRB to the STO on joining and then every week so progress can be checked. Activity work books are inspected monthly and the Master reviews the record book monthly. Check the current IMU circular for the latest version of these guidelines. Whatever your book's scheme, regular reviews by the STO are what make the record believable.

Step by step: from joining to final assessment

  1. Before joining: collect the correct approved book from your institute or company. Check that your name, INDoS/SIRB or other ID details and the book's serial or security features are filled in. Read the instructions pages properly.
  2. On joining: do your safety familiarisation first. Then hand the book to the STO, who will record your joining details and set a training plan.
  3. Every day: keep a rough notebook of what you did. Only ask for a signature when you have done the task yourself and can explain it.
  4. Every week (or as your book or SMS requires): take the book to the STO for review. Ask for tasks to be signed during the watch or job, not saved up.
  5. Monthly: fill in sea service pages, ship particulars and any progress reports the company wants sent ashore.
  6. On change of ship or officer: get every completed task signed by the officer who saw it before they sign off. You cannot get those signatures later.
  7. On signing off: the Master signs and stamps the sea service and final review pages. Make sure the dates match your CDC/SIRB entries and sign-off documents.
  8. Ashore: submit the book, with your sea service evidence, to your institute or the body that assesses it. In India, ask your MTI how the book is assessed and what you need for your course completion certificate and the next stage towards your CoC exam, and check the current DGS training circulars.

Keep a full scan or photo set of every page after each review, stored off the ship.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: the Chief Officer signs off next week. You have done cargo watches and mooring stations with him, but none of it is signed. Go to him now with the book open at the right tasks. If he is not satisfied you can do a task, he will not sign it, and he is right. Ask him to note in the STO review page what is still outstanding so his relief knows where you stand.

Scenario 2: early sign-off for medical reasons. Get the Master to sign the sea service and review pages up to your actual sign-off date, with a clear remark. Do not fill in future dates. Tell your institute and company before you rejoin so they can tell you how the remaining training will be completed and recorded.

Scenario 3: the book is lost or damaged. Report it to the Master, the company and your institute in writing straight away. IMU's guidelines treat loss of the record book as a serious matter. Your scans will help, but whether training can be reconstructed is decided by the institute and administration. Do not assume it can. Never 'recreate' signatures in a new book.

Scenario 4: you were transferred to a different ship type. A record book may have supplements for particular ship types. IMU, for example, uses DGS-approved supplementary work books and additional tasks for special vessel types. Ask the STO whether a supplement applies and whether you need a new one.

Why TAR books get rejected or queried

From the examiner's side, these are the problems that cause trouble at assessment:

  • Batch signing: dozens of tasks signed in the same pen on the last few days of the contract. It looks like the book was filled in after the fact, and it usually was.
  • Signer not on board: a task dated when the signing officer had already signed off, or before he joined. Crew lists will show this up.
  • Incomplete identification: a signature with no printed name, rank, date or ship stamp where the book asks for them.
  • Date mismatch: sea service pages that don't agree with your CDC/SIRB, sign-on/off records or sea service testimonial.
  • Overwriting and whitener: corrections should be struck through once, initialled and dated, never hidden.
  • Wrong or unapproved book: an out-of-date edition, a photocopy, or a book issued to another cadet.
  • Gaps in mandatory sections: for example, hand steering hours, watchkeeping records or STO review pages left blank.
  • Cadet cannot explain signed tasks: if you cannot describe a task signed as competent, the assessor will doubt the whole book.

If your book is queried, answer honestly and bring supporting evidence (sea service testimonial, CDC/SIRB, company letters). Ask the institute exactly what it needs from you.

Oral exam style: what assessors ask about your TAR book

Assessors often open your book at random and ask you to explain a signed task. Practise answering these:

  • Show me the task where you took a position fix by visual bearings. Talk me through how you did it and how you checked it. Explain method, checks and the errors you allowed for, not just the result.
  • Who was your STO and how often did he review your book? Give names, ranks and how the reviews actually worked.
  • How many hours of hand steering did you complete, and in what conditions? Have the hours and the page reference ready.
  • This task is signed on your last day. Why? Answer honestly. If you have a good reason, back it up.
  • Why does STCW require an approved record book for the 12-month route? Because it is the evidence that your sea time was structured, supervised and assessed. That is what justifies the shorter period compared with unstructured service.

For engine cadets, expect the same approach for tasks such as purifier operation, starting and changing over generators, and taking over an engine room watch.

Key points
  • STCW's shorter route to a watchkeeping CoC needs an approved training programme documented in an approved training record book.
  • The flag administration approves the book, so confirm the correct book and edition with your institute or company before joining.
  • In India, shipboard training is covered by DGS Training Circular 18 of 2020. Ask your institute how your completed book is assessed and check the current DGS training circulars.
  • Have tasks signed when you complete them, by the officer who supervised you, with name, rank, date and stamp.
  • Get all outstanding signatures before any officer signs off, because you cannot get them afterwards.
  • Make sure sea service dates in the book match your CDC/SIRB and sign-on/off records.
  • Scan every page regularly and report a lost book in writing straight away.

Frequently asked questions

Is the TAR book the same as a Training Record Book or Cadet Record Book?

Yes, for practical purposes. Different administrations, institutes and publishers call it TAR book, TRB or CRB. What matters is not the name but whether the administration issuing your CoC has approved it.

Can I use any TAR book I buy online?

Not unless your administration and training programme accept it. In India, the book has to fit your DGS-approved training programme and is issued through your institute or company. Philippine cadets should follow MARINA rules and their school's instructions, and check the official MARINA website. Always confirm before you join.

What happens if my TAR book is incomplete when I sign off?

Your sea time may not count towards the 12-month approved programme route until the training is completed and assessed. Tell your institute and company immediately and ask how the outstanding tasks can be completed on your next ship. Do not fill in tasks yourself.

Who is supposed to sign my TAR book?

Usually the officer who supervised and assessed each task signs it. The Shipboard Training Officer reviews the book regularly and the Master countersigns sea service and review pages. Exact roles depend on the book and the company SMS.

Do deck ratings moving towards officer need a training record book?

Rules for ratings moving to officer change. Check the current training circulars on the official DG Shipping website before relying on any pathway. Ask your company or institute which approved record book, if any, applies to you.

Test yourself

Under STCW Regulation II/1, what does recording training in an approved training record book allow a deck candidate to do?

Regulation II/1 allows 12 months of approved seagoing service within an approved programme documented in an approved training record book. Otherwise at least 36 months is required. STCW Regulation II/1.

Which document from India's DG Shipping deals with shipboard training for pre-sea courses leading to a CoC or CoP?

DGS Training Circular 18 of 2020, 'Shipboard Training for Pre-Sea Courses Leading to CoC/CoP' (24 April 2020), covers shipboard training within pre-sea courses. Check the DG Shipping Training Circulars page for current requirements.

Your Chief Officer, who supervised your cargo watches, signs off in three days. What should you do?

Only the officer who supervised and assessed a task should sign it, while he is on board. This is consistent with STCW Code guidance on supervised onboard training (B-II/1).

Which entry is most likely to make an assessor doubt your book?

Batch signing at the end of a contract suggests the tasks were not assessed as they were done. This is consistent with STCW Code guidance on supervised onboard training (B-II/1).

Who approves the training record book that supports your CoC application?

STCW requires an approved training record book, and approval comes from the administration issuing the certificate. In India that is DG Shipping under its training circulars. STCW Regulations II/1 and III/1.
