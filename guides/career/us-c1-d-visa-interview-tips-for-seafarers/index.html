US C1/D visa interview tips for seafarers

Quick answer

The C1/D is a combined transit (C-1) and crewmember (D) visa. It lets you travel through the US to join a ship and work aboard while she is in US waters. You fill in the DS-160 online, book through your country's official scheduling portal and attend an in-person interview. There the officer checks that you are a genuine seafarer who will leave on a vessel. Most refusals come from paperwork that doesn't match or from vague answers, not from your rank or experience.

What the C1/D actually covers (and what it doesn't)

Get this clear before the interview, because officers sometimes ask about it.

  • D (crewmember): for people working aboard a commercial sea vessel who provide services needed for her normal operation and intend to leave the US on the same or another vessel. A D-visa holder must depart on a vessel within 29 days. Crew joining a foreign-trade vessel engaged in lightering (ship-to-ship transfer of liquid cargo) may be admitted for up to 180 days.
  • C-1 (transit): needed if you fly into the US as a passenger to meet and board your ship. If you apply for it together with the D, it is usually issued as one combined C-1/D visa. This depends on the reciprocity schedule for your nationality and on the officer's decision.
  • What it is not: it is not a visitor visa, a business visa or a visa for shore jobs. It does not cover tourism, a course ashore or visiting relatives. Activities outside crew duties, such as tourism or business ashore, need a visitor (B-1/B-2) visa. Check your embassy's crew-visa page for how it handles this.

At a US port, US Customs and Border Protection decides whether you get shore leave. The visa only lets you ask to be admitted. Shore leave is never guaranteed, even with a valid visa.

The D visa is generally not available if your vessel trades only on the US coast or is US-based, or if you will work on a fishing vessel operating from a US port. Ask your company and check the State Department Crewmember Visa page for the full list of exclusions before applying.

Step-by-step: from DS-160 to interview

The process is broadly the same at every US post. However, each post sets its own scheduling portal, fee payment method and document list, so always follow your own embassy's website.

  1. Check your passport. It must be valid for travel to the US. It generally needs to be valid for at least six months beyond your intended stay, unless an agreement with your country exempts you. Keep at least one blank page for the visa.
  2. Complete the DS-160 on the State Department's Consular Electronic Application Center (ceac.state.gov). Choose the crewmember (C-1/D) purpose and upload a photo that meets State Department rules. Save the application ID and print the confirmation page with the barcode.
  3. Pay the application fee in the way your post specifies. Fees and any reciprocity issuance fee change, so check the official fee pages and your nationality's reciprocity table on travel.state.gov. Don't trust figures on agent websites.
  4. Book the appointment on the official scheduling site for your country. The US Embassy in India accepts appointment bookings only online (not by email). Use the scheduling link on in.usembassy.gov/nonimmigrant-visas/. Filipino seafarers should use the scheduling link on the US Embassy in Manila website.
  5. Give your fingerprints and photo (biometrics), if your post uses a separate visit to a visa application centre. Then attend the consular interview.
  6. Get your passport back, or wait for further processing. If you are approved, your passport comes back with the visa. If the officer needs more information, you may get a refusal under section 221(g), with a request for documents or a note that your case needs more checks (administrative processing).

Two recent changes to know:

  • Since 2 September 2025, the State Department has generally required an in-person interview for nonimmigrant visa applicants, with only narrow exceptions. Many seafarers who renewed by the old 'dropbox' route (no interview) now have to attend in person. Check your post's page to see whether any interview waiver applies to you.
  • Since 6 September 2025, applicants have been expected to apply in their country of nationality or residence. Booking a quicker slot in another country usually means much longer waits and may make it harder to qualify.

Documents to carry to the interview

Exact lists vary by post, but expect to need the following:

Document Why it matters
Valid passport, plus any old passports with US visas Identity and travel history
DS-160 confirmation page with barcode Links you to your application
Appointment confirmation and fee receipt Proof of booking and payment
Photo, if your upload failed or your post asks for one Must meet State Department photo rules
Employer letter on company letterhead Confirms your employment, rank, vessel, purpose of travel and expected period in the US
Employment contract (SEA) or crewing agency letter Shows a real job waiting for you
CDC (Seaman's Book) or SIRB Your sea-going history
CoC and key STCW certificates Proves you are qualified for the rank you state

Keep the originals in one folder, in the order above. The officer may ask only for your passport, or may ask for the employer letter first. Don't hand over a stack of papers unless asked.

The employer letter decides most crew interviews. Check that these match your DS-160 and contract:

  • your name, exactly as in the passport
  • your rank
  • the vessel name, and the IMO number if given
  • the joining port

Interview practice: questions officers ask

Treat it like an oral exam: give short, true, specific answers. The officer has a minute or two and applies section 214(b) of US immigration law. This assumes every applicant intends to stay in the US unless they show otherwise. Your job is to show that you are a working seafarer who will go back to sea and home.

Typical questions and what a good answer looks like:

  • Why are you going to the US? "To join MV [name] at Houston as Third Officer. My company is [name] and the manning agent is [name]."
  • What type of vessel is it, and what do you do on board? Name the vessel type and describe two or three real duties of your rank. A Third Officer who says only "navigation" sounds rehearsed.
  • How long will you be in the US? "Only until the vessel sails. I'm going straight from the airport to the ship."
  • How many contracts have you done? What was your last vessel? Know your CDC entries and dates without looking.
  • Have you been to the US before? Did you take shore leave? Answer honestly, because your entries are on record.
  • Who pays for your travel? Usually the company, so say so.
  • Where is your family? What do you do between contracts? Show your ties to home, such as family, property, or studying for your next CoC.

Do: answer in your own words and in plain English, and stop when you have answered the question. If you don't understand a question, ask the officer to repeat it.

Don't: recite a memorised script, argue, guess, or offer documents that weren't asked for.

Common reasons for refusal

  • No clear job or vessel. You can apply for a crewmember visa without being employed at the time. However, freshers, and seafarers between companies who have no company letter, often struggle to show what the visa is for.
  • Information that doesn't match. For example, the DS-160 names one company and the letter another, dates or rank differ, or a past refusal wasn't declared.
  • Weak answers about your own job. Officers notice when a candidate can't describe the duties of their rank or name their last vessel.
  • Wrong visa category. For example, using a crew visa for tourism, training ashore or a shore job.
  • Previous immigration problems. These include overstaying, deserting a ship, or giving false information in any country's application.
  • Doubtful documents. Officers quickly spot fake letters from unknown 'agents'. A finding of fraud or false information can make you ineligible for a long time, which is far worse than a simple refusal.

A 214(b) refusal is not permanent. You can reapply once your circumstances or evidence have genuinely changed, for example when you have a confirmed contract. If you reapply the next week with the same papers, you will usually get the same result.

A 221(g) slip means your case is still pending. Follow the instructions on it exactly.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: fresher deck cadet. A company-sponsored cadet is joining his first ship at a US port. He should carry the sponsorship or joining letter, his training record book (TAR book), CDC and basic STCW certificates. He should be ready to say what a cadet does on board and who his training officer will be. Clear company backing is his strongest evidence.

Scenario 2: renewing an old visa. An AB wants to renew his expiring C1/D before his next contract. Under current rules he should expect an in-person interview in his own country, so he should book early. He brings his old passport so the officer can see he used the previous visa properly.

Scenario 3: previous refusal. An oiler was refused under 214(b) two years ago while he was between jobs. He now has a contract with a known ship manager. He answers "Yes" to the previous-refusal question on the DS-160 and, if asked, explains briefly what has changed. Hiding a refusal does far more damage than the refusal itself.

Scenario 4: an agency asks for extra money for a 'guaranteed visa'. Walk away. Nobody can guarantee a US visa, because only the consular officer decides. Report the agency to your national authority.

Practical tips from the examiner's side of the table

  • Fill in the DS-160 yourself. If the agency fills it in, check every line. You sign it electronically and you answer for what it says.
  • Give complete and truthful contact and social media details. The form asks for them.
  • Know your contract: vessel name, flag, type, joining port, manning agent and roughly how long the contract runs.
  • Dress as you would for a company interview. Arrive early with your folder ready.
  • Speak for yourself. Agents can't come to the window with you.
  • Don't book flights until the visa is issued. The State Department warns against making final travel plans before then.
  • When your passport comes back, check the spelling, visa type (C1/D) and validity on the visa straight away.
  • Keep a scan of the visa and the DS-160 with your sign-on documents.
Key points
  • The C1/D combines a transit visa to reach the ship with a crewmember visa to work aboard. It is not for tourism or shore jobs.
  • A D-visa holder must leave the US on a vessel within 29 days, and US Customs and Border Protection decides on shore leave at arrival.
  • Since 2 September 2025, most applicants have needed an in-person interview. Check your post for any waiver.
  • Apply in your country of nationality or residence. Appointments in another country usually mean much longer waits and may make it harder to qualify.
  • Most crew interviews are won by a clear employer letter and a DS-160 that matches it exactly.
  • You can overcome a 214(b) refusal by reapplying once your circumstances have genuinely changed, never by hiding the refusal.
  • Fees, portals and document lists change, so check your US embassy website and travel.state.gov, not agent sites.

Frequently asked questions

Can I apply for a C1/D visa without a job on a ship?

Yes. The State Department lets you apply for a crewmember visa without being employed at the time. However, you still have to convince the officer that you are a genuine seafarer who will use the visa only to join a vessel. That is much harder without a company letter, so most seafarers apply once they have a contract or joining letter.

Can I still renew my C1/D by dropbox without an interview?

For most applicants, no. Since 2 September 2025 the State Department has generally required in-person interviews for nonimmigrant visas, with only narrow exceptions. Officers can also call anyone for an interview. Check your US embassy's visa page for the current rules before booking.

How long can I stay in the US on a C1/D visa?

A crewmember (D) visa holder must leave on a vessel within 29 days. Crew joining a vessel engaged in lightering may be allowed up to 180 days. The validity printed on your visa is not the same as your permitted stay, which US Customs and Border Protection decides when you enter.

Can I use my C1/D visa to visit the US as a tourist?

No. The C1/D is only for travelling through the US to join a vessel and for working aboard as crew. Activities outside crew duties, such as tourism or business ashore, need a visitor (B-1/B-2) visa. Check your embassy's crew-visa page for how it handles this.

My C1/D was refused under 214(b). When can I reapply?

There is no fixed waiting period, but reapplying only makes sense once something important has changed, such as a confirmed contract or a new employer letter. Declare the earlier refusal truthfully on the new DS-160. A 221(g) refusal is different: it means your case is still pending, so follow the instructions you were given.

Test yourself

A seafarer flies into Los Angeles as a passenger to join his ship. Which visa does he need?

Travelling to meet and board a vessel needs a transit (C-1) visa as well as the D, usually issued as a combined C-1/D (State Department, Crewmember Visa page).

Within what period must a D-visa holder who is not on lightering duties leave the US on a vessel?

D-visa crew who are not on lightering duties must leave on a vessel within 29 days; crew joining a lightering vessel may be allowed up to 180 days (State Department).

What is the main legal test the officer applies in a typical C1/D interview?

Section 214(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act assumes applicants intend to stay in the US unless they show otherwise. Rank and past US visits only support your case.

Since 6 September 2025, where should an Indian seafarer normally apply for a C1/D visa?

The State Department now expects nonimmigrant applicants to apply in their country of nationality or residence. Applying elsewhere usually means much longer waits and may make it harder to qualify (State Department).

A crewing agent offers a 'guaranteed' US crew visa for an extra payment. Which statement is correct?

Only the consular officer decides whether to issue a visa, and the State Department warns there is no guarantee one will be issued (State Department, Crewmember Visa page).
