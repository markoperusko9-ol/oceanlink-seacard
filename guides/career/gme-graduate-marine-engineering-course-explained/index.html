GME (Graduate Marine Engineering) course explained

Quick answer

Graduate Marine Engineering (GME) is a one-year pre-sea conversion course, approved by India's Directorate General of Shipping (DGS), for engineers who already hold a mechanical or naval architecture degree. After the course and the required documented shipboard training as a trainee or engine cadet, you can sit the MEO Class IV (Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch) examination. Your degree stream decides whether you are exempt from MEO Part A subjects. Age limits, structure and fees vary by institute, so check the current DGS requirements and the institute's own notice.

What GME actually is

GME is a pre-sea conversion course. It takes someone who already has an engineering degree and teaches them what a ship's engine room needs: marine diesel engines, auxiliaries, boilers, marine electrical, refrigeration, ship construction, safety and a lot of workshop time. Each institute runs it under a DGS approval, and the syllabus is built on the STCW requirements for an Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch (Regulation III/1).

The course is about one year long, but approved institutes don't all split it the same way:

  • Some run it as two semesters ashore (roughly 12 months in the institute).
  • Some run it as about 8 months ashore plus 4 months of onboard training.
  • The Shipping Corporation of India's training institute (SCI-MTI) advertises 8 months ashore followed by 10 months (4+6) of structured shipboard training on SCI ships. SCI's notices have restricted applications to unmarried candidates and set their own conditions, so read the current SCI-MTI notice.

You don't get a degree from GME. What you get is a DGS-recognised pre-sea training certificate. Together with documented sea service, that certificate makes you eligible for the MEO Class IV Certificate of Competency.

It is the fastest way to sea for a mechanical engineer. The four-year B.Tech Marine Engineering route builds sea training into the degree instead.

Who is eligible

DGS sets the admission criteria. They are now consolidated in the DGS Training and Assessment Programme (TEAP) Part A, Table III/1-3, together with later eligibility circulars such as Training Circular 12 of 2020. TC 5 of 2012 and the letter on Mechanical and Automation degrees are the original sources. Institutes then publish their own entry conditions, which can be stricter. Typical conditions are:

Requirement What institutes commonly ask for
Degree Full-time BE/B.Tech in Mechanical Engineering, a stream where "Mechanical" comes first (e.g. Mechanical and Automation), or Naval Architecture. Some admit non-DGS-approved BE/B.Tech Marine Engineering
Marks Minimum 50% in the final year of the degree (some institutes ask for more)
College AICTE-approved institute, an IIT, or a college run directly by a university
English Minimum 50% in English at 10th, 12th or degree level
Age Commonly a maximum of 28 on the course start date. Some institutes allow category relaxations, so check the notice. Some private institutes set lower limits, so read the actual notice
Medical Fit for sea service, certified by a DGS-approved medical examiner, including eyesight and colour vision standards
Passport Usually needed at admission or before joining a ship

The stream wording matters. If the degree title reads "Automobile and Mechanical" instead of "Mechanical and Automobile", it may not qualify. Check this with the institute, and if it's unclear, with DGS, before you pay anything.

Degree stream and MEO Part A exemption

The MEO exams have Part A (engineering science subjects) and Part B (marine engineering subjects plus orals). Your degree decides how much of Part A you can skip.

  • Pure Mechanical Engineering (and, at some institutes, non-DGS-approved Marine Engineering): institutes generally describe these candidates as exempt from Part A of both MEO Class IV and Class II.
  • Combination streams (Mechanical-first) and Naval Architecture: generally must pass the Part A subjects of Class IV and Class II.

The exemption rules come from DGS and can be revised. Before you build your study plan around an exemption, confirm it in the latest DGS requirements or with the Mercantile Marine Department (MMD).

Step-by-step: from degree to engine room

  1. Shortlist DGS-approved institutes. Check that the institute and the specific GME batch are approved on the DG Shipping website. Ask whether they offer company sponsorship or an assured onboard training berth.
  2. Apply under the institute's own process. GME admission is not one national counselling. Each institute or sponsoring company runs its own written test, interview and medical. Some sponsors (e.g. SCI-MTI) run their own recruitment drives with their own conditions.
  3. Pass the pre-sea medical with a DGS-approved doctor before final admission.
  4. Get your INDoS number. It is normally generated through the institute, and you need it for every STCW course and certificate.
  5. Complete the shore phase. That means theory, workshop, simulator, the in-house exams and the mandatory STCW basic safety courses (whether these are bundled into the course depends on the institute).
  6. Apply for your CDC once the institute or sponsor issues the documents, so you can sign on.
  7. Do your onboard training as trainee marine engineer or engine cadet, and fill in the approved training record book honestly and on time. How long it lasts depends on the scheme you join. Commonly it is around six months of documented sea service after the course, while 8+4 and 8+10 schemes count onboard months differently.
  8. Complete the post-sea requirements for MEO Class IV: the advanced STCW courses (AFF, PSCRB, MFA) and any other course on the current DGS/MMD Class IV checklist (many checklists also include an Engine Room Simulator course). Check the current MMD/DGS e-governance requirements. A preparatory course is optional for most candidates.
  9. Appear for MEO Class IV Part B (written and oral) through the MMD and DGS exam system. Once you pass, you can sail as a junior engineering officer.

Worked scenario: Rahul has a B.Tech in Mechanical and Automation with 58% in the final year, and he turns 27 two months before the batch starts. He is eligible on stream, marks and age. But he should expect to clear Part A subjects, and he should check whether his institute's batch includes the onboard months or whether he must find his own berth.

Documents to keep ready

  • Degree certificate and all semester mark sheets (final-year percentage clearly shown)
  • Proof that the college was AICTE-approved, an IIT, or university-run, for the years you studied
  • 10th and 12th mark sheets (for English marks and date of birth)
  • Category certificate, if the institute allows age relaxation and you are claiming it
  • Valid passport
  • Medical fitness certificate from a DGS-approved examiner, in the approved format
  • Photographs, Aadhaar/ID proof, and any sponsorship or offer letter

Later you will add your INDoS number, CDC, STCW course certificates and the completed training record book. Keep scanned copies in one folder, because the MMD will ask for originals at the exam stage.

Common rejections and how to avoid them

  • Wrong stream wording on the degree. "Mechanical" not first, or a non-engineering degree. Get confirmation in writing before you pay.
  • Part-time, distance or unrecognised degree. Most notices ask for a full-time degree from a recognised college.
  • Final-year marks below the cut-off. The aggregate may be fine while the final year falls short, or the other way round. Read which one the notice uses.
  • Over the age limit on the commencement date. Age is counted on the date the institute states, not the date you apply.
  • Medical failure. Eyesight or colour vision is the usual reason. Get a check from an approved doctor before you spend on applications.
  • Not meeting a sponsor's own conditions. Sponsors such as SCI set extra conditions (SCI's notices have been limited to unmarried candidates). Read the current notice in full.
  • Unapproved institute or batch. If the institute or batch isn't approved, your sea time and exam eligibility can be refused later.
  • Fake placement promises. Agents who promise a guaranteed ship for a fee are a known scam. A legitimate sponsor recruits through its own published process.

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask GME candidates

GME candidates often know their thermodynamics well and their real machinery badly. At the Class IV oral, examiners look for engine-room judgement, not textbook recitation. Expect things like:

  • "Walk me through your engine-room rounds." Levels, temperatures, pressures, leaks, bilges, unusual noises, log entries, and when you call the 2nd Engineer.
  • "Purifier overflowing. What do you do?" Explain the likely causes (seal water lost, wrong gravity disc, temperature) and stopping safely before you go to the textbook theory.
  • "How do you know the OWS is working?" The 15 ppm alarm, the three-way valve, and Oil Record Book entries.
  • "Scavenge fire indications and first actions."
  • "What did you personally overhaul?" They will test your record book entries, so only write what you actually did.

Tip: on board, trace every system on the drawings and then physically along the pipes. That is what carries you through the orals.

Tips before you commit

  • Prefer sponsored seats or batches with assured onboard training. Finding your own first ship as an unsponsored cadet can take a long time. Check each sponsor's own conditions (for example, SCI's notices have restricted applications to unmarried candidates).
  • Read the latest DGS requirements. Course structure, multidisciplinary modules and exemption rules have changed over the years, and the eligibility criteria are now consolidated in TEAP Part A.
  • Treat fees and stipends as institute-specific. Get them in writing from the institute's official notice, never from an agent.
  • Start Part A preparation early if your stream doesn't give you the exemption.
  • Keep your training record book current. Gaps or back-filled entries can create problems when you apply for the exam.
Key points
  • GME is a DGS-approved, roughly one-year pre-sea course for mechanical and naval architecture graduates.
  • Typical entry: full-time degree with 50% in the final year, 50% in English, and an age limit set in the institute's notice (commonly 28; any relaxations depend on the institute).
  • Mechanical-first streams qualify, but the degree title wording matters.
  • Pure mechanical graduates generally get MEO Part A exemption; combination streams and naval architects generally must pass Part A.
  • Structure varies: about a year ashore, 8 months ashore plus 4 onboard, or sponsor-specific schemes.
  • Documented onboard training and the post-sea courses on the current DGS/MMD checklist come before MEO Class IV Part B.
  • Verify institute and batch approval on the DG Shipping site, and never pay agents for guaranteed ships.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join GME with a B.Tech in Mechanical and Automation?

Generally yes, as long as "Mechanical" is the first word of the stream and you meet the marks and age conditions. DGS has issued a specific letter on Mechanical and Automation degrees, and the criteria are now consolidated in TEAP Part A. You will usually have to pass MEO Part A subjects rather than get the full exemption, so confirm with the institute.

What is the age limit for GME?

Many institutes follow a maximum of 28 years on the course start date. Some institutes allow category relaxations, and some private institutes publish lower limits. Always go by the official admission notice of the institute you apply to.

Is GME admission through IMU CET?

GME admission is generally run by each DGS-approved institute or sponsoring company through its own test, interview and medical. Some institutes are IMU-affiliated, so check the specific institute's admission notice for its process.

How long after GME can I become a 4th Engineer?

After the course you need the required documented onboard training, the post-sea courses on the current DGS/MMD checklist, and a pass in MEO Class IV Part B. How long that takes depends on your scheme and how quickly you get a ship. Exam scheduling at the MMD also varies.

Is GME better than B.Tech Marine Engineering?

They suit different people. If you already hold a mechanical degree, GME gets you to sea quickly. If you haven't started a degree yet, B.Tech Marine Engineering builds sea training into the degree itself. Your career progression after Class IV is the same through the MEO grades.

Test yourself

Which degree stream wording is most likely to meet the GME eligibility rule?

DGS criteria require "Mechanical" to be the core and first subject of a full-time BE/B.Tech. Diplomas and B.Sc degrees don't qualify (DGS TEAP A, Table III/1-3 (originally TC 5 of 2012)).

Pure mechanical graduates are generally exempt from Part A. Part B, orals and sea service still apply (DGS GME admission criteria, TEAP A, Table III/1-3).

On what date is the GME age limit usually checked?

Institute notices commonly state the age limit as on the date the course commences, or a reference date they specify.

Which of these is normally completed AFTER sea service and before MEO Class IV Part B?

The advanced STCW courses (AFF, PSCRB, MFA and any other course on the current DGS/MMD checklist) are done after sea time and before Part B. INDoS is generated at the pre-sea stage (DGS requirements).

What is the safest way to confirm a GME institute is genuine?

Only training from DGS-approved institutes and batches counts towards certification. Agents' letters and advertising prove nothing.
