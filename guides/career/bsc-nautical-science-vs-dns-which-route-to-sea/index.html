B.Sc Nautical Science vs DNS: which route to sea

Quick answer

Both routes lead to the same Second Mate (FG) Certificate of Competency from DG Shipping. With DNS you spend one year ashore and then 18 months training on board, and you get the IMU degree only after passing the IMU Semester III–V exams and obtaining your CoC. With B.Sc Nautical Science you spend three years ashore and then 12 months on board, and you hold a degree once you pass all semesters, before you ever sail. For most candidates the deciding factor is not the course but whether a shipping company has sponsored them for an onboard berth.

The two routes side by side

Both courses are pre-sea deck training approved by the Directorate General of Shipping (DGS). Both end with the same Second Mate (Foreign-Going) CoC. What differs is how the time is split between college and ship, and when you get your degree.

DNS (leading to B.Sc) B.Sc Nautical Science
Time ashore before sailing 1 year (Semesters I–II) 3 years
Structured shipboard training (SSTP) 18 months as deck cadet 12 months as deck cadet
Award after the shore phase Diploma in Nautical Science (IMU) B.Sc degree
Award after sea time Advanced Diploma in Nautical Science (IMU), after passing the IMU Semester III–V exams –
Degree in hand When you have passed the IMU Semester III–V exams and obtained your Second Mate CoC When you pass all semesters of the 3-year course
Before the CoC exam 4-month Second Mate Function course (Semester VI), then DGS exams Second Mate (FG) exams after the required STCW/modular courses. The 4-month Function course is required for DNS, and B.Sc graduates are generally exempt (confirm the current rule on dgshipping.gov.in)

The international basis is STCW Regulation II/1. It allows a shorter qualifying sea service when the sea time is part of an approved training programme recorded in an approved Training Record Book. India's 12-month and 18-month figures are national requirements built on top of that rule. Other administrations set their own.

The DNS degree title depends on the institute and scheme. Some institutes award a B.Sc in Applied Nautical Science, others a B.Sc in Nautical Science. Check the exact title in the prospectus.

Eligibility and admission: what both routes share

IMU publishes these baseline criteria. Institutes may add to them.

  • Academics: 10+2 with an average of at least 60% in Physics, Chemistry and Maths. For B.Sc Nautical Science, IMU also requires at least 50% in English in Class 10 or 12. Some DNS schemes have in the past accepted B.Sc (PCM) or engineering graduates, sometimes with a higher age limit. Check the current prospectus of each institute. IMU allows relaxation in the PCM percentage for reserved categories. Check the exact figure in the current IMU notification.
  • IMU CET: IMU requires all candidates in every category to appear for and pass the Common Entrance Test. Many sponsoring companies add their own written test, psychometric test and interview after that.
  • Age: IMU sets maximum ages that vary by category and gender, with extra relaxation for female candidates. These limits are counted from a reference date in each year's notification. Check the current IMU admissions page rather than copying last year's numbers.
  • Marital status: IMU's brochure states that only unmarried candidates are eligible for DNS and B.Sc (Nautical Science). Check the current year's notification.
  • Medical fitness: You need a pre-sea medical from a DGS-approved doctor to DGS standards. Eyesight and colour vision are where deck candidates most often fail.
  • Indian passport and INDoS: SCI's MTI states that it will not admit a DNS candidate without a valid Indian passport, because the INDoS number is generated against it. Apply for your passport early.

Filipino readers: none of this applies to you. Your route is the BS Marine Transportation degree, regulated by CHED and MARINA, with its own shipboard training year. Check MARINA's current issuances.

Sponsorship: the factor that really decides it

Neither certificate lets you skip sea time, and SSTP sea time only counts on a ship where a company has placed you as a cadet. That makes the onboard berth the real bottleneck, whichever course you choose.

  • DNS seats are usually tied to a company sponsorship. You are selected by the company, or by an institute that has a placement arrangement, and that company sends you to sea for the 18 months. SCI's MTI, for example, runs the 18 months on SCI-operated ships.
  • B.Sc Nautical Science at some colleges comes with a sponsoring company and at others does not. If yours does not, you may finish three years and then have to find a cadet berth yourself. That is where many graduates get stuck, and where fake agents target them.

Before you pay anything, ask for these in writing:

  1. Is onboard training guaranteed, and which company provides it?
  2. Which vessel types, and roughly when after the shore phase?
  3. Is there a bond or service agreement, and what happens if you break it?
  4. Is the institute and this exact course on the DGS approved list for the current batch?

Check the approval yourself on the DG Shipping website (dgshipping.gov.in). Do not rely on a brochure.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Arjun, 17, strong PCM, wants to be at sea quickly. He clears IMU CET and gets a company-sponsored DNS seat. One year ashore, 18 months at sea, the IMU Semester III–V exams, the 4-month Second Mate Function course, then the DGS exams. He reaches his CoC sooner than through the B.Sc route, and the degree comes with it as long as he has passed his IMU semester exams. The risk is that if he leaves the sea before the CoC, he holds a diploma and possibly an advanced diploma, not a degree.

Scenario 2: Priya, 18, unsure whether sea life suits her. She takes a B.Sc Nautical Science that comes with confirmed sponsorship. Once she passes all six semesters she holds a degree whatever happens next. She then needs only 12 months of SSTP. It takes longer overall, but she has a safety net if she decides against sailing.

Scenario 3: Rahul, B.Sc Physics graduate, aged 22. Starting a three-year degree again makes little sense for him. If he can find a DNS scheme that accepts graduates in the current year, and he is within its age limit, that is the logical choice. He should check each institute's current prospectus.

Scenario 4: Neha is offered a B.Sc seat with no sponsorship, at a college her family found through an agent. These are warning signs. She should check DGS approval, ask the college for its placement record by name of company, and walk away if the answers are vague.

Step by step: from admission to Second Mate CoC

  1. Check eligibility against the current IMU notification and the institute's prospectus, including age, marital status and academic criteria.
  2. Get a DGS-approved pre-sea medical early, so you don't pay fees and then fail on eyesight.
  3. Apply for an Indian passport if you don't have one.
  4. Register for and pass IMU CET, then any company or institute test and interview.
  5. Join the DGS-approved institute. Your INDoS number is generated, and the mandatory basic safety courses are normally done during pre-sea training.
  6. Complete the shore phase: one year for DNS, three years for B.Sc.
  7. Sail as a deck cadet under SSTP, keeping your Training Record Book signed and current. IMU's SSTP guidelines for DNS include a mid-term review by the pre-sea institute during the 18 months.
  8. Get your sea-time completion letter from the company. Have your TRB verified as your institute directs.
  9. DNS cadets: get the SSTP completion certificate from your institute, then register for and pass the IMU Semester III, IV and V examinations under the distance learning programme. IMU sets a maximum time limit for these, so check the current IMU circular.
  10. DNS cadets: complete the 4-month Second Mate Function course (Semester VI). B.Sc graduates: check whether DGS currently exempts you. Everyone completes the STCW courses DGS lists for the CoC.
  11. Pass the DGS written examination and orals to receive your Second Mate (FG) CoC. On the DNS route, IMU awards your degree at this stage, provided you have also passed the IMU Semester III–V exams.

Common rejections and how to avoid them

  • Failing the medical on eyesight or colour vision after paying. Get the medical done first.
  • Over the age limit on the reference date. The cut-off is a fixed date in the notification, not your application date.
  • Marital status. IMU's brochure states that only unmarried candidates are eligible for DNS and B.Sc (Nautical Science). Check the current year's notification before you apply.
  • English below 50% in both Class 10 and 12, where the course requires it.
  • No passport at admission, which blocks your INDoS number.
  • Unapproved course or institute, or a course run without approval for that batch. Your sea time and exam eligibility can be refused.
  • Incomplete or unsigned TRB. At the CoC stage, a missing task sign-off or a gap in your sea-time records will hold you up longer than any written paper.
  • IMU Semester III–V exams not passed in time on the DNS route. This delays the Advanced Diploma and the degree. Check IMU's current time limits.
  • Sea time on a ship that does not qualify under SSTP, often arranged through an unregistered agent. Only use RPSL-registered agencies or the sponsoring company itself.

Interview corner: what sponsors and examiners ask

Sponsorship panels and, later, MMD oral examiners want to see that you understand the route you have chosen. Typical questions:

  • Why DNS and not B.Sc, or the other way round? Give a real answer: time to CoC, having a degree as a safety net, or sponsorship. "My friend did it" is not an answer.
  • What is the TRB for, and who signs it? It is the approved record of the onboard tasks you have shown you can do. Officers sign the tasks, and the master countersigns as required.
  • What does STCW Regulation II/1 require for an OOW? The requirements are:
    • a minimum age of 18
    • approved seagoing service of at least 12 months within an approved training programme, with onboard training documented in an approved TRB (otherwise 36 months)
    • at least six months of that service spent on bridge watchkeeping duties under supervision
    • education and training meeting Section A-II/1
    • basic training under Regulation VI/1
    • medical fitness
  • What will you do if the cadet berth is delayed? Show patience and keep studying. Don't say you would take any ship an agent offers.

On board, the master will expect the same thing on either route: a cadet who keeps a proper lookout, writes up the TRB without being chased, and asks questions.

Key points
  • Both DNS and B.Sc Nautical Science lead to the same DGS Second Mate (FG) CoC.
  • DNS means 1 year ashore plus 18 months of SSTP; B.Sc means 3 years ashore plus 12 months of SSTP.
  • On the DNS route the IMU degree comes with the CoC and only after you pass the IMU Semester III–V exams, so if you stop early you have no degree.
  • IMU CET, at least 60% PCM, unmarried status, a DGS-approved medical and an Indian passport are the common gateways.
  • A guaranteed sponsored cadet berth matters more than which course you choose.
  • Check the institute and course approval on dgshipping.gov.in and the age rules on the current IMU notification.
  • DNS cadets must do the 4-month Second Mate Function course before the DGS exams; B.Sc graduates are generally exempt (check DGS).

Frequently asked questions

Is DNS better than B.Sc Nautical Science?

Neither is better; both lead to the same Second Mate CoC. DNS gets you to the CoC sooner but needs 18 months of onboard training. B.Sc takes longer ashore but needs 12 months at sea and gives you a degree before you sail.

Do I get a degree after DNS?

Yes, but not straight away. You get the Diploma after the shore year and the Advanced Diploma after your sea time and the IMU Semester III–V exams. The IMU B.Sc (named Nautical Science or Applied Nautical Science depending on the scheme) comes when you obtain your Second Mate CoC, provided you have also passed the IMU Semester III–V exams.

Is IMU CET compulsory for DNS and B.Sc Nautical Science?

IMU's admission notification says candidates in all categories must appear for and pass the CET. Many sponsoring companies run their own test and interview as well. Check the current IMU admissions page for the year's rules.

Can a B.Sc Physics graduate join DNS?

Some DNS schemes have in the past accepted B.Sc (PCM) or engineering graduates, sometimes with a higher age limit. You must still be within the age limit and pass CET and the medical. Check the current prospectus of each institute.

What if I finish B.Sc Nautical Science without sponsorship?

You will need to find a cadet berth with a company that runs SSTP, because only approved onboard training counts towards the CoC. Approach companies directly or through RPSL-registered agencies only. Avoid anyone who asks for money in return for a ship.

Test yourself

How long is the structured shipboard training for a DNS cadet before the Second Mate exam route?

The IMU/DGS DNS scheme is 12 months of pre-sea training ashore followed by 18 months of SSTP on board (IMU SSTP guidelines, Circular 1715).

When does a DNS cadet receive the IMU B.Sc degree?

The Diploma comes after the shore year and the Advanced Diploma after sea time and the IMU Semester III–V exams. The degree is awarded with the Second Mate CoC, provided those exams are passed (IMU DNS scheme).

Which STCW regulation sets the minimum requirements for an officer in charge of a navigational watch?

STCW Regulation II/1 covers OOW certification on ships of 500 GT or more. II/2 is for master and chief mate, VI/1 is basic safety training and III/1 is for engineer officers.

Why does SCI's MTI require a valid Indian passport at DNS admission?

MTI's DNS admission document states that admission is refused without a passport because the INDoS number is created from it.

A B.Sc Nautical Science graduate needs how much SSTP sea service before appearing for the Second Mate (FG) CoC exams?

B.Sc Nautical Science graduates need 12 months of SSTP sea service; DNS cadets need 18 months.
