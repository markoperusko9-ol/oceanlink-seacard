Merchant navy medical fitness test in India: using DG-approved doctors

Quick answer

In India, your seafarer medical fitness certificate must come from a medical examiner approved by the Directorate, and the examiner uploads it online against your INDoS number. Under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, the Director-General of Shipping has been renamed the Director-General of Maritime Administration (DGMA), and new medical rules have been released in draft, so check the Maritime Health Branch page for which rules currently apply. Under STCW and MLC the certificate lasts no more than two years, and eyesight and colour vision are common reasons candidates are found unfit at pre-sea stage.

Who needs the medical and which rules apply

Every Indian seafarer needs a valid certificate of physical fitness. That covers pre-sea cadets, GP ratings, officers, engineers, ETOs and catering crew. Without one you cannot be registered, sign on or sit some exams.

The Indian rules sit on top of two international instruments:

  • STCW Regulation I/9 and Section A-I/9 set the minimum medical standards, the eyesight and colour vision standards, and the maximum validity.
  • MLC 2006, Regulation 1.2 requires every seafarer to hold a valid medical certificate before working on board.

In India these are put into effect by the Merchant Shipping (Medical Examination) Rules, 2000, as amended. The rules are backed by DG Shipping notices and circulars, and the amendments set out the current eyesight tables.

The legal basis is changing. Under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, the Director-General of Shipping has been renamed the Director-General of Maritime Administration (DGMA). The Merchant Shipping (Medical Examination) Rules, 2000 were made under the earlier 1958 Act, and new medical rules have been released in draft. Before your medical, check the DGMA/DG Shipping Maritime Health Branch page for which rules currently apply.

Finding a genuine DG-approved medical examiner

Only a medical practitioner approved by the Directorate can issue a valid seafarer medical. Approval is not automatic for any MBBS or MD doctor. Examiners are approved by the Directorate under conditions set in its circulars, and approval can be withdrawn.

How to check you are in the right place:

  • Use the official list. The Directorate publishes the list of approved medical examiners on its website, under the Maritime Health Branch section. Don't rely on old blog lists, because examiners retire, move clinics or lose approval.
  • Check the clinic address matches. Make sure the doctor's name and clinic address match the current official list.
  • Be wary of anyone offering a certificate without an examination. The rules require you to be present in person. A certificate that never appears online against your INDoS number will be rejected by institutes, crewing offices and the MMD.
  • Agents who "arrange" medicals for a fee and guarantee "fit" are a classic warning sign. Report them, don't pay them.

Step by step: on the day of the medical

Clinics differ in the details, but the process usually runs like this:

  1. Book an appointment with an examiner from the official list. Some clinics let you fill in registration and a self-declaration online beforehand. Many also ask you to come fasting for the blood sugar test, so check this when you book.
  2. Carry your documents. Bring your passport, CDC (if issued), INDoS number, recent passport photographs and any previous medical certificates. Also bring your spectacles or contact lenses if you wear them, plus reports and prescriptions for any ongoing condition.
  3. Complete the medical declaration honestly. You sign a declaration of your medical history. Hiding a condition such as diabetes, epilepsy, heart disease or past surgery can make the certificate invalid and cost you your job later.
  4. Physical examination. This covers height, weight, blood pressure, a systemic examination and a check of hearing.
  5. Eyesight and colour vision tests, against the table for your department and stage (pre-sea or in-service).
  6. Investigations. These are the laboratory and other tests the rules require, such as blood and urine tests and chest X-ray. The examiner may order more depending on age, findings or the type of ship.
  7. Assessment and upload. If you meet the standards, the examiner issues the certificate and uploads it to the Directorate's online system against your INDoS. Check the current DGMA circular for how certificates are issued.
  8. Verify it yourself. Before you leave town, log in and confirm the certificate shows in your profile with the right dates and no restrictions you weren't told about.

Worked scenario: A GP rating trainee gets a "fit" paper certificate from a clinic near the agent's office. The institute later finds nothing uploaded against his INDoS. The doctor turns out not to be on the current approved list. The certificate is rejected and the trainee has to repeat the whole medical with a genuine examiner. Two minutes on the official list would have prevented it.

Eyesight and colour vision: pre-sea versus in-service

Eyesight ends many careers at the entry stage, so test it before you pay course fees.

  • Pre-sea standards are stricter than in-service standards. For entry, the Indian tables set minimum unaided distance vision that differs by department: deck, engine, electrical/electronic and catering. Glasses do not get you around a pre-sea unaided requirement.
  • Colour vision is tested with Ishihara plates. The pre-sea tables set colour vision requirements by department, so check the official table for yours. Colour-correcting lenses are not accepted.
  • In-service standards follow the STCW Table A-I/9 approach: aided distance vision and CIE colour vision standards that differ for deck and engine.
  • Periodic eye tests are also needed for some nautical CoC examinations. Check the current DG Shipping requirement before you apply for the exam.

The exact figures sit in the eyesight tables of the current rules and notifications. Read the official table for your department rather than trusting a coaching centre's summary.

Practical tip: Before applying for a pre-sea course, have an optometrist check your unaided vision in each eye and do an Ishihara test. If you fall short of the deck standard but meet another department's, consider that route before giving up on the sea. Some companies also apply stricter standards of their own in their sponsorship rules.

Validity, expiry at sea, and flag or company medicals

Item Rule (STCW I/9 / MLC A1.2)
Medical certificate Valid for a maximum of 2 years
Seafarer under 18 Valid for a maximum of 1 year
Colour vision certificate Valid for a maximum of 6 years
Expiry during a voyage Stays in force until the next port of call with an approved examiner, for no more than 3 months (MLC: in urgent cases, provided the seafarer holds an expired certificate of recent date)

Keep in mind:

  • Plan renewals around contracts. Don't join a nine-month contract with a medical that expires in month four and hope for the three-month extension. That extension is a safety net, not a plan.
  • Flag state medicals. On foreign-flag ships, some administrations accept the Indian certificate. Others require their own form or an examiner they approve. This depends on the flag, so ask your company before you travel.
  • Company pre-employment medicals (PEME). Many managers and P&I clubs send you to their own nominated clinic as well as the statutory medical. That is a company requirement, not a replacement for the DG-approved certificate.
  • Filipino seafarers follow their own national system. Examinations are done at DOH-accredited medical facilities under MARINA requirements, so check MARINA guidance.

Common reasons for being found unfit, and what to do

From the examiner's side, these are the usual problems:

  • Failing the eyesight or colour vision standard, especially deck candidates at pre-sea stage.
  • Uncontrolled blood pressure or blood sugar found on the day. Many seafarers didn't know they had a problem until the medical.
  • Undisclosed conditions that turn up in investigations.
  • Obesity with complications, where weight affects fitness for emergency duties such as escape, firefighting or donning breathing apparatus. BMI alone is not the test. Can you actually do the job safely?
  • Hearing loss below the required standard.
  • Recent illness or surgery not yet recovered from. This often leads to "temporarily unfit" rather than permanent rejection.

If you are found unfit or restricted:

  1. Ask the examiner to explain the finding, whether it is temporary or permanent, and what treatment or reports would change it.
  2. Get treated and come back. A controlled condition with specialist reports often leads to a fit certificate, sometimes with limitations.
  3. Seek a review. MLC Standard A1.2 says a seafarer who is refused a certificate or given a limitation must be able to have a further examination by another independent practitioner or a medical referee. Check the current procedure with the Maritime Health Branch or your nearest Mercantile Marine Department.
  4. Never go clinic-shopping with a different story. Contradictory declarations are easy to trace once records are online.

Oral exam style questions

MMD examiners like to check that officers understand medical certification, because the Master and Chief Officer must make sure every crew member is properly certificated.

  • Q: What is the maximum validity of a seafarer's medical certificate? Two years, or one year if the seafarer is under 18. The colour vision certificate lasts up to six years.
  • Q: A crew member's medical expires mid-ocean. Is the ship non-compliant? Not necessarily. It stays valid until the next port where an approved examiner is available, for no more than three months. Under MLC this applies in urgent cases, provided the seafarer holds an expired certificate of recent date. Plan the renewal at that port.
  • Q: Who may issue the certificate for an Indian seafarer? Only a medical practitioner approved by the Directorate under the Merchant Shipping (Medical Examination) Rules.
  • Q: What should the medical certificate state as a minimum? That hearing and eyesight (and colour vision where relevant) are satisfactory. It must also state that the seafarer has no condition likely to be made worse by sea service, or that would make them unfit or endanger others on board. Any limitations must be stated.
  • Q: As Chief Officer at sign-on, what do you check? That the certificate is current, covers the contract or will be renewed in time, has no limitations that conflict with the job, and meets any flag requirement.
Key points
  • Only a medical examiner approved by DG Shipping/DGMA can issue a valid Indian seafarer medical certificate.
  • Check your examiner on the official approved list from the Maritime Health Branch, not old lists on blogs or agents' word.
  • Certificates are uploaded against your INDoS number, so check yours appears online before you leave.
  • Pre-sea eyesight and colour vision standards are stricter than in-service ones and require unaided vision, so test early.
  • The medical lasts a maximum of 2 years (1 year if under 18). The colour vision certificate lasts up to 6 years.
  • If your medical expires during a voyage, it can stay valid until the next port with an approved examiner, for no more than 3 months.
  • Flag-state and company pre-employment medicals can be extra requirements. Ask your company before you travel.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find a DG Shipping approved doctor near me?

Use the list of approved medical examiners published on the Directorate's website under the Maritime Health Branch section. Check that the doctor's name and clinic address match the current list. Lists on blogs and agents' sites are often out of date.

How long is the merchant navy medical certificate valid?

STCW and MLC allow a maximum of two years, or one year if you are under 18. The colour vision part can be valid for up to six years. Your company or flag may want it renewed sooner to cover a full contract.

Can I join the merchant navy if I am colour blind?

The pre-sea tables set colour vision requirements by department, and colour-correcting lenses are not accepted. Read the current official eyesight table for your department, and have an Ishihara test done before you pay for any course.

Can I wear glasses for the pre-sea medical?

Pre-sea standards set a minimum unaided distance vision for each department. Glasses alone will not get you past them. In-service standards for working seafarers are based on aided vision, but you still have to meet them.

What happens if I am declared unfit in my seafarer medical?

Ask the examiner whether it is temporary or permanent and what would change it. Many findings, such as high blood pressure, can be treated and you can be examined again. Under MLC you are entitled to seek a review by another independent doctor or medical referee. Check the current procedure with the Maritime Health Branch or your MMD.

Test yourself

What is the maximum validity of a medical certificate for a seafarer aged 25?

The maximum is two years, or one year for seafarers under 18 (STCW Regulation I/9; MLC Standard A1.2).

A seafarer's medical expires during an ocean passage. What applies?

STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC Standard A1.2 allow continued validity until the next suitable port of call, for no more than three months. Under MLC this applies in urgent cases, provided the seafarer holds an expired certificate of recent date.

Who can issue a valid seafarer medical certificate to an Indian seafarer?

Only approved medical examiners may examine seafarers and issue certificates (Merchant Shipping (Medical Examination) Rules, 2000, as amended).

What is the maximum validity of a colour vision certificate under STCW?

A certificate of colour vision is valid for a maximum of six years (STCW Regulation I/9).

A deck cadet applicant meets the vision standard only when wearing glasses. What is the most accurate statement?

The Indian pre-sea eyesight tables set unaided distance vision minimums for each department. The in-service standards in STCW Table A-I/9 are based on aided vision.
