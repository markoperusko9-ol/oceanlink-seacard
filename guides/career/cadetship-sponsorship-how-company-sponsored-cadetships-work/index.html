Cadetship sponsorship: how company-sponsored cadetships work

Quick answer

In a company-sponsored cadetship, a shipping company or a licensed manning or RPSL company picks you for pre-sea training. It then commits to giving you a berth for the onboard training that STCW requires before your first CoC, usually in return for a service or bond agreement. In India, the Directorate General of Maritime Administration (DGMA, formerly DG Shipping) requires pre-sea institutes to hold verifiable MoUs with shipping companies or RPSL companies for that onboard training. In the Philippines, MARINA rules govern the shipboard training part of BSMT and BSMarE.

What a sponsored cadetship actually is

Your officer CoC depends on two things: classroom training ashore and a set period of structured onboard training logged in an approved Training Record Book (TRB). Ashore training is easy to find. The sea berth is not. A sponsored cadetship solves the berth problem before you start.

In practice you will meet three models:

  • Company cadetship. A shipowner or ship manager runs its own selection, sends you to a named institute for pre-sea, and you sail on its fleet for onboard training. Some companies pay part or all of the training cost. Others just guarantee the berth.
  • Institute-placed cadet. You get into an approved institute on merit. The institute then places you on a ship through its MoU with a shipping company or RPSL company. In India this is now the regulated default for pre-sea courses.
  • Scholarship or cadetship by a manning agency or principal. This is common in the Philippines. The agency or its principal funds or supports your maritime degree and gives you the shipboard training berth. In return you commit to serve with them.

Each model has its own terms, and you sign them. Never assume one company's terms apply to another.

The rules behind it: STCW, DGMA (formerly DG Shipping) and MARINA

STCW. For an officer in charge of a navigational watch, Regulation II/1 lets you qualify with at least 12 months' approved seagoing service. That service must be part of an approved training programme, with onboard training that meets Section A-II/1 and is documented in an approved TRB. Without such a programme the minimum is much longer. Regulation III/1 for engineers follows the same idea: a shorter route through an approved programme with documented onboard training. That is why a properly structured sea berth is worth so much.

India (DGMA, formerly DG Shipping).

  • In 2001, Training Circular No. 3 withdrew mandatory sponsorship for the then 3-month pre-sea deck cadet and GME pre-sea courses at DG-approved private institutes, and told institutes to select on merit.
  • The 2018 Training Manual moved responsibility for sea berths onto the institutes. A Maritime Training Institute (MTI) must hold verifiable MoUs with shipping companies that have an office in India, or with DGS-approved RPSL companies. Those MoUs must cover onboard training for the institute's whole intake. If a company does not honour the MoU, completing the training is still the institute's responsibility. A revised Training Manual was circulated in draft in 2024. Check dgma.gov.in for the version in force.
  • For GP Rating courses, the guidelines say institutes may admit only candidates for whom a shipping company has sponsored six months of onboard training. The sponsorship letters go to DGS before the batch starts.
  • DGS Order No. 3 of 2026 revises the Comprehensive Inspection Programme for pre-sea institutes, in which placement carries significant weight. A placement counts only when the trainee has actually been placed on board a ship.

Philippines (MARINA). Under RA 10635, MARINA is the single maritime administration for implementing STCW. Shipboard training is a required part of BSMT and BSMarE and is logged in the approved training record book. Overseas deployment goes through manning agencies licensed by the DMW. Check the current MARINA memorandum circulars on shipboard training (marina.gov.ph) and the DMW licensed-agency list (dmw.gov.ph).

These rules change. Before you rely on a number, check the latest circulars and orders on the DGMA website (dgma.gov.in, formerly dgshipping.gov.in) or the MARINA website.

Step by step: getting a sponsored cadetship (India)

  1. Check eligibility for your course (DNS, B.Sc Nautical Science, Marine Engineering, GME, ETO or GP Rating) in the current DGMA guidelines and the IMU notice. Eligibility includes age, subjects, marks and eyesight.
  2. Sit IMU CET. Admission to most DG-approved officer pre-sea courses goes through it. Check the current IMU notice and DGMA course guidelines to see whether your course (for example GME, ETO or GP Rating) is exempt.
  3. Apply directly to the company. Use the careers page of a known shipowner or ship manager, or apply through your institute's placement cell. Do not apply through a middleman who asks for money.
  4. Get through selection. Expect a written or online test (maths, physics, English, aptitude), often a psychometric test, then an interview.
  5. Pass the medical with a DGMA-approved doctor. Eyesight and colour vision are where most people fail. Get checked before you pay anything.
  6. Get your INDoS number through the institute and complete the mandatory STCW basic courses as the programme requires.
  7. Sign the sponsorship or service agreement. Read it properly first (see below).
  8. Finish pre-sea, get your CDC and sign on through the company or its RPSL. Your onboard training then starts and your TRB is opened.

Worked scenario. Ravi finishes DNS at an approved institute. The institute's MoU partner has no berth for four months. He is not stuck. Under the Training Manual, onboard training is still the institute's responsibility. He should first ask the institute in writing for a placement timeline. If nothing happens, he can raise a grievance with the DGMA. Paying an outside agent for a 'berth' is the wrong move.

Documents you will normally need

Exact lists differ by company, institute and administration. A typical file includes:

  • Passport, valid long enough for the programme
  • Mark sheets and pass certificates for 10th and 12th (or the Philippine equivalent), and your degree or diploma if you have one
  • IMU CET admit card and result, where needed
  • Medical fitness certificate from an approved doctor, including eyesight and colour vision
  • INDoS number (India) or SRN from MARINA (Philippines), once issued
  • STCW basic safety certificates as required
  • CDC (India) or SIRB (Philippines) before sign-on
  • The signed sponsorship, scholarship or service agreement, and the institute's admission letter
  • Police or character certificate, if the company asks for one

Keep scanned copies in one folder. Crewing departments often ask for the same document twice.

Reading the sponsorship or bond agreement

This is where cadets get hurt. Before you sign, check:

Clause What to look for
Service commitment How long you must serve after getting your CoC, and in which rank
Training cost Who pays for what, and whether you repay it if you leave early
Bond or penalty How the amount is worked out, and whether it reduces with service
Failure clauses What happens if you fail an exam, the medical or onboard assessment
Placement timeline Any written commitment on when you will join a ship
Release Conditions for leaving, and whether your TRB and documents are handed back

Get the full agreement in writing, read it with a family member, and keep a signed copy. Do not hand over original certificates as 'security'. If a clause is unclear, ask for it in writing.

Common rejections and red flags

Why candidates get rejected:

  • They fail the eyesight or colour vision test after paying for coaching
  • They do not meet the subject or marks rules in the current eligibility notice
  • Their aptitude or English score is weak, or their interview answers are memorised
  • Their documents do not match, such as name spellings or date of birth across certificates
  • Their attitude is poor at a group discussion or interview

Red flags. Walk away if:

  • Someone sells a 'sponsorship letter' or 'guaranteed ship' for cash
  • The RPSL licence cannot be found on the DGMA website (dgma.gov.in, formerly dgshipping.gov.in)
  • The 'company' has no fleet, office or traceable ships
  • They ask for money into a personal account
  • They promise 'sign-on within one week' before you have even finished pre-sea

In the Philippines, check that the manning agency holds a current DMW licence (dmw.gov.ph) before signing anything.

Selection interview: oral exam-style questions

Company panels are not CoC examiners, but many use a similar style. Prepare honest, short answers to:

  • Why sea, and why this company? Know their fleet type (tanker, bulker, container) and main trades.
  • What does a deck or engine cadet do on board? Watches under supervision, maintenance, drills, TRB tasks, and learning the safety management system.
  • What is STCW? The IMO convention that sets minimum standards for training, certification and watchkeeping.
  • What is a TRB and who signs it? The approved training record. Tasks are signed by the qualified officers who supervise you. A designated shipboard training officer, under the Master's overall responsibility, monitors progress, and the company training officer oversees the programme.
  • Name some life-saving appliances. Lifeboats, liferafts, lifejackets, immersion suits, lifebuoys, EPIRB, SART.
  • What would you do if a senior asked you to skip a safety step? Stop, ask, and report through the chain. The answer they want is 'safety first, speak up politely'.
  • Basic physics and maths. Density, buoyancy, pressure, simple trigonometry.

Panels mark attitude as much as knowledge. Saying 'I don't know, but I would find out from the officer of the watch' scores better than bluffing.

Key points
  • A sponsored cadetship is mainly about securing the onboard training berth STCW requires before your first CoC.
  • Under STCW II/1 and III/1, approved onboard training documented in a TRB gives the shortest route to an officer CoC.
  • DGMA (formerly DG Shipping) requires pre-sea MTIs to hold verifiable MoUs with shipping companies in India or DGS-approved RPSL companies for onboard training.
  • DGS Order No. 3 of 2026 counts a placement only when the trainee has actually been placed on board a ship.
  • Read every bond or service clause and keep a signed copy. Never surrender original certificates.
  • Anyone selling sponsorship letters or guaranteed berths for cash is a red flag. Verify the RPSL or DMW licence.
  • Filipino cadets: MARINA governs shipboard training under STCW, and overseas deployment goes through DMW-licensed agencies.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need company sponsorship to join a pre-sea course in India?

You do not usually need to find your own sponsor. Under the current Training Manual, the institute must arrange onboard training through verifiable MoUs with shipping companies or RPSL companies, and some intakes need sponsorship letters. A 2001 circular had already withdrawn mandatory individual sponsorship for the older deck cadet and GME pre-sea courses. GP Rating admission is tied to six months of sponsored onboard training, so check the current DGMA training circulars.

My institute has not placed me on a ship after pre-sea. What can I do?

Ask the institute in writing for its placement plan. Under the DGS Training Manual, the institute remains responsible for completing onboard training even if a partner company fails to honour its MoU. A revised Training Manual was circulated in draft in 2024, so check dgma.gov.in for the version in force. If you get no response, use the DGMA grievance channels, and do not pay a third party for a berth.

Is a cadetship bond legal and how much is it?

Service agreements and bonds are common, but the terms differ by company and are a contract matter. No rule fixes the amount. Before signing, check how the amount is calculated, whether it reduces with service, and what happens if you fail a medical or exam. Get legal advice if any clause is unclear.

How do I know a sponsorship offer is genuine?

In India, check that the company holds a valid RPSL licence on the DGMA website (dgma.gov.in, formerly dgshipping.gov.in). In the Philippines, check for a DMW licence. The company should also have a real office and fleet, and genuine companies run tests and interviews rather than selling sponsorship letters. Payment into a personal account, or a 'guaranteed ship' for cash, is a scam signal.

How do cadetships work for Filipino maritime students?

BSMT and BSMarE include shipboard training, which MARINA regulates as the STCW administration. Many manning agencies and principals offer scholarships or cadetships that fund or support your studies and give you a shipboard training berth, in exchange for a service commitment. Before signing, check the agency on the DMW licensed-agency list (dmw.gov.ph) and read the current MARINA memorandum circulars on shipboard training (marina.gov.ph).

Test yourself

Why is approved onboard training documented in a TRB so valuable for a deck cadet?

Under STCW Regulation II/1, at least 12 months' seagoing service counts towards an OOW CoC when it is part of an approved training programme with onboard training documented in an approved TRB.

Under the DGS Training Manual, who must an Indian pre-sea MTI hold verifiable MoUs with for onboard training?

The DGS Training Manual (2018) requires verifiable MoUs with shipping companies that have an office in India, or with DGS-approved RPSL companies, covering the institute's intake.

Under DGS Order No. 3 of 2026, when is a pre-sea trainee's placement treated as achieved?

DGS Order No. 3 of 2026 counts a placement only when the trainee has been placed on board a ship.

What did DGS Training Circular No. 3 of 2001 do?

Training Circular No. 3 of 2001 withdrew mandatory sponsorship for the then pre-sea deck cadet and GME pre-sea courses and told institutes to select on merit.

An agent offers you a 'guaranteed sponsorship letter and sign-on' for cash. What is the right first step?

Genuine sponsorship is not sold. Check the licence on the DGMA website (RPSL) or the DMW site, and report the offer to the authorities.
