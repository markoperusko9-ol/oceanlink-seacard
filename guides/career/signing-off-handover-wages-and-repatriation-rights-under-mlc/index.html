Signing off: handover, wages and repatriation rights under MLC

Quick answer

Under MLC 2006, the shipowner pays to send you home when your contract ends. That normally covers travel, food and lodging on the way, and luggage, as set by your flag state's law. The shipowner cannot ask you to pay any of it in advance. Before you leave the gangway you should have a proper handover, a final account of your wages and a sign-off entry in your CDC or SIRB. Once home, check that your RPSL or manning agency has updated your sea service with the administration.

What signing off actually involves

Signing off is a legal step as well as a trip to the airport. When you sign off, four things happen at once:

  • Your employment on that ship ends. Your Seafarer Employment Agreement (SEA) is closed. MLC Standard A2.1 says you must get a document recording your employment on board. That record must not comment on how well you worked or what you were paid.
  • Your wages are settled. Standard A2.2 requires a monthly account of wages. At sign-off you should get the final account.
  • Repatriation starts. This is covered by Regulation 2.5 and Standard A2.5.1, plus your flag state's law and any CBA.
  • Your sea service is recorded. This means the master's entry in your discharge book and the agency's entry in the national system. That record feeds your next CoC application.

The MLC sets minimum standards. Each flag state writes them into its own law. In India that is the Merchant Shipping (Maritime Labour) Rules, 2016. For Filipino seafarers, the POEA/DMW Standard Employment Contract also applies. Details such as notice periods, sick pay periods and how claims are handled can differ between flags, CBAs and company SMS. Always read your own SEA and CBA.

Before your relief date: contract length, notice and planning

Maximum time on board. Standard A2.5.1 requires national law to set a maximum period of service, after which you are entitled to repatriation. That period must be less than 12 months. Your SEA normally gives a shorter period, often with a plus or minus tolerance. Check your flag administration's requirements and know your exact dates.

Notice. Under Standard A2.1, national law must set a minimum notice period for ending a SEA early. That period cannot be shorter than seven days. National law must also take into account your need to end the SEA at shorter notice, or without notice and without penalty, for compassionate or other urgent reasons. Check the period written in your own SEA.

Practical steps about a month before relief:

  1. Remind the master in writing (email or a request form) of your contract end date. Keep a copy.
  2. Ask the company or agency whether a reliever has been arranged. If your contract is about to expire and nobody has been named, raise it formally.
  3. Check your passport, visa and medical certificate are valid for the travel route. Valid documents make the transit easier for everyone.
  4. Start your handover notes now, not on the last night.

Handover step by step

A poor handover is the most common cause of trouble after you leave. The ISM Code requires the company to have procedures for familiarising new personnel. Most SMS forms include a handover checklist that both of you sign.

A good handover covers:

  • Written notes. Include outstanding defects, PMS jobs that are due or overdue, open non-conformities and observations from the last audit or PSC inspection.
  • Documents and records. Cover the logbooks, Oil Record Book and Garbage Record Book entries (if you kept them), chart corrections and publications status, certificates nearing expiry, and the keys and passwords under your control.
  • Inventory. Check stores, spares and safety equipment you were responsible for. Count them, don't estimate.
  • Walk-round. Go round your spaces with your reliever: bridge equipment, steering gear, the emergency generator, LSA and FFA, or whatever your rank covers.
  • Signatures. Both of you sign the checklist, and the HOD or master countersigns. Keep a photo or copy.

Worked scenario: a 2nd Engineer signs off with an overdue purifier overhaul shown as 'done' in the PMS, but no parts were actually changed. A month later the purifier fails at sea. The handover checklist that the reliever signed, listing the real status, is what protects the officer who left. Never sign a handover that hides the true state of the plant.

Wages and your final account

Under Regulation 2.2 and Standard A2.2, wages must be paid at least monthly. You are also entitled to a monthly account showing what you were paid, any additional payments and any deductions. Allotments to your family are allowed and must be made as your SEA says.

Before you sign off, check your final wage account for:

  • the correct days on board, up to and including your sign-off date
  • overtime and fixed allowances, matched against your own records
  • leave pay. Standard A2.4 sets annual leave with pay at a minimum of 2.5 calendar days per month of employment. Agreements to give up the minimum leave in return for cash are generally not allowed.
  • every deduction explained: cash advances, slop chest, phone cards
  • allotments already paid, so nothing is paid twice or missed

How and when the balance is paid (cash on board, bank transfer, through the agency) depends on your SEA, CBA and flag law. If anything is wrong, tell the master before you sign, and write your disagreement on your copy. MLC gives you an on-board complaint procedure (Regulation 5.1.5). It also gives you the right to complain to a port state control officer ashore (Regulation 5.2.2).

Keep your monthly wage accounts, the signed final account and your SEA together. Without them, a later claim is hard to prove.

Repatriation: what the shipowner must pay

When you are entitled to repatriation (Standard A2.5.1):

  • your SEA expires while you are abroad
  • the shipowner ends your SEA, or you end it for justified reasons
  • you can no longer carry out your duties, or cannot be expected to in the circumstances

Guideline B2.5.1 gives examples: illness or injury, shipwreck, the shipowner's insolvency, sale or change of flag, the ship heading into a war zone without your consent, and termination or interruption of employment under an arbitral award or collective agreement.

What the shipowner normally covers (Guideline B2.5.1; exact items set by flag law/CBA):

  • travel by an appropriate and quick means, normally by air
  • accommodation and food from leaving the ship until you reach your destination
  • pay and allowances during that time, where national law or the CBA provides for it
  • transport of 30 kg of personal luggage
  • medical treatment, where needed, until you are fit to travel

Destination. This can be the place where you agreed to the engagement, a place set by the CBA, your country of residence, or any other place mutually agreed at the time of engagement.

What they cannot do. The shipowner may not ask you to pay for repatriation in advance. They may not take the cost out of your wages. The only exception is where you are found, under national law, to be in serious default of your employment obligations.

Guidance on waiting time. Under the guideline, time spent waiting for repatriation and the travel time home should not be taken out of your earned paid leave.

Paperwork after you land: CDC, SIRB and sea service

Indian seafarers:

  1. Check the master has made the sign-off entry in your CDC, with the ship's stamp and his signature.
  2. Your RPSL agency or shipping company enters the sign-on and sign-off dates in the DG Shipping e-governance system. Log in to your seafarer profile on the DG Shipping site and check the dates are correct.
  3. If an entry is missing, ask your company first. DG Shipping's Crew Matters FAQ covers missed sea service entries. DGS Circular 29 of 2021 sets out a procedure for cases where the RPSL has been suspended, withdrawn or had its online access switched off.
  4. Get your sea service testimonial now, while the master and company still remember you. You will need it for your CoC exams.

Filipino seafarers:

  1. Make sure the sign-off is entered in your SIRB.
  2. Collect your record of service and your final wage documents from the manning agency.
  3. Check on the DMW website (dmw.gov.ph) which Standard Employment Contract governs your contract: the POEA-SEC 2010 or any later DMW-issued version. Check the deadlines for the post-employment medical exam and claims in that version. Under the 2010 POEA-SEC (Section 20(A)(3)), a seafarer signed off for illness or injury must report for a post-employment medical exam within three working days of arriving home. Don't miss this deadline under whichever contract applies.

When it goes wrong: medical sign-off, refusal and abandonment

Medical sign-off. Under Standard A4.2.1, the shipowner pays medical care, board and lodging for sickness or injury arising during your service. You keep receiving full wages while you remain on board or until you are repatriated. After that, national law may limit how long the shipowner must keep paying. The minimum that law may set is 16 weeks from the injury or start of the sickness. Get the port doctor's report and your medical records before you leave.

Relief refused or delayed. Standard A2.5.1 says repatriation must not be refused because the shipowner is in financial difficulty or cannot find a replacement. If the shipowner does not arrange it, the flag state must. Failing that, the port state or your own country may arrange it and recover the cost from the flag state.

Abandonment (Standard A2.5.2). You count as abandoned if the shipowner:

  • fails to pay for your repatriation, or
  • leaves you without necessary maintenance and support, or
  • has otherwise cut ties with you, including not paying contractual wages for at least two months.

Ships that must hold an MLC certificate (generally 500 GT and over on international voyages) must carry and post on board a certificate or other documentary evidence of financial security for abandonment, usually issued by a P&I club. It covers:

  • up to four months' outstanding wages and entitlements
  • repatriation
  • essential needs such as food, water, accommodation and medical care

Photograph that certificate. It has the insurer's contact details.

Where to complain:

  • the on-board procedure first
  • then a PSC officer in port
  • then your flag state
  • Indian seafarers: DG Shipping's grievance mechanism
  • Filipino seafarers: the DMW

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask

These questions come up in Second Mate, MEO Class 4 and Chief Mate orals, often in the MLC or ship management part.

  • 'What is the maximum period a seafarer can serve on board?' Less than 12 months under Standard A2.5.1. The exact limit is set by flag state law and the SEA.
  • 'Who pays for repatriation, and can it be recovered?' The shipowner pays. They cannot ask for payment in advance. The cost can only be recovered from wages if the seafarer is found to be in serious default under national law.
  • 'What is abandonment and what does the financial security cover?' Answer with the three triggers and the cover. Mention the four-month wage limit.
  • 'What must the record of employment not contain?' Any statement about how well the seafarer worked, or their wages.
  • 'As master, the company tells you the reliever is delayed six weeks and the AB's contract has expired. What do you do?' Record the AB's request. Tell the company in writing that the entitlement exists and does not depend on finding a relief. Check the 12-month limit and the flag's rules. Log what you did.

Examiners want the principle, the reference and a sensible practical action. Reciting paragraph numbers alone won't get you through.

Key points
  • Under MLC the shipowner pays repatriation. It cannot be demanded in advance or taken from wages, except for serious default under national law.
  • Maximum continuous service on board must be less than 12 months. Your SEA and flag law set the exact limit.
  • Repatriation cover normally includes air travel, food and lodging on the way, 30 kg of luggage and medical care until you are fit to travel. Flag law and your CBA set the exact items.
  • Check your final wage account line by line, and raise any dispute before you sign.
  • Make a written, countersigned handover and keep a copy. It protects you after you leave.
  • Indian seafarers: check your sign-off dates in your DG Shipping e-governance profile. Filipino seafarers: check the SIRB entry and the medical and claim deadlines in the Standard Employment Contract that governs your contract.
  • On MLC-certified ships, the required financial security covers abandoned seafarers for up to four months' wages, repatriation and essential needs.

Frequently asked questions

Can the company deduct my air ticket home from my wages?

Normally no. MLC Standard A2.5.1 does not allow the shipowner to ask you to pay for repatriation in advance, or to recover it from your wages. The only exception is where you are found, under national law or other applicable rules, to be in serious default of your employment obligations. Ask for the legal basis of any such deduction in writing.

My contract has expired but the company says there is no reliever. Do I have to stay?

The MLC says repatriation must not be refused because the shipowner cannot replace you. Make your request to the master in writing and keep a copy. If nothing happens, use the on-board complaint procedure, then contact a port state control officer or your flag state.

My sign-off date is not showing in my DG Shipping profile. What should I do?

Sign-on and sign-off entries are made by your RPSL agency or shipping company in the DG Shipping e-governance system, so ask them first. If the agency has been suspended or deactivated, DGS Circular 29 of 2021 sets out a procedure for correcting the missing entries. The DG Shipping Crew Matters FAQ also covers missed sea service entries.

What happens if I sign off sick in a foreign port?

The shipowner pays your medical care, board and lodging, and your repatriation once you are fit to travel. You keep receiving full wages while on board or until repatriated. After that, national law may limit the shipowner's liability, but to no less than 16 weeks. Filipino seafarers must also meet the post-employment medical deadline in the Standard Employment Contract that governs their contract (three working days under the 2010 POEA-SEC).

How do I know if the ship has abandonment insurance?

Ships that must hold an MLC certificate (generally 500 GT and over on international voyages) must carry and post on board a certificate or other documentary evidence of financial security for abandonment, usually issued by a P&I club. Photograph it, because it shows the insurer's contact details. The cover includes up to four months' outstanding wages, repatriation costs and essential needs.

Under MLC Standard A2.5.1, the maximum period of service on board before repatriation must be:

Standard A2.5.1 requires national law to set a maximum service period of less than 12 months. Flag states and SEAs may set shorter periods. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.5.1)

When can a shipowner recover repatriation costs from a seafarer's wages?

Advance payment and recovery from wages are not allowed, except where the seafarer is found in serious default under national law or other applicable rules. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.5.1)

The financial security for abandonment must cover outstanding wages and entitlements up to:

Under the 2014 amendments, the security covers up to four months' outstanding wages and entitlements, plus repatriation and essential needs. Two months of unpaid wages is one of the triggers for abandonment, not the limit of cover. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.5.2)

The record of employment given to a seafarer on sign-off must NOT contain:

Standard A2.1 says the record of employment must not contain any statement about the quality of the seafarer's work or their wages. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.1)

A shipowner says it cannot repatriate a seafarer because it has no money. Under MLC, the correct position is:

Repatriation cannot be refused because of the shipowner's finances. If the shipowner fails, the flag state arranges it. Failing that, the port state or the seafarer's own state may arrange it and recover the cost from the flag state. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.5.1)
