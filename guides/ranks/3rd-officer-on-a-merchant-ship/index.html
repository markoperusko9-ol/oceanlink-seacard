3rd Officer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The 3rd Officer is usually the most junior watchkeeping deck officer, although some companies also carry a 4th or Junior Officer holding the same CoC. They stand a navigational watch, traditionally the 8-12, and usually look after life-saving and fire-fighting equipment under the company's SMS. You need an STCW Regulation II/1 Certificate of Competency (Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch) and its supporting proficiencies, and the usual next step is 2nd Officer.

What a 3rd Officer actually does

On most deep-sea ships the 3rd Officer is usually the most junior watchkeeping deck officer, although some companies also carry a 4th or Junior Officer holding the same CoC. You are a full Officer of the Watch (OOW). When the Master is not on the bridge, you are responsible for the safe navigation of the ship during your watch. That responsibility is real from day one, so nobody should treat it as a training berth.

Typical responsibilities are below. The exact split is set by your company's Safety Management System (SMS) and the Master's standing orders, so check your familiarisation documents.

  • Navigational watchkeeping at sea and at anchor: lookout, collision avoidance under COLREG, position fixing, monitoring the passage plan, and weather and navigational warnings.
  • Life-saving appliances (LSA) and fire-fighting appliances (FFA): on many ships the 3rd Officer keeps the inspection and maintenance records for lifeboats, liferafts, lifejackets, immersion suits, extinguishers, fire hoses and breathing apparatus. These follow the weekly and monthly inspection regime of SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20 for LSA and SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 14 (with MSC.1/Circ.1432) for fire-fighting appliances, together with the ship's planned maintenance system.
  • Drills: helping prepare and record abandon ship, fire and other drills.
  • Cargo watches in port: under the Chief Officer's instructions. Duties include draughts, loading/discharge sequence, mooring lines, gangway, security and ballast where delegated.
  • Mooring stations: usually forward or aft with the Bosun, depending on company practice.
  • Paperwork: some companies give the 3rd Officer flag and signal upkeep, the garbage record book, port documents or crew-list support. Others give these to the 2nd Officer or Chief Officer. There is no single international rule on this.

On smaller ships with a two-watch system, or where there is no 3rd Officer, these duties are shared differently.

Watch pattern, hours of rest and reporting lines

Watch pattern. On a ship running three watches, the 3rd Officer traditionally keeps the 0800-1200 and 2000-2400 watch. The 2nd Officer usually has the 12-4 and the Chief Officer the 4-8. Some companies vary this, and many ships change pattern in port, for example to a 6-on/6-off cargo watch. Coastal and short-sea trades often run tighter rotations.

Hours of rest. STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 sets these minimums for watchkeepers, and they match the hours-of-rest option in MLC 2006:

  • At least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period.
  • At least 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours.
  • The gap between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.

MLC 2006 also allows flag States to regulate by maximum hours of work (14 in 24 hours, 72 in 7 days) instead, but STCW A-VIII/1 rest minimums apply to watchkeepers. Administrations may allow limited exceptions, and drills and emergencies have their own provisions. Record your hours honestly. Port State Control inspectors do compare rest records against logbooks and cargo timings.

Who you report to:

Matter Report to
Navigation, watchkeeping, any doubt on the bridge Master
Cargo, deck work, LSA/FFA maintenance planning Chief Officer
Safety and drills Master and Chief Officer, per SMS

Who reports to you:

  • The lookout or helmsman on your watch, usually an AB or OS.
  • Deck cadets assigned to you. Supervising and signing off their training record book tasks is part of the job.

The Master's standing and night orders tell you exactly when to call them. If you are unsure whether to call, call.

Certificates and competencies you need

The core qualification is a Certificate of Competency (CoC) as Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch under STCW Regulation II/1, for ships of 500 GT or more. The competence standard is in STCW Code Section A-II/1. In general terms, Regulation II/1 requires the following.

  • Age: at least 18.
  • Sea service, one of two routes:
    • At least 12 months of approved seagoing service as part of an approved training programme, documented in an approved training record book; or
    • At least 36 months of approved seagoing service.
  • Supervised bridge watchkeeping: at least six months of the sea service must be bridge watchkeeping duties under the supervision of the Master or a qualified officer.
  • Training and exams: an approved education and training programme meeting A-II/1, with assessment. Most administrations use written and oral exams.
  • Radio: the appropriate GMDSS radio certificate, usually the General Operator's Certificate on SOLAS ships.
  • Medical fitness: a valid seafarer medical certificate.
  • STCW Chapter VI proficiencies:
    • Basic training.
    • Proficiency in survival craft and rescue boats.
    • Advanced fire fighting.
    • Medical first aid.

Other certificates commonly required by flag, ship type or company:

  • ECDIS: generic training is covered within the A-II/1 competence for ships fitted with ECDIS. Type-specific familiarisation is required under ISM and company procedures.
  • Radar/ARPA and bridge teamwork or BRM: often delivered as part of the CoC programme. Companies may require refreshers.
  • Security: security-awareness training under Regulation VI/6, and designated security duties training if you are assigned security duties under the Ship Security Plan, which is usual for officers.
  • Tanker training: basic oil and chemical, or liquefied gas, tanker training under Regulation V/1-1 or V/1-2 if you have specific duties on tankers. Advanced training is mandatory only for those with immediate responsibility for cargo operations, though some companies require it.
  • Flag state endorsement: recognition of your CoC by the flag of the ship under Regulation I/10, when the flag differs from the issuing administration.
  • Revalidation: your CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years (Regulation I/11), with evidence of sea service or approved alternatives.

National detail varies. Indian candidates go through DG Shipping's examination system and need an INDoS number. Filipino candidates go through MARINA. Each has its own rules on approved courses, exam format, sea-time counting and validity. Confirm current requirements with your administration rather than relying on forum posts.

How people reach 3rd Officer and what comes next

Common routes in:

  1. Cadet route. A pre-sea degree or diploma, then structured onboard training as a deck cadet with a training record book. After that come post-sea courses and the OICNW exams. This is the most common route for Indian and Filipino officers.
  2. Rating route. Ratings with enough approved sea service and supervised bridge watchkeeping can qualify under the longer-service route, then complete the required courses and exams.
  3. Junior officer step. Some companies first employ newly certified officers as Junior Officer, 4th Officer or Trainee Officer for a contract before giving them a full 3rd Officer berth. This is company policy, not an STCW rank.

Next step: 2nd Officer. The 2nd Officer usually holds the same II/1 CoC. Promotion is a company decision based on your appraisals, sea time in rank and the Master's recommendation. There is no fixed international number of contracts.

Longer term: Chief Mate and Master. For a Chief Mate CoC on ships of 3,000 GT or more, STCW Regulation II/2 requires at least 12 months of approved seagoing service as an officer in charge of a navigational watch. You also need to pass the management-level training and exams. Administrations decide which service counts, for example by GT band, trading area or actual watchkeeping, so keep your sea-service testimonials and discharge book entries accurate from your first contract.

What crewing agencies look for in a 3rd Officer CV

Crewing officers screen many CVs. Make theirs easy:

  • Put CoC details at the top: issuing administration, number, capacity, limitations and expiry, plus GMDSS and any flag endorsements.
  • Show sea service in a table: vessel name, type, GT, flag, engine type if relevant, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates. Dates must match your CDC or seaman's book exactly. Mismatches are a common reason for rejection.
  • Match your experience to the vessel type. Tanker companies want V/1-1 endorsements and tanker sea time. Container and bulk operators look for experience on similar size and trade.
  • List ECDIS makers and models you have used, with type-specific certificates.
  • Show document validity: passport, medical, US C1/D visa if held, yellow fever certificate, and STCW proficiencies with expiry dates.
  • Give references: previous Master or company contact, and appraisals if available.
  • Explain gaps in a line, for example study leave or exam preparation.

What counts against you: unexplained short contracts, expired certificates, padded sea time, and generic CVs that clearly weren't read before sending.

Oral exam and interview: questions 3rd Officers get asked

Whether it is a CoC oral or a company interview, expect practical questions like these.

  • "You are OOW at 2200. A vessel is crossing from starboard on a steady bearing with closing range. Walk me through it."
    • Confirm risk of collision with radar or ARPA and compass bearings (Rule 7). You are give-way (Rule 15).
    • Take early, substantial action (Rules 8 and 16), normally an alteration to starboard to pass astern.
    • Check the action is effective.
    • Call the Master if the standing orders require it or you are in doubt.
  • "Visibility drops to one mile. What do you do?"
    • Call the Master as per standing orders and inform the engine room.
    • Go to safe speed and start fog signals.
    • Post an additional lookout, switch to manual steering if required, and apply Rule 19.
    • Remember that Rule 19 has no stand-on vessel.
  • "What weekly and monthly checks are you responsible for on LSA?" Know the SOLAS Chapter III inspection regime and how your PMS records it.
  • "How often must each crew member take part in abandon ship and fire drills?" At least one of each every month under SOLAS III/19. If more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in abandon ship and fire drills on that ship in the previous month, the drills must be held within 24 hours of the ship leaving port.
  • "When would you not hand over the watch?" When the relieving officer is not fit for duty, or during a manoeuvre or avoiding action, until it is completed.

Examiners want reasoning, not recitation. Say what you would check, who you would inform and why.

Practical tips for your first contracts as 3rd Officer

  • Read the standing orders and night orders properly and sign them. Know the call-the-Master criteria by heart.
  • Do the LSA/FFA round in your first week. Walk every lifeboat, raft station, fire station and SCBA set, and compare them against the records. Discrepancies found early belong to your predecessor. Found later, they're yours.
  • Get a proper handover with the inventory and a list of outstanding defects in writing.
  • Fix the position by more than one means. Don't let ECDIS and GNSS make you lazy. Visual bearings, radar ranges and parallel indexing still matter.
  • Use your cadets well. Teach them, sign their books honestly, and don't leave them as lookouts while you do paperwork.
  • Keep your own record of sea service, ship particulars and signed testimonials for your next exam.
  • Ask questions. No Master thinks less of a 3rd Officer who calls them early. Many have had to deal with one who didn't.
Key points
  • The 3rd Officer is a certified Officer of the Watch, traditionally keeping the 0800-1200 and 2000-2400 watches.
  • LSA and FFA maintenance is commonly the 3rd Officer's job, but the exact duty split is set by company SMS.
  • The required CoC is STCW Regulation II/1 (OICNW), plus GMDSS and the Chapter VI proficiencies.
  • Watchkeepers must have at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days (STCW A-VIII/1).
  • The next step is 2nd Officer, usually on the same CoC; promotion is a company decision.
  • Chief Mate (3,000 GT+) requires at least 12 months' seagoing service as OICNW under Regulation II/2.
  • CV dates must match your CDC exactly. Your national administration (for example DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines) sets the rules on exams and sea-time counting.

Frequently asked questions

What watch does a 3rd Officer keep?

On ships running a three-watch system, the 3rd Officer traditionally keeps the 8-12, from 0800 to 1200 and 2000 to 2400. Companies can vary this, and in port many ships switch to cargo watch rotations. Always follow your Master's standing orders and the ship's watch schedule.

Which certificate do I need to sail as 3rd Officer?

You need a Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch under STCW Regulation II/1. Alongside it you need a GMDSS radio certificate, a valid medical certificate and the Chapter VI proficiencies: basic training, survival craft, advanced fire fighting and medical first aid. Ship type or flag may require more, such as tanker training or a flag state endorsement.

What is the difference between a 3rd Officer and a 2nd Officer?

Both usually hold the same II/1 CoC and both stand navigational watches. The 2nd Officer is more senior and is traditionally the navigating officer, responsible for passage planning and charts and publications. The 3rd Officer commonly handles LSA and FFA. Promotion between the two is decided by the company, not by a separate STCW certificate.

How long before a 3rd Officer can sit for Chief Mate?

For a Chief Mate CoC on ships of 3,000 GT or more, STCW Regulation II/2 requires at least 12 months of approved seagoing service as OICNW, plus the required training and exams. Your administration decides exactly which service counts and may add its own conditions. Check DG Shipping, MARINA or your own administration's current rules.

Is a 4th Officer or Junior Officer the same as a 3rd Officer?

Not quite. Junior Officer and 4th Officer are company titles, often given to newly certified officers who are gaining experience before taking a full 3rd Officer berth. They usually hold the same II/1 CoC, but the duties, and whether they keep an independent watch, depend on company policy.

Test yourself

On a ship using a traditional three-watch system, which watch does the 3rd Officer normally keep?

The 3rd Officer traditionally keeps the 8-12, the 2nd Officer the 12-4 and the Chief Officer the 4-8. This is custom and company practice; STCW Section A-VIII/2 sets watchkeeping principles but does not allocate watches by rank.

Under which STCW regulation is the CoC for an Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (500 GT or more) issued?

Regulation II/1 covers OICNW on ships of 500 GT or more. II/2 is Master and Chief Mate, II/3 is ships under 500 GT, and III/1 is engineering watch.

What are the minimum hours of rest for a watchkeeper under STCW and MLC 2006?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 requires watchkeepers to have at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days. MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 uses the same rest figures, though flag States may instead regulate by maximum hours of work.

At what maximum interval must a CoC be revalidated under STCW?

STCW Regulation I/11 requires revalidation at intervals not exceeding five years, with evidence of continued professional competence.

What sea service does STCW require for a Chief Mate CoC on ships of 3,000 GT or more?

STCW Regulation II/2 requires at least 12 months of approved seagoing service as an officer in charge of a navigational watch. Six months of supervised watchkeeping is part of the II/1 requirement.
