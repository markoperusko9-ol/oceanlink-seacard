Trainee on a Merchant Ship: Career Guide

Quick answer

A trainee is anyone on board who is completing structured, supervised seagoing service towards a certificate. That includes deck and engine cadets, ETO trainees, and trainee ratings in deck, engine or catering. The job is to learn, work safely under supervision, and get the training record book or sea-service record properly completed, because that record is what qualifies you for the next certificate.

What "trainee" actually means on board

"Trainee" is not one fixed rank. Companies and flag states use the word for several different positions, and your crew list or articles may show any of these:

  • Deck cadet / deck trainee. Working towards the Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch CoC (STCW Regulation II/1).
  • Engine cadet / engine trainee. Working towards the Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch CoC (STCW Regulation III/1).
  • Trainee ETO / electro-technical cadet. Working towards the Electro-Technical Officer CoC (STCW Regulation III/6).
  • Trainee ratings. Trainee OS, trainee wiper or GP (general purpose) trainee, working towards watch-rating certification under Regulations II/4 or III/4.
  • Catering trainees. Trainee cook or messman, where the flag and company run such a scheme.

What these positions have in common is that the person is supernumerary or junior, learns under supervision, and is collecting approved seagoing service. For officer cadets on the usual approved-training-programme route, STCW requires that service to be documented in an approved training record book. If your sea time is not recorded the way your administration wants it, it may not count. That is the most important thing to understand about being a trainee.

Duties and responsibilities

Your duties depend on the department and on the company's training programme. The pattern is broadly the same in all of them:

  • Learn and get signed off. Work through the tasks in your training record book (TRB) and have each one witnessed and signed by the responsible officer.
  • Assist under supervision. On deck this covers mooring, cargo watches, maintenance, pilot ladder rigging and bridge watches with the OOW. In the engine room it covers rounds, purifier cleaning, overhauls and soundings. The ETO trainee assists with electrical and automation maintenance.
  • Safety duties. You will have a muster list station and must take part in drills and know your emergency duties. You get these from familiarisation and basic training, not from experience.
  • Housekeeping and routine work. Expect to do your share of cleaning, painting, chipping and stores. These are part of the job, not a punishment.
  • Keep your own records. Maintain your TRB, sea-service records and any project or assignment work your institute or administration requires.

What you are not is a substitute for a certified crew member. You must not be left in charge of a watch, a permit-controlled job or a machinery space on your own. If you are asked to do something beyond your training, say so. This is not insubordination; it is what the ISM Code, your company's SMS and good practice expect.

Working pattern, hours and age limits

Typical pattern. Many cadets start on day work for familiarisation. Later they keep watches alongside an officer, often rotating so they see every watch and the cargo, manoeuvring and port work. Engine trainees on UMS ships usually do day work plus duty-engineer rounds with an engineer. The master and the training officer decide the pattern.

Hours of rest. The rest-hour rules apply to trainees in exactly the same way as to everyone else.

  • STCW Section A-VIII/1 requires at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • MLC 2006 (Standard A2.3) lets the flag state set either maximum hours of work (14 in 24, 72 in 7 days) or minimum hours of rest (10 in 24, 77 in 7 days).
  • Most flags and companies apply the rest-hour limits, which also satisfy STCW for watchkeepers.

Record your hours honestly. Falsified rest-hour records are a common Port State Control finding.

Under-18s. MLC 2006 sets a minimum age of 16 for work at sea. It also restricts night work and hazardous work for seafarers under 18.

  • The administration can allow exceptions to the night-work restriction where an approved training programme requires it.
  • Exactly what counts as hazardous work, and how the exceptions apply, is defined by your flag state and company.
  • If you are under 18, ask the master which tasks you are restricted from. Enclosed space entry, working aloft and hot work are the usual concerns.

Lookout duty. Some companies use cadets as lookout once they are properly instructed. Others do not count a cadet towards the lookout requirement under COLREG Rule 5. Follow your SMS and the master's standing orders.

Nobody reports to a trainee. Where the company appoints a designated shipboard training officer, find out on day one who it is. That person signs your TRB and reports your progress to the office. Who holds the role varies by company.

Certificates and documents you need

The exact list is set by your administration (for example DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines) and by the flag state of the ship. Typically a trainee needs the following before joining:

  • STCW basic training under Section A-VI/1. It covers:
    • personal survival techniques
    • fire prevention and fire fighting
    • elementary first aid
    • personal safety and social responsibilities
  • Security training under Section A-VI/6. This is security awareness, or designated security duties if you will be assigned them.
  • Valid seafarer medical certificate, issued under STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC.
  • Passport and seaman's book / CDC, plus any national registration (for example the INDoS number for Indian seafarers).
  • Visa and vaccinations for the trade. This may mean a US C1/D crew visa or a yellow fever certificate, depending on the company.
  • Training record book issued by your institute, company or administration. Officer cadets need it, and some rating schemes use one too.
  • Pre-sea course completion certificate. Many administrations require an approved pre-sea course before sea service counts.

Ship-type courses, such as tanker familiarisation, are normally done later. Check whether your company or administration expects them earlier. Whatever the rules, you must receive ship-specific familiarisation on joining before you are given safety duties.

How you get here and the next step up

Routes in:

  • Officer cadets usually come through an approved maritime degree, diploma or pre-sea programme. Examples are nautical science or marine engineering courses in India, or BSMT/BSMarE in the Philippines. Their institutes or sponsoring companies then place them on ships.
  • Trainee ratings usually complete an approved pre-sea rating course, such as the GP rating course in India. They then get a berth through a manning agent.

Sea time. STCW sets different minimums for each officer route:

  • OICNW (II/1): at least 12 months' approved seagoing service as part of an approved training programme documented in a TRB. Without such a programme, 36 months are required.
  • OICEW (III/1): at least 12 months of combined workshop skill training and approved seagoing service within an approved programme documented in a TRB. Without such a programme, 36 months are required, of which at least 30 must be at sea.
  • ETO (III/6): at least 12 months of combined workshop training and seagoing service, of which at least 6 months is at sea.

Your administration sets:

  • how that service is calculated
  • whether workshop time counts
  • how many days at sea versus in port are accepted

Check with your institute or administration. Do not rely on what someone on board did ten years ago.

Next step:

  • Deck cadet: OICNW CoC, then Third Officer (or Fourth/Junior Officer in some fleets).
  • Engine cadet: OICEW CoC, then Fourth/Third Engineer or Junior Engineer.
  • Trainee ETO: ETO CoC, then Electro-Technical Officer.
  • Trainee OS: watch rating certificate (II/4), then OS and later AB with Able Seafarer Deck.
  • Trainee wiper: watch rating certificate (III/4), then wiper/oiler and later Able Seafarer Engine.
  • Catering trainee: cook, with a ship's cook certificate where the flag requires it.

What crewing agencies look for in a trainee CV

Agencies know you have little or no sea time. They screen for reliability and completeness.

  • Clean, valid documents. List every certificate with its number, issuing authority and expiry date. An expired medical or basic training certificate is the fastest way to be rejected.
  • Approved pre-sea training. Name the institute and confirm it is approved by your administration.
  • Academic results and any shipboard or workshop practicals.
  • Sea time already done, if any. Give vessel type, GRT/kW, flag and dates exactly as shown in your CDC.
  • English ability and a clean record. Disciplinary history and references matter.
  • Availability and willingness for any trade. Cadets who will join bulk carriers or general cargo get berths faster than those holding out for tankers.

Avoid anyone who asks for payment in exchange for a berth. Check the agency's licence with your administration. In India, check DG Shipping's RPSL list. In the Philippines, check the DMW/licensed manning agency list. MLC 2006 Regulation 1.4 sets out the rules on recruitment and placement fees.

Oral-exam style: what the master or chief will ask you

Expect informal questioning in the first week and before any TRB sign-off. Typical questions:

  1. "What is your muster station and duty for fire and abandon ship?" Answer from the muster list, including your lifeboat or liferaft and your role.
  2. "What do you do if you find someone collapsed in a tank?" Raise the alarm. Do not enter. Follow the enclosed-space rescue procedure.
  3. "What are the minimum rest hours?" 10 in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days (STCW A-VIII/1; MLC rest-hour option).
  4. "Who signs your TRB?" The designated training officer, or the officer who witnessed the task, as your scheme requires.
  5. Deck cadets: "What does the OOW do before taking over the watch?" Engine cadets: "How do you sound a bilge or tank, and why record it?"

Worked scenario. It is your second week and the bosun tells you to rig the pilot ladder alone because everyone else is busy. You have seen it done once. The correct response is to say you have not yet been trained and checked on it, and to ask that an officer supervise. Pilot ladder rigging is safety-critical, and the officer must check it anyway. You are not refusing work. You are refusing to be the weak link.

Key points
  • "Trainee" covers deck and engine cadets, ETO trainees and trainee ratings; check what your crew list shows.
  • Your training record book and sea-service records are what count towards your next certificate, so keep them accurate and signed.
  • Trainees work under supervision and must never be left in sole charge of a watch or a permit-controlled job.
  • Rest-hour rules (STCW: 10 in 24, 77 in 7 days) apply to trainees exactly as to everyone else.
  • Under-18 trainees have restrictions on night and hazardous work under MLC 2006; the exact scope is set by flag and company.
  • Required documents and sea-time counting rules are set by your administration and flag state, so verify them yourself.
  • Never pay for a berth; check the manning agent's licence with your administration.

Frequently asked questions

Is a trainee the same as a cadet?

Not always. Cadet usually means an officer trainee on the deck, engine or ETO route. Trainee is also used for ratings such as trainee OS or trainee wiper. Your crew list and contract show which scheme you are on.

Does every day on board count as sea time for my CoC?

Not necessarily. Your administration decides what counts as approved seagoing service, including how port days and repair periods are treated and whether your TRB is complete. Check the rules with your institute or administration before you sign off.

Can a cadet keep a watch alone?

No. A cadet has no CoC and cannot be the officer in charge of a navigational or engineering watch. You keep watch with a certified officer, and whether you count as the lookout depends on the master's orders and the company SMS.

What happens if my training record book is lost or incomplete?

Report it to the master and your company immediately. Replacement and verification are handled by whoever issued the book, which may be the institute, company or administration. Without proper documentation your sea time may not be accepted, so keep photos of signed pages.

Do I need tanker courses before my first ship as a trainee?

That depends on the company, the ship type and your administration. STCW basic training, security training and a valid medical are the usual minimum, and tanker courses are normally done later. Some companies expect tanker courses earlier for tanker berths.

Test yourself

What is the minimum hours of rest in any 7-day period under STCW Section A-VIII/1?

STCW Section A-VIII/1 requires at least 77 hours' rest in any 7-day period and at least 10 hours in any 24. MLC 2006 sets the same rest limits as one of two options, the other being maximum hours of work.

Under STCW, how must a deck cadet's approved seagoing service within a training programme be documented?

STCW Regulation II/1 and Section A-II/1 require onboard training on the approved programme route to be documented in an approved training record book.

What is the minimum age for working on board a ship under MLC 2006?

MLC 2006 Regulation 1.1 and Standard A1.1 set a minimum age of 16, with further restrictions on night and hazardous work for under-18s.

Which STCW section covers basic training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR)?

Basic training is in STCW Code Section A-VI/1. A-VI/6 covers security training, A-II/4 covers watch ratings and A-VIII/1 covers fitness for duty and rest.

A bosun tells an untrained cadet to rig the pilot ladder alone. What is the correct response?

Pilot ladder rigging is safety-critical and must be supervised by a responsible officer under SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 23. Trainees work under supervision and should flag gaps in their competence.
