Oiler on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

An oiler is an engine-room rating who stands engineering watches or does day work under the direction of the engineer officers. The job covers machinery rounds, lubrication, tank soundings, cleaning and assisting with maintenance. You need STCW basic training and a certificate of proficiency as a rating forming part of an engineering watch (Regulation III/4), and many companies also want Able Seafarer Engine (III/5).

What an oiler actually does

The oiler is the engineers' extra pair of eyes, ears and hands. The title comes from the days when ratings went round the engine with an oil can. The modern job is broader, but the core is the same: keep the machinery running, spot trouble early and report it.

Typical duties include:

  • Machinery rounds: checking main and auxiliary engines, purifiers, compressors, pumps, coolers and the steering gear. You look for temperatures, pressures, levels, leaks, unusual noise and vibration.
  • Recording readings in the log or round sheet, if your ship uses one.
  • Lubrication: topping up sump and gravity tanks, and greasing points on the planned schedule.
  • Soundings: sounding fuel, lube oil, sludge, bilge and water tanks, and reporting the figures to the engineer.
  • Housekeeping: wiping leaks, cleaning strainers and filters when told to, and keeping plates and bilges clean. A clean engine room shows leaks quickly.
  • Maintenance support: helping engineers with overhauls of pumps, purifiers, auxiliary engine units, valves and heat exchangers, including rigging and lifting gear.
  • Bunkering and transfers: manning sounding points or manifolds, under the officer in charge.
  • Stores and spares: receiving and stowing engine stores.

What you do not do on your own authority matters just as much:

  • You do not discharge bilge water overboard.
  • You do not change the setpoints of automation.
  • You do not isolate safety devices.
  • You do not start work on live systems without the permit and isolation your company's procedures require.

Those decisions belong to the engineer officers.

Watch pattern, reporting lines and hours of rest

Your routine depends on how the engine room is run.

Engine room type Typical oiler routine
Manned (watchkeeping) engine room Watches alongside an engineer officer, commonly a three-watch system such as 4-on/8-off. Exact times are set by the ship.
Periodically unmanned (UMS) Mostly day work, often with a share of evening or pre-UMS rounds. Manned watches may still be kept in port, in restricted waters or during manoeuvring.

Who you report to:

  • On watch, you answer to the engineer officer in charge of the watch.
  • On day work, you normally take your jobs from the 2nd Engineer, who plans engine-room work.
  • The Chief Engineer has overall responsibility.
  • Some fleets also have a No. 1 Oiler, a Motorman or a Fitter who leads the ratings day to day.

Who reports to you: formally, usually nobody. In practice, experienced oilers guide wipers and engine cadets. Teaching them safe habits is part of being a good oiler.

Names vary by fleet. In some companies "oiler" and "motorman" mean the same job. In others the motorman is a separate, more senior rating. Check the crew list and your contract rather than assuming.

Hours of rest: STCW Section A-VIII/1 requires watchkeepers to have at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The flag may allow limited exceptions. MLC 2006 sets equivalent rest limits, or maximum working hours if the flag chooses that system. Your rest hours must be recorded. Make sure the record is honest. False rest records hurt you in any later investigation.

Certificates and competencies you need

The exact list depends on the flag state, your national administration and the company. The common international baseline is:

  • STCW basic training (Section A-VI/1): personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities. It must be kept valid. Refresher requirements apply to some modules.
  • Security training (Section A-VI/6): security awareness at minimum, or the designated security duties course if you are assigned security tasks.
  • Rating forming part of an engineering watch (Regulation III/4): this applies on seagoing ships with main propulsion power of 750 kW or more, for watches in a manned engine room or duties in a UMS engine room.
    • It covers basic watch routines, understanding orders, and reporting machinery faults, alarms and emergencies.
    • STCW sets the minimums. You must be at least 16, and you need either approved seagoing service that includes at least 6 months' training and experience, or special training that includes at least 2 months' approved seagoing service. Your administration may add requirements and decides how service is documented.
  • Able Seafarer Engine (Regulation III/5): a higher rating certificate. It builds on III/4 with more seagoing service and/or approved training.
    • Many companies prefer or require it for an oiler post, and some flags expect it for the more senior engine rating posts.
  • Medical fitness certificate: issued under STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006 Regulation 1.2 by a doctor recognised by your administration.
  • Identity and travel documents: passport and seaman's book (CDC), plus visas and vaccinations for your trade where needed.

Tanker, gas carrier or IGF-fuelled ships may require extra basic cargo or fuel training for ratings with related duties.

Where certificates are issued, and how much qualifying service counts, differs between administrations. Examples are DG Shipping in India and MARINA in the Philippines. Confirm with your administration or a recognised training institute, not with forum posts.

How people become oilers, and the next step up

Common routes in:

  1. Pre-sea rating course, then wiper. Most people start with an approved pre-sea course for engine ratings, which usually includes basic training. They then sail as a wiper or trainee rating to build seagoing service.
  2. Get the III/4 certificate. With the required service and/or training completed and signed off in your record book, you can apply for the III/4 certificate of proficiency.
  3. Promotion to oiler. Promotion depends on the company's assessment and a vacancy. A good word from your Chief and 2nd Engineer matters a lot here.

Next steps from oiler:

  • Able Seafarer Engine (III/5), if you do not already hold it.
  • Senior rating posts such as No. 1 Oiler, Motorman or Fitter. The fitter route usually needs proven welding and workshop skills, and some companies test this before hiring.
  • Engineer officer. Becoming an officer in charge of an engineering watch (Regulation III/1) requires approved education and training as well as seagoing service.
    • STCW Regulation III/1 itself allows a longer-service route: 36 months of combined workshop training and sea service, of which at least 30 months must be at sea in the engine department.
    • Approved education and training is still required on that route.
    • How administrations implement this varies. Get the current rules from your administration before committing time or money.

What crewing agencies look for in an oiler's CV

Crewing staff screen fast. Make the important facts easy to find.

  • Valid documents with expiry dates: passport, CDC, III/4 and/or III/5, basic training, security course and medical. Add your national identifiers, such as an INDoS number for Indian seafarers.
  • Sea service table: vessel name, type, GT, main engine make and model, propulsion power (kW), flag, rank, and sign-on and sign-off dates. No unexplained gaps.
  • Machinery experience: main engine family (for example MAN B&W or WinGD/Wärtsilä two-stroke), auxiliary engine makes, purifier makes, and boilers, OWS, fresh water generator, scrubber or ballast water treatment system if you have worked on them.
  • UMS or manned experience, and vessel types you know (tanker, bulk, container, gas).
  • Practical skills: welding, lathe work, refrigeration, and any certificates for them.
  • References and appraisals: a good appraisal from a Chief Engineer is worth more than a long list of courses.
  • Clean record: be honest about early sign-offs. Agencies check with previous employers.

Oral-exam style: questions a Chief Engineer will ask you

Whether at interview, an onboard assessment or a III/5 assessment, expect practical questions. Here are good-quality answers.

"What do you check on main engine rounds?"

  • Lube oil, jacket water, fuel and scavenge temperatures and pressures.
  • Exhaust temperatures, compared across units.
  • Cylinder lubricator operation.
  • Leaks, unusual noise or vibration.
  • Sump and expansion tank levels.
  • Report anything abnormal to the watch engineer straight away.

"On rounds you find the bilge well much higher than last round. What do you do?"

  1. Report to the watch engineer.
  2. Look for the source: gland leaks, pipe failures, a cooler, the stern tube area.
  3. Note the level and the rate of rise.
  4. Do not pump it overboard yourself. Bilge water goes through the oily water separator under engineer control, and the operation is recorded in the Oil Record Book by the officers, as MARPOL Annex I requires.

"The purifier is overflowing or alarming. Action?"

  • Tell the engineer.
  • If you have been trained and authorised on that machine, follow the posted procedure to stop the feed safely.
  • Never open a running bowl.

"The CO2 release alarm sounds in the engine room."

  • Leave immediately by the nearest safe escape route.
  • Do not finish your job or go to look for the cause.
  • Muster as the muster list requires and report so that everyone is accounted for.

"Before working on a pump, what must happen?"

  • A permit if your safety management system requires one.
  • Electrical and mechanical isolation with lock-out and tags.
  • Pressure released and system drained.
  • A check that it is safe before breaking any joint.

Practical tips from the engine room

  • Learn the ship in your first days. Escape routes, emergency fuel shut-offs, emergency fire pump, EEBDs, the fire alarm and the CO2 alarm. Your familiarisation should cover these, but walk them again yourself.
  • Carry a notebook. Write down normal readings. You can only spot "abnormal" if you know "normal".
  • Report early, even if unsure. No good engineer will scold you for a report that turned out to be nothing.
  • Protect your hands, ears and eyes. Gloves suited to the task, hearing protection in the engine room, goggles when grinding or handling chemicals.
  • Watch hot surfaces and leaks. Fuel spray on a hot surface is a classic cause of engine-room fires. Report missing lagging or spray shields.
  • Keep your sea service paperwork tidy. Get record book entries and testimonials signed before you sign off. Chasing signatures later is hard.
  • Ask to learn. Volunteer for overhauls and ask the engineers why things are done. That is how oilers become fitters, motormen and engineers.
Key points
  • An oiler is an engine rating who keeps watch or does day work under the engineer officers.
  • Core duties are machinery rounds, lubrication, soundings, cleaning and helping with maintenance.
  • STCW basic training, security training, a medical and a III/4 certificate are the usual minimum; many companies also want III/5.
  • On watch you report to the engineer in charge; on day work, usually the 2nd Engineer.
  • Never discharge bilges, override alarms or work on live systems on your own authority.
  • Sea service and certification rules vary by administration, so check with yours before planning a promotion.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an oiler and a wiper?

A wiper is the entry-level engine rating, mainly on cleaning and basic support work while gaining sea service. An oiler is more experienced and qualified to form part of an engineering watch, so they handle rounds, readings and soundings. The exact split of duties varies by company.

Is an oiler the same as a motorman?

It depends on the fleet. Some companies use the two titles for the same job. Others treat the motorman as a more senior rating above the oiler. Check the crew list and your contract for how your company defines the ranks.

Do I need Able Seafarer Engine (III/5) to sail as an oiler?

STCW sets III/4 as the requirement for a rating forming part of an engineering watch. Some flag states and many companies expect III/5 for an oiler post. Check the job requirement and your administration's rules.

Can an oiler become an engineer officer?

Yes, but you need a certificate of competency under STCW Regulation III/1. That requires approved education and training plus seagoing service. STCW itself allows a longer-service route of 36 months of combined workshop training and sea service, with at least 30 months at sea in the engine department, but approved education and training is still required. How administrations implement this varies, so ask yours for the current requirements before you apply.

Do oilers keep watches on UMS ships?

On periodically unmanned engine rooms, oilers usually work day hours, often with a share of rounds. Manned watches are commonly kept during manoeuvring, in port or in restricted waters. The exact routine is set by the Chief Engineer and the company's procedures.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers ratings forming part of an engineering watch?

Regulation III/4 covers engine ratings forming part of a watch. II/4 is the deck equivalent, III/1 is for engineer officers and VI/1 is basic training (STCW Convention, Regulation III/4).

On rounds you find the bilge level rising quickly. What is the correct first action?

A rating must report at once and help find the source. Oily bilge discharge is controlled and recorded by officers under MARPOL Annex I.

The engine-room CO2 release alarm sounds while you are cleaning a strainer. What do you do?

The pre-discharge alarm gives time to evacuate before CO2 is released. Staying in the space can be fatal (SOLAS Chapter II-2 and FSS Code fixed gas systems).

What is the normal minimum rest required for a watchkeeping rating?

STCW sets the normal minimum for watchkeepers at 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period, subject to limited exceptions allowed by the flag (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3).

Above what propulsion power does the STCW III/4 requirement apply to engine-room watch ratings?

Regulation III/4 applies on seagoing ships with main propulsion machinery of 750 kW propulsion power or more. Ratings under training and those doing unskilled work are excluded (STCW Convention, Regulation III/4).
