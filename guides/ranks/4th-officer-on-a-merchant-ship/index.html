4th Officer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

A 4th Officer is the most junior certificated deck officer on ships whose manning includes the rank. They hold an STCW Regulation II/1 Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch CoC and usually keep watches, sometimes alongside a senior officer, while assisting the senior officers with day work. The next step is normally 3rd Officer on the same CoC, then 2nd Officer, then Chief Mate after the next CoC.

Where the 4th Officer fits in

Most cargo ships carry three watchkeeping deck officers: Chief Officer, 2nd Officer and 3rd Officer. A 4th Officer is an additional junior officer. You find the rank mainly on:

  • Cruise ships and large passenger vessels, where bigger bridge teams are normal
  • LNG and LPG carriers and some large tankers, where cargo watches in port need extra officers
  • Some container ships and Japanese-managed or owner-operated fleets, which use the 4th Officer as a training berth for newly certificated officers

The title is not standardised. Some companies call the same berth "Junior Officer", "Fourth Mate" or "Junior 3rd Officer". Others don't use the rank at all, so newly certificated officers join directly as 3rd Officer.

In certification terms a 4th Officer is an Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (OICNW). That is the same STCW level as a 3rd Officer or 2nd Officer. The rank describes your place in the shipboard hierarchy, not a separate certificate.

Duties and responsibilities

Duties depend on the company's Safety Management System (SMS), the ship type and the master's standing orders. The usual pattern is below.

At sea

  • Keeping a navigational watch, either as the OOW or doubled up with a senior officer while you gain experience. The master decides which.
  • Position fixing, collision avoidance under COLREG, monitoring ECDIS, radar/ARPA and other equipment, logbook entries, and calling the master as the standing orders require.
  • Assisting the 2nd Officer with chart and publication corrections, NAVTEX and navigational warnings, and passage plan preparation.
  • Assisting the 3rd Officer, or taking over part of the work, with life-saving appliances (LSA) and fire-fighting appliances (FFA): inspections, checklists and drill preparation.

In port

  • Gangway and deck watches, including draft and mooring checks and security duties under the Ship Security Plan.
  • Cargo watches under the Chief Officer's instructions. On gas carriers and tankers this is often the 4th Officer's main port job.
  • A mooring station, usually aft, under the Chief Officer or a senior officer.

Administrative

  • Keeping records the senior officers delegate to you. Typical examples are drill records, equipment checklists and the hours of rest records for your own watch.

The principle examiners keep returning to: when you are the OOW, you are the master's representative on the bridge. That holds whatever your rank title.

Reporting lines and working pattern

Situation Reports to Directs
Navigational watch Master Lookout/helmsman (AB or OS) on watch
Day work (cargo, deck, LSA/FFA) Chief Officer, sometimes via 2nd/3rd Officer Ratings assigned to the task
Emergencies As per muster list As per muster list

Watch pattern. No single pattern applies. Common arrangements include:

  • Sharing a watch with the master or a senior officer
  • Taking one of the three sea watches so another officer is freed for day work
  • Being a dedicated day worker at sea who keeps cargo watches in port

The master sets this in line with the SMS.

Hours of rest. Whatever the pattern, the STCW Code (Section A-VIII/1) and MLC 2006 (Standard A2.3) set minimums:

  • At least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of them at least 6 hours long.
  • The interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.
  • Flag states may permit limited exceptions under strict conditions.

Under MLC 2006 a flag state may instead set maximum hours of work (not more than 14 in any 24 hours and 72 in any 7 days). For watchkeepers the STCW rest minimums apply in all cases.

Record your hours honestly. Port State Control inspectors check these records, and falsified rest records are a serious matter.

Certificates and competencies

Exact requirements are set by your administration (the country that issued your CoC) and the flag state of the ship. Company policy and charterer requirements are layered on top. In general terms a 4th Officer needs the following.

Certificate of Competency

  • An OICNW CoC under STCW Regulation II/1, with competence shown against the standards in Section A-II/1.
  • In India this is the Second Mate (FG) CoC issued by the DG Shipping. In the Philippines it is issued by MARINA.

Endorsement

  • A flag state endorsement where the ship's flag differs from the CoC-issuing country.

Radio

  • A GMDSS General Operator's Certificate (GOC) under STCW Regulation IV/2. This is normally required for officers performing radio duties on ships trading beyond sea area A1. A Restricted Operator's Certificate (ROC) may be enough for A1-only ships where the administration allows.

Proficiency certificates

  • Basic training (A-VI/1)
  • PSCRB (A-VI/2)
  • Advanced fire fighting (A-VI/3)
  • Medical first aid (A-VI/4, paragraphs 1–3)
  • Security training appropriate to your duties (A-VI/6)

Ship-type and equipment training

  • Tanker or gas carrier endorsements (STCW Chapter V), for tankers and gas carriers.
  • ECDIS generic training, plus type-specific familiarisation for the equipment fitted, as the flag and SMS require.
  • Bridge Resource Management, which many companies require.

Documents

  • A valid seafarer medical certificate
  • Seaman's book (Indian CDC, Philippine SIRB)
  • Passport
  • Visas as required

Check revalidation dates on everything. A CoC or proficiency certificate that expires mid-contract can force a sign-off.

How you get here and where you go next

Getting to 4th Officer

The usual route is deck cadet, then OICNW CoC. Under STCW Regulation II/1 the minimums are:

  • Age at least 18
  • At least 12 months of approved seagoing service within an approved training programme with a documented training record book, or at least 36 months otherwise
  • At least six months of bridge watchkeeping under supervision of the master or a qualified officer within that service

Administrations add their own requirements on top of these minimums: pre-sea courses, written and oral exams, and the routes they accept. Use your own administration's current rules, not someone else's.

In some fleets ratings who qualify for a CoC also enter this way. Some companies also put newly certificated officers in a 4th Officer or Junior Officer berth for their first contract.

Next steps

  1. 3rd Officer. This is normally a company promotion on the same CoC, based on appraisal reports and the master's recommendation.
  2. 2nd Officer. Usually also on the OICNW CoC, again a company decision.
  3. Chief Mate CoC (STCW Regulation II/2). This needs further approved seagoing service as an officer in charge of a navigational watch, plus courses and exams. STCW sets a minimum, and many administrations demand more or specify how it must be served. Confirm with your administration before you plan your sea time.

Sea service as 4th Officer generally counts as OICNW service if it appears correctly in your discharge book or sea service testimonial. Make sure it does.

What crewing agencies look for in a 4th Officer CV

Manning agents shortlist quickly, so make the essentials easy to find:

  • CoC details at the top: issuing country, grade, number and expiry, plus any flag endorsements already held.
  • Ship-type experience: cadet time on LNG, tankers or cruise counts. State the vessel type, GT/DWT and engine type for each ship.
  • Exact sea service dates that match your discharge book. Mismatches are a frequent reason for a CV to be set aside.
  • ECDIS makes you have used, plus GOC, tanker or gas endorsements and BRM.
  • Document validity: passport, seaman's book, medical, visas (for example a US C1/D) and yellow fever where relevant.
  • Previous company and references, and your reason for leaving if it was mid-contract.
  • Registry numbers where your country uses them, such as an INDoS number for Indian seafarers.

Keep it to two pages, use a plain format, and don't pad it. A clear, accurate CV from a new officer beats a decorated one.

Oral exam and interview corner

Company interviews and CoC orals for junior officers often probe the same ground. Try answering these before reading the notes.

1. "The master is on the bridge. Who has the watch?" You do, until the master tells you specifically that they have taken over and you both understand it (STCW Code A-VIII/2).

2. "When do you call the master?" Cover the following:

  • Whenever the standing orders require it
  • Restricted visibility
  • Traffic or other ships' movements causing concern
  • Difficulty keeping course
  • Failure to sight expected land or a navigation mark
  • An unexpected change in soundings
  • Equipment failure
  • Heavy weather damage concerns
  • Any doubt at all

Finish with: you take immediate action for safety first if needed.

3. "A target is on a steady bearing with the range closing. What do you do?" Work through Rules 5, 7, 8 and the relevant Rule 13–17 or Rule 19. Make the action early, substantial and readily apparent, and use all available means to confirm.

4. "Your relief arrives and is clearly unfit. What do you do?" Don't hand over. Inform the master.

Worked scenario: your first watch on a new ship

  1. Before taking over, complete your safety familiarisation.
  2. Read the master's standing orders and the night order book.
  3. Locate the emergency steering changeover, BNWAS reset, VHF/DSC and the general alarm.
  4. At handover, check position, course, speed, traffic, the passage plan ahead, and equipment status.
  5. Take over only when your eyes have adjusted to the dark and you are satisfied with the situation.
Key points
  • A 4th Officer is a junior deck officer holding an STCW II/1 OICNW CoC, the same level as a 3rd Officer.
  • The rank is mainly found on cruise ships, gas carriers and some large-ship fleets; many companies don't use it.
  • On watch you report to the master; for day work, usually to the Chief Officer.
  • Watchkeepers must get at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days under STCW.
  • Certificate requirements vary by administration, flag and company, so check your own administration's current rules.
  • Promotion to 3rd and 2nd Officer is usually a company decision; Chief Mate needs a new CoC under STCW II/2.
  • A CV with clear CoC details and sea service dates matching your discharge book gets shortlisted faster.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 4th Officer the same as a Junior Officer?

Often, yes. Companies use different titles for the same berth, including 4th Officer, Fourth Mate and Junior Officer. What matters is the CoC you hold and the duties the master assigns under the SMS.

Do I need a different CoC to be promoted from 4th to 3rd Officer?

Normally no. Both ranks sit at the OICNW level under STCW Regulation II/1, so promotion is a company decision based on performance and appraisals. A new CoC is needed when you move up to Chief Mate.

Does a 4th Officer keep a watch alone?

It depends on the master and the company. Some 4th Officers keep a full watch as OOW. Others are doubled up with a senior officer until the master is satisfied with their experience on that ship.

Does sea time as 4th Officer count towards the Chief Mate CoC?

Generally it counts as service as an officer in charge of a navigational watch, provided it is correctly recorded in your discharge book and testimonials. Each administration sets its own rules on what qualifies, so confirm with yours before relying on it.

Which ship types usually employ 4th Officers?

Cruise and passenger ships, LNG and LPG carriers, and some large tanker, container and Japanese-managed fleets most commonly carry the rank. On most bulk carriers and smaller cargo ships the deck team stops at 3rd Officer.

Test yourself

Under STCW, what are the minimum hours of rest for a watchkeeping officer?

The minimum is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period (STCW Code A-VIII/1; also MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 where the flag regulates hours of rest).

Which STCW regulation covers the CoC normally held by a 4th Officer?

Regulation II/1 covers officers in charge of a navigational watch. II/2 is master and chief mate, II/4 is ratings, and VI/1 is basic safety training.

The master comes to the bridge during your watch. Who is responsible for the watch?

The OOW stays responsible despite the master's presence until the master specifically states they have assumed it (STCW Code A-VIII/2).

Within the approved seagoing service for an OICNW CoC, how much supervised bridge watchkeeping does STCW require as a minimum?

Regulation II/1 requires at least six months of bridge watchkeeping under the supervision of the master or a qualified officer.

Your relief arrives for the watch but is clearly unfit to take over. What should you do?

The watch must not be handed over to an officer who is not capable of carrying out the duties; the master must be informed (STCW Code A-VIII/2).
