Assistant Engineer on a Merchant Ship

Quick answer

An Assistant Engineer is a junior engine-room officer who works under the watch engineers and the Second Engineer. On many fleets they have finished cadetship and are working towards, or have just obtained, an STCW Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch (OICEW) certificate. Whether the rank needs a CoC, and whether the holder keeps a watch alone, depends on the company, the flag state and the ship's Safe Manning Document. The usual next step is Fourth or Third Engineer once a CoC is held.

What the title actually means

'Assistant Engineer' is not a single, defined STCW rank. STCW defines certificates and functions, and companies give them rank titles. Check what a particular employer means before you apply.

The common uses are:

  • Uncertified junior engineer. Many fleets, including some Indian and Filipino crewing operations, use this meaning. The person has completed engine cadetship or a graduate marine engineering programme and is gaining sea service or preparing for CoC exams. Some companies call this rank Junior Engineer, Fifth Engineer or Trainee Marine Engineer.
  • Certified junior engineer. Some fleets, including some cruise and passenger operators, use 'Assistant Engineer' for an officer who already holds an OICEW CoC but is the most junior watch officer.
  • US-style titles. On US-flag ships, Third, Second and First Assistant Engineer are licensed engineer ranks. They are a different thing altogether.

This guide covers the first two meanings, which are the ones most seafarers in our readership meet. When you read a job advert, check two things: whether a CoC is required, and whether the post is a watchkeeping post on the Safe Manning Document.

Duties and day-to-day work

The Second Engineer allocates the work, and the Chief Engineer is responsible for the whole department. Typical duties are:

  • Watchkeeping support. You keep a watch with a certified engineer, or do duty rounds on a UMS (periodically unattended machinery space) ship. That means taking readings, checking levels, temperatures and pressures, looking for leaks, and reporting anything abnormal straight away.
  • Planned maintenance. You overhaul purifiers, pumps, valves, heat exchangers, compressors and auxiliary engine components under supervision, and record the jobs in the planned maintenance system.
  • Fuel and lube oil. You help with sounding tanks, transfers, purifier operation and bunkering, following the bunker plan and checklist.
  • Environmental equipment. You assist with the oily water separator, sewage plant and incinerator, and keep the record entries accurate. Port State Control officers look closely at these entries.
  • Safety. You prepare enclosed space entries, hot work and isolations under the permit-to-work and lock-out tag-out system. You also carry out your duties on the muster list during drills.
  • Stores and housekeeping. You keep spares inventories and tool stores, and keep the engine room clean.

A good Assistant Engineer is often given one or two systems to own, such as the purifiers, the freshwater generator or the air compressors. You learn far more from that than from general rounds.

Reporting line and working pattern

Direction Who
Overall authority Chief Engineer, responsible to the Master
Day-to-day tasking Second Engineer
On watch The officer in charge of the engineering watch (Third or Fourth Engineer, depending on fleet)
May supervise Engine cadets, wipers, and oilers or motormen on specific jobs, as the Second Engineer directs

Working pattern depends on the ship:

  • Manned engine room. You usually keep watches, often the traditional 4-on/8-off pattern, plus day work as the Second Engineer arranges.
  • UMS ship. You usually do day work, often around 0800 to 1700 with breaks, plus a share of duty-engineer nights or alarm response as the company's procedures allow. An uncertified Assistant Engineer is not normally the sole duty engineer.

The working pattern must stay within the hours of rest set by STCW and MLC 2006. These require at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and at least 77 hours in any 7-day period. Rest may be split into no more than two periods, and one of them must be at least 6 hours long. Record your hours honestly. False records hurt you at an inspection and hide fatigue.

Certificates and competencies

Usually needed to sign on (the exact list depends on the flag, the company and the ship type):

  • STCW Basic Training under Regulation VI/1: personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Security awareness training under Regulation VI/6. If you have designated security duties, you need the higher level of training.
  • A valid seafarer medical fitness certificate, under STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006.
  • Passport, seaman's book or CDC, and any national registration. For Indian seafarers this is the INDoS number.
  • Ship-type training where required. For example, tankers need Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations.
  • Flag state documents, such as an endorsement where a CoC is held, as the flag requires.

What you are working towards. The OICEW certificate is issued under STCW Regulation III/1, with the standard of competence in Section A-III/1. That standard covers:

  • Marine engineering at the operational level: watchkeeping, main and auxiliary machinery, and fuel, lube, ballast and other pumping systems.
  • Electrical, electronic and control engineering.
  • Maintenance and repair, including workshop skills.
  • Controlling the operation of the ship and care for persons on board, including pollution prevention, stability awareness, fire and LSA, and leadership and teamwork.

The minimum sea service and training routes are set out in Regulation III/1. Each administration implements them in its own way, for example through:

  • an approved training programme documented in a training record book, or
  • a longer service route.

Check your own administration's current rules before you plan your exams. Do not rely on what someone did ten years ago.

How you get there and where you go next

Common entry routes:

  1. An approved marine engineering degree or diploma, followed by sea time as an engine cadet with a completed training record book.
  2. A graduate entry scheme for mechanical or electrical engineers, where the administration recognises one. These schemes usually include a pre-sea course and approved sea service.
  3. Promotion from engine rating (oiler, motorman or fitter) after enough approved service and the required training. Some administrations allow this, but the conditions vary.

Next step: once you pass the OICEW exams and receive the CoC, companies usually promote you to Fourth Engineer, or to Third Engineer on fleets without a Fourth.

The later path runs Third Engineer, Second Engineer, then Chief Engineer. Second Engineer and Chief Engineer need the management-level certificates under Regulation III/2 for ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more, or III/3 for ships of 750 kW up to 3,000 kW. Each of those certificates requires further sea service and exams.

National names for the first certificate differ. India's DG Shipping and the Philippines' MARINA use their own grade titles and exam structures. All of them lead to the STCW III/1 level.

Worked scenario: your first purifier alarm

You are on rounds with the watch engineer busy on the auxiliary engine. The HFO purifier alarms for low discharge pressure and starts losing oil through the sludge outlet.

  1. Make it safe. Follow the maker's procedure and the ship's instructions. Typically that means stopping the oil feed so the oil recirculates or goes back to the settling tank, so that fuel is not lost to the sludge tank.
  2. Check the service tank level at once. Your first concern is keeping the main engine and generators supplied.
  3. Inform the watch engineer. If you cannot reach them, call the duty engineer by the agreed method. Do not try a restart cycle on your own unless you have been trained on that unit and authorised to do it.
  4. Look for likely causes with the watch engineer:
    • operating water pressure or supply
    • feed temperature
    • the wrong gravity disc for the current oil density
    • a worn bowl seal or dirty bowl
  5. Record it. Write up the alarm, the action taken and the sludge tank level change. Sludge tank quantities link to the Oil Record Book, so they must add up.

Examiners and Chief Engineers want this order: safe, supply, report, diagnose, record. Do not try to be a hero.

Oral-exam style questions to start preparing now

Use your time as Assistant Engineer to build answers from the actual plant on board, not just from books.

  • What do you check before taking over a watch? Machinery running and on standby, any special instructions, tank levels, any ongoing work or isolations, and the condition of the bilges. Also confirm that the relieving engineer is fit to take over. STCW Section A-VIII/2 covers watch handover.
  • What is the OWS discharge limit and how is it enforced? 15 ppm under MARPOL Annex I (Regulations 14 and 15). The 15 ppm filtering equipment is monitored by a 15 ppm bilge alarm. On ships of 10,000 GT and above, an automatic stopping device must also halt discharge when the limit is exceeded. Know where the overboard valve is, how it is sealed or controlled, and how operations are recorded in the Oil Record Book.
  • What precautions apply after a crankcase oil mist alarm? Follow the maker's and the company's procedure. That generally means reducing load or stopping, keeping clear of the relief doors, and not opening the crankcase until it has cooled sufficiently. Be ready to explain why opening it early is dangerous.
  • How is a starting air line protected against explosion? Know the fittings on your own engine, such as non-return valves, flame arresters and relief or bursting devices, and why each one is there.
  • How is the emergency generator tested and how quickly must it take load? Learn the requirement from SOLAS Chapter II-1 and the actual test routine on your ship.
  • How do you isolate a pump for overhaul? Walk through the permit, electrical isolation and tagging, valve isolation, draining, and the checks you make before work starts.

What crewing agencies look for, and practical tips

What a strong CV for this rank shows:

  • Exact sea service: vessel names, IMO numbers, ship types, flags, sign-on and sign-off dates, and main engine make and power. Avoid unexplained gaps.
  • Machinery makes you have actually worked on, such as main engine make and model, purifier and OWS makers, and boiler type. Only list equipment you could talk about at interview.
  • A completed training record book, and your CoC exam status or a realistic date for it.
  • All certificates in date, with expiry dates listed. An expired medical or basic training certificate is a common reason for a CV to be rejected.
  • Appraisals or recommendations from your Chief Engineer, plus a clean record with no unexplained early sign-offs.

Tips from the engine room:

  • Carry a notebook. Sketch every system you work on, and trace lines physically from tank to pump to consumer.
  • Ask the Second Engineer to give you ownership of a system and the maintenance on it.
  • Read the Chief Engineer's standing orders and the company SMS procedures for the engine room in your first week.
  • Never sign a record for work you did not do or see done.
  • If you are fatigued or unsure, say so. A good engine room depends on people speaking up early.
Key points
  • 'Assistant Engineer' is a company rank title, not a defined STCW rank. Whether a CoC is required depends on the fleet and flag.
  • You report day to day to the Second Engineer, with the Chief Engineer in overall charge.
  • You need STCW basic training, security awareness, a valid medical and the national documents to sign on.
  • The target certificate is the OICEW CoC under STCW Regulation III/1, and service requirements are set by your administration.
  • Hours of rest under STCW and MLC are at least 10 hours in 24 and 77 hours in 7 days.
  • The usual promotion is to Fourth or Third Engineer once the OICEW CoC is held.
  • Agencies want exact sea service, real machinery experience, in-date certificates and good appraisals.

Frequently asked questions

Is an Assistant Engineer the same as a Junior Engineer or Fifth Engineer?

On many fleets these titles describe the same uncertified junior engine officer post. Some companies, however, use 'Assistant Engineer' for a certified watchkeeper. Always check whether the advert requires an OICEW CoC.

Can an Assistant Engineer keep an engine-room watch alone?

Only if they hold the appropriate OICEW CoC, with a flag endorsement where needed, and the company and Safe Manning Document allow it. An uncertified Assistant Engineer works under the supervision of a certified watch engineer.

Does sea time as Assistant Engineer count towards my CoC?

Generally yes, if it is approved seagoing service in the engine department and properly documented. However, each administration decides what counts and what records it needs. Confirm with your administration and keep your discharge book and training record book complete.

What is the next rank after Assistant Engineer?

Once you hold the OICEW CoC, most companies promote you to Fourth Engineer. Fleets without that rank promote you to Third Engineer. The timing depends on vacancies and your appraisals.

What makes an Assistant Engineer CV stand out to a crewing agency?

Clear, verifiable sea service with vessel and engine details, in-date certificates, and a completed training record book. Good appraisals from Chief Engineers help, and so does a clear plan for your CoC exams.

Test yourself

Under STCW and MLC 2006, what is the minimum rest a watchkeeping engineer must have?

The minimum is 10 hours in 24 and 77 hours in 7 days. The other figures are common misremembered versions. Reference: STCW Code Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006 Standard A2.3.

Which STCW regulation covers the Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch certificate?

III/1 is the OICEW certificate. III/2 is for Chief and Second Engineers on ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more, III/4 is for ratings forming part of an engineering watch, and III/6 is for ETOs. Reference: STCW Convention, Chapter III.

Who holds overall responsibility for the engine department on board?

The Chief Engineer is responsible for the engine department under the Master's overall authority. The Second Engineer runs the daily work. Reference: STCW Code Section A-VIII/2.

The engineer coming to relieve you appears clearly unfit to take over the watch. What should you do?

The watch must not be handed over to an engineer who is obviously not capable, and the Chief Engineer must be notified. Reference: STCW Code Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-2.

What is the oil content limit for discharge of machinery space bilge water through the OWS?

The limit is 15 ppm (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 15). The equipment requirements, including the 15 ppm bilge alarm and, on ships of 10,000 GT and above, automatic stopping, are in Regulation 14.
