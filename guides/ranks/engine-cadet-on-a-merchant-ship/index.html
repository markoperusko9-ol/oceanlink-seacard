Engine Cadet on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Training and Career Path

Quick answer

An engine cadet (called a Trainee Marine Engineer in some companies) is a trainee who completes approved seagoing training in the engine department. That training, recorded in a training record book, is used to qualify for a Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch under STCW Regulation III/1. You report to the Chief Engineer through a designated training officer, usually the Second Engineer. The next step is a junior engineer officer post, such as 4th Engineer or equivalent.

What an engine cadet actually does on board

The engine cadet is in the engine room to learn. Your output is not just work done. It is competence, and that competence must be shown and signed off in your training record book (TRB). In practice you will also be expected to pull your weight with the engine crew.

Typical work across a contract:

  • Machinery rounds with the duty engineer: checking temperatures, pressures, levels and leaks, and taking log readings.
  • Planned maintenance under supervision: purifier overhauls, pump and valve overhauls, heat exchanger cleaning, compressor decarbonising, and main and auxiliary engine unit overhauls when they come up.
  • Fuel and lube oil work: sounding tanks, assisting at bunkering, and transferring fuel to settling and service tanks.
  • Environmental systems: running the oily water separator, sewage plant and incinerator with an engineer present, and learning which records go in which logbook.
  • Workshop jobs: lathe work, welding and fabrication when the engineers allow it and supervision is in place.
  • Manoeuvring: standing by in the engine control room during arrival and departure, and learning what the engineers do and why.
  • Drills and safety rounds: taking an assigned role on the muster list.

You are not a certificated watchkeeper. You must not be left as the person in charge of a machinery space. Any job carrying real hazard, such as enclosed space entry, hot work or electrical isolation, must go through the ship's permit and isolation system with a responsible officer in charge.

Working pattern and watches

Your pattern depends mainly on whether the engine room is manned or periodically unattended (UMS).

Engine room type Typical cadet pattern
UMS (most modern cargo ships) Day work with the engine crew. You may shadow the duty engineer on evening rounds or answer alarms with them.
Manned watches You may be placed on a watch, often 4-on/8-off, alongside a qualified engineer officer.

Many companies move cadets between day work and watches during a contract. The aim is to give you both maintenance experience and the supervised watchkeeping your TRB needs.

Hours of rest apply to you in the same way as everyone else:

  • STCW Section A-VIII/1 sets a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 sets the same rest limits, or equivalent maximum working hours, depending on the flag state's choice.
  • Record your hours honestly on the rest-hour sheets.
  • If you are under 18, MLC 2006 restricts night work and certain hazardous work. Your company's procedures will set out how this is applied.

Expect long days during port stays, bunkering and major overhauls. Rest must still be managed and recorded.

Chain of command: who you report to

Reporting line:

  • Chief Engineer: overall responsibility for the engine department, including your training on board.
  • Second Engineer: runs daily work and planned maintenance. Usually your direct supervisor and very often the designated shipboard training officer for engine cadets.
  • Third and Fourth Engineers / Junior Engineers: the officers you will work and watch alongside most. They often sign individual TRB tasks you complete with them.
  • ETO: you will work with them on electrical jobs and should ask to join when safe.
  • Company training officer or superintendent ashore: reviews your TRB and progress reports.

Who reports to you: formally, no one. Ratings such as the Fitter, Motorman, Oiler and Wiper are not under your command. In practice they will teach you a great deal, so treat them accordingly. You may be asked to supervise a simple job under an engineer's direction. That is training in leadership, not authority.

The exact titles and the split between 3rd and 4th Engineer vary by company. The principle does not: an engine cadet works under supervision at all times.

Certificates and documents you need to sail

The exact list is set by the flag state, your national maritime administration and the company. These are the general requirements you will meet almost everywhere.

Mandatory STCW training (minimum for anyone with designated safety duties):

  • Basic Safety Training under STCW Section A-VI/1:
    • Personal Survival Techniques
    • Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting
    • Elementary First Aid
    • Personal Safety and Social Responsibilities
  • Security training under STCW Section A-VI/6. For cadets this is usually security awareness. Some companies require designated security duties training.

Other documents:

  • A valid seafarer medical certificate (STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006).
  • Passport and seafarer's identity or discharge book (CDC / Seaman's Book) issued by your administration.
  • Your national registration. For Indian seafarers this is the INDoS number. Other countries have their own systems.
  • Vessel-type courses where needed. For example, basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations (STCW Section A-V/1-1) if you will join an oil or chemical tanker, or basic training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations (STCW Section A-V/1-2) for a gas tanker.
  • Visas such as the US C1/D if the company trades there.

Pre-sea training: most administrations require an approved pre-sea course or degree before your first sailing as a cadet. Examples include marine engineering degree or diploma programmes, or graduate conversion courses for mechanical engineers in some countries. Names, durations and entry rules differ widely, so check your own administration's current approved list.

Only use training institutes approved by your administration. Uncertified or fake course certificates will end a career before it starts.

From cadet to engineer officer: the route and the next step

What STCW requires

The target certificate is Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch (OICEW) under STCW Regulation III/1. It covers ships with main propulsion of 750 kW or more. In outline, the Convention requires:

  • a minimum age of 18;
  • approved education and training to the standard in STCW Section A-III/1;
  • combined workshop skill training and approved seagoing service of either:
    • at least 12 months as part of an approved training programme documented in an approved training record book, or
    • a longer route of at least 36 months, of which at least 30 must be engine department sea service;
  • engine-room watchkeeping duties performed during the required seagoing service, under the supervision of the chief engineer officer or a qualified engineer officer, for a period of not less than six months.

What your administration adds

Your administration builds its own scheme on top of these STCW minimums. That covers how the pre-sea course, workshop training, sea time and exams fit together, what the certificate is called, and any extra courses. Those rules are what actually decide your eligibility, so get them from the administration's own published requirements, not from a forum.

The usual sequence

  1. Finish approved pre-sea training.
  2. Sail as engine cadet and complete your TRB.
  3. Collect sea service testimonials.
  4. Complete any required post-sea courses.
  5. Pass the written and oral examinations for the OICEW CoC.

The next rank

After the CoC you are usually appointed as Fourth Engineer, Junior Engineer or Assistant Engineer, depending on company structure. Some go straight to Third Engineer. Promotion then depends on sea service and company appraisal. It leads towards the Second Engineer and Chief Engineer CoC under STCW Regulation III/2 (3,000 kW or more) or III/3 (750–3,000 kW).

What crewing agencies look for in a cadet CV

Cadet berths are competitive. A clean, verifiable CV matters more than a long one.

Include:

  • Pre-sea training: institute name, approval details and results. State the name of the course, not just "marine engineering".
  • STCW certificates: certificate numbers and expiry dates, listed in a table.
  • Medical and documents: validity of medical, passport, CDC and national registration such as INDoS. List any visas held.
  • Workshop experience: lathe, welding, fitting. Be honest about your actual level.
  • Previous sea time, if any: vessel type, engine make and type, propulsion power in kW, and dates. Precision here shows you were paying attention.
  • TRB progress: for a second cadet contract, state roughly how much of the record book is complete.
  • References: from your institute or previous Chief Engineer, with contact details.

Avoid:

  • Gaps you cannot explain.
  • Inflated claims, such as "experienced in main engine overhaul" after watching once.
  • Photos of certificates that do not match what you will present on joining.

Agencies check documents against issuing authorities. A small inconsistency, such as a misspelled name or wrong date of birth across documents, can delay a joining. Fix those before you apply.

Oral exam style: what a cadet should already be able to answer

At your OICEW oral, an examiner will expect your cadet time to show. These are typical questions with the kind of answer that passes.

"Why is a flame arrester or bursting disc fitted on the starting air line to each unit?" A leaking cylinder start valve can let hot combustion gas into the air line. If there is oil in the line, this can cause a starting air line explosion. The arrester or relief device limits the damage and stops it spreading through the manifold. Prevention means draining air bottles and keeping start valves maintained.

"The oily water separator alarm sounds while pumping bilges. What happens and what do you do?" If oil content exceeds 15 ppm, the bilge alarm activates. Where an automatic stopping device is fitted (mandatory on ships of 10,000 GT and above), overboard discharge stops and the flow is recirculated. Otherwise, stop discharge manually. Then investigate, for example the filter, emulsions or bilge contents. Make the correct Oil Record Book entries under the ship's procedures. Never tamper with or bypass the monitor.

"How do you prepare for a crankcase inspection after stopping the main engine?" - Follow the maker's waiting time for cooling. - Engage the turning gear and isolate starting air. - Get the correct permit and isolation in place. - Ventilate, then check for oil mist and heat before entry.

Opening a hot crankcase can admit air to oil mist and risk an explosion.

"What did you personally do in your TRB?" Be ready to describe one job end to end: isolation, tools, readings, problems, reassembly and test. Examiners spot borrowed answers quickly.

Practical tips for your first contract

Learning the ship

  • Trace systems with a drawing in hand. Follow fuel, lube oil, cooling water and bilge lines physically from tank to consumer. It is the single best habit a cadet can build.
  • Keep a personal notebook. Record readings, valve positions, faults and how they were fixed. It becomes your revision for the CoC exam.

Your training record book

  • Get TRB tasks signed as you go. Do not leave it for the last month, or for when your training officer is signing off.
  • Ask before you touch. If you are not sure whether a valve, breaker or pump is safe to operate, stop and ask. Nobody fails a contract for asking. People do get hurt by guessing.

Safety and people

  • Respect permits, lock-out tag-out and enclosed space rules. You may be the person a procedure exists to protect.
  • Learn from the ratings. An experienced Fitter or Motorman will show you practical skills no book covers.
  • Speak up about fatigue or harassment. Use the company's reporting channels and the ship's complaint procedure under MLC 2006.

Signing off

Collect your sea service testimonial and confirm the CDC entries are correct before you leave the ship. Correcting them later is slow and sometimes impossible.

Key points
  • An engine cadet is a supervised trainee whose main output is documented competence in an approved training record book.
  • You report to the Chief Engineer, usually through the Second Engineer as shipboard training officer; no one formally reports to you.
  • STCW basic safety training, security training, a valid medical and your administration's documents are needed before sailing.
  • The target is the OICEW CoC under STCW Regulation III/1, but your national administration sets the detailed route and exams.
  • Rest-hour rules apply to cadets: STCW sets at least 10 hours per 24 hours and 77 per 7 days, MLC 2006 sets equivalent limits, and under-18s face extra restrictions.
  • The next step is usually 4th, Junior or Assistant Engineer, depending on company structure.
  • A clean, consistent and verifiable CV beats an inflated one with crewing agencies.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an engine cadet need to sail before taking the CoC exam?

STCW Regulation III/1 sets a minimum of 12 months of combined workshop training and approved seagoing service in an approved training programme. A longer route of at least 36 months also exists. Your administration decides exactly how this applies to your course and what counts as qualifying sea time. Check its current published rules.

Can an engine cadet keep an engine room watch alone?

No. A cadet does not hold a Certificate of Competency, so they cannot be the officer in charge of an engineering watch. You stand watches only alongside or under the supervision of a qualified engineer officer, and that supervised watchkeeping is part of your training.

What is the difference between an engine cadet and a Trainee Marine Engineer?

They are usually the same role under different names. Some companies and administrations use 'Trainee Marine Engineer' or 'Engine Trainee' for graduates on approved training programmes. Check your contract and training record book for how your administration recognises the sea time.

Do engine cadets need tanker courses?

Only if you will serve on tankers. Basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations (STCW Section A-V/1-1) or basic training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations (STCW Section A-V/1-2) is normally required for anyone assigned specific duties and responsibilities related to cargo or cargo equipment. Many companies require it for all tanker crew. Your company will tell you which applies before joining.

What rank comes after engine cadet?

Once you hold the OICEW Certificate of Competency, you are typically appointed as Fourth Engineer, Junior Engineer or Assistant Engineer. Some companies appoint directly as Third Engineer. Further promotion leads to Second and Chief Engineer under STCW Regulation III/2 (3,000 kW or more) or III/3 (750–3,000 kW).

Test yourself

What minimum rest does STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 set, which also matches the MLC 2006 rest-hour limits?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 sets 10 hours per 24 hours and 77 hours per 7 days. MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 sets the same rest minimums (or equivalent maximum working hours, at flag discretion).

Who has overall responsibility on board for an engine cadet's training?

The Chief Engineer is responsible for the engine department, including cadet training. Daily supervision is commonly delegated to a designated shipboard training officer, usually the Second Engineer, under the approved onboard training programme (STCW Regulation III/1; guidance in STCW Code Section B-III/1).

What is the main purpose of the approved training record book?

The TRB documents on-board training meeting the standard of STCW Section A-III/1 for the approved-programme route (STCW Regulation III/1).

On a ship fitted with an automatic stopping device, the bilge alarm activates while pumping bilges through the oily water separator because oil content exceeds 15 ppm. What should happen?

When oil content exceeds 15 ppm the alarm activates and discharge must stop, automatically where a stopping device is fitted (MARPOL Annex I Reg 14.7). Bypassing the monitor is a serious violation (MARPOL Annex I, Regulations 14 and 15).

Which STCW regulation sets the requirements for the certificate an engine cadet is working towards?

Regulation III/1 covers the officer in charge of an engineering watch. III/2 is for Chief and Second Engineer on ships of 3,000 kW or more, II/1 is the deck watchkeeper, and VI/1 is basic safety training.
