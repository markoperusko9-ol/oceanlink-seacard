Steward on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

A steward is a catering-department rating who serves meals, cleans the accommodation and helps the cook with galley, provisions and hygiene. They normally report to the Chief Cook, or to a Chief Steward on larger ships. To sail you need STCW basic training, security awareness training, a valid seafarer medical certificate and identity documents. Any catering or food-hygiene qualification is set by your flag state, national administration and company. The usual next step is assistant or second cook and then Chief Cook, which requires a ship's cook qualification.

What a steward actually does on board

On most cargo ships the catering department is small. Often it is a Chief Cook plus one steward or messman. Larger ships, cruise ships and some offshore units have bigger teams. The steward is the person who keeps the accommodation fed, clean and healthy.

Typical daily duties:

  • Mess service: laying tables, serving meals in the officers' and crew messes, clearing and washing up.
  • Accommodation cleaning: cleaning the master's and senior officers' cabins (as the company SMS directs), public rooms, alleyways, the hospital and the ship's office.
  • Pantry and galley support: preparing salads, breakfasts and snacks, washing pots, cleaning galley surfaces and equipment.
  • Laundry: bed linen and towels, and running the laundry machines safely.
  • Provisions: helping take stores on board, checking quantities and dates, stowing the dry store, chiller and freezer rooms, and rotating stock so the oldest is used first.
  • Garbage: segregating food waste, plastics and other garbage according to the ship's garbage management plan.
  • Hygiene: keeping the galley, mess rooms and provision rooms ready for the master's inspection and for port state control.

On passenger ships and ferries the role is closer to hotel service, and the job titles and duties vary widely between operators.

Working pattern, hours of rest and chain of command

Stewards are day workers, not watchkeepers. Your day is built around meal times, so it starts early for breakfast and finishes after dinner is cleared. The work is spread out, with breaks between meals.

Hours of rest. The Maritime Labour Convention sets limits that apply to every seafarer, including catering staff. Your flag state will use one of two systems:

  • at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days, or
  • no more than 14 hours of work in any 24 hours and 72 hours in any 7 days.

Rest may be split into no more than two periods, and one of them must be at least 6 hours. Record your hours honestly. Port state control inspectors check hours-of-rest records, and port days are when rest-hour limits are most easily breached.

Who you report to:

Ship type Steward reports to Department head
Most cargo ships Chief Cook Chief Cook
Larger crews, some passenger/offshore Chief Steward Chief Steward / hotel manager

On every ship the Master has overall command. On cargo ships accommodation matters are often handled via the Chief Officer.

On small crews, nobody reports to the steward. Where a messman is carried alongside you, you may guide them day to day, but the Chief Cook directs the department.

Certificates and training you need

Stewards do not hold a certificate of competency. They still need these mandatory documents:

  • STCW Basic Training (Regulation VI/1). This covers Personal Survival Techniques, Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting, Elementary First Aid, and Personal Safety and Social Responsibilities. It must be kept valid. Every five years you must provide evidence of maintained competence (refresher training) in Personal Survival Techniques and Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting, as required by STCW Regulation VI/1 and Section A-VI/1. Your administration sets how the refresher is delivered and approved.
  • Security awareness training under STCW Section A-VI/6. Many companies also accept or provide equivalent onboard training approved by the flag.
  • Seafarer medical fitness certificate issued by a doctor recognised by your administration. Catering staff are often checked carefully for conditions relevant to food handling.
  • Identity documents: passport, seaman's book or continuous discharge certificate, and any national registration. For Indian seafarers this includes an INDoS number.
  • Flag state documentation where the flag requires it.

Catering-specific training. Under MLC 2006, catering staff must be properly trained or instructed for their jobs. How that is met is up to each administration and company:

  • Some countries have approved pre-sea catering courses or national vocational qualifications in ship's catering.
  • Many companies want a food hygiene or food safety certificate.
  • Others rely on onboard instruction recorded in the SMS.

Check with your national administration and the crewing agency before you pay for any course.

Passenger ships. If your duties involve assisting passengers in emergencies, you will also need crowd management training under STCW Regulation V/2. Depending on your role and the flag's requirements, further passenger-ship training may apply.

Emergency duties: you are not just catering

Every seafarer has a station on the muster list, and stewards are no exception. On cargo ships, catering staff are commonly given duties such as:

  • the first aid or stretcher party,
  • bringing blankets, extra water or provisions to survival craft,
  • checking accommodation spaces are clear,
  • closing doors and ventilation in the accommodation,
  • assisting in the hospital.

Your exact duty depends on the ship's muster list, not on a general rule. Read it on your first day.

The galley is also one of the most common places for a fire to start. As steward you should know:

  1. where the galley fire blanket and extinguishers are, and which type to use on a fat fire (never water),
  2. how to shut off the electric supply to the galley range and the galley exhaust fan or damper,
  3. how to raise the alarm and report to the bridge,
  4. that the range hood and grease filters must be kept clean, because grease build-up feeds duct fires.

You take part in fire and abandon ship drills like everyone else. Treat them as real training, not a break from the galley.

How people become stewards and where it leads

Entry routes. Most stewards start as messman or trainee, or go straight to steward if they have hotel, restaurant or catering experience ashore plus the mandatory STCW documents. Shore hospitality experience is valued, especially on passenger ships.

Usual progression:

  • Messman or trainee → Steward
  • Steward → Assistant or Second Cook, on ships that carry one
  • → Chief Cook
  • On larger catering teams: Chief Steward, and in the hotel side of passenger ships, supervisory hotel roles

The step to cook is the big one. Under MLC 2006, a ship's cook responsible for food preparation must be trained and qualified as the flag state requires, and must be at least 18 years old. The flag can relax this for ships with a small manning level or for special circumstances. A ship's cook certificate or equivalent therefore becomes essential.

Use your time as steward well. Learn menu planning, costing, ordering, stock control and cooking from the Chief Cook, and keep evidence of what you have done.

Some stewards move across to deck or engine ratings. That means a completely different training and sea-service route, which your administration sets.

What crewing agencies look for in a steward's CV

A crewing manager scans a catering CV quickly. Make the important facts easy to find:

  • Valid documents with expiry dates: passport, seaman's book, STCW basic training, security training, medical, and any visas (for example US C1/D if the company trades there).
  • Sea service in a clear table: ship name, type, flag, gross tonnage, crew size, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates. Crew size matters because cooking for 20 is different from serving 60.
  • Ship types: tanker, bulk and container fleets have different routines. Cruise and ferry experience should be described separately.
  • Catering skills: baking, specific cuisines, special diets, and food hygiene training.
  • Mixed-nationality crews: multinational crews eat differently. Saying you can cook or serve for them is a real selling point.
  • References and appraisals from previous masters or Chief Cooks.
  • Clean continuity: unexplained gaps or early sign-offs raise questions, so give a one-line reason.

Avoid long objectives and photos of food unless asked. Do not list certificates you do not hold.

Interview and inspection questions: oral-exam style

These are the kinds of questions a Chief Cook, master or crewing interviewer may ask a steward. Port state control officers may also ask some of them during an inspection.

  • "How do you store raw meat in the chiller?" Below cooked and ready-to-eat food, covered and labelled. This prevents drips and cross-contamination.
  • "What is first in, first out?" Use the oldest stock first, and check dates whenever you receive stores.
  • "Can food waste go overboard?" Only under the conditions MARPOL Annex V allows. These depend on distance from land, whether the waste is comminuted, and whether you are in a special area. It is never allowed if mixed with plastics. Follow the garbage management plan and tell the responsible officer, who records discharges.
  • "A pan of oil catches fire. What do you do?" Don't use water and don't move the pan. Shut off the heat, smother it with the lid or fire blanket, raise the alarm and use the correct extinguisher if needed.
  • "What is your muster station and duty?" Know it by heart within your first day.
  • "A crew member has diarrhoea and vomiting. Can he keep working in the galley?" No. Report it to the officer responsible for medical care. Ill food handlers are kept away from food in line with the company's procedures.

Practical tips from the galley

  • Join the ship knowing your documents' expiry dates. Being stopped at the gangway for an expired medical is avoidable.
  • Learn the crew's preferences early. Note allergies, religious diets and who is on watch at meal times. Keep plates for the watchkeepers.
  • Clean as you go. An inspection-ready galley every day means no panic before port state control.
  • Look after your hands and back. Wear gloves for chemicals, use non-slip shoes, and secure trolleys and pots in heavy weather. Take care lifting provisions on deck.
  • Record your hours of rest truthfully. If port days push you over the limits, tell the Chief Cook and master.
  • Ask to learn cooking. A Chief Cook who sees interest will teach you, and that is your route to promotion.
  • Be discreet. You clean senior officers' cabins and offices. Never move or read documents, and report anything damaged straight away.
Key points
  • A steward is a day-working catering rating who handles mess service, accommodation cleaning, laundry, provisions and galley hygiene.
  • Stewards normally report to the Chief Cook, or to a Chief Steward on larger crews, with the master as overall head.
  • Mandatory documents include STCW basic training, security awareness training, a valid medical certificate and seafarer identity documents.
  • Catering or food-hygiene qualifications vary by flag state, administration and company, so check before paying for courses.
  • MLC 2006 hours of rest apply fully to catering staff, and records are checked by port state control.
  • Every steward has a muster list duty and must know galley fire precautions, especially for fat fires.
  • The usual career path is messman, then steward, then assistant cook, then Chief Cook, which requires a ship's cook qualification set by the flag state.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a messman and a steward on a ship?

The duties overlap and companies use the titles differently. Generally a messman is the entry-level catering rating focused on mess rooms and washing up. A steward takes on more cabin service, provisions and galley support. On small cargo ships one person may do both jobs.

Do I need a CoC to work as a ship's steward?

No. Stewards do not need a certificate of competency, but they must hold STCW basic training, security awareness training and a valid seafarer medical certificate. Some administrations and companies also require a food hygiene or catering qualification.

Can a steward be promoted to Chief Cook?

Yes, this is the most common progression, often through assistant or second cook first. To become Chief Cook you need the ship's cook training and qualification required by the flag state, and you must be at least 18 under MLC 2006.

Does a steward keep watches on board?

Normally no. Stewards are day workers whose hours follow meal times. Their working and rest hours are still covered by MLC 2006 limits and must be recorded.

Is hotel or restaurant experience ashore useful for getting a steward job?

Yes. Shore hospitality and catering experience is valued, particularly by passenger ship operators. You still need the mandatory STCW documents and medical before any company can deploy you.

Test yourself

Under MLC 2006, what are the minimum hours of rest for a steward?

MLC 2006 sets minimum rest of 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days, and this applies to catering staff too. (MLC 2006, Standard A2.3)

Which of these is NOT one of the four elements of STCW basic training?

Basic training covers PST, FPFF, Elementary First Aid and PSSR. PSCRB is a separate proficiency under Regulation VI/2. (STCW Code, Section A-VI/1)

Which instrument regulates the discharge of food waste from ships?

Food waste is garbage under MARPOL Annex V. Annex IV covers sewage, Annex I oil and Annex VI air pollution. (MARPOL Annex V)

What does a steward need to move up to Chief Cook?

MLC 2006 requires ship's cooks to be trained and qualified as the flag state prescribes. Sea service alone is not enough. (MLC 2006, Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2)

On a typical cargo ship, who does the steward report to day to day?

The steward works in the catering department under the Chief Cook, with the master as overall head. Deck and engine officers do not direct catering work. (Company SMS and muster list organisation; SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 37 for emergency duties)
