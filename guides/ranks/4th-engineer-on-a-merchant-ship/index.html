4th Engineer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The 4th Engineer is the most junior certificated engineer officer. They hold an STCW Regulation III/1 Certificate of Competency (Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch), keep a watch or a UMS duty rotation, and look after auxiliary machinery under the 2nd Engineer. Most reach the rank through a cadetship, a graduate marine engineering programme or, in some administrations, from the ratings. The usual next step is 3rd Engineer and then a management-level CoC (III/2, or III/3 on smaller ships) for 2nd Engineer.

What a 4th Engineer actually does

The 4th Engineer is an operational-level officer. You are trusted to run the engine room on your own watch, but you are still learning the plant. The 2nd Engineer gives you most of your work, and the Chief Engineer holds overall responsibility.

The exact split of machinery between the 3rd and 4th Engineer depends on the company and on the Chief. A typical 4th Engineer's list includes:

  • Purifiers. Fuel and lube oil separators: daily checks, sludge discharge, cleaning and overhauls.
  • Air compressors and starting air. Running checks, valve overhauls and draining receivers.
  • Fresh water generator and potable water treatment.
  • Sewage treatment plant and incinerator on many ships.
  • Daily soundings and tank checks. Sludge, bilge, drain and lube oil storage tanks.
  • Fuel and lube oil transfers within the ship, under the 2nd or Chief's instructions.
  • Planned maintenance. Recording jobs in the PMS for your own machinery.
  • Assisting on major jobs. Main engine unit overhauls, generator decarbonising, pump overhauls and dry-dock work.
  • Supervising ratings. Leading wipers, oilers and motormen on the tasks you are given.

On some ships the 4th also keeps up stores records or the spares inventory for their own machinery. On tankers and gas carriers the 4th may also help with cargo machinery, such as inert gas plant auxiliaries or pump room checks. This is always under the direction of senior engineers.

Watch pattern and chain of command

There are two common working patterns, and you need to be comfortable with both.

Manned engine room (traditional watches)

The engine department often runs a three-watch system of four hours on and eight hours off. A common arrangement puts the 4th Engineer on the 8-12 watch with a rating, the 3rd Engineer on the 12-4 and the 2nd Engineer on the 4-8. Companies vary this. On some ships the 2nd Engineer supervises a newly joined 4th on watch at first.

Periodically unattended machinery space (UMS)

Most modern ships are certified for unattended operation. In practice this means:

  • Day work for all engineers.
  • Duty engineer rotation. Engineers take turns as duty engineer and answer alarms out of hours. The 4th Engineer takes a full share once the Chief is satisfied.
  • Evening rounds before the space goes unattended.
  • Response to every alarm that reaches the cabin or public rooms.

Chain of command

Direction Who
Reports to 2nd Engineer for daily work; Chief Engineer for standing and night orders
Works alongside 3rd Engineer, ETO, other duty engineers
Supervises Motorman/oiler and wiper on watch or job

Hours of rest

Rest hours follow STCW and MLC 2006. The minimum is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any seven-day period. Your flag and company may set stricter rules. Record your hours honestly. Port State Control inspectors do check rest records against the logs.

Certificates and competencies required

Certificate of Competency

The core document is a CoC issued under STCW Regulation III/1, Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch. It applies to ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 750 kW or more. Each administration gives this certificate its own national name and grade. India and the Philippines, for example, use different titles. Check what your own administration calls it.

To get a III/1 CoC, STCW sets minimum requirements on:

  • age
  • approved education and training
  • workshop skills training
  • seagoing service, documented in a training record book, including supervised engineering watchkeeping
  • medical fitness
  • written and oral examination against the competence tables in STCW Code Section A-III/1.

Administrations may set requirements above the STCW minimum. Always check your national maritime administration's current rules, not a colleague's memory of them.

Other certificates normally required or expected

  • STCW Basic Training (A-VI/1). Personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (A-VI/2-1).
  • Advanced Fire Fighting (A-VI/3).
  • Medical First Aid (A-VI/4-1).
  • Security training. Designated security duties (A-VI/6-2) or security awareness, depending on your role in the ship security plan.
  • Valid STCW/MLC medical certificate.
  • Flag state endorsement where the ship's flag differs from your issuing administration.
  • Tanker or gas basic training for service on those ship types.

Companies may also ask for:

  • Engine Room Resource Management (ERM)
  • high voltage training
  • type-specific courses for electronically controlled engines or particular equipment.

Whether these are mandatory depends on the administration and the ship. Leadership and teamwork competence is part of III/1 itself.

Revalidation

A CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years. Keep your sea service records and course certificates in order.

How people reach 4th Engineer and what comes next

Common routes into the rank

  1. Engine cadetship. Pre-sea marine engineering degree or diploma, then onboard training with a record book, then the III/1 examination.
  2. Graduate entry. Mechanical or marine engineering graduates complete an approved programme and sea service. Graduate marine engineering courses in India and BSMarE programmes in the Philippines are examples.
  3. From the ratings. Some administrations allow experienced engine ratings to qualify for III/1 through longer service and approved training. Whether this route exists, and on what terms, varies by country.

Many companies first employ new CoC holders as junior or assistant engineers. They promote them to 4th Engineer after a satisfactory contract.

Next steps

  • 3rd Engineer. This is usually the same III/1 CoC. Promotion is a company decision based on appraisals, sea time in rank and the Chief's recommendation.
  • 2nd Engineer. This needs a management-level CoC under STCW Regulation III/2 (or III/3 for ships of 750–3,000 kW propulsion power). That means further qualifying sea service as an operational-level engineer and passing the administration's examinations.
  • Chief Engineer. This needs the Chief Engineer grade of the management-level CoC (III/2, or III/3 for ships of 750–3,000 kW) and further service.

Required sea time between grades differs by administration. Read your own administration's rules early so you log the right service from day one.

Worked scenario: alarm at 0300 as duty engineer

The situation: You are duty engineer on a UMS ship at sea. At 0300 the cabin panel shows FO purifier – water in oil / oil in water outlet alarm.

A sound response:

  1. Call the bridge. Tell the officer of the watch you are going down, as the company procedure and dead-man system require. Arm the dead-man alarm if fitted.
  2. Check the alarm panel first. Note what else is active. A single alarm is different from several that suggest a bigger problem.
  3. Check the purifier locally. Look at the back pressure, throughput, feed temperature, operating water and the sludge tank level. Typical causes include low operating water pressure, a seal-ring problem or an incorrect gravity disc (on conventional purifiers) after a fuel change.
  4. Protect the main engine. Check the service tank level. If the purifier must stop, change over to the standby unit if one is available. Never let the service tank run down while you investigate.
  5. Isolate before you touch it. Any opening-up follows your company's lock-out tag-out and permit procedures.
  6. Know when to call the Chief. If you cannot restore normal supply, or the service tank is falling, call the Chief Engineer. Do not wait until the main engine is at risk. A Chief would rather be woken at 0315 than at 0500 with a blackout.
  7. Record everything. Log the event and your actions, and tell the 2nd Engineer at the start of the day.

Oral-style questions a Chief or examiner may ask

These come up both in III/1 orals and when a Chief tests a newly joined 4th Engineer.

  • "What do you check before taking over the watch?"
    • standing and night orders
    • work in progress
    • machinery running and on standby
    • tank levels, bilges and sludge tanks
    • limitations on any machinery
    • the state of fire detection and other safety systems.
  • "When should the watch not be handed over?" When the relieving officer is clearly not capable of carrying out watch duties effectively, in which case the Chief must be informed. Also when an important operation is under way. The officer being relieved should conclude it unless the Chief Engineer orders otherwise.
  • "When do you call the Chief Engineer?" In any of these cases:
    • damage or malfunction that may endanger safe operation
    • any condition that could cause loss of propulsion, steering or power
    • whenever you are in doubt.
  • "Walk me through a generator changeover and synchronising."
  • "What does the Oil Record Book require of you?" Every relevant operation is recorded without delay. Each entry is signed by the officer or officers in charge, and each completed page is signed by the Master.
  • "Why does air compressor first-stage discharge temperature rise?"

Answer from your own ship's equipment where you can. Examiners and Chiefs quickly notice answers learned word for word without understanding.

What crewing agencies look for in a 4th Engineer CV

Documents up front

List your CoC grade and expiry, flag endorsements, passport, seaman's book, medical, visas and STCW courses with expiry dates. Missing or expired documents get CVs rejected first.

Sea service in a table

Include:

  • vessel name and type
  • flag
  • gross tonnage
  • main engine make and model
  • kW rating
  • rank
  • sign-on and sign-off dates.

Machinery experience

State it specifically. Useful examples include:

  • electronically controlled engines
  • particular purifier makes
  • scrubbers
  • ballast water treatment systems
  • high voltage plant
  • dual-fuel systems.

Ship type match

Tanker, gas or container experience counts for more when you apply to the same type.

Other things agencies check

  • References and appraisals. A positive Chief Engineer appraisal carries real weight.
  • Contract record. Agencies notice premature sign-offs, so be ready to explain any.
  • English ability. Clear written and spoken English matters, because most engine rooms work in mixed-nationality crews.

Keep the CV to two pages and update it after every contract.

Practical tips for the first contracts

  • Trace the systems with drawings. In your first weeks, trace the fuel, lube oil, cooling, bilge and compressed air systems line by line. Knowing where every valve is will save you in an emergency.
  • Learn the emergency gear early. Find the quick-closing valves, the emergency fire pump, the emergency generator and the steering gear emergency changeover in your first week. Do not wait for the drill.
  • Write your own notes. Keep a pocket notebook of setpoints, normal readings and quirks of each machine.
  • Never skip permits or isolation to finish a job faster, even when a senior tells you to hurry.
  • Respect your ratings. An experienced motorman often knows the ship better than you do. Ask, listen, and then make your own decision.
  • Prepare for promotion as you go. Keep your training record, sea service letters and testimonials organised from your first contract.
Key points
  • The 4th Engineer is the junior engineer officer holding an STCW III/1 (Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch) CoC.
  • Typical duties cover auxiliaries such as purifiers, air compressors and the FWG, under the direction of the 2nd Engineer.
  • Work is either a traditional engine-room watch, often the 8-12, or day work with a UMS duty engineer rotation.
  • Basic Training, PSCRB, Advanced Fire Fighting, Medical First Aid, security training and a valid medical certificate are normally required.
  • Required sea service and exam rules are set by each administration within STCW minimums, so check your own.
  • The usual progression is 4th, then 3rd Engineer, then a management-level CoC (III/2 or III/3) for 2nd Engineer and later Chief Engineer.
  • Agencies value a clear sea-service table, specific machinery experience and good Chief Engineer appraisals.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a 4th Engineer and a 3rd Engineer?

Both normally hold the same STCW III/1 operational-level CoC. The 3rd Engineer is the more experienced officer and usually looks after heavier machinery such as generators and boilers. Promotion from 4th to 3rd is a company decision based on performance and time in rank.

Can a motorman or oiler become a 4th Engineer?

Some administrations allow experienced engine ratings to qualify for a III/1 CoC through extended sea service and approved training. Others mainly expect a cadetship or graduate route. Check your own maritime administration's current rules before planning on this route.

How long does it take to get promoted from 4th Engineer?

There is no single figure. Promotion to 3rd Engineer depends on company policy, appraisals and vacancies. Moving to 2nd Engineer requires a management-level CoC (III/2, or III/3 on ships of 750–3,000 kW), and the qualifying sea service for that is set by your administration.

Does a 4th Engineer need high voltage training?

STCW places high voltage competence at management level and for ETOs. Many companies still require HV training for all engineers on ships with HV plant. Check your company's requirements and your administration's rules.

Is a 4th Engineer the same as a junior or assistant engineer?

Not always. Many companies use junior or assistant engineer for a new engineer who is still being assessed, sometimes before a watchkeeping certificate is issued. Anyone keeping an engineering watch in charge on a ship of 750 kW or more must hold at least a III/1 CoC, or a higher engineer CoC.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers the Certificate of Competency normally held by a 4th Engineer?

Regulation III/1 covers officers in charge of an engineering watch at operational level. III/2 and III/3 are management level, and II/1 is deck. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation III/1.

You arrive to relieve the watch while the outgoing engineer is changing over generators. What should happen?

An important operation in progress at handover should be concluded by the officer being relieved, unless the Chief Engineer directs otherwise. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

What are the minimum hours of rest under STCW and MLC 2006?

The minimum is 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any seven-day period. Reference: STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 and MLC 2006 Standard A2.3.

As duty engineer, when should you call the Chief Engineer?

The engineer in charge of the watch should notify the Chief Engineer promptly of damage or malfunction that may endanger safe operation, and whenever in doubt. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

When must a newly joined 4th Engineer receive shipboard safety familiarisation?

Seafarers must receive safety familiarisation before being assigned to shipboard duties. Reference: STCW Regulation VI/1 and STCW Code Section A-VI/1.
