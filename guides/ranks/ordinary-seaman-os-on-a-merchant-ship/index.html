Ordinary Seaman (OS) on a merchant ship

Quick answer

The Ordinary Seaman is the entry-level deck rating. On watch they act as lookout and helmsman, and on day work they handle mooring and maintenance under the bosun. Most OS hold Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch (RFPNW) under STCW Regulation II/4 on top of basic training, and the usual next step is Able Seaman once they qualify as Able Seafarer Deck under Regulation II/5.

What an OS actually does on board

'Ordinary Seaman' is an industry and contract title, not an STCW term. STCW talks about ratings forming part of a navigational watch and able seafarers. In practice the OS is the junior deck rating. You are expected to learn fast and work safely, and no one expects you to know everything yet.

Typical duties:

  • Lookout on the bridge watch, reporting everything seen or heard to the officer of the watch (OOW).
  • Helmsman when the ship is in hand steering: pilotage, traffic, narrow waters, or when the OOW orders it.
  • Mooring and unmooring fore and aft. You handle ropes and stoppers and pass messages under the officer and bosun in charge.
  • Deck maintenance: chipping, painting, greasing, wire and rope care, cleaning, and sounding tanks where trained.
  • Gangway and security watch in port. You check IDs, keep the visitor log and watch the moorings and gangway.
  • Cargo watch support depending on ship type: lashing checks on container ships, draught readings, hold inspections, or manifold watch on tankers under supervision.
  • Stores and provisions handling, pilot ladder rigging under supervision, and assisting with drills.
  • Emergency duties as written on the muster list for fire, abandon ship and man overboard.

Watch pattern and working hours

Your pattern depends on the ship, the trade and the company's manning.

  • Watchkeeping OS: usually on one of the bridge watches (commonly 4-on/8-off) at sea. Daytime watch hours are often partly spent on deck work near the bridge. Night watches are lookout.
  • Day-working OS: works with the bosun on maintenance during the day and is called out for mooring, pilot transfers and anchor stations.
  • In port: gangway and cargo watches, often 6-on/6-off or as the ship's routine sets.

Hours of rest are set by STCW Section A-VIII/1 and MLC 2006 Standard A2.3:

  • At least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period.
  • At least 77 hours' rest in any 7-day period.
  • Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of them at least 6 hours long.
  • The gap between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.

Flag states may allow certain exceptions, and drills and emergencies are handled under specific provisions. Record your hours honestly. Rest-hour records are checked by Port State Control and company audits, and falsified records protect nobody.

If you are under 18, MLC 2006 restricts night work and hazardous work. Your company and flag state set the detail.

Who you report to and who reports to you

Situation You report to
Bridge watch (lookout/helm) Officer of the watch, or the master if on the bridge
Day work on deck Bosun
Mooring stations Officer in charge of the station, often via the bosun
Gangway/cargo watch Duty officer
Overall deck department Chief Officer

Normally no one reports to you. You may be asked to show a deck cadet or trainee rating a routine job, but you are not their supervisor. Your direct mentors are usually the ABs and the bosun. Ask them questions. Most good ABs were OS once and remember it.

Certificates and competencies you need

Exact requirements depend on your flag state, your national administration and your company's SMS. In general terms an OS will need:

  • STCW basic training (Regulation VI/1, Section A-VI/1): personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Security training: all seafarers receive security-related familiarisation, and those on ships required to comply with the ISPS Code need security-awareness training under Section A-VI/6. Because an OS often keeps gangway watch, many companies also want Designated Security Duties.
  • Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch (Regulation II/4, Section A-II/4). This is required for ratings forming part of a navigational watch on seagoing ships of 500 GT or more, other than trainees and those doing unskilled duties. Most companies expect an OS to hold it.
  • Medical fitness certificate under STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006 Regulation 1.2, valid for the whole contract where possible.
  • Seafarer's identity and record book, plus a passport and visas for the trade. Examples are the CDC in India and the SIRB in the Philippines.
  • National registration, such as an INDoS number in India or MARINA records in the Philippines.
  • Ship-type training where your flag and company require it, for example basic tanker training for oil, chemical or gas tankers.

The competencies in Section A-II/4 are practical. You must be able to:

  • Steer and follow helm orders in English.
  • Keep a proper lookout by sight and hearing.
  • Report to the OOW.
  • Understand watch handover.
  • Know emergency signals and procedures.

How people become an OS and the step to AB

Getting in. The common route is an approved pre-sea course for deck or general-purpose ratings. Examples are the GP Rating course approved by DG Shipping in India, or the rating training and certification administered through MARINA in the Philippines. This is followed by sea service on board, usually as trainee or OS. Some people start in another department, such as catering, and cross over after getting the deck certificates. Minimum age for any work at sea is 16 under MLC 2006, but companies and administrations often set a higher entry age.

STCW Regulation II/4 sets minimum seagoing service and training for RFPNW. Administrations add their own detail on how service is counted and documented. Check your national requirements before you sign off, not afterwards.

Next step: Able Seaman. To sail as AB you normally need Able Seafarer Deck certification under Regulation II/5 and Section A-II/5. In general terms this means:

  • holding RFPNW;
  • meeting the minimum age;
  • completing a further period of deck seagoing service after RFPNW, the length depending on whether you also complete approved training;
  • showing the A-II/5 competencies, such as mooring, rigging, maintenance, cargo and emergency duties at a more independent level.

From AB the usual progression is to bosun. Some ratings go on to officer certification through the routes their administration allows.

Keep your evidence. Use signed sea-service testimonials, discharge book entries and a completed training record book if your administration uses one. Missing or incomplete paperwork is a common cause of delays in AB certification.

What crewing agencies look for in an OS CV

Agencies screen OS applications quickly. Make the important facts easy to find:

  • Certificates with numbers and expiry dates: basic training, RFPNW, security, any tanker certificates, medical.
  • Documents: passport, seafarer's book, and visas such as US C1/D if the fleet calls there, all with validity dates.
  • Sea service table: ship name, type, GT, flag, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates. Do not leave gaps unexplained.
  • Ship types and trades you know, for example bulk carrier mooring, container lashing, or tanker manifold watch.
  • Practical skills: hand steering hours, mooring experience, painting, rigging, any welding or crane familiarity.
  • Reason for sign-off where it may raise questions.
  • References from master, chief officer or bosun, if you have them.

Red flags for agencies include:

  • certificates that expire mid-contract;
  • a CV that does not match the discharge book;
  • very short contracts with no explanation;
  • fake or 'bought' certificates. These are verified with administrations and can end a career.

Oral and interview questions you should expect

Interviewers and masters on joining often check the basics with questions like these:

  1. 'You see a light on the starboard bow. What do you do?' Report it to the OOW at once with its relative bearing. For example: 'Light, two points on the starboard bow.' Keep watching it. Do not wait until you have identified it.
  2. 'The OOW orders port ten. What do you do?' Repeat the order back, apply ten degrees of port helm, and report 'ten port' when the rudder indicator shows it. Then wait for the next order.
  3. 'What does midships mean?' Rudder fore-and-aft, at zero.
  4. 'Where is the snap-back zone?' Anywhere a parted line could whip. Painted markings may not show the full danger area. Stay out of the bight and away from the line of pull.
  5. 'Can you enter a ballast tank to check a sounding pipe?' Not without an enclosed-space entry permit, atmosphere testing and authorisation under the SMS.
  6. 'What is the general emergency alarm signal?' Seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast, on the ship's whistle and alarm bells. Go to your muster station as per the muster list.
  7. 'Who is your relief and what do you tell them?' Tell them about traffic in sight, lights or targets being watched, and any special instructions from the OOW.

Practical tips from experience

  • Learn the ship's layout in your first days. Know the muster stations, escape routes, fire stations and lifebuoys. Your familiarisation is a requirement, so ask if it has not been done.
  • Always repeat orders back. On the helm, on the radio and at mooring stations. Closed-loop communication prevents accidents.
  • Carry a small notebook. Write down helm orders, knots and splices you are shown, and the names of mooring lines and fittings.
  • Wear your PPE properly. That means a hard hat with chin strap, gloves, safety shoes, and a harness when working aloft or overside under a permit.
  • Never walk on deck alone in heavy weather without the bridge knowing.
  • Use your watch time to learn. Ask the OOW to explain the radar picture or the chart when traffic is quiet, but never at the cost of your lookout.
  • Look after your documents. Keep scanned copies, track expiry dates, and collect sea-service testimonials before you sign off.
  • Report fatigue, injuries and unsafe acts. Where your company's SMS gives stop-work authority, it applies to you too.
Key points
  • OS is an industry title; the STCW qualification behind it is usually RFPNW under Regulation II/4.
  • Core duties are lookout, helm, mooring, deck maintenance and gangway or cargo watches.
  • You report to the OOW on watch, the bosun on day work and the Chief Officer overall.
  • Minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days under STCW and MLC 2006.
  • The next rank is AB, which normally requires Able Seafarer Deck under Regulation II/5.
  • Service, age and documentation details vary by administration, so check your national rules early.
  • Agencies want clear certificate numbers, validity dates and a gap-free sea service record.

Frequently asked questions

Is Ordinary Seaman the same as RFPNW?

No. OS is a job title on the crew list. RFPNW is the STCW certification under Regulation II/4 that allows a rating to form part of a navigational watch. Most OS hold RFPNW, but the two are not the same thing.

How long does it take to go from OS to AB?

It depends on your administration and whether you complete approved training. In general you need RFPNW, the minimum age, a further period of deck sea service and the Section A-II/5 competencies. Check your national requirements, and keep signed sea-service records from day one.

Can an OS steer the ship?

Yes. Steering under the OOW's orders is a core RFPNW competency, and an OS commonly takes the helm in pilotage, traffic and narrow waters. The OOW or master remains responsible for the ship's navigation.

Do I need tanker training to work as an OS on a tanker?

Usually yes. Under STCW Regulations V/1-1 (oil and chemical tankers) and V/1-2 (liquefied gas tankers), seafarers assigned specific duties and responsibilities related to cargo or cargo equipment must hold the relevant basic tanker training, and many companies require it for all deck ratings. Confirm the exact requirement with the company and flag before joining.

What is the minimum age to work as an OS?

MLC 2006 sets 16 as the minimum age for work on board, and seafarers under 18 have restrictions on night and hazardous work. Many administrations and companies set higher ages for ratings or for particular certificates, so check local rules.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers ratings forming part of a navigational watch?

Regulation II/4, with Section A-II/4, covers RFPNW. II/1 is for officers of the watch, II/5 for able seafarers and VI/1 for basic training (STCW Convention and Code).

The OOW orders 'port ten'. What is the correct response?

A helm order is a rudder angle, not a heading change, and it must be repeated back and confirmed when carried out. Steering and helm orders are RFPNW competencies (STCW Code, Section A-II/4).

What is the minimum rest a seafarer must have in any 24-hour period?

At least 10 hours' rest is required in any 24-hour period, and at least 77 hours in any 7-day period (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3).

On night lookout you see a dim light on the port bow. What should you do first?

Lookout reports must be made immediately so the OOW can assess risk of collision. Proper lookout is required by COLREG Rule 5 and is an RFPNW competency.

Which certification does an OS normally need to be promoted to AB?

Promotion to AB normally requires Able Seafarer Deck certification (STCW Regulation II/5 and Section A-II/5). The OOW certificate is for officers, and SSO and AFF are separate qualifications.
