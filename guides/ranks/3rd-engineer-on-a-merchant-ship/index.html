3rd Engineer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The 3rd Engineer is an operational-level engineer officer holding an STCW Regulation III/1 Certificate of Competency (Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch). The 3rd Engineer keeps a watch or UMS duty and maintains an assigned group of machinery, commonly generators, boilers or purifiers depending on the company, and reports to the 2nd Engineer. Most reach the rank through cadetship and time as 4th Engineer. The next step is a management-level CoC as 2nd Engineer.

What a 3rd Engineer actually does

The 3rd Engineer (3/E) sits in the middle of the engine department. You are trusted to run a watch alone and to own a group of machinery, but you are not yet responsible for planning the department. That is the 2nd Engineer's job.

The machinery you are given depends on the company and the ship, so there is no fixed list. On many ships the 3/E is assigned some of the following:

  • Auxiliary engines and generators: routine running checks, scheduled overhauls under the planned maintenance system (PMS), and load and paralleling checks.
  • Auxiliary boiler and exhaust gas economiser: water treatment and tests, blowdown, burner maintenance, safety checks.
  • Fuel and lube oil purifiers: on some ships these belong to the 4th Engineer instead.
  • Air compressors, fresh water generator, sewage plant or OWS: again, this often shifts between 3/E and 4/E.

Your first job on joining is to find out exactly what is yours. Read the company's job description in the SMS, then confirm it with the 2nd Engineer.

Beyond your own machinery, you will typically:

  • Assist with main engine overhauls, piston pulling and other major jobs.
  • Take part in bunkering, either on the manifold or by sounding tanks, as the bunkering plan directs.
  • Keep PMS records, running hours and spares consumption for your equipment up to date.
  • Make correct entries in the engine logbook and, where you perform the operation, the Oil Record Book.
  • Supervise and train ratings and cadets working with you.

Watch pattern, reporting line and who works under you

Reporting line. You report to the 2nd Engineer day to day. The 2nd Engineer reports to the Chief Engineer, who answers to the Master for the engine department. On a watch, you are the Chief Engineer's representative in the engine room. You must call the Chief Engineer when the standing and night orders require it, or whenever you are in doubt.

Who reports to you. This varies with manning. Typically it includes:

  • The motorman or oiler on your watch.
  • Wipers and engine cadets assigned to your jobs.
  • On some ships, a fitter during repair work.

Watch patterns. These depend on the ship:

Ship arrangement Typical 3/E pattern
Manned engine room, three watchkeepers A fixed watch. Many companies give the 3/E the 12–4, but this is a custom, not a rule.
UMS (periodically unattended) Day work plus a rotating duty engineer night. Machinery alarms come to your cabin when you are on duty.
Port / manoeuvring Stand-by in the ECR during arrival and departure, plus port duties. These are usually split by the 2nd Engineer.

Rest hours. Whatever the pattern, rest hours are governed by STCW and MLC 2006: at least 10 hours' rest in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days, with limited exceptions.

UMS duty nights, call-outs and overhauls can push you close to the limit. Record your hours honestly. If a rest period is broken by an alarm, the record should show it.

Certificates and competencies you need

The core document is the Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch under STCW Regulation III/1. The competence standard is set out in STCW Code Section A-III/1. That section covers four areas:

  • Marine engineering
  • Electrical, electronic and control engineering
  • Maintenance and repair
  • Controlling the operation of the ship and care for persons on board, including leadership, teamwork and resource management

Other engine officers often call it the 'watchkeeping ticket'. National names differ. In India it is commonly the MEO Class IV issued by DG Shipping. In the Philippines it is issued by MARINA as an OICEW CoC. Requirements, exams and sea-service rules are set by each administration on top of the STCW minimum, so check your own.

You will also normally need:

  • Basic Safety Training: STCW Code A-VI/1.
  • Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats: A-VI/2-1.
  • Advanced Fire Fighting: A-VI/3.
  • Medical First Aid: A-VI/4-1.
  • Security training under A-VI/6: security awareness for all seafarers, plus designated security duties training if you are assigned security duties in the ship security plan.
  • A valid seafarer medical certificate: Regulation I/9.
  • Flag state endorsement where the ship's flag differs from your issuing administration: Regulation I/10.
  • Ship-type training, where applicable:
    • Tanker basic training for oil/chemical or liquefied gas tankers.
    • Advanced training if you have immediate responsibility for cargo-related equipment.
    • IGF Code training on gas-fuelled ships: STCW Regulation V/3 requires advanced IGF training for engineer officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for the care and use of fuels and fuel systems.
  • ERM and High Voltage courses. These often appear on company checklists. Whether they are separate mandatory certificates depends on your administration and on the ship's electrical plant.

Your CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years (Regulation I/11). You must also show continued competence every five years for basic training, PSCRB and Advanced Fire Fighting (A-VI/1, A-VI/2, A-VI/3). Some administrations add requirements for other courses. A refresher course expiring mid-contract can cost you a contract. Track expiry dates yourself rather than relying on the crewing office.

How people reach 3/E and what comes next

The usual routes in:

  • Pre-sea engineering cadet. Graduates from a marine engineering degree or diploma, completes onboard training, then passes the OICEW exams.
  • Graduate mechanical engineers. Complete an approved conversion course and workshop/sea-service requirements, then sit for the CoC.
  • Ratings. Some move up from motorman/oiler through approved programmes, where the administration allows.

Getting to 3/E. With a new III/1 CoC, most officers join as 4th Engineer or junior engineer. They are promoted to 3/E after a satisfactory contract or two, based on appraisals and the Chief Engineer's recommendation. On ships carrying only 2/E and 3/E, a newly certificated officer may go straight to 3/E. The rank is about company trust as much as the certificate.

Next step: 2nd Engineer. This needs a management-level CoC:

  • STCW Regulation III/2 for ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more.
  • Regulation III/3 for 750–3,000 kW.

STCW sets minimum seagoing service, but administrations add their own requirements. These can include specific sea time, approved courses and written plus oral exams. In India this is the MEO Class II.

Use your 3/E time to fill your record book and get real exposure to the plant beyond your own machinery:

  • Main engine overhauls
  • Purifier and boiler troubleshooting
  • Electrical fault-finding
  • Planning work

Worked scenario: blackout on your watch

Examiners and Chief Engineers both like this one. The steps below are general good practice. Your ship's blackout procedure and the Chief Engineer's standing orders take precedence.

Situation: Mid-ocean, manned watch. One generator trips and the ship blacks out. The standby set does not come on load automatically.

  1. Stay calm and inform the bridge. The Officer of the Watch must know at once, because propulsion and steering are affected.
  2. Call the Chief Engineer and raise the alarm as your procedure requires.
  3. Confirm the emergency generator has started and is supplying the emergency switchboard.
  4. Find out why the standby set did not come on. Check for:
    • A start failure
    • Low starting air
    • The set not being in auto
    • A fault that would trip it again
  5. Start a healthy generator manually, check voltage and frequency, and close it onto the board.
  6. Restore essential services in a controlled sequence. These include steering gear, main engine lube oil and cooling pumps, fuel supply, and compressors. Do not throw everything on at once and trip the set again.
  7. Restart the main engine only with the Chief Engineer's permission, in coordination with the bridge.
  8. Investigate the root cause. Preserve alarm printouts, log the times and actions, and report through the SMS non-conformity or near-miss system.

The point the examiner is looking for is order and communication, not speed alone.

Oral-exam style questions for a 3/E stepping up

If you are preparing for your 2nd Engineer orals, or simply for a sharp Chief Engineer, expect questions like these:

  • 'Your purifier is overflowing. Walk me through it.' Talk about:
    • Gravity disc or interface settings against oil density and temperature.
    • Sealing water and operating water.
    • Bowl seals and sludge discharge timing.
    • Heater temperature.
  • 'How do you take over an engine-room watch?' Cover:
    • Standing and night orders.
    • Machinery state and any defects.
    • Tank levels and soundings.
    • Work in progress and permits in force.
    • Isolations.
    • Bridge requirements.

Do not accept a watch if you are not satisfied it is under control. - 'What makes an Oil Record Book entry valid?' Know the correct operation codes. Know that the officer in charge of the operation signs the entry, and that the Master signs each completed page. - 'You need to open a seawater cooler on your watch. What's the sequence?' Cover: - Permit to work. - Isolation and lock-out/tag-out. - Draining and checking for pressure. - Informing the bridge if sea suction valves are involved. - 'Low water level alarm on the auxiliary boiler. Actions?' Check the gauge glass yourself first. Do not add cold feed to a possibly dry, overheated boiler. Follow the safety cut-out logic and the manual.

Practise answering out loud in short, ordered steps. Clear structure scores better than long lists of facts.

What crewing agencies look for, plus practical tips

Manning agents shortlist quickly. For a 3/E, make these easy to see:

  • Your documents:
    • CoC grade, issuing administration and expiry date.
    • Flag endorsements.
    • All STCW course certificates with expiry dates.
    • Medical certificate and passport validity.
    • Visas such as the US C1/D where you hold one.
    • INDoS number for Indian seafarers.
  • Your sea service, in a clear table:
    • Vessel name, type and flag.
    • Main engine make and model, and propulsion power in kW.
    • Rank and sign-on/sign-off dates.

The dates must match your CDC or seaman's book exactly. - Your experience: - Plant types you know: specific two-stroke main engine makes, electronically controlled engines, scrubbers, ballast water treatment systems. - Vessel types: tanker, gas, bulk, container. - Your record: - Appraisals and reliefs completed without early sign-off. - A brief, honest explanation of any gaps.

Practical tips from the engine room:

  • Own your machinery. Know its running hours, last overhaul and spares position before anyone asks.
  • Log as you go. Write PMS and logbook entries the same day, not from memory at month-end.
  • Never cut corners on isolations and permits. Many serious engine-room injuries occur during maintenance work, so isolations matter.
  • Teach your cadets and ratings. Chief Engineers notice officers who develop people, and you will need those skills as 2/E.
  • Keep your own training record and certificate expiry calendar. Plan refreshers during leave.
Key points
  • A 3rd Engineer holds an STCW Regulation III/1 Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch CoC.
  • The 3/E reports to the 2nd Engineer and supervises the watch rating, wipers and cadets assigned to them.
  • Assigned machinery (generators, boilers, purifiers and so on) varies by company: confirm it on joining.
  • Watch patterns range from fixed watches to UMS duty rotas, always within STCW/MLC rest-hour limits.
  • The CoC is revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years; basic training, PSCRB and AFF need five-yearly proof of continued competence.
  • The next step is a management-level CoC (III/2 or III/3) to sail as 2nd Engineer, with sea-service rules set by your administration.
  • Agencies want accurate sea service matching your CDC, engine makes and power, and valid certificates.

Frequently asked questions

Which certificate do I need to sail as 3rd Engineer?

You need an STCW Regulation III/1 Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch, plus the mandatory safety and medical certificates. If the ship's flag differs from your issuing country, you also need a flag state endorsement. The exact national name and exam route depend on your administration.

What is the difference between 3rd and 4th Engineer?

Both normally hold the same III/1 CoC. The difference is rank and responsibility within the company. The 3/E is usually given more critical machinery, such as generators or boilers, and more supervisory duties. Promotion from 4/E to 3/E is based on experience and appraisals, not a new certificate.

How long before a 3rd Engineer can become 2nd Engineer?

You must earn a management-level CoC under STCW Regulation III/2 or III/3. STCW sets minimum seagoing service, but your administration sets the actual sea time, courses and exams. Company promotion also depends on performance. Check your national administration's current rules rather than relying on hearsay.

Does a 3rd Engineer keep a watch on a UMS ship?

On a UMS ship the engine room is periodically unattended. The 3/E usually does day work and takes turns as duty engineer, answering alarms out of hours. Hours of rest still apply, and alarm call-outs should be recorded honestly.

Is a High Voltage course mandatory for a 3rd Engineer?

This depends on your administration and on whether the ship has high-voltage plant. Many companies require it for engineers on ships with HV systems regardless. Check both your administration's rules and the company's requirements before joining.

Test yourself

Under which STCW regulation is the Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch CoC issued?

The OICEW CoC for ships of 750 kW propulsion power or more is issued under Regulation III/1. III/2 is for chief and second engineers, III/6 is for ETOs, and II/1 is a deck certificate. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation III/1.

Who does a 3rd Engineer normally report to for day-to-day work?

The 2nd Engineer plans and supervises daily engine-room work and reports to the Chief Engineer. The company SMS job descriptions confirm the line on each ship.

Before opening a purifier for maintenance, what must come first?

Safe isolation under the company permit-to-work and lock-out/tag-out procedure protects against unexpected start-up and stored energy. Reference: ISM Code, Element 7 (shipboard operations) and the company SMS.

What is the maximum revalidation interval for a CoC under STCW?

CoC holders must revalidate at intervals not exceeding five years by showing continued medical fitness and professional competence. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation I/11.

Who signs Oil Record Book Part I entries for engine-room operations?

Each entry is signed by the officer or officers in charge of the operation, and each completed page is signed by the Master. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17.
