Chief Officer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The Chief Officer (Chief Mate) is second in command. They head the deck department, are responsible for cargo, ballast and stability, and are the Master's deputy. To hold the post you need a management-level Chief Mate CoC under STCW Regulation II/2, plus the ship-type training your flag and company require. Most Chief Officers aim for command after building sea time and confidence in the rank.

What the Chief Officer actually does

The Chief Officer is the senior deck officer below the Master and is ready to take command if the Master is incapacitated. The company's Safety Management System (SMS) defines the exact split of duties. On almost every ship the Chief Officer owns these areas:

  • Cargo operations: load and discharge planning, stowage, securing, cargo documentation and the cargo watch arrangements in port.
  • Stability and ballast: calculating and monitoring stability, trim, draught, stress and ballast operations. This includes ballast water management records where the Chief Officer is the designated officer.
  • Deck maintenance: hull, hatch covers, mooring and anchoring gear, deck machinery, accommodation upkeep, paint and stores.
  • Deck crew management: planning daily work with the Bosun, enforcing permits to work, and monitoring ratings' rest hours and training.
  • Safety and emergency organisation: usually leads the emergency party in fire and damage-control drills. Often supervises life-saving and fire-fighting equipment, though some companies hand day-to-day LSA/FFA checks to a junior officer.
  • Enclosed space entry and permits: often the officer who authorises entry into tanks and holds, under the company procedure.

Companies differ on two common extras. One is Ship Security Officer (SSO), which goes to whoever the company designates and is frequently the Chief Officer. The other is the person in charge of medical care, which on some ships is the Chief Officer and on others the 2nd Officer or the Master.

Watch pattern, working hours and reporting lines

On a traditional three-watch bridge, the Chief Officer keeps the 0400–0800 and 1600–2000 watches. The hours between are spent on deck work, paperwork and planning. Some ships, particularly those carrying extra officers, put the Chief Officer on day work. Offshore and short-sea trades often run 6-on/6-off or company-specific rotas.

In port the workload peaks. The Chief Officer is responsible for cargo and stability throughout, while the junior officers keep the cargo watches. Managing your own rest hours is a professional duty, not a nicety.

STCW (Code Section A-VIII/1) requires watchkeepers to have at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. It also limits how that rest may be split. Administrations may allow limited exceptions. MLC 2006 lets the flag choose between two regimes: minimum rest hours on the same basis, or maximum working hours. Check your flag's rules. Inaccurate or non-compliant rest-hour records are a frequent Port State Control finding.

Reporting lines

Reports to Reports to the Chief Officer
Master 2nd, 3rd and 4th Officers (for deck and cargo work)

Pumpman (on tankers), and fitter/welder when working on deck jobs

The Chief Officer and Chief Engineer work as equals in their own departments. Ballast, bunkers, deck machinery and hatch covers depend on that relationship working well.

Certificates and competencies required

The core requirement is a Certificate of Competency as Chief Mate under STCW Regulation II/2. There are separate endorsements for ships of 3000 GT or more and for ships of 500–3000 GT. For the unlimited (3000 GT+) certificate, STCW sets these minimums:

  • Hold a valid OOW certificate (Regulation II/1).
  • Have at least 12 months of approved seagoing service as OOW.
  • Meet the management-level competence in STCW Code Section A-II/2.

Section A-II/2 covers three functions: navigation, cargo handling and stowage, and controlling the operation of the ship and care for persons on board.

These are STCW minimums. Your administration (DG Shipping in India, MARINA in the Philippines, or another flag) sets its own course, written and oral exam structure, and any extra service requirements. Always check the current rules of the administration issuing your CoC. If the ship's flag differs, you will also need that flag's endorsement of your CoC.

Other certificates normally expected for the rank:

  • Advanced Fire Fighting (STCW VI/3), as you are likely to lead fire-fighting.
  • Medical First Aid (STCW Regulation VI/4, Code Section A-VI/4-1), and Medical Care (Section A-VI/4-2) if designated in charge of medical care.
  • GMDSS GOC, as required by your administration.
  • ECDIS generic training, and type-specific familiarisation per company and flag requirements.
  • Ship Security Officer (VI/5) if designated SSO.
  • Tankers and gas carriers: advanced cargo training (V/1-1 for oil or chemical, V/1-2 for liquefied gas), because you have immediate responsibility for cargo operations. Many charterers and oil majors also expect documented experience on the specific ship type.
  • A valid medical fitness certificate, and revalidation of the CoC every five years under Regulation I/11.

How people reach Chief Officer and what comes next

The usual route is cadet, 3rd Officer, 2nd Officer, then Chief Officer. Some fleets add a 4th Officer or junior officer step at the start.

To make the move from 2nd Officer:

  1. Build the required OOW sea time, keeping your discharge book, sea service letters and training record book accurate as you go.
  2. Complete the management-level preparatory course and mandatory short courses your administration specifies.
  3. Pass the written and oral exams for the Chief Mate CoC.
  4. Sail as junior Chief Officer, or promote in-house. Many companies want to see you perform as a senior 2nd Officer first, often taking on cargo planning under supervision.

Next step: Master. Under STCW Regulation II/2, the Master certificate for 3000 GT+ needs at least 36 months of approved seagoing service as OOW. This can be reduced to at least 24 months if at least 12 months of that was served as Chief Mate. Administrations add their own course and exam requirements.

Companies typically want a proven record as Chief Officer on their ship type before promoting to command. Some Chief Officers move ashore instead, into roles such as marine superintendent, cargo surveyor, vetting inspector or training.

What crewing agencies look for in a Chief Officer CV

Crewing managers screen Chief Officer CVs fast. Make these easy to find:

  • CoC details: grade, issuing administration, expiry date, and flag endorsements held.
  • Sea service table: ship name, type, GT/DWT, flag, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates, and engine type. Gaps should be explainable.
  • Time in rank on the specific ship type: tanker and gas operators in particular look for experience in rank on similar ships.
  • Cargo experience: grades or cargoes handled, terminals, and loading computer or cargo software used.
  • Inspection record: PSC, vetting (SIRE, CDI) and flag inspections you prepared for, with honest outcomes. Saying you prepared for an inspection that closed with zero observations is a real selling point.
  • STCW certificates: listed with numbers and expiry dates, plus ship-type and ECDIS types.
  • References: from Masters and superintendents, and appraisals from previous companies.

Keep it to two pages, with no photos of certificates in the body. Make sure dates match your CDC exactly, because mismatches are the fastest route to rejection.

Worked scenario: stability problem on the last parcel

You are loading a bulk carrier. With two pours left, the shore tells you the final tonnage has increased. Your plan was close to the maximum permitted draught.

  1. Stop and recalculate before accepting the extra cargo. Run the revised figures through the approved loading computer: draught against the load line and port limits, trim, shear force and bending moments, and GM.
  2. Check the ballast. Is there residual ballast you can still pump? Are any tanks slack, adding free surface effect? Remember that deballasting rate and loading rate must stay in step.
  3. Talk to the terminal. Agree on reduced loading rates for trimming pours and confirm the final figure in writing.
  4. Inform the Master with your figures and a recommendation. Do not just report a problem.
  5. Final draught survey. Read draughts on both sides, compare with the calculation, and record any discrepancy.

The point the examiner wants to hear is that commercial pressure never overrides the load line, the ship's strength limits or the approved stability information.

Oral exam style questions for Chief Mate candidates

Expect questions like these. Answer from practice, citing the regulation where you know it.

  • What is free surface effect and how do you reduce it? It is the virtual loss of GM caused by slack tanks. Reduce it by minimising the number of slack tanks and pressing up or emptying tanks.
  • The ship develops a list during loading. What do you do? Stop cargo. Determine whether the cause is unequal distribution, a slack tank, or negative GM, since the remedies are different. Correct with care, then resume.
  • Who can authorise enclosed space entry, and what must be in place? Answer from your company procedure: permit, risk assessment, isolation, ventilation, atmosphere testing, an attendant, communication, and rescue gear ready.
  • What are the Chief Officer's roles in a PSC inspection? Deck documents, LSA/FFA readiness, rest hours, drills and crew familiarity.
  • How do you prepare for heavy weather? Securing cargo and deck, checking hatches and vents, ballast condition, and briefing the crew.
  • What does the ISM Code expect of you when you find a non-conformity? Report it through the SMS, take corrective action, and avoid hiding it.

Practical tips for new Chief Officers

  • Read the SMS and cargo manuals in your first week: stability booklet, cargo securing manual, ballast water management plan and loading computer instructions.
  • Walk the ship with the Bosun and the outgoing Chief Officer. Get a written handover covering defects, pending jobs and inspection findings.
  • Plan maintenance a week ahead, matched to weather, port stays and rest hours.
  • Delegate properly: give junior officers real cargo duties. Training them is part of your job.
  • Keep records honest and current: oil record book entries, garbage, ballast and rest hours. These are where PSC and vetting inspectors look first.
  • Build the Chief Engineer relationship early: ballast pumps, hatch hydraulics, mooring winches and bunkers all cross departments.
Key points
  • The Chief Officer is second in command, heading deck, cargo, stability and ballast.
  • Traditionally keeps the 0400–0800 and 1600–2000 watches, though company and trade rotas vary.
  • Requires a Chief Mate CoC under STCW Regulation II/2 and, if the flag differs, a flag state endorsement.
  • STCW minimum for the 3000 GT+ Chief Mate CoC is 12 months' approved OOW service; administrations add their own requirements.
  • Tanker and gas Chief Officers need advanced cargo training under STCW V/1-1 or V/1-2.
  • Crewing agencies check time in rank on the ship type, inspection record, and CV dates that match your CDC.
  • The next step is Master, which needs further sea service, exams and usually a solid record as Chief Officer.

Frequently asked questions

Is Chief Officer the same as Chief Mate?

Yes. Both titles describe the same rank, the senior deck officer below the Master. STCW uses 'chief mate' for the certificate, while many companies use 'Chief Officer' on board.

How much sea time do I need as 2nd Officer before Chief Mate CoC?

STCW sets a minimum of 12 months of approved seagoing service as OOW for the 3000 GT+ Chief Mate certificate. Your administration may set different or additional requirements, such as service on particular ship sizes. Check the current rules of the authority issuing your CoC.

Does the Chief Officer keep a navigational watch?

On most ships, yes, traditionally the 4–8 watches. Some vessels with extra officers or specific trades put the Chief Officer on day work. The company SMS and the Master decide.

Is the Chief Officer always the Ship Security Officer?

No. The SSO is whoever the company designates under the ISPS Code, and that is often but not always the Chief Officer. Whoever holds the post needs the SSO certificate under STCW Regulation VI/5.

Can I sail as Chief Officer on a tanker without tanker experience?

You need the advanced tanker cargo training certificate for the cargo type, and that training has its own sea service prerequisites. Beyond that, most operators, charterers and vetting regimes expect previous tanker experience before appointing a Chief Officer. Expect companies to want time on the ship type first.

Test yourself

Under STCW, what is the minimum approved seagoing service as OOW for a Chief Mate CoC on ships of 3000 GT or more?

Regulation II/2 sets a minimum of 12 months as OOW. 36 months relates to the Master certificate. STCW Convention, Regulation II/2.

What is the minimum rest a Chief Officer must have in any 24-hour period?

STCW requires at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7 days (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1). MLC 2006 lets the flag choose an equivalent minimum-rest regime or a maximum-hours regime.

Which training is required for a Chief Officer with immediate responsibility for cargo on an oil tanker?

Officers with immediate responsibility for oil cargo operations need advanced oil tanker training; basic training alone is not enough. STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-1.

Which STCW regulation covers certification of chief mates and masters on ships of 500 GT or more?

II/1 covers OOW, III/2 covers chief and second engineers, and VI/1 covers basic safety. STCW Convention, Regulation II/2.

For a 3000 GT+ Master CoC, how does service as Chief Mate affect the STCW sea time requirement?

STCW allows the 36-month requirement to be reduced to 24 months where at least 12 months were served as Chief Mate. STCW Convention, Regulation II/2.
