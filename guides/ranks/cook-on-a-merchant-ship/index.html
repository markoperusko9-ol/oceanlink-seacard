Cook on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The ship's cook runs the galley and provision stores and is responsible for safe, nutritious food for the whole crew. MLC 2006 requires cooks to be trained and qualified and at least 18 years old, and they also need STCW basic safety training and a valid medical certificate. Most cooks come up from messman or second cook, and the usual next step is chief cook or, on larger ships, chief steward or a catering supervisory role.

What the cook actually does on board

On most cargo ships the cook is the head of the catering department. The title may be Cook or Chief Cook. On a typical bulk carrier, tanker or container ship there may be one cook and one messman. Larger ships and passenger vessels have a bigger galley team.

Core duties:

  • Menu planning and cooking for three main meals, plus night lunch for watchkeepers. You plan within the provisions budget and the voyage length.
  • Catering for a mixed crew. MLC 2006 expects food to take account of the crew's religious and cultural practices. On an Indian or Filipino crew, or a mixed one, that means vegetarian, halal, no-beef or no-pork requirements are part of the job, not an extra.
  • Provision management. You raise provision orders, check and receive stores against the order, rotate stock (first in, first out) and keep stock records.
  • Hygiene. This covers the galley, cold rooms, dry stores, mess rooms and pantries, plus personal hygiene of everyone handling food.
  • Galley safety. You are responsible for safe use of ranges, fryers and electrical equipment, keeping exhaust hoods and grease filters clean, and secure stowage before bad weather.
  • Garbage segregation of food waste, cooking oil and packaging under MARPOL Annex V and the ship's Garbage Management Plan.
  • Emergency duties. You carry out whatever the muster list assigns to you. Cooks are often given tasks such as bringing extra provisions or blankets to survival craft, or joining a first-aid or support party. This varies ship to ship.

Under MLC, food and drinking water must be provided to seafarers free of charge. The cook is the person who makes that standard real every day.

Working pattern and reporting lines

The cook is a day worker, not a watchkeeper. A typical day starts early to prepare breakfast and finishes after dinner and clean-down. Night lunch is usually prepared in advance. Exact times are set by the master and the company.

Hours of rest under MLC 2006 still apply to the galley. A seafarer must get at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days, or the flag must instead apply the equivalent maximum hours of work. Some flags use one method and some the other. Your records must be filled in honestly. Port state inspectors may cross-check rest-hour records against actual working patterns, including meal times.

Reporting lines depend on the company SMS, so check your ship's organisation chart. Commonly:

Direction Typical arrangement
Reports to Master. The chief officer often oversees stores, hygiene rounds and the catering budget day to day.
Reports to cook Messman/steward. On larger ships also a second cook or assistant cook.
Works closely with Chief officer (provisions, garbage), chief engineer (galley equipment, cold-room machinery), agent and chandler (provision deliveries)

MLC requires frequent documented inspections of food and water supplies, storage spaces and galley equipment. These are carried out by the master or an officer acting under the master's authority. Many companies set a weekly inspection, but the frequency comes from the flag and the SMS. Expect the inspecting officer to open your fridges, check temperatures and look behind equipment.

Certificates and competencies required

Requirements come from two conventions, and the details are set by each administration.

MLC 2006 (Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2)

  • A ship's cook must be trained and qualified for the position. Training must be through a course approved or recognised by the competent authority. It covers practical cookery, food and personal hygiene, food storage, stock control, and environmental protection and catering health and safety.
  • Minimum age 18 to be employed as ship's cook.
  • Ships with a small prescribed manning may be permitted by the flag not to carry a fully qualified cook. Anyone processing food in the galley must still be trained or instructed in hygiene and food handling.
  • What counts as a valid cook's certificate, and whether a foreign one is recognised, differs between administrations. Indian, Filipino and other national requirements are not identical. Always check what the flag of your next ship accepts.

STCW and general documents

  • Basic safety training (STCW Code A-VI/1): PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR, kept valid with refreshers as your administration requires.
  • Security awareness training (A-VI/6). Designated security duties training is needed if you are assigned security duties under the Ship Security Plan.
  • Medical fitness certificate recognised by the flag. Some companies or ports also ask for food-handler medical tests. This varies.
  • National documents: passport, seaman's book/CDC, INDoS for Indian seafarers, and visas such as the US C1/D where the trade requires.

Beyond certificates, the competencies an examiner or chief officer looks for are:

  • safe temperature control;
  • avoiding cross-contamination (raw and cooked, separate boards and utensils);
  • allergen awareness;
  • galley fire response;
  • correct garbage segregation.

How people reach the rank and where they go next

Typical routes in:

  1. Messman or steward → second cook/assistant cook → cook. This is the most common route on cargo ships. You learn the galley under an experienced cook, complete an approved cook's course, and get promoted when the company sees you can run the galley alone.
  2. Shore chef → ship's cook. Hotel or restaurant cooks with culinary qualifications do an approved ship's cook course, STCW basic training and a seafarer medical. Shore experience helps, but you still have to learn ship conditions: rolling galleys, long voyages without resupply, and strict stores budgets.
  3. Catering cadet or trainee programmes run by some maritime schools and manning agents, especially for the Philippine market.

Usual next steps:

  • Chief cook on ships that carry more than one cook.
  • Chief steward or catering officer on larger crews, offshore units or passenger ships, with stores, budget and staff management.
  • Passenger and cruise sector. Galleys there follow a shore-style brigade (commis, chef de partie, sous chef, and so on). Moving across usually means proving you can work at volume.

Switching to deck or engine is possible but is a separate STCW path. It needs its own approved training and sea service. Catering sea time generally does not count as qualifying deck or engine service for a CoC. Check your administration's rules.

What crewing agencies look for in a cook's CV

Manning agents filter fast. Make these easy to find:

  • Rank held and vessel types. List each contract with vessel type, flag, approximate crew size and nationalities fed. "Cook, bulk carrier, 21 crew, mixed Indian/Filipino" says a lot.
  • Cook's certificate with issuing body and date, plus all STCW certificates with expiry dates.
  • Cuisine range. Indian (veg and non-veg), Filipino, Western, halal, and anything else you can genuinely cook. Mixed-crew experience is a strong selling point.
  • Clean PSC and inspection record. If you served on ships with no galley deficiencies in port state or vetting inspections, say so.
  • Stores and budget skills. Ordering, stock control, and staying within the company's daily provision allowance.
  • Continuity. Repeat contracts with the same company show reliability. Explain gaps briefly.
  • References from masters or chief officers, and appraisal reports if the company allows you to share them.

Keep the CV to two pages. Attach clear scans of documents and do not pad it with general shore jobs from years ago.

Interview and inspection questions you should be ready for

Chief officers, masters, PSC inspectors and company interviewers ask variations of these. Know the answers cold.

"Your deep fat fryer catches fire. What do you do?"

  • Raise the alarm.
  • Isolate the power or heat to the fryer and the galley range if safe.
  • If the fryer has a fixed extinguishing system, activate it as per the ship's procedure.
  • Smother the fire with the fire blanket or the wet chemical extinguisher if fitted.
  • Never use water.
  • Do not try to carry the pan.
  • Be aware the fire can spread into the exhaust duct. Follow the ship's fire plan, including closing ventilation dampers where that is part of the drill.

"How do you handle food waste at sea?"

  • Keep it separate from plastics and other garbage.
  • Discharge it only where MARPOL Annex V allows. Outside special areas, comminuted food waste (passing a 25 mm screen) may go overboard en route not less than 3 nm from the nearest land. Uncomminuted food waste may only go at 12 nm or more. Inside special areas, discharge is more restricted: generally 12 nm or more, and comminuted.
  • Follow what the Garbage Management Plan and the officer responsible tell you, including any local or company rules stricter than MARPOL.
  • Make sure discharges are recorded.

"What about used cooking oil?"

It is a separate garbage category and must not go overboard. Retain it in a sealed container and land it ashore, unless the SMS allows another approved method.

"How do you prevent cross-contamination?"

  • Separate storage for raw and cooked food.
  • Raw meat below ready-to-eat food.
  • Colour-coded or separate boards and knives.
  • Hand washing between tasks.
  • Clean and sanitise surfaces.

"How do you check provisions on delivery?"

  • Check quantity against the order.
  • Check condition, expiry dates and packaging damage.
  • Check that frozen goods arrive actually frozen.
  • Reject anything unsafe and tell the chief officer before the chandler leaves.

"What temperatures do you keep?"

State the figures in your company's catering manual and how often you log them. Do not guess.

Practical tips from the galley

  • Read the catering section of the SMS on joining. Ask for the provision budget, the ordering cycle and the next port where you can store. This is part of your familiarisation.
  • Plan for the longest leg. Carry enough dry stock and frozen protein to cover a delayed port call. Fresh vegetables run out first, so plan your menus around that.
  • Secure for sea every evening, and always before forecast bad weather. Pots, oil containers and loose stores cause burns and injuries when the ship rolls.
  • Clean grease filters and hoods on schedule. Galley duct fires usually start in neglected grease.
  • Talk to the crew. A short chat about likes, dislikes and religious requirements at the start of a contract prevents months of complaints. Food is one of the biggest morale factors on board.
  • Keep your own records tidy: temperature logs, cleaning schedule and stock sheets. They protect you during inspections and handovers.
  • Hand over properly when signing off: stock levels, pending orders, equipment defects and crew dietary requirements.
Key points
  • The cook runs the galley and provision stores and is usually a day worker, not a watchkeeper.
  • MLC 2006 requires ship's cooks to be trained and qualified through an approved or recognised course, and to be at least 18.
  • STCW basic safety training, security awareness and a valid medical certificate are also required; national details vary by administration.
  • Food and galley spaces are subject to frequent documented inspections by the master or an officer under the master's authority.
  • Food waste, cooking oil and packaging must be segregated and handled under MARPOL Annex V and the Garbage Management Plan.
  • The common route is messman → second cook → cook, with chief cook or chief steward as the next step.
  • A strong CV shows vessel types, crew nationalities fed, cuisine range, certificates with expiry dates and a clean inspection record.

Frequently asked questions

What certificate do I need to work as a cook on a ship?

You need a ship's cook qualification from a course approved or recognised by the administration, as required by MLC 2006. You also need STCW basic safety training, security awareness training and a valid seafarer medical certificate. Which cook's certificates are accepted varies between flag states, so check with the company or manning agent for the specific ship.

What is the minimum age to work as a ship's cook?

Under MLC 2006, no seafarer under 18 may be employed or engaged as ship's cook. Younger seafarers, where national law permits them to go to sea, may work in other catering roles but not as the cook.

Does a ship's cook keep watches?

Normally no. The cook is a day worker whose hours follow the meal schedule. Hours of rest rules under MLC 2006 still apply, and the cook also has emergency duties under the muster list.

Can a messman become a cook?

Yes. It is the most common route on cargo ships. The messman gains galley experience, completes an approved ship's cook course and is promoted once the company is satisfied they can run the galley. The exact training and certificate needed depend on the administration.

Can I throw food waste overboard?

Only where MARPOL Annex V allows it. Outside special areas, comminuted food waste may be discharged en route at 3 nm or more from the nearest land, and uncomminuted waste at 12 nm or more. Special areas have stricter rules, and food waste must never be mixed with plastics or other garbage. Follow the ship's Garbage Management Plan and make sure discharges are recorded.

Test yourself

Under MLC 2006, what is the minimum age for a seafarer employed as ship's cook?

MLC 2006 prohibits employing or engaging anyone under 18 as ship's cook, even though 16 is the general minimum age for seafarers. Reference: MLC 2006, Standard A3.2.

A deep fat fryer in the galley catches fire. What is the correct first response after raising the alarm?

Water on burning oil causes a violent flare-up, and carrying the pan spreads the fire. Isolating the heat and smothering it is the accepted method, followed by the ship's fire plan. Reference: the ship's fire training manual and fire plan; SOLAS II-2 requires certain deep-fat cooking equipment to have its own extinguishing system and heat cut-off.

Outside special areas, comminuted food waste (passing a 25 mm screen) may be discharged en route at what minimum distance from the nearest land?

MARPOL Annex V allows comminuted food waste to be discharged not less than 3 nm from the nearest land outside special areas. Uncomminuted food waste requires 12 nm. Reference: MARPOL Annex V, Regulation 4.

Under MLC 2006, who must carry out the frequent documented inspections of food, water and galley spaces?

MLC requires frequent documented inspections by or under the authority of the master. The cook usually accompanies the inspection but does not replace it. Reference: MLC 2006, Standard A3.2.

How should used cooking oil from the galley be handled?

Cooking oil is a separate garbage category under MARPOL Annex V and may not be discharged into the sea. It is normally landed to a reception facility, and draining it to the bilge contaminates the oily water system. Reference: MARPOL Annex V, Regulation 3 (general prohibition), with Regulations 4 and 6 (no permitted discharge of cooking oil).
