Rigger on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

A rigger is a deck rating who specialises in slinging, rigging and signalling lifts, and in looking after the lifting gear that makes them possible. The post is most common on offshore, heavy-lift, construction and cable-lay vessels. On conventional cargo ships the same work is usually done by the bosun and ABs. STCW does not define a "rigger" rank, so you need the normal seafarer certificates plus the rigging and lifting qualifications your company or contractor specifies.

What a rigger actually does on board

A rigger's job is to get loads lifted, moved and landed without anyone getting hurt and without damaging the load, the ship or the gear. The exact scope depends on the vessel type and the company's Safety Management System (SMS). It typically includes:

  • Rigging and slinging loads. You choose and fit slings, shackles, spreader bars and other lifting accessories that suit the load's weight, centre of gravity and attachment points.
  • Signalling (banksman duties). You act as the designated signaller to the crane or winch operator, by hand signals or radio, as the lift plan sets out.
  • Inspecting and maintaining loose gear. You check slings, shackles, wire ropes, chain blocks and lifting points before use. You quarantine any defective or unmarked items and help keep the lifting gear register up to date.
  • Tag lines and load control. You control swinging loads from a safe position and never stand under a suspended load.
  • Supporting specialist operations. Depending on the ship, this can mean anchor handling, cargo securing, sea-fastening of project cargo, cable or pipe handling, or ROV and diving support.
  • General deck work. On many vessels the rigger also takes part in mooring, maintenance and other deck jobs, much as an AB does.

In short, the rigger is the person between the crane operator and the load. A competent rigger slows a job down when it isn't safe and speeds it up when it is.

Where you will find riggers and who they report to

Titles and reporting lines vary a lot between companies, so treat this table as typical rather than fixed.

Vessel type Usual reporting line Notes
Offshore construction, cable lay, pipe lay Deck foreman or rigging supervisor, then the Chief Officer or the client's project team The rigger may be marine crew or contractor (project) personnel
Heavy-lift / project cargo Bosun or deck foreman, then the Chief Officer Lifts are planned by the Chief Officer and/or a cargo superintendent
AHTS / OSV Bosun or Chief Officer Rigger duties are often combined with AB duties
Conventional cargo ships Usually no separate rigger post The bosun and ABs do the rigging

Who reports to you: often nobody formally. A senior or lead rigger may direct trainee riggers, deck ratings or roustabouts assigned to a particular lift.

Work closely with the crane operator. During a lift, the operator should take signals from one designated signaller only, although anyone may give an emergency stop.

Working pattern and hours of rest

Riggers are normally day workers or shift workers, not bridge watchkeepers.

  • On offshore and construction vessels, 12-hour shifts on a day/night split are common. Rotation lengths are set by the contract.
  • On heavy-lift ships the hours follow the cargo operation, with intense periods in port and lighter maintenance days at sea.

Hours of work and rest rules under STCW and MLC 2006 still apply where you are covered by them. Typically this means at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period, with limits on how rest can be split. Some flags instead apply maximum working hours (14 in 24, 72 in 7 days), and STCW allows limited flag-approved exceptions. Contractor personnel may be covered by different rules, so check your contract and the flag's requirements. Record your hours honestly.

Fatigue is a recognised contributor to lifting incidents. If a long job pushes you past your limits, raise it with your supervisor before the lift, not after it.

Certificates and competencies

Because "rigger" is not an STCW rank, there is no single international certificate called a rigger's licence. What you need falls into three groups.

1. Mandatory seafarer documents (if you sign on as crew): - STCW basic training (STCW Code Section A-VI/1): PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR - Security training at the level your duties require (Section A-VI/6) - A valid seafarer medical fitness certificate - A seaman's book or continuous discharge certificate, and a passport

2. Watchkeeping certificates (if you also keep a watch or are signed on as a deck rating): - Rating forming part of a navigational watch (A-II/4) - Able seafarer deck (A-II/5), where the post is filled as an AB

3. Rigging and lifting qualifications. These are set by the company, the client or the flag/coastal state, not by STCW. Common examples are: - A recognised rigging, slinging and signalling (banksman) course, often with a competence assessment - Offshore survival and helicopter escape training (for example BOSIET/HUET) where the work is offshore - Vessel-type training, such as tanker basic training, if the ship requires it

Acceptable rigging certificates differ between operators and regions. Some offshore areas insist on a particular industry scheme, while others accept in-house assessment. Before you pay for any course, check exactly which certificate the contract names.

How people become riggers and where they go next

Common routes in: - From deck ratings. An OS or AB on offshore or heavy-lift vessels picks up rigging experience, completes a rigging course and moves into a dedicated rigger post. - From shore industry. Experienced construction, shipyard or industrial riggers join offshore contractors. They must then add the STCW basic training and medical certificate required to sail. - From roustabout or deckhand roles in the offshore sector.

Typical next steps: - Senior rigger or lead rigger - Deck foreman or rigging supervisor - Crane operator, which needs separate crane training and assessment - Bosun, if you stay on the marine side - Officer, through approved training and sea service toward an OICNW certificate under STCW Regulation II/1. Whether rigger service counts toward the qualifying sea service depends on your administration and on how you were signed on, so ask your national maritime administration before you plan around it.

What crewing agencies look for in a rigger's CV

Agencies and offshore contractors want evidence, not adjectives. Make it easy for them:

  • Vessel types and operations. For example: "AHTS, anchor handling in 300 m water depth" or "heavy-lift, tandem lifts of project cargo".
  • Lifting experience. Give the types of lifts, the cranes or winches used and your role (rigger, signaller, lead).
  • Certificate list with expiry dates. Include STCW basic training, medical, security, rigging/banksman and offshore survival certificates.
  • Continuous service record. Make sure it matches your seaman's book. Explain any gaps honestly.
  • Clean safety record and references from bosuns, foremen or Chief Officers.
  • Familiarity with permit-to-work systems, toolbox talks and lift plans.

Avoid vague lines such as "good at rigging". A line such as "rigged and signalled routine supply-boat lifts daily on a PSV over a 4-month contract" tells a recruiter far more.

Oral exam style: questions a superintendent or assessor may ask

Interviews and competence checks for riggers are practical. Be ready to talk through questions like these.

Q: You find a wire sling with no legible tag. What do you do? Don't use it. Take it out of service, quarantine it and report it, as your SMS and lifting gear register require.

Q: What do you check before a lift? Check that the load weight is known and within the gear's rated capacity, and that the centre of gravity is understood. Make sure the lifting points and accessories are inspected and certified and that the area is clear. Confirm there is one designated signaller with agreed signals and communications, that tag lines are fitted where needed and that the permit and toolbox talk are done.

Q: The crane operator can't see you and the radio fails mid-lift. The operator should stop. Restore communications or reposition before continuing. Nobody should guess.

Q: When would you stop a lift? Stop it whenever conditions change from the plan. Examples are rising wind or swell, people entering the drop zone, gear damage, unclear signals or a load behaving unexpectedly. Most company SMS give every crew member stop-work authority, so use it.

Practical tips from the deck

  • Never put any part of your body between a load and a fixed object, or under a suspended load. Landing the load is one of the highest-risk moments for crush injuries.
  • Learn your hand signals until they are automatic, and agree before every lift which signals and radio channel you will use.
  • Keep your own log of lifts and operations. It strengthens your CV and supports any later application for sea service recognition.
  • Look after your gloves, boots and hard hat, and report any PPE that is past its best.
  • Read the lift plan and ask questions in the toolbox talk. Experienced riggers respect a new hand who asks; they worry about one who stays silent.
  • Keep certificates in date. An expired medical or survival certificate can stop you joining however good your rigging is.
Key points
  • A rigger slings and signals lifts and maintains lifting gear, mainly on offshore, heavy-lift and construction vessels.
  • "Rigger" is not an STCW-defined rank; titles and reporting lines vary by company.
  • Riggers signed on as crew need STCW basic training, security training and a valid medical; contractor personnel's requirements depend on the flag, coastal state and client; watchkeeping certificates apply if they also keep a watch.
  • Rigging and banksman qualifications are set by the company, client or region, so confirm the exact certificate before paying for a course.
  • Hours of rest rules normally apply (typically at least 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days), though some flags use a maximum-hours-of-work regime instead.
  • Progression usually leads to lead rigger, deck foreman, crane operator or bosun, and to officer only through an approved CoC route.
  • A strong CV lists vessel types, specific lifting operations and certificate expiry dates.

Frequently asked questions

Is rigger an officer or a rating on a ship?

A rigger is a rating-level position, usually in the deck department or contractor deck crew. It is not a certificated officer rank under STCW. Senior riggers may supervise others on a lift, but they still report to a bosun, deck foreman or Chief Officer.

Which certificates do I need to work as a rigger on an offshore vessel?

You generally need STCW basic training, security training, a valid seafarer medical certificate and an offshore survival course such as BOSIET/HUET where the operation requires it. On top of that, the company or client will specify a rigging, slinging and banksman qualification, and the accepted scheme differs by operator and region. If you are contractor (project) personnel rather than ship's crew, the flag and coastal state may set different requirements, so confirm with the contractor. In every case, check the job advert or ask the contractor before booking courses.

Can a shore-side industrial rigger move to ships?

Yes, this is a common route into offshore and construction vessels. You will still need the mandatory seafarer certificates, including STCW basic training and medical fitness, plus any offshore survival training. Your shore rigging experience counts heavily, but the employer may require a marine-specific rigging assessment.

Does sea time as a rigger count toward an OICNW certificate?

It depends on your administration and on how you were signed on. Service as a deck rating on articles is more likely to be accepted than contractor time. Approved training and specific onboard training records are also required. Check with your national maritime administration before relying on it.

What is the difference between a rigger and a crane operator?

The rigger prepares and slings the load, inspects the lifting accessories and usually gives signals. The crane operator controls the crane and is responsible for operating it within its limits. Crane operation needs its own training and assessment, and many riggers move into it as their next step.

Test yourself

Under STCW and MLC 2006, what is the minimum rest a rigger must have in any 24-hour period?

STCW A-VIII/1 requires at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period for personnel with designated duties. MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 sets the same minimum rest limits where the flag uses a rest-based regime (the alternative is maximum hours of work of 14 in 24 and 72 in 7 days). Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3.

During a routine crane lift, from whom should the crane operator take signals?

Lift plans and safe working practices specify a single designated signaller to avoid conflicting signals, while any person may signal an emergency stop. Reference: company SMS lifting procedures under ISM Code, element 7.

A rigger finds a wire sling with no legible identification or rated capacity marking. What is the correct action?

Unidentified lifting gear cannot be confirmed as certified or within capacity, so it must be withdrawn and reported. Reference: company SMS and lifting gear register under ISM Code, element 10.

Which STCW Code section sets out basic training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR) that a seagoing rigger needs?

Basic safety training for all seafarers with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties is in Section A-VI/1. A-II/4 and A-II/5 cover deck ratings, and A-VI/6 covers security. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

Which statement about the rigger rank is correct?

STCW has no rigger rank. Seafarer certificates still apply to anyone signed on, while rigging qualifications come from industry or company requirements. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1 and Part A, Chapter II.
