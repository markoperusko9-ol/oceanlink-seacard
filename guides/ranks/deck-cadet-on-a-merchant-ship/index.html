Deck Cadet on a Merchant Ship: Role, Training and Career Path

Quick answer

A deck cadet is a trainee officer who completes structured seagoing service under supervision to qualify for a Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (OICNW). Your job on board is to learn bridge, cargo, mooring and safety work while documenting it in a training record book. Once your sea time, record book and exams are complete, the usual next step is Third Officer.

What a deck cadet actually does on board

A deck cadet is on board to be trained. You will also do real work, but the purpose of your contract is to build the competences STCW requires of a watchkeeping officer, under supervision, and to prove them.

A typical spread of duties:

  • Bridge: understudy watches with the officer of the watch (OOW). You take bearings, plot positions, read the radar and ECDIS, keep a lookout, help with log entries and learn COLREG in practice.
  • Deck and maintenance: you work with the bosun and ABs on chipping, painting, greasing, rigging and lashing. This teaches you how the deck crew works, which you will need when you are giving them orders.
  • Cargo work: you assist the duty officer with draft surveys, ullaging or sounding, hatch and lashing checks, and cargo documentation. The detail depends on ship type.
  • Mooring and anchoring: you stand fore or aft stations with an officer and learn line handling, communications and the danger zones.
  • Safety and maintenance of equipment: you help the officer responsible for LSA and FFA with inspections, and you take part in every drill.
  • Navigation support: you help the navigating officer with chart and publication corrections and passage plan preparation.

You are not a spare AB and you are not a steward. If your training is being neglected, raise it politely with your shipboard training officer or Chief Officer, and with the company training officer if needed.

Watch pattern and working day

There is no single pattern. It depends on the company's training plan, the Master's standing orders and the ship's trade. Common arrangements are:

  • Day work with periods on the bridge, especially early in the contract.
  • Understudy watches on the 4–8 with the Chief Officer, or with another officer the training plan assigns, often later in the contract.
  • Cargo watches in port, alongside the duty officer.
  • All stations (arrival, departure, anchoring, shifting) on top of your normal routine.

Hours of rest still apply to you. Under MLC 2006 your flag State sets either minimum hours of rest or maximum hours of work. Most use the rest standard, which matches STCW: at least 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. STCW applies these limits to anyone on watch or with designated safety, security or pollution-prevention duties, which normally includes cadets. Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of them at least 6 hours long. Administrations may allow limited exceptions, so check the ship's hours-of-rest table.

If you are under 18, MLC 2006 places extra restrictions on night work and hazardous tasks for young seafarers. Exceptions for approved training programmes are set by each flag state. Record your hours honestly, because falsified rest records are a port state control finding and a safety issue.

Chain of command: who you report to

Person Relationship to the cadet
Master Overall responsibility for everyone on board, including your training. Usually signs off key parts of the record book.
Chief Officer Head of the deck department. Normally directs your day-to-day work and often reviews your progress.
Shipboard training officer A designated officer, often the Chief Officer or another deck officer, who supervises and records your training.
Officer of the watch Your supervisor on the bridge. They hold the watch, not you.
Bosun Directs you during deck work and maintenance.
Company training officer (ashore) Oversees the whole training programme and reviews your record book.

Nobody reports to a cadet. You may be asked to guide a new cadet or an OS on a simple job. Do it with care, but you hold no authority over ratings.

When you are in doubt about an instruction, especially on safety, clarify it with the officer in charge before acting.

Certificates and documents you need

Exact requirements are set by your administration (for example DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines), your training institute and the company. In general terms:

Before joining your first ship

  • STCW basic training: personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Security training appropriate to your duties.
  • A valid seafarer medical fitness certificate from an approved doctor.
  • Identity and sea service documents:
    • passport;
    • seaman's book or CDC (in the Philippines, the SIRB);
    • national registration such as the INDoS number in India.
  • Visas as required by the trade, for example a US C1/D for US calls.
  • Any pre-sea course your administration makes compulsory for entry as a cadet.

Before you can sit for your OICNW Certificate of Competency

On top of sea time and exams, STCW expects certain proficiencies. Candidates are commonly required to hold:

  • proficiency in survival craft and rescue boats;
  • advanced fire fighting;
  • medical first aid;
  • radar/ARPA and ECDIS training;
  • bridge resource management;
  • a GMDSS operator's certificate.

Which courses are mandatory, and in what order, varies by administration, so check the current rules issued by your administration before you plan leave between contracts.

The training record book and the step up to Third Officer

Under STCW Regulation II/1, an OICNW candidate must be at least 18 years old. The sea service must be either:

  • at least 12 months as part of an approved training programme, with onboard training documented in an approved training record book; or
  • at least 36 months without such a programme.

Most cadets follow the approved-programme route. Administrations and institutes can, and often do, add their own conditions on top.

How the route usually works

  1. Enter a pre-sea programme. In India this is typically a nautical science degree or diploma route. In the Philippines it is typically the BS Marine Transportation programme with shipboard training. Course structure and duration are set by the institute and administration.
  2. Get sponsored or placed on a ship by a company or manning agency.
  3. Complete the training record book task by task. Each task is signed by the supervising officer and periodically reviewed by the Master.
  4. Return ashore for any required post-sea courses, written examinations and an oral or practical assessment.
  5. Obtain the OICNW CoC. Many companies then promote to Third Officer. Some place new officers as Fourth Officer or Junior Officer first.

Record book discipline

  • Fill it in as you go. Never back-fill a month of tasks the night before sign-off.
  • Get each signature from the officer who actually saw you do the task.
  • Keep sea service testimonials and discharge entries correct. A wrong date or missing stamp can delay your exam eligibility.
  • Photocopy or scan the completed pages regularly, and before you sign off.

What crewing agencies look for in a cadet CV

A cadet CV is mostly a document check plus evidence that you will be trainable and safe. Agencies typically screen for:

  • Valid documents with dates: passport, CDC or SIRB, medical, STCW basic training, security training, national registration numbers and visas. Show the expiry date for each.
  • Pre-sea training details: institute, course, year of passing and results.
  • Any sea time already done: ship name, type, flag, GT, dates and company. Make sure they match your discharge book exactly.
  • Ship-type relevance: for example, tanker basic training if you are applying to a tanker fleet.
  • A clean record: no unexplained gaps, early sign-offs or disciplinary issues.
  • Communication: a clear, honest CV in good English. Many companies also interview or test English and basic navigation knowledge.

Tips

  • Keep it to one or two pages.
  • List certificates in a table: certificate name, number, issue date, expiry date.
  • Do not exaggerate. Agencies verify documents, and fake certificates end careers.
  • Avoid any agent who asks for payment for placement. MLC 2006 requires that seafarers are not charged recruitment or placement fees. Check that the agency is licensed by your administration.

Oral exam style: questions you should expect

Officers on board and examiners ashore often test cadets with quick questions. Be ready to answer these clearly and practically:

  • 'You are on lookout and see a red light fine on the starboard bow. What do you do?' Report it to the OOW at once, with bearing and approximate distance, and keep watching it. The OOW decides the action. You should also be able to explain what the OOW is likely to consider under COLREG Rules 7, 15 and 16.
  • 'What do you check before entering a space that has been closed?' Never enter without the permit and the gas readings. Then walk through the enclosed space entry procedure: ventilation, testing, standby person, rescue equipment and communication.
  • 'Where are the lifejackets, immersion suits and your muster station?' You must know these from your familiarisation on joining. Examiners and PSC inspectors ask this to the newest person on board.
  • 'How do you find the ship's position without GNSS?' Visual bearings, radar ranges, celestial observation, and dead reckoning or estimated position.
  • 'What is your role during a man-overboard alarm?' State your muster list duty. Many first-trip cadets are assigned to keep visual contact or to mark the position.

Worked scenario

At 0530 the Chief Officer asks you to take compass error by amplitude.

  1. Note the time, the observed gyro bearing of the sun and the DR position.
  2. Calculate the true amplitude using the method you were taught.
  3. Compare true with observed to get the error and record it in the compass error book.
  4. Report the result to the Chief Officer.

If the error is unusual, say so straight away. Do not quietly 'adjust' your working.

Practical tips from the bridge

  • Carry a notebook. Write down every new term, valve, pump or procedure, then look it up in the ship's manuals.
  • Ask to do, not just watch. Request to plot the next position, prepare the pilot card or take the draft yourself, under supervision.
  • Learn the ship, not just the book. Walk the fire main, trace the ballast lines and find every emergency stop.
  • Respect the deck crew. ABs and the bosun will teach you more seamanship than any textbook.
  • Stay safe.
    • Wear correct PPE.
    • Never stand in a snap-back zone.
    • Use the permit-to-work system every time.
    • Speak up if something looks wrong. Many companies' safety management systems give every crew member, including cadets, authority to stop unsafe work.
  • Look after yourself. Contracts are long. Keep contact with home, use the welfare facilities and report harassment or bullying through the ship's on-board complaint procedure.
Key points
  • A deck cadet is a trainee officer working under supervision towards the OICNW Certificate of Competency.
  • Under STCW Regulation II/1 the minimum is 12 months of sea service within an approved programme with a training record book, or 36 months without one; administrations may add conditions.
  • You report to the Chief Officer and shipboard training officer under the Master's overall responsibility; no one reports to you.
  • STCW basic training, a valid medical and identity documents are needed before your first ship; further proficiencies are required before the CoC.
  • Hours-of-rest limits (normally 10 hours in 24, 77 in 7 days) apply to cadets on watch or with designated safety duties.
  • The usual next step is Third Officer, though some companies place new officers as Fourth or Junior Officer first.
  • Agencies want valid, verifiable documents, accurate sea time and honesty; legitimate placement should not cost you a fee.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a deck cadet need to sail before becoming an officer?

STCW Regulation II/1 sets a minimum of 12 months of seagoing service within an approved training programme with a training record book, or 36 months otherwise. Your administration may add its own conditions, such as specific ship types or post-sea courses. You must also pass the required examinations to get the OICNW Certificate of Competency.

Can a deck cadet keep a bridge watch alone?

No. A cadet does not hold a Certificate of Competency, so the navigational watch remains the responsibility of a qualified officer of the watch. You may act as lookout or carry out tasks on the bridge, but always under the OOW's supervision.

Who signs my training record book?

Individual tasks are normally signed by the officer who supervised you doing them. The shipboard training officer and the Master review and sign at set intervals. The company training officer may also review it, but the exact procedure follows the record book's own instructions and your company's system.

Do I have to pay an agency to get a cadet berth?

Under MLC 2006, recruitment and placement services must not charge seafarers fees for finding employment. Check that the agency is licensed by your administration and be wary of anyone demanding money for a berth. Report suspicious agents to your national maritime administration.

What comes after deck cadet?

After completing sea time, the training record book and the OICNW examinations, you are eligible for an officer post. Most companies appoint new officers as Third Officer, while some use Fourth Officer or Junior Officer as an intermediate step. From there the path leads to Second Officer, Chief Officer and Master, with further certificates at each stage.

Test yourself

Under STCW Regulation II/1, what is the minimum seagoing service for an OICNW candidate following an approved training programme with a training record book?

Regulation II/1 requires at least 12 months within an approved programme documented in a training record book; 36 months applies without such a programme (STCW Regulation II/1).

What is the minimum rest a cadet must receive under STCW and MLC 2006?

The standard minimum is 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days. Being a trainee does not reduce it, although administrations may permit limited exceptions (STCW Code Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006 Standard A2.3).

Where is a cadet's onboard training formally documented for the OICNW route?

Onboard training under an approved programme must be documented in an approved training record book (STCW Regulation II/1; Section A-II/1).

A cadet is on the bridge during the 4–8 watch. Who is responsible for the navigational watch?

A cadet holds no Certificate of Competency, so the qualified OOW keeps the watch under the Master's direction (STCW Code Section A-VIII/2).

Which STCW Code section sets the standard for basic training a cadet needs before joining?

Section A-VI/1 covers basic training. A-II/1 covers the OICNW standard, A-VIII/1 fitness for duty and rest, and A-VI/6 security training (STCW Code, Section A-VI/1).
