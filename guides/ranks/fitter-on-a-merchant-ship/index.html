Fitter on a Merchant Ship: Career Guide

Quick answer

The Fitter is the engine department's skilled tradesman. They weld, cut, fabricate, machine and repair pipework and machinery parts, normally as a day worker under the Second Engineer. You need STCW basic training and security training, usually an engine watch rating certificate, and a solid welding and fitting background. The exact certificate requirements depend on the flag, the manning agency and the company.

What a Fitter actually does on board

On most merchant ships the Fitter is the most hands-on tradesman in the engine department. Engineers diagnose and plan. Oilers and motormen keep machinery running. The Fitter makes and repairs things.

Typical work includes:

  • Welding and cutting: arc welding (SMAW/stick is the shipboard workhorse) and oxy-acetylene cutting and brazing. On some ships this extends to TIG or MIG work where the equipment is carried.
  • Pipe work: renewing corroded sections of sea water, bilge, ballast, steam and fuel lines. Fabricating flanges, fitting doublers and clamps as temporary repairs, and pressure-testing afterwards.
  • Machining: turning bushes, sleeves, studs and spacers on the workshop lathe. Drilling, tapping and threading.
  • Machinery overhauls: assisting engineers with pumps, valves, purifiers, compressors, heat exchangers and auxiliary engine units.
  • Deck support: repairs to railings, ladders, hatch cover fittings, cleats, pipe supports and lashing points, usually coordinated between the Chief Officer and the Second Engineer.
  • Workshop upkeep: keeping the lathe, drill, grinders, welding plant and gas cylinders in good order, and reporting spares and consumables.

On some ships the rank is combined as Fitter/Welder, or a separate Welder is carried. On smaller crews the Fitter may also stand engine room watches. The job title and scope vary between companies, so read the contract and the company's job description.

Working pattern, reporting lines and hours of rest

Who you report to: day to day, the Second Engineer, who plans engine room maintenance and allocates jobs. The Chief Engineer has overall responsibility. When a job is supervised by the Third or Fourth Engineer, you take instructions from them on that job.

Who reports to you: formally, usually nobody. In practice you will often have a wiper, an oiler or an engine cadet helping you. That makes you responsible for their safety on the job and for showing them how to do it properly.

Typical pattern:

Ship type / setup Usual pattern
UMS (periodically unattended) engine room Day work, often roughly 0800–1700 with breaks; may join the duty rota or call-outs
Manned engine room, smaller crew May stand watches with the engineer on duty, plus day work
Port stays, drydock, breakdowns Extended hours; overtime arranged under the contract

Whatever the pattern, hours-of-rest rules apply. STCW (Section A-VIII/1) covers watchkeepers and anyone with designated safety, pollution-prevention or security duties. Under it you must have at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. Rest may be split into no more than two periods, and one of them must be at least 6 hours.

MLC 2006 requires the flag State to set either maximum hours of work (14 in 24 / 72 in 7 days) or minimum hours of rest (10 in 24 / 77 in 7 days). Most flags use the rest-hours standard.

The Master may suspend the schedule in an emergency, with compensatory rest afterwards. Drills must be conducted so as to minimise disturbance to rest. Some administrations permit limited exceptions under STCW.

Record your hours honestly. Inspectors do cross-check rest records against permit times and the engine log.

Certificates and competencies

The Fitter is an engine rating. The STCW baseline is the same as for other engine ratings, but the exact package depends on the flag administration, your national administration and the company. Typical requirements:

  • STCW basic training (personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, personal safety and social responsibilities). STCW requires you to keep it valid by providing evidence of maintained competence (refresher training) in PST and FPFF every five years. National implementation details vary.
  • Security training: security awareness, or designated security duties if you are assigned security duties.
  • Rating forming part of an engineering watch (STCW Regulation III/4). Many companies require this even for day workers, because you may be put on the watch rota.
  • Able seafarer engine (Regulation III/5). Some companies ask for it, especially where the Fitter is graded above the motormen or oilers.
  • Medical fitness certificate, seaman's book (CDC) and passport, plus any flag state documents the ship requires.
  • Ship-type training where applicable, for example basic training for oil and chemical tanker or liquefied gas tanker cargo operations.

The welding qualification is the part that varies most. STCW does not define a "ship's fitter" welding certificate. Instead, companies and agencies look for national trade qualifications and welding test certificates. In India this is often an ITI or equivalent trade certificate. In the Philippines it is often a TESDA-assessed welding qualification. Some owners run their own practical welding test before signing you on. Ask the crewing agency exactly what the principal wants, and do not assume another company's list applies.

How people become Fitters, and where they go next

Common routes in:

  1. Shore trade first. You train as a fitter, welder or machinist, often with shipyard, fabrication or workshop experience. You then complete STCW basic training and join as an engine rating or directly as Fitter where the company accepts shore experience.
  2. Promotion from within the engine ratings. You start as wiper, then become oiler or motorman. You build up welding and lathe skills on board, and the Chief Engineer recommends you for Fitter when a vacancy arises.
  3. Shipyard or repair-yard background. These candidates are often picked for ships with heavy maintenance loads, such as older bulk carriers or vessels heading for a docking.

Next steps up:

  • Senior rating roles such as Senior/Chief Fitter or Engine Foreman where the company has these grades, or Fitter on larger or more complex vessels. Rank titles and grading vary by company.
  • Specialist ships where fabrication skills are valued, such as offshore support vessels, dredgers, heavy-lift vessels and repair or salvage units.
  • Moving to officer level. Some administrations allow experienced engine ratings to work towards an officer in charge of an engineering watch CoC. This depends on recognised sea service, approved education and training, and examination. Sea-time and course requirements differ by administration, so check your national administration's current rules before planning on it.
  • Ashore: ship repair, superintendent support roles or workshop supervision. Shipboard welding experience is well regarded in these jobs.

What crewing agencies look for in a Fitter's CV

A crewing manager scanning your CV wants proof that you can weld reliably, work safely and keep out of trouble. Make these points easy to find:

  • Welding processes and positions. List what you can actually do (for example SMAW in vertical and overhead positions, gas cutting, brazing). Add any test certificates and their validity.
  • Machining. Lathe work, threading, and any milling or shaping experience.
  • Vessel types, engine makes and GRT/kW. Write them in the usual sea-service table format. Name the main engine and auxiliary engine makers you have worked on.
  • Notable jobs. Name two or three, for example: renewed overboard discharge pipe sections under class attendance; fabricated replacement ladder sections; rebuilt sea water pump with new shaft sleeve turned on board.
  • Certificates. List them with numbers and expiry dates, and check none expire mid-contract.
  • Clean references. Chief Engineer appraisals carry weight. A reference stating "safe hot work practice" is worth more than a long list of adjectives.

Avoid vague claims like "expert in all welding". Interviewers will test you, and some principals run a practical test. Overstating your skills is the quickest way to lose a principal's trust.

Interview and oral-check questions you should be ready for

Chief Engineers and technical superintendents often ask practical questions. Have short, confident answers ready:

  • "What do you need before hot work outside the workshop?" A hot work permit issued under the company SMS. A risk assessment. The area cleared of combustibles and oil residues. Adjacent spaces and the other side of bulkheads checked. Gas testing where required. Fire-fighting equipment at hand. A dedicated fire watch kept during the work and for the period after it that the SMS requires.
  • "How do you store and handle low-hydrogen electrodes?" Keep them dry, in a heated electrode oven or quiver, following the manufacturer's instructions. Damp electrodes cause porosity and hydrogen cracking.
  • "Oxygen and acetylene: what are the hazards?" Never let oil or grease near oxygen fittings. Keep cylinders secured and upright. Store them separately in their designated locations. Fit flashback arrestors and check hoses. Shut the cylinder valves when work stops.
  • "You are renewing a section of sea water line. Walk me through it." Agree isolation with the engineer. Apply lock-out/tag-out to valves and pumps. Drain and vent the line. Confirm zero pressure. Cut out the section and fabricate the new one. Weld, then clean and coat. Pressure-test as instructed, then restore the system and remove the tags.
  • "What PPE for grinding and welding?" Correct shade welding shield, face shield or goggles for grinding, leather gloves and apron, safety footwear and hearing protection. Use respiratory protection and ventilation for fumes, especially galvanised or coated material.

If you don't know something, say so and explain how you would find out. That impresses examiners more than guessing.

Practical tips from the engine room

  • Never start hot work on verbal permission alone. If the permit isn't signed, don't strike the arc. Many engine room fires start from welding spatter landing on lagging or oily bilges.
  • Check the other side of the bulkhead. Fuel tanks, cable trays and stores behind the plate you are welding have caused serious fires.
  • Look after the workshop. Keep it clean, with labelled consumables, a guarded lathe and secured cylinders. Inspectors and superintendents judge the whole department by it.
  • Measure twice, machine once. Record dimensions of anything you make for future reference, and hand them to the engineer for the maintenance record.
  • Teach the cadets and wipers. It builds your reputation and makes your next promotion easier.
  • Keep your own job notebook. Note jobs done, materials used and lessons learned. It feeds your CV and your handover.
  • Do a proper handover when signing off. Cover pending jobs, consumable stock, workshop machine defects and any temporary repairs that still need permanent attention.
Key points
  • The Fitter is the engine department's skilled tradesman, mainly welding, fabrication, pipe work and machining.
  • Usually a day worker reporting to the Second Engineer, but may stand watches on some ships.
  • STCW basic training and security training are standard; an engine watch rating certificate is commonly required.
  • Welding qualifications are national or company-specific, not an STCW Fitter certificate, so confirm what the principal needs.
  • Hot work only under a signed permit with a fire watch, as set out in the company SMS.
  • Hours-of-rest rules (STCW: minimum 10 hours in any 24 and 77 in any 7 days) apply to Fitters; check how your flag implements MLC 2006.

Frequently asked questions

Is Fitter an officer or a rating on a merchant ship?

The Fitter is an engine rating, not an officer. Many companies grade the Fitter as a senior engine rating because of the trade skills involved, but it is not a certificate of competency rank under STCW.

Do I need a welding certificate to sail as Fitter?

Most companies and crewing agencies expect proof of welding competence, such as a national trade qualification or a welding test certificate. Some also run their own practical test. STCW itself does not set a specific welding certificate for ship's fitters, so the requirement comes from the company and principal.

Does a Fitter need the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate?

It depends on the flag administration and the company, but it is commonly required. Fitters may be placed on a watch rota or the UMS duty roster. Check the job requirement with the agency before applying.

Can a Fitter become an engineer officer?

In some countries, experienced engine ratings can work towards an officer in charge of an engineering watch certificate of competency. They need qualifying sea service, approved training and examination. The route, sea-time and courses differ by administration, so confirm the current rules with your national maritime administration.

Who gives the Fitter their daily work?

Normally the Second Engineer, who plans engine room maintenance under the Chief Engineer. Deck repair jobs are usually agreed between the Chief Officer and the Second Engineer before the Fitter is assigned.

Test yourself

On a typical merchant ship, who does the Fitter normally take daily work instructions from?

The Second Engineer plans and allocates engine room maintenance work under the Chief Engineer's overall responsibility. Organisation follows the company SMS under the ISM Code.

Before carrying out welding in the engine room outside the workshop, what is required?

Hot work outside designated areas is controlled through the permit to work system required by the company SMS. This includes area preparation and a fire watch (ISM Code; SOLAS Chapter II-2 fire safety principles).

Which STCW regulation covers the rating forming part of an engineering watch?

STCW Regulation III/4 covers ratings forming part of an engineering watch. III/1 is for officers in charge of an engineering watch, and II/4 is for navigational watch ratings.

What are the minimum hours of rest that apply to a Fitter on day work?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 sets minimum rest of 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period for watchkeepers and those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties. MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 sets the same minimum-rest standard, or the alternative maximum hours of work, at the flag's choice.

How should low-hydrogen welding electrodes be stored on board?

Moisture in the flux causes porosity and hydrogen cracking, so manufacturers specify dry, heated storage for low-hydrogen electrodes.
