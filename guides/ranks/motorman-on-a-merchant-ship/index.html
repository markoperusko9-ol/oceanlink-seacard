Motorman on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

A motorman is an engine-room rating who assists the engineers with watchkeeping, routine maintenance, purifiers, soundings and bunkering. Most fleets expect a motorman to hold the STCW engine rating certificate (Regulation III/4), and increasingly Able Seafarer Engine (Regulation III/5), plus basic safety training and a valid medical. The usual route is wiper, then oiler or motorman, then fitter or senior motorman, and some administrations also allow experienced ratings to work towards an engineer officer's certificate.

What a motorman is, and why the title varies

The motorman is one of the engine-room ratings. Under the engineer officers, the motorman does much of the hands-on work that keeps the plant running: watch rounds, cleaning and overhauling auxiliaries, fuel and lube oil handling, and general upkeep of the engine room.

The title is not standardised. You will see different rating structures depending on owner, manning agency and nationality mix:

  • Wiper → Oiler → Fitter. Common on many Asian-managed fleets. Here 'motorman' is often another name for oiler.
  • Wiper → Motorman → No.1 Motorman / Donkeyman → Fitter. Motorman is the main watch rating and the senior motorman leads the others.
  • 'Motorman' as a general engine rating. Some European and offshore operators use it this way, sometimes combined with fitter duties.

Before you sign a contract, read the job description, not just the rank name. Two 'motorman' jobs on two ships can carry quite different responsibilities.

Day-to-day duties and responsibilities

The exact work is set by the chief engineer and the company Safety Management System. Typical tasks include:

  • Watch rounds: checking temperatures, pressures and levels on the main engine, generators, compressors, boilers and coolers. You report anything abnormal straight away.
  • Purifiers: starting, stopping, desludging and dismantling fuel and lube oil separators for cleaning, under an engineer's direction.
  • Tank soundings: sounding fuel, lube oil, bilge, sludge and water tanks and reporting the figures accurately. Engineers use these for daily consumption records and the Oil Record Book.
  • Bunkering and transfers: manning sounding points, watching levels and keeping communication with the engineer in charge.
  • Maintenance: helping with overhauls of pumps, valves, heat exchangers, generator units and filters. Also cleaning strainers and filters.
  • Housekeeping: keeping bilges, tank tops and plates clean and free of oil. A dirty engine room is a fire risk and a Port State Control finding.
  • Stores and spares: helping receive, stow and keep track of engine stores.
  • Emergency duties: your muster list role, often in an engine-room fire party or on the emergency fire pump and emergency generator.

A good motorman notices things early, such as a seeping gland, a weeping pipe or an unusual noise, and reports them before they become breakdowns.

Watch and working pattern

Your routine depends mainly on whether the engine room is manned continuously or certified for periodically unmanned operation (UMS).

Engine-room type Typical motorman routine
Manned engine room Watchkeeping with an engineer officer, often a 4-on/8-off pattern, plus some day work as directed
UMS engine room Mainly day work during working hours, with engineers on duty overnight on alarm call; may join pre-UMS rounds
In port / manoeuvring Extra hours for bunkering, standby, repairs and stores, all within rest-hour limits

Whatever the pattern, hours of rest under STCW and MLC 2006 apply:

  • at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period
  • at least 77 hours in any 7-day period
  • rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours
  • the interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours

Administrations may permit limited exceptions, and the limits may be suspended in an emergency affecting the safety of the ship, persons on board or cargo. Check your flag's rules.

Record your hours honestly. Falsified rest-hour records are a common PSC deficiency and hide real fatigue.

Who you report to, and who reports to you

On watch, you take instructions from the engineer officer in charge of the watch.

For day work, you normally receive jobs from the second engineer, who plans engine-room maintenance under the chief engineer.

Below you, depending on the rating structure, there may be wipers and sometimes oilers or junior motormen. A senior motorman or fitter may supervise and train them.

On some ships you will also work with the electrician or ETO when electrical isolation is needed, for example before work on a pump motor. Never assume something is isolated because someone told you so. Check the lock-out tag-out arrangement and the permit yourself.

Certificates and competencies you need

The STCW Convention sets the minimum standards. Your national administration (flag or issuing state) applies them and may add its own requirements.

  • Rating forming part of an engineering watch (Regulation III/4, Section A-III/4). The basic engine rating certificate. It covers watchkeeping duties, understanding orders, and safe operation of machinery and emergency equipment. It is required for ratings forming part of an engineering watch, or with designated duties in a UMS engine room, on seagoing ships of 750 kW propulsion power or more.
  • Able Seafarer Engine (Regulation III/5, Section A-III/5). The senior engine rating certificate. The Convention requires you to already qualify as a III/4 rating and to have approved sea service in the engine department. That means 12 months, or 6 months combined with approved training. Many companies now expect this for a motorman post.
  • Basic training (Section A-VI/1): personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities. STCW requires you to provide evidence every five years that you have maintained the standard of competence in personal survival techniques and in fire prevention and fire fighting. Your administration sets how refresher training and assessment are done.
  • Security awareness (Section A-VI/6), or designated security duties training if the ship's security plan assigns you duties.
  • Medical fitness certificate meeting STCW and MLC requirements.
  • Identity and travel documents: passport, seaman's book (CDC), and visas as trading requires.

National processes differ. Indian ratings typically need an INDoS number and DG Shipping-approved courses. Filipino ratings obtain certificates of proficiency through MARINA. Course durations, validity periods and refresher rules are set by each administration, so check your own issuing authority rather than relying on another country's figures.

How you get there and what comes next

Getting in. Most motormen start as a trainee or wiper. They complete pre-sea rating training and the approved sea service needed for the III/4 certificate. In practice, crewing agents usually want to see completed contracts as wiper or oiler before offering a motorman post.

Moving up. The usual progression is:

  1. Wiper
  2. Oiler or motorman (III/4)
  3. Senior motorman (III/5)
  4. Fitter, if you have strong welding and fabrication skills, or senior rating roles such as No.1 motorman

Other paths:

  • Electro-technical rating. If you are interested in electrical work.
  • Engineer officer (OICEW, Regulation III/1). Some administrations offer routes for experienced ratings. These normally require further approved education, assessment and documented sea service. The rules vary a great deal between countries, so check with your administration and an approved training institute before committing.

Whichever path you choose, keep every sea-service testimonial, discharge entry and training record. Missing paperwork is a frequent reason a promotion or certificate application stalls.

What crewing agencies look for in a motorman's CV

Agencies screen fast. Make the important information easy to find:

  • Valid certificates with expiry dates: III/4 and/or III/5, basic training, security, medical, passport and CDC. Expired documents will get your CV rejected.
  • Sea service by vessel type: ship type, main engine make and model, power, and UMS or manned. For example: bulk carrier, slow-speed two-stroke, UMS.
  • Machinery experience: purifier models, generator makes, boilers, OWS, fresh water generator, sewage plant, incinerator, scrubber or BWTS if you have worked with them.
  • Contract completion. Repeated early sign-offs raise questions. If one was for medical or family reasons, be ready to explain.
  • Recommendations or appraisals from chief engineers, and re-hire with the same company.
  • Extra skills: welding, lathe work, tank cleaning or tanker familiarisation, all of which suit fitter or tanker posts.

Keep the CV to one or two pages and list contracts in reverse date order. Never overstate experience. A chief engineer will find out within a week.

Interview-style questions and practical tips

Chief engineers and company superintendents often ask practical questions before hiring. Be ready to answer clearly:

  • How do you prepare a fuel oil purifier for opening? Stop the unit and isolate the feed and heating. Ensure it has come to a complete stop. Apply lock-out tag-out per the SMS, then dismantle with the correct tools.
  • You find water in the main engine lube oil sump sample. What do you do? Report immediately to the duty engineer. Do not try to fix it yourself.
  • What do you check on a round? Levels, temperatures, pressures, leaks, unusual noise or vibration, bilge levels and fire hazards such as oil-soaked lagging.
  • What is your role during bunkering? Know the tanks being filled, sound as ordered, report levels promptly, and know where the emergency stop and SOPEP equipment are.
  • How do you start the emergency fire pump? Describe the steps for your last ship. Do not guess a generic procedure.

Practical tips:

  • Learn the fuel, lube and bilge piping on each new ship during familiarisation. Trace the lines yourself.
  • Use PPE every time, especially ear defenders, gloves and eye protection when cleaning purifiers.
  • Never enter a tank, sump or void space without a permit, gas test and standby person.
  • Write down soundings at the moment you take them. Do not rely on memory.
  • Ask the engineers to explain jobs as you do them. This is how you learn enough to step up to fitter, III/5 or beyond.
Key points
  • A motorman is an engine-room rating who supports engineers with watchkeeping, maintenance, purifiers, soundings and bunkering.
  • The title varies between fleets, so read the job description, not just the rank name.
  • The core certificate is STCW Regulation III/4, and many companies now expect Able Seafarer Engine (III/5).
  • Basic training, security awareness, a valid medical and travel documents are also mandatory.
  • Rest-hour minimums of 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days apply, subject to limited exceptions allowed by the flag administration.
  • The usual progression is wiper, then oiler or motorman, then senior motorman or fitter. Officer routes depend on your administration.
  • Agencies want valid documents, clear vessel and machinery experience, and completed contracts.

Frequently asked questions

Is a motorman the same as an oiler?

In some fleets, yes: the titles are used interchangeably for the main engine-room watch rating. In others, the motorman is a more senior rating above the oiler. Check the job description and the certificate the company requires.

Which STCW certificate does a motorman need?

At minimum, the certificate for a rating forming part of an engineering watch under STCW Regulation III/4. Many companies also want Able Seafarer Engine under Regulation III/5. Basic training, security awareness and a medical certificate are required as well.

How long does it take to go from wiper to motorman?

There is no single answer. It depends on the approved sea service your administration requires for III/4 and on company promotion policy. Most people complete at least one or two contracts as wiper or oiler first. Keep all sea-service records so your eligibility can be verified.

Can a motorman become an engineer officer?

Some administrations offer routes for experienced engine ratings to qualify as an officer in charge of an engineering watch. These normally require further approved education, examinations and documented sea service. Requirements differ widely, so confirm them with your national maritime administration.

Does a motorman keep watches on a UMS ship?

On a ship certified for periodically unmanned engine-room operation, the motorman usually works mainly day work, with engineers covering nights on alarm call. On a manned engine room, the motorman normally keeps watches with the engineer officer in charge. The chief engineer sets the actual arrangement.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers the certificate for Able Seafarer Engine?

Able Seafarer Engine is covered by Regulation III/5. III/4 is the basic engine watch rating, II/5 is Able Seafarer Deck, and III/1 is the engineer officer of the watch. Ref: STCW Convention, Regulation III/5.

On a manned engine-room watch, who gives the motorman direct instructions?

During the watch, the rating works under the engineer officer in charge of the engineering watch. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-2.

What is the minimum rest a motorman must have in any 24-hour period?

Both STCW and MLC 2006 require at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1, and MLC 2006, Standard A2.3.

Before opening a fuel oil purifier bowl for cleaning, what must be done first?

The unit must be stopped, fully at rest and isolated under the company's lock-out tag-out and permit procedures before dismantling. Ref: ISM Code, Section 7 (shipboard operations under the SMS).

Basic safety training for seafarers is specified in which section of the STCW Code?

Basic training (PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR) is in Section A-VI/1. A-VI/6 covers security, A-III/4 the engine watch rating, and A-VIII/1 fitness for duty. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.
