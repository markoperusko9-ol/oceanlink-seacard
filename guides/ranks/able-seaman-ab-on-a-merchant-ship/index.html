Able Seaman (AB) on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

An Able Seaman (AB) is a senior deck rating who keeps navigational watches as helmsman and lookout and carries out skilled deck work under the bosun. The STCW qualification is the Able Seafarer Deck certificate of proficiency (Regulation II/5), built on top of the Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch (Regulation II/4). From AB, the usual next steps are bosun or, through further study and sea service, a watchkeeping officer's CoC, with the exact route depending on your administration.

What an AB actually does on board

The AB is the backbone of the deck crew. You are trusted to work with little supervision, and you are expected to teach the ordinary seamen and cadets alongside you.

Typical duties fall into two groups.

On the bridge (watchkeeping AB): - Steering by hand when ordered, especially in pilotage waters, narrow channels and heavy traffic - Keeping a proper lookout by sight and hearing, and reporting lights, shapes, vessels, floating objects and anything unusual to the OOW - Bridge rounds, fire and security rounds, and gangway watch in port - Rigging and attending the pilot ladder

On deck (day work and operations): - Mooring and unmooring, and anchoring stations forward and aft - Maintenance: chipping, painting, greasing, rope and wire work, splicing and seizing - Cargo watches and lashing work, depending on ship type: container lashing, hatch cover operations, tank cleaning support on tankers, and so on - Rigging staging, bosun's chairs and gangways, under a permit where your SMS requires one - Attending enclosed space entry as a standby or entrant when trained and authorised - Mustering and crewing survival craft in drills and emergencies

On tankers, gas carriers and passenger ships the cargo and safety role is larger, and you will need extra training for that ship type.

Watch and working pattern

Most deep-sea ships carry three watchkeeping ABs on a 4-on, 8-off bridge watch: 00-04/12-16, 04-08/16-20 and 08-12/20-24. Other ABs, or the watchkeepers in their off-watch hours, work as day workers under the bosun. On some ships, particularly short-sea and offshore, you may find 6-on/6-off or 12-hour patterns.

Watchkeeping ABs are normally pulled from the bridge for mooring, anchoring and cargo operations, so the pattern is never as neat as the watch bill suggests.

Hours of rest are the hard limit. Under STCW and MLC 2006 the minimum is: - 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period - 77 hours in any 7-day period - Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours long

The MLC alternative expressed as maximum hours of work is applied by some flags. For watchkeepers, the STCW minimum hours of rest apply regardless of which MLC option the flag uses. Drills and emergencies have their own provisions. Record your hours honestly. Falsified rest records are a common Port State Control finding, and they protect nobody when there is an accident.

Who you report to and who reports to you

Situation You report to
On bridge watch The Officer of the Watch (OOW)
Deck work The bosun, who takes direction from the chief officer
Mooring/anchor stations The officer in charge of that station
Port gangway/cargo watch The duty officer

In practice the chief officer is the head of department for all deck ratings, and the master holds overall command.

Below you: ordinary seamen (OS) and deck cadets or trainees work with you. You are not formally their line manager, but you are expected to supervise and instruct them on the job. On many ships a senior AB acts as bosun's mate and leads small work parties.

Certificates and competencies

Under STCW the international qualification is Able Seafarer Deck (Regulation II/5). Some administrations still print "Able Seaman" or similar national titles on their documents, so check what yours actually issues.

The broad STCW framework:

  • Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch (II/4): the entry-level watchkeeping qualification
  • Able Seafarer Deck (II/5): requires a minimum age of 18 and that you already meet the II/4 requirements. The seagoing service requirement, completed while already qualified as a II/4 rating, can be met in one of two ways:
    • 18 months of approved service in the deck department, or
    • 12 months of approved service plus approved training
  • Competence is assessed against STCW Code Section A-II/5, which builds on the A-II/4 watch-rating competences (steering, lookout). A-II/5 adds contributing to a safe navigational watch, mooring and anchoring, cargo and stores handling, safe operation of deck equipment, occupational health and safety, pollution prevention, and shipboard maintenance and repair.

Other documents you will normally hold: - STCW Basic Safety Training (A-VI/1): PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR, kept current with refreshers as your administration requires - Security training (A-VI/6): security awareness for all seafarers, plus designated security duties training if you are assigned security duties (commonly the case for ABs doing gangway watch) - PSCRB (A-VI/2): many companies require this for ABs who are lifeboat or rescue boat crew. Whether it is mandatory for you depends on the flag and the ship's muster list. - Tanker, gas or IGF Code basic training if you serve on those ships - A valid seafarer medical certificate - Seaman's book or record book, passport and any visas your trade needs - Flag state endorsement or recognition where the ship's flag requires it

Service requirements, how sea time is documented, the assessment method and the issuing authority differ by administration. India and the Philippines each have their own process, so confirm the current rules with your administration or an approved training institute before you plan.

How people reach AB and what comes next

The usual route to AB: 1. Pre-sea rating training at an approved institute, plus basic safety training 2. Join as an OS or trainee and complete your training record book or on-board assessments 3. Obtain the II/4 watch rating qualification 4. Build the required deck service 5. Apply for Able Seafarer Deck, with assessment as your administration requires 6. Sign on as AB

Some administrations also have shorter routes for graduates of specific approved courses. Do not rely on an agent's word for this. Check against the official rules.

Next steps from AB: - Bosun: the most common progression. Companies look for several contracts as AB, solid seamanship, people management and a clean record. Bosun is a company appointment rather than a separate STCW certificate in most systems. - Watchkeeping officer (OICNW, STCW II/1): some administrations provide routes for experienced ratings to qualify as officers through approved education and sea service. The entry conditions, academic requirements and service rules vary widely, so research your own administration's route early. Keep every discharge entry and testimonial in order, because you will need them. - Specialist roles: crane operator, DP-related deck roles offshore, or rigger, depending on the sector.

What crewing agencies look for in an AB's CV

Manning agents screen AB CVs fast. Make theirs easy:

  • Sea service table: vessel name, type, flag, GT/DWT, main engine (if asked), rank, sign-on and sign-off dates. This should match your seaman's book exactly.
  • Ship-type experience: tanker, bulk, container, gas, offshore. Same-type experience is often the deciding factor.
  • Certificates with numbers and expiry dates: II/5, basic training and refreshers, security, PSCRB, tanker endorsements and medical.
  • Continuity and completion: completed contracts and reliable re-joining count for a lot. Explain any early sign-off briefly and honestly.
  • References and appraisals: a good word from a previous master or chief officer carries real weight.
  • Practical skills: welding, crane operation, painting systems or rigging experience, if you have it.
  • Valid travel documents: passport validity and any visas the trade needs, such as a US C1/D.

Avoid inflated titles and invented sea time. Agents and companies verify with previous employers, and some administrations keep online records, such as India's INDoS.

Oral exam style: questions masters and assessors ask ABs

These come up in assessments, company interviews and the master's first chat with a new AB.

  • "I order 'Port twenty'. What do you do?" Repeat the order clearly, put the wheel to port 20°, and report "Wheel is port twenty" when the rudder indicator shows it. Never act on an order you have not understood. Ask.
  • "How do you report a light?" Give what you see, its relative bearing (in degrees or points, as your bridge prefers) and anything you notice about it, such as "Green light, two points on the starboard bow". Report early, and report again if it changes.
  • "Why is lookout so important when we have radar and AIS?" COLREG Rule 5 requires a proper lookout by sight and hearing as well as all available means. Small craft, fishing gear and unlit objects often do not show on radar or AIS.
  • "What is a snap-back zone?" It is the area a parted line can whip through. Stay out of it, and remember that on modern mooring arrangements the whole mooring deck may be at risk.
  • "When can you enter an enclosed space?" Only with a permit, after atmosphere testing, ventilation, a standby person, communications and rescue arrangements are in place, as your SMS requires. Never go in to rescue someone without breathing apparatus.
  • "Who checks the pilot ladder?" You rig it under an officer's supervision. A responsible officer must be present and in communication with the bridge when the pilot boards.

Practical tips from the deck

  • Learn the ship in your first week. Muster station, lifeboat duties, fire stations, emergency steering and the mooring arrangement. Walk them yourself.
  • Be the AB the OOW trusts. Report early, stay off your phone on lookout, and wake the OOW's attention with your voice, not a hint.
  • Look after your hands and your back. Wear gloves near wires, use the right lifting technique, and wear proper fall protection aloft.
  • Speak up. If a job looks unsafe, stop it. Stop-work authority is in every decent SMS, and good officers respect an AB who uses it sensibly.
  • Teach the OS. It sharpens your own skills, and bosun selection often hinges on it.
  • Keep your paperwork straight. Get your discharge entries, appraisals and testimonials signed before you leave every ship. Track your certificate expiry dates months ahead.
  • Study while you sail if you are aiming for officer. Rules of the Road, chartwork basics and English pay off whichever route you take.
Key points
  • An AB is a senior deck rating: helmsman and lookout on watch, and skilled deck hand under the bosun.
  • The STCW qualification is Able Seafarer Deck (Regulation II/5), which builds on the II/4 watch rating.
  • Typical deep-sea watch pattern is 4-on/8-off, plus mooring and cargo work as required.
  • Minimum rest is 10 hours in 24 and 77 hours in 7 days. Record it honestly.
  • On watch you report to the OOW. For deck work you report to the bosun under the chief officer.
  • The next steps are bosun, or an officer's CoC where your administration offers a route.
  • Sea service details, the issuing authority and extra courses such as PSCRB vary by flag and company.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an OS and an AB?

An OS is an entry-level deck rating, typically holding the II/4 watch rating qualification or still working towards it. An AB holds the Able Seafarer Deck qualification (II/5), has more sea service and is trusted with more skilled and unsupervised work. ABs are also expected to guide OSs on the job.

How much sea time do I need to become an AB?

STCW Regulation II/5 sets a minimum of either 18 months of approved deck service, or 12 months plus approved training, completed while already qualified as a II/4 rating. Your administration decides how that service is counted, documented and assessed. Check the current rules with your administration before planning.

Is PSCRB mandatory for an AB?

It depends on the flag state and your duties on the muster list. Many companies require PSCRB for ABs because they crew lifeboats and rescue boats. Check with your company and the flag requirements for the ships you are aiming for.

Can an AB become a deck officer?

Yes, in many systems, but the route depends on your administration. It usually involves approved education, further sea service, examinations and assessments leading to an Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch CoC under STCW II/1. Research the entry conditions early and keep your service records complete.

Does an AB have to do night watches?

Watchkeeping ABs on a 4-on/8-off pattern each take a share of the night hours, because every watch includes a night period. Day-working ABs normally work daytime hours but can be called for mooring, anchoring or emergencies. Hours of rest limits apply either way.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers the Able Seafarer Deck certificate of proficiency?

Able Seafarer Deck is under Regulation II/5. II/4 is the watch rating, II/1 is the OOW certificate and VI/1 is basic safety training (STCW Convention, Chapters II and VI).

What is the minimum hours of rest a seafarer must have in any 24-hour period?

The minimum is 10 hours in any 24-hour period. Six hours is the minimum length of one of the two rest periods, not the daily total (STCW Code A-VIII/1; MLC 2006 Standard A2.3).

While on bridge watch, an AB reports directly to whom?

On watch, the AB works under the OOW. The bosun directs deck work, not the navigational watch (STCW Code A-VIII/2).

The OOW orders "Starboard ten". What is the correct helmsman's response?

Closed-loop communication requires repeating the order, executing it and confirming it. You would only ask for a repeat if the order was unclear (STCW Code A-II/4, steer the ship and comply with helm orders).

What is the minimum hours of rest required in any 7-day period?

The minimum is 77 hours of rest in any 7-day period (STCW Code A-VIII/1; MLC 2006 Standard A2.3).
