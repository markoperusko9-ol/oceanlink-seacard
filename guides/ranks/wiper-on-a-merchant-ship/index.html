Wiper on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The wiper is the entry-level rating in the engine department. The job is cleaning, carrying, assisting with maintenance and learning the machinery under the engineers and senior engine ratings. You need STCW basic training, security awareness and a valid medical. The usual next step is oiler or motorman, which normally requires the STCW Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate.

What a wiper actually does

The wiper is the first rung of the engine room ladder. The title comes from the job. Much of the work is keeping the engine room clean, dry and safe, and a clean engine room is a safe one. Leaks, cracks and loose fittings show up on a clean plate long before they show up on a dirty one.

Typical duties include:

  • Cleaning engine room plates, tank tops, bilges, purifier room, workshop and steering gear flat
  • Wiping down machinery and reporting any leak, drip, unusual noise or hot spot to the engineer on duty
  • Collecting and segregating engine room waste (oily rags, filters, metal scrap) into the designated containers
  • Carrying tools and spares, and helping fitters and engineers during overhauls of pumps, purifiers, compressors and auxiliary engines
  • Chipping, scraping, priming and painting in the engine room, funnel and casing
  • Helping with stores and spares loading, and with bunkering when assigned under the supervision of the responsible engineer
  • Standing as fire watch during hot work when detailed and briefed
  • Carrying out muster list duties in fire, abandon ship and other emergency drills

The exact duties depend on the ship type, the size of the engine crew and the company's Safety Management System (SMS). On a small crew a wiper may do far more hands-on mechanical work than on a large one.

Reporting line and working pattern

A wiper normally reports to whoever is directing the day's engine room work. Depending on the ship, that is the oiler or motorman, the fitter, or the engineer in charge of the job. The 2nd Engineer usually plans the daily engine room work. The Chief Engineer has overall responsibility for the department. Nobody normally reports to a wiper. You are the person being trained.

Working patterns vary:

Engine room type Typical pattern for a wiper
Periodically unattended (UMS) Day work, with call-outs for manoeuvring, bunkering or breakdowns
Manned engine room with watches May be placed in a watch with an engineer and oiler, or kept on day work

Your working and rest hours are protected. MLC 2006 requires the flag State to set one of two limits:

  • Maximum hours of work: no more than 14 in any 24 hours and 72 in any 7 days
  • Minimum hours of rest: at least 10 in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days

Most flags use the rest-hours standard. STCW applies the same 10/77 minimum to watchkeepers and to those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties. The administration may allow limited exceptions. Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, and the interval between rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.

Record your hours honestly. Inspectors and auditors check the records, and false records hurt everyone on board.

If you are under 18, MLC 2006 restricts night work and work likely to jeopardise your health or safety. The minimum age for any work on board is 16.

Certificates and documents you need

In general terms, a wiper joining a seagoing ship needs the following:

  • STCW Basic Training (Section A-VI/1): Personal Survival Techniques, Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting, Elementary First Aid, and Personal Safety and Social Responsibilities. Refresher requirements for PST and FPFF apply.
  • Security awareness training (Section A-VI/6), or designated security duties training if the ship assigns you such duties
  • Medical fitness certificate issued by a recognised medical practitioner, as required by STCW and MLC 2006
  • Seaman's book / Continuous Discharge Certificate and a passport
  • Any national registration your administration requires. Indian seafarers, for example, need an INDoS number.
  • Visas and vaccination certificates as required by the trade, such as a US C1/D visa or a yellow fever certificate

A wiper who is not part of an engineering watch does not need the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate (STCW Regulation III/4). STCW requires that certificate for two groups on seagoing ships with 750 kW propulsion power or more:

  • Ratings forming part of a watch in a manned engine room
  • Ratings designated for duties in a periodically unattended engine room

Some companies, flags or manning arrangements expect the certificate anyway. Ship-type training such as basic tanker training (Regulation V/1-1) may also be required if you have tanker cargo-related duties.

Entry routes, approved pre-sea courses, validity periods and documentation differ between administrations, such as India's DG Shipping and the Philippines' MARINA. Check the current requirements with your administration and your crewing agency rather than relying on what a friend did years ago.

How people become wipers, and the next step up

Common routes into the rank include:

  • An approved pre-sea rating course recognised by your administration. India's general purpose (GP) rating course is one example.
  • A trade background, such as fitter, welder, machinist or electrician, plus the mandatory STCW courses
  • Marine engineering students or graduates who cannot find a cadet berth and sail as wiper to get sea experience. Whether that service counts towards an officer certificate depends entirely on your administration's rules, so check before you assume it does.

The usual progression:

  1. Wiper: learn the plant, safety culture and maintenance basics.
  2. Oiler / Motorman: normally requires the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate (STCW III/4). This needs approved seagoing service and/or approved training as set by your administration.
  3. Able Seafarer Engine certificate (STCW III/5): you must be at least 18 and qualified as an engine watch rating. You also need further approved seagoing service and/or approved training as set by your administration. Many companies use it as the qualification for senior oiler/motorman roles rather than as a separate rank.
  4. Fitter or senior rating roles, depending on your skills and the company's structure

Some ratings later move to officer level through an approved route. The rules for that vary widely by administration.

Throughout, keep your discharge book entries, sea service testimonials and training record book (if issued) complete and signed. Missing paperwork, not missing skill, is what most often delays a promotion.

Safety rules a wiper must never bend

New ratings get hurt in the engine room because they are keen, unsupervised or embarrassed to ask. Treat these as fixed rules:

  • Never enter an enclosed space (tank, void, crankcase, sewage tank) without a valid permit, a gas test, and being briefed as part of the entry team.
  • Never work on isolated machinery unless you have confirmed the lock-out/tag-out is in place under the engineer's instruction. Never remove someone else's lock or tag.
  • Never pump bilges overboard yourself. Discharges of oily water are controlled under MARPOL Annex I through the oily water separator and must be recorded. Report oil in the bilge to the duty engineer.
  • Wear your PPE: ear defenders, safety shoes, gloves, goggles, and a face shield when grinding or chipping.
  • Hot surfaces and rotating machinery: keep sleeves, rags and fingers clear of couplings, belts and shafts. Never reach past a guard.
  • Know your muster station and duties from day one. SOLAS requires familiarisation and drills, and the muster list shows your role.

Worked scenario: your first oil leak

You are cleaning the purifier room on day work. You notice fuel oil weeping from a flange on the HFO purifier supply line, and the drip tray is half full.

  1. Do not touch valves or try to tighten the flange yourself.
  2. Tell the engineer on duty immediately. Use the phone or go in person, and give the exact location and what you see.
  3. Keep clear of hot surfaces nearby. Fuel spray onto a hot exhaust is a classic engine room fire cause.
  4. Contain the oil if instructed, using absorbent material from the designated locker.
  5. Dispose of oily rags and absorbent in the oily waste container, not in general garbage.
  6. Afterwards, ask what caused it and how it was fixed. That question is how wipers become good oilers.

Oral exam style: what the chief or crewing office may ask

Interviewers and senior engineers often test a new wiper with simple practical questions:

  • What is your muster station and duty for a fire alarm? Know it without looking.
  • Where is the emergency fire pump and how is it started? Know its location, and that it is started on the engineer's instruction.
  • What is a permit to work, and why do we need one for enclosed spaces? A permit confirms the hazards are identified and controlled before work starts.
  • What would you do if you found water rising in the bilge? Report it immediately. Do not start or stop pumps yourself.
  • Name the main machinery in this engine room. Main engine, generators, purifiers, compressors, boiler, fresh water generator, OWS, sewage plant.
  • What are your hours of rest limits? Normally at least 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days, as shown on the ship's table of working arrangements.
  • What PPE do you need for grinding? Goggles or face shield, gloves, ear protection and safety shoes.

Short, honest answers beat long guesses. "I don't know, but I would ask the duty engineer" is a perfectly good answer for a wiper.

What crewing agencies look for in a wiper's CV

  • Valid documents first: STCW basic training, security awareness, medical, passport, seaman's book, with expiry dates clearly listed. An expired certificate gets a CV binned quickly.
  • Sea service details: vessel names, types, flags, gross tonnage, main engine make and power, and dates on and off
  • Trade skills: welding, lathe work, electrical or pipe-fitting experience, with any certificates
  • Ship-type experience: tanker, bulk, container, offshore. Many companies prefer candidates with experience in their type.
  • References or appraisals from previous Chief Engineers
  • No unexplained gaps: if you were ashore, say why.

Practical tips:

  • Carry a small notebook and sketch the systems you work on.
  • Ask the oiler to walk you through the plant line by line.
  • Volunteer for overhauls.
  • Never fake sea time or certificates. Fraudulent documents are checked, and the consequences can end a career.
Key points
  • The wiper is the entry-level engine rating: cleaning, assisting, carrying, painting and learning the plant.
  • Wipers normally report to the oiler/motorman, fitter or engineer directing the work, with the 2nd Engineer usually planning the day's work.
  • Minimum documents are STCW Basic Training, security awareness, a valid medical, seaman's book and passport, plus any national registration.
  • Most flags apply minimum rest of 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days (STCW and the MLC rest-hours standard); check your ship's posted table of working arrangements.
  • Never enter enclosed spaces, remove locks or pump bilges without authority. Report every leak.
  • The usual next step is oiler/motorman, which normally requires the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate (STCW III/4).
  • Entry routes and service requirements differ by administration, so confirm with your own flag or national authority.

Frequently asked questions

Is a wiper the same as an oiler or motorman?

No. The wiper is the junior rating. Oilers and motormen are more senior engine ratings who normally hold the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate and can stand watches. Some companies use 'oiler' and 'motorman' interchangeably, but the wiper sits below both.

Does a wiper need the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate?

Not if you are not part of an engineering watch. STCW requires it for ratings who form part of a watch in a manned engine room, or who are designated for duties in a periodically unattended one, on seagoing ships with 750 kW propulsion power or more. Some companies or manning arrangements still expect it, so check with your crewing agency.

How long does it take to go from wiper to oiler?

It depends on how quickly you gain the required approved seagoing service and/or training for the STCW III/4 certificate. Your performance and vacancies matter too. Administrations set their own detailed requirements, so check the current rules with your flag or national authority.

Can sea time as a wiper count towards becoming an engineer officer?

Sometimes, but it depends entirely on your administration's rules and the approved route you are on. Some administrations accept certain rating service towards officer training routes, and others do not. Confirm in writing before you plan your career around it.

Do I need to know engineering before joining as a wiper?

No formal engineering knowledge is required beyond the mandatory STCW training. Practical trade skills such as welding, fitting or basic electrical work make your CV stronger and help you progress faster. A willingness to learn and to follow safety rules matters most.

Test yourself

Which STCW provision covers the certificate normally needed to move from wiper to oiler/motorman?

Regulation III/4 covers ratings forming part of an engineering watch. III/5 is the next level up, III/1 is for officers, and VI/1 is basic safety training. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation III/4.

What is the minimum rest a wiper must have in any 24-hour period?

STCW (for watchkeepers and those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties) and the MLC 2006 rest-hours standard both require at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. MLC alternatively allows flags to set maximum hours of work instead. Reference: STCW Code A-VIII/1; MLC 2006 Standard A2.3.

You are told to clean around a purifier that has been stopped for maintenance. What must you confirm first?

A stopped motor or local switch can be restarted remotely, and only the person who applied a lock should remove it. Isolation is confirmed through the company's LOTO procedure under its SMS. Reference: ISM Code, Section 7.

Under MLC 2006, what is the minimum age for working on board a ship?

MLC 2006 sets 16 as the minimum age, with further restrictions on night and hazardous work for seafarers under 18. Reference: MLC 2006, Regulation 1.1 and Standard A1.1.

During day work you find oil collecting in the engine room bilge. What should you do?

Oily bilge water may only be discharged through approved equipment within MARPOL limits and must be recorded. Its source also needs finding, so report it. Reference: MARPOL Annex I.
