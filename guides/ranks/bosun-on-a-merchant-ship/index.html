Bosun on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The bosun is the senior deck rating who, working under the Chief Officer, plans and supervises daily deck maintenance, mooring, anchoring and cargo-related work by the ABs and OSs. There is no separate STCW 'bosun certificate': most bosuns hold Able Seafarer Deck and the standard safety certificates. The rank is earned through years as a reliable AB, and the exact requirements depend on the flag, the administration and the company.

What a bosun actually does

The bosun (boatswain) is the foreman of the deck crew. The Chief Officer decides what needs doing. The bosun turns that into a daily job list, puts the right people on each task and makes sure the work is done safely and properly.

Typical responsibilities:

  • Planned maintenance on deck: chipping, painting, greasing, wire and rope renewal, hatch cover seals and cleats, deck machinery lubrication. Most of it is recorded in the company's planned maintenance system through the Chief Officer.
  • Mooring and unmooring: usually in charge of the forward station under the officer there. Responsible for line handling, winch operation and crew positioning.
  • Anchoring: preparing the windlass, clearing away the anchor, reporting cable direction and number of shackles to the bridge.
  • Cargo-related work: hold cleaning on bulkers, lashing supervision on container ships, deck line and manifold preparation on tankers. The bosun works under the duty officer's direction.
  • Rigging: pilot ladder and accommodation ladder rigging, gangway nets, stages and bosun's chairs for overside work.
  • Stores: deck stores, paint, ropes, wires and consumables. The bosun keeps the inventory and raises requisitions with the Chief Officer.
  • Safety on deck: making sure the permit, risk assessment and toolbox talk are done before hazardous work, and that PPE is worn.
  • Training: showing OSs, trainees and deck cadets the practical seamanship that no college fully teaches.

The bosun is also the link between officers and ratings. Good bosuns raise crew concerns early and pass instructions down clearly.

Working pattern and who reports to whom

On most deep-sea ships the bosun is a day worker, not a watchkeeper. A typical pattern is daytime working hours at sea, plus being called out for:

  • arrival and departure
  • anchoring
  • pilot boarding
  • shifting berth
  • bunkering
  • emergencies

In port, hours follow the cargo operation and the mooring schedule. On some smaller ships and some trades, the bosun also stands bridge or gangway watches. This depends on manning and the company's arrangements.

Hours of rest still apply to day workers. Under STCW and MLC 2006, the minimum is 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. MLC 2006 lets administrations set either maximum hours of work or minimum hours of rest. However, seafarers covered by STCW Section A-VIII/1, which normally includes a bosun with designated safety duties, must still get the STCW minimum rest of 10 hours in 24 and 77 hours in 7 days, subject only to the permitted exceptions. Record the hours honestly; port State control inspectors do check them against logbooks.

Reporting lines (typical):

Direction Who
Reports to Chief Officer (day-to-day); duty officer during cargo, mooring and anchoring
Supervises ABs, OSs, deck trainees; often the deck fitter or carpenter where carried
Works closely with Bosun's counterpart in the engine room (fitter/motorman) on deck machinery; pumpman on tankers

In an emergency, the bosun's duties are those given on the muster list. These often include leading a fire party or a lifeboat launching team.

Certificates and competencies

STCW does not define a 'bosun' grade. The bosun's papers are those of a senior deck rating, plus whatever the flag, the ship type and the company require. In general terms you should expect to need:

  • Able Seafarer Deck (STCW Regulation II/5, Section A-II/5). Many administrations and companies treat it as the baseline for a bosun.
  • Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch (Section A-II/4). This is normally already held from earlier in your career.
  • Basic training (Section A-VI/1): personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (Section A-VI/2). This is required where you are designated to take charge of survival craft or rescue boats, and most companies expect a bosun to hold it.
  • Security training (Section A-VI/6). The level depends on whether you have designated security duties.
  • Tanker or gas endorsements (Chapter V) if serving on those ship types.
  • Valid medical fitness certificate (STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006).
  • Seaman's book / continuous discharge certificate, passport and any visas the trade needs.

Which certificates are mandatory, and whether national courses are added on top, varies by administration. India's DG Shipping, the Philippines' MARINA and the flag State may each have their own requirements, and individual companies often add more. Always check the current requirements with your administration and your manning agent rather than relying on a colleague's old list.

Competence matters as much as paper. A bosun is expected to be confident in:

  • splicing
  • rigging and wire handling
  • mooring line management and snap-back awareness
  • windlass and winch operation
  • paint systems and surface preparation
  • enclosed space and working-aloft precautions
  • basic stability awareness when moving weights or ballast-related work on deck

How seafarers reach bosun, and the next step

The usual route is:

  1. Join as a deck trainee, cadet rating or OS.
  2. Gain sea service and certification as a watchkeeping rating.
  3. Qualify as Able Seafarer Deck and serve as AB.
  4. Build a record of reliable contracts, good appraisals and recommendations from Chief Officers.
  5. Get promoted to bosun, often first within the same company, sometimes initially on smaller ships.

There is no fixed number of years. Companies look at how many contracts you have done as AB, on which ship types, and what your officers say about your leadership.

After bosun, seafarers usually go one of three ways:

  • Stay as a career bosun. Experienced bosuns are valued and often retained by the same company for years.
  • Move to officer rank. Some administrations allow ratings with qualifying sea service to undertake approved training and examinations for an Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch CoC (STCW Regulation II/1). The sea service, education and course requirements differ widely by administration, so check the current rules with yours before planning around it.
  • Specialise. Some bosuns move into offshore, tug, anchor-handling or heavy-lift work, where rigging and deck skills are in demand. Some move ashore as riggers, stevedore supervisors or marine superintendent assistants, depending on qualifications.

What crewing agencies look for in a bosun CV

Manning agents shortlist quickly. Make it easy for them:

  • Sea service table: vessel name, type, GT/DWT, flag, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates, and manning agent. Keep the dates consistent with your seaman's book.
  • Bosun or acting-bosun time: show it clearly, including any time as AB doing bosun duties.
  • Ship type experience: tanker, bulk, container, gas, offshore. Agents usually prefer bosuns who have done the same ship type.
  • Certificates with numbers and expiry dates, and no expired essentials.
  • Practical skills:
    • wire splicing
    • welding (if you hold a qualification)
    • crane operation
    • mooring on specific systems
    • hatch cover types
    • painting systems
  • Clean record: no unexplained gaps, early sign-offs explained honestly, references from Chief Officers or Masters.
  • English ability: you will be giving instructions and safety briefings to a mixed crew.

Avoid padding. An agent who calls a former Chief Officer will spot invented experience immediately.

Interview-style questions you should be able to answer

Superintendents and Chief Officers often test a prospective bosun with practical questions. Prepare short, confident answers.

  • "How do you prepare for mooring?"
    • Check the winches, brakes and lines.
    • Brief the team on the plan and the snap-back zones.
    • Confirm communications with the bridge.
    • Make sure nobody stands in a bight or in line with a loaded rope.
  • "An AB says the cargo hold atmosphere smells fine, can he go down?"
    • No.
    • Entry only with a permit, an atmosphere tested by a competent person, ventilation, a standby person and rescue equipment ready, as per the company's enclosed space procedure.
  • "How do you rig a pilot ladder?"
    • Rig to the side and height requested.
    • Check the ladder condition and its securing to designated strong points (never to rails or stanchions), with shackles and securing ropes at least as strong as the side ropes.
    • Fit the manropes if requested, plus lighting, a lifebuoy with self-igniting light, and a heaving line.
    • The responsible officer checks it before the pilot boards.
  • "A crew member refuses to wear a harness aloft. What do you do?"
    • Stop the job.
    • Explain the reason, and report to the Chief Officer if he still refuses.
    • No one works aloft without fall protection and a permit.
  • "How do you plan the week's work?"
    • Agree the priorities with the Chief Officer.
    • Use the planned maintenance list.
    • Allow for weather, port calls and hours of rest.
    • Check that the stores are available first.

Practical tips for new bosuns

  • Your first week: walk the whole deck with the off-signing bosun. Learn every store, every machinery quirk and the condition of every mooring line.
  • Write things down: keep a notebook of jobs done, stores used and defects found. It makes reports to the Chief Officer quick and accurate.
  • Never let schedule pressure beat the permit system: many of the most serious deck accidents involve mooring lines, enclosed spaces, working aloft or falling objects. You set the standard the crew follows.
  • Lead by example, not by shouting: a mixed-nationality crew responds to clear instructions and fairness.
  • Look after the ratings' rest hours: overworking the crew to finish painting is a safety and compliance problem.
  • Teach: the OSs and cadets you train now will judge you as a leader.
  • Hand over properly when signing off, so your relief starts where you finished.
Key points
  • The bosun is the senior deck rating and supervises ABs, OSs and trainees under the Chief Officer.
  • Most bosuns are day workers but are called out for mooring, anchoring, pilot boarding and emergencies.
  • STCW has no separate bosun certificate; Able Seafarer Deck plus basic and safety training is the usual baseline.
  • Exact certificate requirements depend on the flag, administration, ship type and company.
  • Promotion to bosun comes through proven AB service and leadership, not a fixed number of years.
  • Next steps include career bosun, an officer route where the administration permits, or specialist offshore/rigging roles.
  • Safety leadership on mooring, enclosed spaces and work aloft is central to the job.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an STCW certificate for bosun?

No. STCW does not define a bosun grade. Bosuns normally hold Able Seafarer Deck, a watchkeeping rating certificate and the required safety training. Some administrations and companies set extra requirements, so check with yours.

Who does the bosun report to?

The bosun normally reports to the Chief Officer for day-to-day work. During cargo work, mooring or anchoring, the bosun also takes instructions from the officer in charge of that operation.

Does a bosun keep watches?

On most deep-sea ships the bosun is a day worker. On some smaller ships or in some trades the bosun may stand bridge or gangway watches, depending on manning and company arrangements.

How long does it take to become a bosun?

There is no fixed period. Companies promote ABs who have several good contracts, relevant ship-type experience, strong appraisals and proven leadership. Some promote internally first, often on smaller ships.

Can a bosun become a deck officer?

In many administrations, yes. Ratings with qualifying sea service can follow approved training and examinations for an Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch CoC. The sea service and education requirements vary considerably, so check the current rules with your administration.

Test yourself

On a typical merchant ship, who is the bosun's direct line manager for daily deck work?

The Chief Officer is head of the deck department and directs the bosun's daily work, as set out in the company's ISM-based safety management system.

Which STCW Code section sets the standard of competence for Able Seafarer Deck?

Able Seafarer Deck is covered by STCW Regulation II/5 and Section A-II/5. A-II/4 is the watchkeeping rating and A-VI/2 is survival craft proficiency.

Under STCW, what is the minimum rest for a bosun working as a day worker?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 requires at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. This applies to a day-working bosun who has designated safety duties, as bosuns normally do through the muster list.

An AB wants to enter a cargo hold that 'smells fine'. What must be in place before entry?

Enclosed space entry requires a permit, atmosphere testing, a standby person and rescue readiness under the company's procedure, in line with SOLAS and IMO enclosed space guidance.

During mooring, where should crew stand while lines are under tension?

Crew should stay clear of bights and snap-back zones. Current mooring safety guidance stresses that marked zones do not cover all risk, so the whole area must be treated as hazardous.
