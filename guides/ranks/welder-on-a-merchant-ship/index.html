Welder on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

A welder on a merchant ship is an engine-department day-work rating who carries out welding, gas cutting and fabrication repairs, normally under the direction of the 2nd Engineer. You need a recognised trade welding qualification, the mandatory STCW safety certificates and a valid seafarer medical certificate, and many companies also expect an engineering watch rating certificate. The usual step up is to Fitter, supported by an Able Seafarer Engine certificate where your administration issues it, and some ratings go on to an engineering officer route, depending on your administration's rules.

What a ship's welder actually does

On most deep-sea cargo ships there is no dedicated welder. Welding is done by the Fitter (often signed on as Fitter/Welder). A separate Welder rank is more common in these settings:

  • Cruise ships
  • Offshore units
  • Heavy-lift and specialist vessels
  • Larger fleets with bigger engine teams
  • Riding squads sent aboard for a voyage repair campaign

Typical work includes:

  • Repairs to pipework: seawater, ballast, bilge, fire main, steam and drain lines, plus doublers and clamps as temporary measures.
  • Fabricating brackets, supports, guards, ladders, gratings and handrails.
  • Gas cutting and gouging of corroded or damaged steel.
  • Deck repairs, when approved: hatch coaming fittings, cleats, vent heads, mooring fittings and railings.
  • Brazing and soldering on copper and non-ferrous lines, where the job allows it.
  • Keeping welding equipment, gas cylinders, hoses, regulators and flashback arrestors in good order.
  • Assisting the Fitter and engineers with overhauls, lifting and general machinery work.

One point matters above all. Welding on hull structure, pressure parts or other class items is not a decision for the welder, or even for the ship alone. Permanent repairs to such items normally need the classification society's agreement and are often done by approved shore welders. The Chief Engineer and Master decide, in line with company instructions, what can be done on board.

Working pattern and chain of command

The welder is almost always a day worker, not a watchkeeper. The working day follows the engine-room routine set by the 2nd Engineer.

Overtime is common during repairs and port calls, but your hours must still comply with the rules on hours of work and rest:

  • Under STCW, and under MLC 2006 where the flag uses the rest-hours regime (most do), you must have at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • MLC also permits flags to set maximum working hours instead: 14 hours in any 24 and 72 hours in any 7 days.
  • Your hours are recorded on the ship's rest-hour records.

On deck jobs you will also deal with the Chief Officer, who controls the deck and the cargo areas. On tankers, no hot work goes ahead without the Chief Officer's and the Master's involvement.

When the engine room is manned and machinery is running, you may be asked to assist in emergencies. Your muster list duty will often be in a fire party or an engine-related emergency team.

Certificates and competencies

Requirements are set by the flag state and the company, so the exact list on your contract depends on both. In general terms, expect the following.

Mandatory for everyone on board:

  • STCW Basic Safety Training (Section A-VI/1). It covers personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, elementary first aid, and personal safety and social responsibilities.
  • Security training (Section A-VI/6). You need security awareness at minimum, or designated security duties training if you are assigned those duties.
  • A valid seafarer medical certificate recognised by the flag (STCW Regulation I/9 and MLC 2006 Regulation 1.2).
  • Identity and travel documents: passport, seaman's book/CDC, any flag endorsement, and visas or vaccinations for the trade.

Commonly required by companies:

  • Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch (Section A-III/4). Whether this is mandatory for a day-work welder varies by flag and company. Many crewing agents treat it as standard for engine ratings.
  • Vessel-type training when joining those ships, such as basic oil and chemical tanker training or gas tanker training.

Trade qualification:

  • A recognised welding trade qualification from your home system. For Indian seafarers this is typically an ITI welder trade certificate. For Filipinos it is typically a TESDA welding qualification.
  • Welder qualification tests to recognised standards (for example ISO or AWS based), by process and position, add weight. Some employers, especially offshore, ask for these specifically.

Check current requirements with your administration (DG Shipping, MARINA or the relevant flag). Rules and approved course lists change.

Safe hot work: a worked scenario

Scenario: at anchor on a product tanker, the 2nd Engineer asks you to weld a doubler on a corroded section of a seawater line in the engine room. Here is how it should run under a typical SMS.

  1. Plan and risk assessment. Confirm the Chief Engineer has approved the job. Hot work outside the designated workshop needs a hot work permit, and on a tanker the Master must be involved.
  2. Isolate. Drain and isolate the line. Lock out and tag out the pumps. Confirm the valves are shut and the line is depressurised.
  3. Check the area.
    • Remove combustibles and oily rags.
    • Clean the bilges and tank tops below the work.
    • Check what is on the other side of bulkheads and decks, because heat travels through steel.
    • Cover openings with fire blankets.
  4. Atmosphere. Gas test the area, and any adjoining spaces or pipes the permit requires, with a calibrated detector. Record the results on the permit.
  5. Fire watch and equipment.
    • Post a dedicated fire watch with a suitable extinguisher, and a charged hose if the SMS requires it.
    • If fire detectors are isolated, this is done only as authorised on the permit (normally by the Chief Engineer or as the SMS specifies), with the bridge informed. They must be restored immediately afterwards.
  6. Equipment check.
    • Inspect cables, the earth/return clamp, holders and hoses.
    • Place the return lead close to the work, never with the current path running through bearings, pipes or machinery.
    • For gas work, fit flashback arrestors, secure cylinders upright, and check hoses are free of damage.
  7. Do the job.
    • Wear proper PPE: a welding helmet with the correct shade, leather gloves, apron and safety boots.
    • Use local exhaust ventilation, especially with galvanised or coated steel.
  8. After the job.
    • The fire watch stays on for the period set in the SMS and re-checks adjacent spaces.
    • Restore isolations under the permit and lock-out/tag-out procedure.
    • Close the permit and report to the 2nd Engineer.

If the gas reading, ventilation or isolation is not right, stop. Anyone can and should stop hot work that looks unsafe.

How people become ship's welders and where it leads

Typical entry routes:

  • Shore welders, often from shipyard, fabrication or pipeline work, with a trade certificate. They complete the STCW basic courses and a medical, then join through a crewing agency as Welder or Fitter/Welder.
  • Engine ratings (Wiper, Oiler, Motorman) who build welding skill on board and back it up with a formal trade qualification ashore.
  • Riding squad or repair-team work, which can lead to permanent crew positions.

Usual next steps:

  • Fitter. This is the most natural move. It adds machining, lathe work, pump and valve overhauls, and broader mechanical repair.
  • Able Seafarer Engine certificate (Section A-III/5). This is a certificate of proficiency you hold alongside your rank, not a rank above Fitter. Holding it, where your administration issues it, supports a move to Fitter and other senior engine rating posts.
    • Eligibility rests on approved seagoing service and training, as set by your administration.
    • Day-work service may or may not count in the way you expect, so check before you plan around it.
  • Engineering officer. This route needs approved education and training, qualifying seagoing service, and a Certificate of Competency under Regulation III/1. Some administrations offer routes from ratings. Requirements differ widely, so get them in writing from your administration or an approved institute.
  • Offshore and specialist fabrication. Experienced marine welders with coded qualifications often move into offshore, heavy-lift or repair roles.

What crewing agencies look for in your CV

A welder's CV is judged on proof, not adjectives. Include:

  • Trade certificate and welder qualification tests, listing processes and positions. For example: SMAW, GTAW, oxy-fuel cutting, and plate or pipe positions.
  • Materials worked: mild steel, stainless steel, copper alloys, galvanised steel.
  • Sea service with vessel type, flag, GRT and main engine details where known, rank and dates. These should match your seaman's book exactly.
  • STCW certificates with expiry dates, plus your medical certificate and its expiry.
  • Typical jobs done on board: pipe replacements, fabrication, brazing, deck fittings.
  • Clean references from Chief Engineers or 2nd Engineers.

Red flags for agencies include:

  • Unexplained gaps in your record
  • Expired documents
  • Sea service that does not match the CDC
  • Vague claims like "all types of welding"

Indian seafarers should keep their INDoS details and course records consistent across all documents.

Interview-style questions you should be ready for

Chief Engineers and superintendents often test practical knowledge in a short interview. Be ready for questions like these.

  • Why must oxygen and acetylene cylinders be stored separately and secured? Oxygen intensifies fire and acetylene is highly flammable, so a leak or fire involving both is far more serious. Securing them stops cylinders falling and damaging their valves.
  • Where do flashback arrestors go? At the regulator outlets, and commonly at the torch end as well, depending on equipment design and company rules.
  • Why keep the welding return lead close to the job? So stray current does not pass through bearings, pipes, rails or electronics, where it can cause arcing damage.
  • Why ventilate when welding galvanised steel? Zinc fumes can cause metal fume fever.
  • Can you weld on a fuel line that has been drained? Only after proper isolation, cleaning and gas testing under a permit. Drained does not mean gas free.
  • Who can authorise hot work outside the workshop? Whoever the SMS names. This is normally the Chief Engineer, with the Master's approval where the procedures require it.

Answer from your own experience. A concrete example from a real job is worth more than a textbook line.

Key points
  • A ship's welder is an engine-department day-work rating reporting to the 2nd Engineer under the Chief Engineer.
  • On many cargo ships the Fitter does the welding; dedicated welders are more common on cruise, offshore and specialist vessels.
  • Basic Safety Training, security training and a valid medical certificate are mandatory; an engineering watch rating certificate is often expected.
  • A recognised trade welding qualification and documented process and position tests strengthen your CV.
  • All hot work outside the workshop needs a permit, covering isolation, gas testing, a fire watch and checks of adjacent spaces.
  • The Chief Engineer and Master decide on welding to class items or hull structure under company instructions; permanent repairs normally need class agreement.
  • The usual step up is to Fitter, supported by an Able Seafarer Engine certificate where issued; some ratings go on to an officer route.

Frequently asked questions

Is Welder a separate rank on merchant ships?

On some ships, yes, particularly cruise ships, offshore units and specialist vessels. On most cargo ships welding is part of the Fitter's job, and the Fitter is often signed on as Fitter/Welder. Check the actual job description before accepting a contract.

Do I need a CoC to work as a ship's welder?

No. A welder is a rating, not an officer, so a Certificate of Competency is not required. You do need the STCW basic safety and security training and a valid medical certificate. Depending on the flag and company, you may also need a rating certificate such as Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch.

Can a shore welder join a ship directly?

Usually yes, provided you hold a recognised trade qualification and complete the mandatory STCW courses and a seafarer medical. Your administration's documents, such as a seaman's book and, for Indian seafarers, an INDoS number, must also be in place. Agencies favour candidates with shipyard or marine repair experience.

Does welder sea service count towards Able Seafarer Engine or an engineering CoC?

It depends on your administration and how the service is recorded and approved. Some routes accept engine-department rating service, and some set specific conditions. Confirm with your maritime administration or an approved training institute before relying on it.

Can the ship's welder carry out permanent hull repairs?

Not on your own authority. Permanent repairs to hull structure or other class items generally need the classification society's agreement. The work may have to be done or approved by class-approved welders. On board, the Chief Engineer and Master decide on such jobs, following company instructions.

Test yourself

To whom does a ship's welder normally report for day-to-day work?

The welder is an engine-department rating, and the 2nd Engineer plans day-to-day engine department work under the Chief Engineer. Reference: company SMS organisation under the ISM Code.

What are the minimum rest hours required for a welder working overtime during repairs?

STCW Section A-VIII/1 and the MLC 2006 rest-hours option (Standard A2.3) set minimums of 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days. Being a day worker does not exempt you.

A fuel line has been drained for repair. What must happen before welding?

Drained does not mean gas free, so hot work requires isolation, cleaning, atmosphere testing and a permit as set out in the SMS. Reference: ISM Code, Section 7.

Why should the welding return (earth) clamp be placed close to the work?

Stray welding current through bearings or machinery can cause arcing damage, and a short, direct return path prevents this. Reference: company hot work and electrical safety procedures under the ISM Code.

Which STCW training is mandatory for every welder joining a seagoing ship?

All seafarers with designated safety duties need Basic Safety Training, while the other options apply only to specific ranks or duties. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.
