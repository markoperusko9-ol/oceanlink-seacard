Master on a Merchant Ship: Role, Responsibilities and the Road to Command

Quick answer

The master is the person in command of the ship. The master has overriding authority and responsibility for safety, security and pollution prevention, and answers to the company and the flag state. You reach command by holding a Certificate of Competency issued under STCW Regulation II/2, and you normally serve as chief officer first. Your flag administration may add its own requirements on top of the STCW minimum, and the company then decides when you are ready for a first command.

What the master actually does

The master is in command of the ship and carries ultimate responsibility on board for safety of life, the ship, the cargo, security and protection of the marine environment. Three instruments set out that authority:

  • ISM Code, Section 5. The company must define the master's responsibilities in its Safety Management System (SMS). It must also state clearly that the master has overriding authority and responsibility to make decisions on safety and pollution prevention, and to ask the company for help.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34-1. The owner, charterer or operating company must not prevent or restrict the master from taking any decision that, in the master's professional judgement, is needed for safety of life at sea or protection of the marine environment.
  • SOLAS Chapter XI-2, Regulation 8. This gives similar protection to the master's discretion on ship safety and security.

In day-to-day terms, the job covers the following:

  • Navigation. Approving the passage plan and setting standing orders and night orders. The master takes the con in pilotage waters, congested traffic, restricted visibility and anywhere else the master or the standing orders require.
  • SMS on board. Implementing the SMS, running drills, reviewing it and reporting non-conformities to the company.
  • Commercial work. Cargo operations oversight, charter party compliance, notices of readiness, statements of facts and protests where needed.
  • Port State Control (PSC), flag and vetting. Dealing with these inspections, plus any class surveys and audits.
  • People. Crew welfare and discipline. Under MLC 2006 this includes onboard complaint procedures, inspections of food and accommodation, and hours of rest records.
  • Statutory records and reporting. The official log or equivalent records required by the flag, plus oil and garbage record books (with the chief officer and chief engineer) and casualty reports.
  • Pilots. A pilot on board does not relieve the master of responsibility. STCW Code Section A-VIII/2 says this plainly.

Working pattern and hours

On most deep-sea ships with three watchkeeping deck officers, the master does not keep a fixed bridge watch. The master is effectively on call around the clock. Expect to be on the bridge for:

  • arrivals and departures
  • pilotage, channels and traffic separation schemes
  • restricted visibility
  • heavy weather
  • any situation the OOW calls you up for

On smaller vessels, coasters and some offshore or short-sea trades, the master may stand a watch as well. This depends on the minimum safe manning document and company practice.

The hours of rest and work rules under STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 and MLC 2006 Regulation 2.3 apply to the master too:

  • STCW minimum rest. STCW sets a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The administration may permit limited exceptions.
  • MLC flag choice. Under MLC, the flag may regulate either maximum hours of work or minimum hours of rest.
  • Emergency suspension. The master may suspend the schedules when needed for the immediate safety of the ship, persons on board or cargo, or to assist others in distress. Rest must be restored afterwards.

The heavy load comes in port. Port calls stack PSC, terminal inspections, agents, surveyors and cargo documents on top of the arrival and departure. Managing your own fatigue is part of the job, not an afterthought.

Contract length and rotation vary widely by company, flag and trade.

Chain of command: who the master reports to and who reports to the master

Direction Who Typical contact
Reports to Company management, usually through the technical/marine superintendent Defects, budgets, crew, inspections
Safety link ashore Designated Person Ashore (DPA), ISM Code Section 4 Safety and pollution concerns, non-conformities
Commercial instructions Charterers, through the company or operator Voyage orders, speed and consumption, ports
Reports to master Chief Officer Deck department, cargo, stability, safety equipment
Reports to master Chief Engineer Engine department, machinery, bunkers
Reports to master All other crew, through their heads of department Through the chain of command

The chief engineer runs the engine department and is the master's technical counterpart. On a well-run ship the two work as a team, and a good master does not micro-manage the engine room.

Charterers give commercial instructions. Those instructions never override the master's safety discretion described above.

Certificates and competencies

The core qualification is a Certificate of Competency as Master issued under STCW Regulation II/2. STCW Code Section A-II/2 sets out the competencies at management level.

STCW provides for separate limits:

  • ships of 3,000 gross tonnage or more
  • ships of 500 to 3,000 gross tonnage

Some administrations also issue near-coastal or other limited certificates.

Alongside the CoC, a master normally needs the following. Exact requirements depend on the flag, the ship type and the trade:

  • Flag state endorsement recognising your CoC, if the ship's flag is not the issuing administration (STCW Regulation I/10).
  • Medical fitness certificate (STCW Regulation I/9).
  • GMDSS GOC for ships in the GMDSS.
  • Medical care certificate. Many administrations require the master to hold the STCW A-VI/4 medical care qualification, because the master is often the person in charge of medical care on board.
  • ECDIS training. Generic ECDIS training is required for ECDIS-fitted ships. Companies must also ensure type-specific ECDIS familiarisation for the equipment fitted (STCW I/14 and the ISM Code). Flags and PSC may check evidence of it.
  • Basic and advanced safety courses. Basic training, PSCRB and advanced fire fighting, kept in date.
  • Tanker and gas endorsements. Advanced tanker or gas endorsements (STCW V/1-1, V/1-2) for tankers and gas carriers.
  • Other endorsements as relevant. IGF Code training for gas-fuelled ships and Polar Code training for polar waters.
  • SSO certificate, where the master is designated as Ship Security Officer.

The CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years (STCW Regulation I/11). Revalidation requires proof of continued professional competence, typically through recent sea service or an approved alternative. Check your administration's rules well before expiry.

Getting to command, and where it leads

STCW Regulation II/2 sets the minimum for a master's CoC on ships of 3,000 GT or more:

  • You must first meet the requirements for officer in charge of a navigational watch.
  • You then need at least 36 months of approved seagoing service as OICNW.
  • That period can be reduced to at least 24 months if at least 12 months of it was served as chief mate.

Many administrations build their own structure on top of this. For example, some require you to hold the chief mate certificate first, or to complete specific courses and written exams before the oral. Your own administration's rules are what count, so check the current requirements directly with it.

A typical route looks like this:

  1. Cadet, then OICNW CoC and service as 3rd/2nd officer.
  2. Chief mate CoC under II/2 and promotion to chief officer.
  3. Service as chief officer. This is where you learn cargo, stability, crew management and inspections in real conditions.
  4. Master CoC once eligible under your administration's rules.
  5. Company assessment for first command. Many companies use an interview, a sailing period under observation, or a supernumerary or familiarisation voyage. Holding the CoC does not mean automatic promotion.

After command. There is no higher seagoing rank. Experienced masters often move ashore as:

  • marine superintendent, DPA or fleet manager
  • port captain or loading master
  • marine surveyor or vetting inspector
  • PSC or flag inspector, through government service
  • maritime lecturer or assessor
  • marine pilot, where the national pilotage system allows

Each of these has its own entry requirements.

What crewing agencies look for in a master's CV

Certificates

  • Clear CoC details: issuing administration, grade, limitations and expiry.
  • Flag endorsements you already hold.
  • Medical certificate, GMDSS, ECDIS (with types), and tanker or gas endorsements, each with an expiry date.
  • Valid passport, seaman's book and visas such as the US C1/D.

Sea service

  • A sea service table listing vessel name, type, GT, engine type and power, flag, trade, rank, and sign-on/off dates.
  • Command time on the type in question, shown separately from chief officer time.

Inspection record

  • PSC inspection results.
  • On tankers, the outcome of oil major vetting inspections. Tanker operators check officers against the experience requirements used in vetting, so make time-in-rank and time-on-type easy to read.

Other items agencies look for

  • Trade experience: specific terminals, ice, STS operations, dry-docking or newbuilding delivery.
  • References from previous companies.
  • No unexplained gaps.

Keep it factual. Inflated or vague claims are easy to check and are the quickest way to lose credibility.

Oral exam style: questions examiners like to ask

Masters' orals test judgement as much as knowledge. Expect questions like these:

  • What gives you authority to refuse a charterer's instruction you consider unsafe? Answer: SOLAS V/34-1 and ISM Code Section 5. Then explain how you would document the refusal and inform the company and DPA.
  • A pilot is on board and you disagree with his manoeuvre. What do you do? Answer: you remain responsible, so challenge early and clearly, and take over if necessary.
  • PSC detains your ship. Walk me through the next 24 hours. Cover:
    • informing the company and DPA
    • understanding each deficiency and its code
    • rectification and evidence
    • requesting re-inspection
    • the SMS non-conformity process
  • Your chief officer reports a crew member has not met hours of rest for three days. What is your responsibility?
  • Scenario: a fire in the engine room at night, mid-ocean. Give your first five actions as master, from alarm and muster through to the decision to release a fixed system and external communications.

Worked scenario. At anchorage, the charterer orders you to berth tonight. The pilot says the wind is at the terminal's limit and the tug count is below what your SMS requires.

The examiner wants to hear you:

  1. Gather the facts: forecast, terminal limits, tug availability, under-keel clearance.
  2. Check the SMS requirement.
  3. Discuss it with the pilot.
  4. Decide.
  5. Record the decision and reasons in the log.
  6. Inform the company and charterer promptly.

They are not looking for bravado or blind compliance.

Practical tips for new and aspiring masters

  • Learn your SMS before you need it. On joining, read the master's responsibilities section, the emergency plans and the company reporting matrix. Know when to call the DPA.
  • Write clear standing and night orders. Officers should know exactly when to call you. Then never criticise anyone for calling you.
  • Handovers matter. Get a written handover covering outstanding PSC or class items, defects, crew issues and pending claims. Sign off the same way.
  • Protect your rest. Delegate port paperwork to the chief officer and the agent where your SMS allows, and plan around arrivals.
  • Build the bridge team. Use BRM principles. Junior officers who feel able to speak up are your best safety barrier.
  • Document decisions. A short, factual log entry made at the time is worth far more than a long explanation written afterwards.
  • Keep learning. Rules change through SOLAS, MARPOL Annex VI, the IGF Code and new MLC amendments. Masters are expected to keep up, not wait for the superintendent to tell them.
Key points
  • The master has overriding authority on safety and pollution prevention under ISM Code Section 5 and SOLAS V/34-1.
  • The master's CoC is issued under STCW Regulation II/2, with management-level competencies in Section A-II/2.
  • STCW sets minimum sea service; your flag administration may add further requirements and exams.
  • Masters on deep-sea ships usually do not keep a fixed watch but are always on call, and hours of rest/work rules still apply.
  • A pilot on board does not relieve the master of responsibility for the ship.
  • The CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years.
  • Common next steps are shore roles such as superintendent, DPA, surveyor, inspector or pilot.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to become a master on a merchant ship?

STCW sets a minimum sea service after you qualify as a watchkeeping officer. The real time depends on your administration's rules, how quickly you complete exams and when your company promotes you. Most officers spend several years as junior officers and a meaningful period as chief officer before first command, so check your own administration's current requirements rather than relying on someone else's timeline.

Does a master keep a navigational watch?

On most deep-sea ships with three watchkeeping deck officers, no. The master is on call and takes the con when required. On smaller ships the safe manning and company practice may require the master to keep a watch.

Can a master refuse an order from the charterer or company?

Yes, where the master judges it necessary for safety of life or protection of the marine environment. SOLAS V/34-1 and ISM Code Section 5 protect this discretion. The master should record the reasons and inform the company and DPA promptly.

Is a master's CoC from one country valid on a ship of another flag?

Normally only after the flag state issues an endorsement recognising your CoC under STCW Regulation I/10. The flag must recognise your issuing administration, and some flags have additional conditions. Under STCW I/10, a flag may allow you to serve for up to three months while your endorsement application is being processed, if you carry documentary proof that it has been submitted. Apply in good time, often through your company or manning agent.

What jobs can a master do ashore?

Common routes include marine superintendent, DPA, port captain, marine surveyor, vetting inspector, flag or PSC inspector, lecturer or assessor, and pilot. Each has its own entry criteria, and some, such as pilotage, are governed by national or port rules.

Test yourself

Which part of the ISM Code covers the master's responsibility and authority?

ISM Code Section 5 covers the master's responsibility and authority, including overriding authority. Section 3 covers company responsibilities, Section 4 the DPA and Section 10 maintenance.

Under which STCW regulation is a Certificate of Competency as master issued?

STCW Regulation II/2 covers master and chief mate. II/1 is OICNW, III/2 is chief and second engineer, and VI/1 is basic safety familiarisation and training.

How often must a master's CoC be revalidated under STCW?

STCW Regulation I/11 requires revalidation at intervals not exceeding five years, with evidence of continued professional competence.

What does SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34-1 establish?

SOLAS V/34-1 protects the master's professional discretion on safety of life at sea and protection of the marine environment.

How do the STCW/MLC hours of rest requirements apply to the master?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 requires these minimum rest periods for watchkeepers and those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties, including the master. MLC 2006 Regulation 2.3 also applies to all seafarers, with the flag regulating either hours of rest or hours of work.
