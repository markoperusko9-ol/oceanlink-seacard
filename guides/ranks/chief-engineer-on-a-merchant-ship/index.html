Chief Engineer on a Merchant Ship: Role, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

The Chief Engineer is the head of the engine department and answers to the Master for the safe, efficient and compliant running of all machinery on board. You reach the rank with a management-level CoC under STCW Regulation III/2 (or III/3 for smaller propulsion power), built on sea service as Second Engineer. Most people get there by sailing as Second Engineer first. The usual next steps are larger or more specialised ships, or a shore role such as technical superintendent.

What the Chief Engineer actually does

The Chief Engineer is the senior engineer officer on board. They are responsible to the Master for everything mechanical, electrical and technical that keeps the ship moving, powered, habitable and legal. In practice the job is less about turning spanners and more about judgement, planning and accountability.

Typical responsibilities, subject to the company Safety Management System (SMS):

  • Machinery reliability: main engine, generators, boilers, steering gear, purifiers, compressors, pumps and auxiliaries. This includes final say on running condition and on when a machine is taken out of service.
  • Planned maintenance: keeping the planned maintenance system (PMS) honest, prioritising jobs and signing off critical equipment work.
  • Fuel and lubricants: bunker planning, bunker quantity and quality checks, fuel changeover for emission control areas, and fuel consumption reporting.
  • Environmental compliance: MARPOL Annex I (oil, OWS, Oil Record Book), Annex IV (sewage), Annex VI (air emissions, fuel sulphur, IAPP), and ballast water management, as split between departments by the SMS.
  • Safety: isolation and permit-to-work for machinery, engine room fire readiness, enclosed space entry in engine spaces, and drills.
  • Surveys and inspections: class surveys, flag inspections, port state control, and vetting inspections on tankers.
  • Administration: requisitions for spares and stores, budget awareness, technical reports to the office, defect reporting and records.
  • People: training, appraisals and supervision of the engine department. This includes making sure work and rest hours are planned and recorded correctly.

The STCW Code sets out one specific duty clearly. The Chief Engineer, in consultation with the Master, decides in advance what the voyage needs and sets engineering watch arrangements to suit. In practice this includes deciding, within the ship's class notation and SMS, when unmanned machinery space (UMS) operation is suitable and when it is not.

Chain of command and working pattern

Reports to: the Master on board. Ashore, the Chief Engineer works day to day with the technical superintendent or fleet manager. The Master keeps overriding authority under the ISM Code.

Reports to the Chief Engineer (the exact structure varies by company and ship type):

Rank Typical relationship
Second Engineer Deputy and day-to-day manager of the engine room and maintenance
Third / Fourth Engineers Watchkeeping or duty engineers and their assigned machinery
Electro-Technical Officer Usually reports to the Chief Engineer, although some companies split this differently
Fitter, Motorman, Oiler, Wiper Ratings, normally directed through the Second Engineer

Working pattern: the Chief Engineer is normally a day worker and does not stand a fixed watch.

  • On UMS ships, the duty engineer answers alarms out of hours. The Chief Engineer is still called for anything serious.
  • On manned engine rooms, the watch rotation is run by the Second Engineer under the Chief Engineer's standing orders.
  • Most SMS manuals expect the Chief Engineer in the engine control room for arrival, departure, narrow-channel transits, bunkering, fuel changeover, and any abnormal condition such as a blackout or main engine slowdown.

Rest-hour rules under STCW and MLC 2006 apply to the Chief Engineer as much as to anyone else. Contract lengths and rotation are set by the employment agreement and any collective agreement, not by STCW.

Certificates and competencies required

The core document is a management-level Certificate of Competency (CoC) for engine officers:

  • STCW Regulation III/2 covers Chief Engineer on ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more.
  • STCW Regulation III/3 covers ships between 750 kW and 3,000 kW.

Both require that the candidate already meets the operational-level (III/1) requirements and has completed the minimum approved seagoing service set by STCW. Service as Second Engineer is either required (III/3) or shortens the minimum period (III/2). Each administration applies its own counting rules, so check them.

STCW sets the minimums. Each administration publishes the exact sea-time counting rules, its exam structure and the courses it requires. Check those with your own administration, such as DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines. Local names also differ, for example 'MEO Class I' in India.

The competence standard is in STCW Code Table A-III/2. It covers four functions at management level:

  1. Marine engineering.
  2. Electrical, electronic and control engineering.
  3. Maintenance and repair.
  4. Controlling the operation of the ship and care for persons on board.

Since the Manila amendments, this standard includes leadership and managerial skills. It also includes high-voltage systems above 1,000 V, for which many administrations require a separate approved course.

Other documents you will normally need:

  • Valid STCW medical certificate (Regulation I/9).
  • Revalidated basic safety training and Advanced Fire Fighting, Medical First Aid and PSCRB, as your administration requires.
  • Flag state endorsements (Regulation I/10) for each flag you sail under, if your CoC was issued by a different state.
  • Ship-type training where relevant: tanker, gas or IGF Code advanced training.
  • Security awareness or designated duties training.
  • Revalidation of the CoC at intervals not exceeding five years (Regulation I/11).

How people reach the rank, and what comes next

The usual route:

  1. Engine cadet or trainee: structured onboard training, then the OICEW (III/1) exam.
  2. Fourth or Third Engineer: watchkeeping and ownership of specific machinery such as purifiers, generators and boilers.
  3. Second Engineer: running the engine room, the planned maintenance system and the ratings, after passing the management-level exam. Many candidates sit the Chief Engineer exam after completing the qualifying service as Second Engineer.
  4. Chief Engineer: often a first appointment as Chief Engineer on a smaller or less complex ship, under the eye of the superintendent.

Some engineers enter from shore workshops or the navy. Their recognised service and any extra requirements are decided by the administration case by case.

Typical next steps after Chief Engineer:

  • Larger or specialised ships: LNG, dual-fuel and electronically controlled engines, or offshore DP vessels.
  • Technical superintendent or fleet manager: the most common move ashore.
  • Newbuilding site team or owner's representative at a shipyard.
  • Classification society surveyor, flag inspector or port state control officer. Each employer or authority sets its own entry requirements.
  • Maritime lecturer or examiner at an approved training institute.

What crewing agencies look for in a Chief Engineer CV

A Chief Engineer CV is read for risk. The agency wants to know that you will not get the ship detained or leave the owner with a machinery claim.

Make these clear on the first page:

  • CoC grade, issuing administration, number and expiry, plus every flag endorsement with its expiry.
  • Sea service by rank: ship name, type, deadweight or GT, main engine make and model, propulsion power in kW, and sign-on and sign-off dates.
  • Engine experience: for example MAN B&W MC versus ME (electronic), or WinGD/Sulzer RTA versus RT-flex and X-type. Note any dual-fuel experience.
  • Ship-type endorsements: tanker or gas cargo training, and IGF Code training if you have sailed on gas-fuelled ships.
  • Inspection record: port state control results, vetting outcomes on tankers, and class surveys handled. Be honest, because agencies check.
  • Dry docks and major overhauls you have managed.
  • References from Masters or superintendents, and appraisal reports from the last two or three contracts if available.
  • Gaps explained briefly: for example a study break for the CoC or a medical leave.

Principles to follow:

  • Keep it to two pages. Use a table for sea service and make the dates consistent with your CDC or seaman's book.
  • Never inflate time in rank or engine types. One phone call to a previous Master will find it.
  • Promotion cases need extra support. A new Chief Engineer applying to a new company is usually helped by a letter from the previous employer confirming suitability for promotion.

Worked scenario: your first day as Chief Engineer on a new ship

You join at anchor and the outgoing Chief Engineer leaves in 24 hours. A structured handover saves you later.

  1. Machinery status: go through outstanding defects, equipment on restriction and any condition of class. Ask what is running in manual that should be automatic.
  2. Critical equipment: check the last tests of the emergency generator, emergency fire pump, steering gear and quick-closing valves, then see the latest test records.
  3. Records: check that Oil Record Book Part I entries are complete and match the sounding records and OWS operation. Review the bunker delivery notes and retained samples, and the fuel changeover log.
  4. PMS: look at overdue jobs and why they are overdue, plus spares for critical machinery.
  5. Pending items: check outstanding requisitions, upcoming surveys, the next port state control window and the dry dock date.
  6. People: check who is new, who is due off, and any competence concerns.
  7. Walk the engine room together: cover bilges, leaks, temporary repairs and alarms that have been 'inhibited'.

Document the handover and sign it with the outgoing Chief Engineer, as your SMS form requires. Raise anything serious with the Master and the office before the reliever leaves. Problems you sign for become yours.

Oral exam style: questions examiners ask Chief Engineer candidates

At management level, examiners care less about textbook definitions and more about how you would manage a situation and protect the ship. Expect questions such as:

  • 'Blackout while the ship is in a pilotage channel. Walk me through the first five minutes.' Cover:
    • informing the bridge;
    • the emergency generator coming on load;
    • finding the cause before restarting;
    • restoring essential services in priority order;
    • steering availability;
    • reporting and the follow-up investigation.
  • 'High oil mist detector alarm. What do you do and what don't you do?' Cover:
    • slow down or stop as your procedures require;
    • keep people away from crankcase doors;
    • do not open the crankcase until it has cooled for the period your procedures specify.
  • 'Port state control finds the OWS bypassed.' Discuss:
    • MARPOL Annex I obligations;
    • your own personal exposure;
    • the office's role;
    • how culture and supervision prevent this in the first place.
  • 'You doubt a bunker's quality.' Discuss:
    • sampling;
    • the bunker delivery note;
    • fuel test results;
    • issuing a note of protest;
    • not using the fuel until its quality is confirmed.
  • 'The superintendent asks you to defer a critical maintenance job to save off-hire.' Discuss:
    • risk assessment;
    • written records;
    • informing the Master;
    • your duties under the SMS.

Answer like a manager. State the immediate safety action, then the communication, then the root cause and record-keeping.

Key points
  • The Chief Engineer heads the engine department and is responsible to the Master for all machinery, safety and technical compliance.
  • The CoC is issued under STCW Regulation III/2 (3,000 kW and above) or III/3 (750 to 3,000 kW), with details set by each administration.
  • Sea time as a qualified Second Engineer is the usual stepping stone; check your administration's counting rules.
  • The Chief Engineer normally works days, not a watch, but is present for manoeuvring, bunkering and abnormal conditions.
  • Flag endorsements, medical fitness and five-yearly revalidation must be kept current to stay employable.
  • Crewing agencies focus on engine types, propulsion power, inspection record and verifiable time in rank.
  • Common next steps are bigger or specialised ships, or shore roles such as technical superintendent or surveyor.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to become a Chief Engineer?

It depends on how quickly you gain the qualifying sea service at each rank and pass each exam. STCW sets minimum service periods, and your administration applies its own counting rules. Promotion also depends on companies trusting you with the rank, not just on holding the certificate.

Is a Chief Engineer CoC for 3,000 kW and above valid on smaller ships?

Generally yes. An unlimited III/2 certificate covers ships below 3,000 kW as well. A III/3 certificate is limited to propulsion power below 3,000 kW. Always check the limitations printed on your own CoC and endorsements.

Does the Chief Engineer keep a watch?

Normally not. The Chief Engineer is a day worker who sets the watch arrangements with the Master and is called for significant alarms and operations. On some small ships, or under certain manning arrangements, this may differ, so follow your safe manning document and SMS.

Who does the Chief Engineer report to, the Master or the superintendent?

On board, the Chief Engineer reports to the Master, who has overriding authority. The technical superintendent is the main shore contact for technical matters. Anything affecting safety or compliance should reach the Master as well.

Do I need high-voltage training to sail as Chief Engineer?

STCW includes high-voltage competence at management level. Many administrations require a separate approved HV course before issuing or revalidating a management-level CoC. Check your administration's current rules, and expect companies with HV ships to ask for it.

Test yourself

Under which STCW regulation is a Chief Engineer CoC for ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more issued?

Regulation III/2 covers Chief and Second Engineers on ships of 3,000 kW or more. III/3 covers 750 to 3,000 kW, III/1 is operational level, and II/2 is for deck officers (STCW Convention, Chapter III).

Who decides in advance the engineering watch arrangements needed for a voyage?

The STCW Code places this duty on the Chief Engineer, in consultation with the Master. The safe manning document sets minimum numbers, not watch arrangements (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2).

How often must a Certificate of Competency be revalidated?

STCW requires revalidation at intervals not exceeding five years, with proof of continued medical fitness and professional competence (STCW Convention, Regulation I/11).

Who signs each completed page of the Oil Record Book Part I?

Each entry is signed by the officer in charge of the operation, and each completed page is signed by the Master (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17).

When does a Chief Engineer need a flag state endorsement?

A CoC issued by another party must be recognised by the flag state through an endorsement before the holder serves on its ships (STCW Convention, Regulation I/10).
