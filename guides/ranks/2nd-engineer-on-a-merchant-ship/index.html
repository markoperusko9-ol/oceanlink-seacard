2nd Engineer on a Merchant Ship: Role, Responsibilities and Career Path

Quick answer

The 2nd Engineer is the Chief Engineer's deputy and runs the engine room day to day. They plan maintenance, allocate work, lead the engine team and take over if the Chief is unavailable. The rank needs a management-level STCW engineering certificate of competency (CoC) for the ship's propulsion power, and it is the usual last step before Chief Engineer.

What the 2nd Engineer actually does

Think of the 2nd Engineer as the engine room's working manager. The Chief Engineer sets priorities and answers to the Master and the office. The 2nd turns those priorities into a daily job list and makes sure the work gets done safely and properly.

Typical responsibilities are below. Exact division of work depends on the company's Safety Management System (SMS) and the Chief's standing orders.

  • Planned maintenance: running the planned maintenance system (PMS), scheduling overdue and upcoming jobs, and recording completed work.
  • Main engine and major machinery: on many ships the 2nd takes direct ownership of the main engine, including unit overhauls, crankcase inspections and performance monitoring.
  • Work allocation: the morning meeting with engineers and ratings, job assignment, and checking the quality of completed work.
  • Safety of work: preparing or checking permits to work, isolations (lock-out tag-out), enclosed space entry and hot work in the machinery spaces.
  • Spares and stores: monitoring inventory, raising requisitions and receiving spares.
  • Bunkering and transfers: often in charge of the practical side under the Chief's overall responsibility. Some companies assign this to the 3rd Engineer instead.
  • Emergencies: usually leads the engine room party or a fire team as shown on the muster list.
  • Training: supervising junior engineers and engine cadets, and signing off their training record book tasks.

The 2nd must also be ready to step up. If the Chief Engineer falls ill or is incapacitated, the 2nd takes charge of the department. That is one reason the rank needs a management-level certificate.

Reporting lines and working pattern

Reports to: the Chief Engineer.

Supervises (varies by ship and manning):

  • 3rd and 4th Engineers, and junior or assistant engineers
  • Engine cadets and trainees
  • Fitter, motorman, oilers and wipers

The ETO normally reports to the Chief Engineer. In practice the ETO works closely with the 2nd on maintenance planning and isolations.

Watch and working pattern:

Engine room type Usual pattern for the 2nd
Periodically unattended (UMS) machinery space Day work, plus a share of the duty engineer rota or alarm call-outs as set by the Chief
Continuously manned engine room Often keeps a watch, traditionally the 4–8, alongside day work
Manoeuvring, arrival and departure Usually in the engine control room with the Chief or as directed

STCW (Section A-VIII/1) requires watchkeepers and officers with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties to have at least 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. MLC 2006 sets equivalent limits, which flag states apply either as maximum hours of work or as minimum hours of rest. These apply to the 2nd as well. Because the 2nd plans the work, they are also responsible for planning it within their team's rest hours. Overhauls in port that run all night and quietly break everyone's rest record are a common finding in Port State Control inspections.

Certificates and competencies required

Under STCW, the 2nd Engineer is a management-level position.

  • Regulation III/2 covers Chief and Second Engineer Officers on ships with main propulsion machinery of 3,000 kW or more.
  • Regulation III/3 covers ships between 750 kW and 3,000 kW.

The competence standards are in STCW Code Section A-III/2. The headline areas are:

  • managing the operation of propulsion plant and machinery
  • planning and scheduling operations, including maintenance
  • managing fuel, lubrication and ballast operations
  • managing electrical and electronic control equipment, including high-voltage systems where fitted
  • detecting and diagnosing machinery faults
  • ensuring safe working practices and compliance with pollution-prevention rules
  • using leadership and managerial skills

For a III/2 Second Engineer certificate, STCW requires you to:

  • meet the requirements for Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch,
  • have at least 12 months' approved seagoing service as an assistant engineer officer or engineer officer, and
  • have completed approved education and training and meet the standard of competence specified in STCW Code Section A-III/2.

That is the international minimum. Your administration sets the actual route. This includes:

  • extra sea time requirements
  • mandatory courses (for example management-level ERM, leadership and managerial skills, high voltage or medical first aid)
  • written and oral examinations
  • in some cases a specific number of months served on certain engine types

Certificate titles also differ. India uses its MEO class grades. Other administrations simply issue a "Second Engineer" CoC with a power limit. Always check your own administration's current rules.

Alongside the CoC you will need:

  • valid basic safety training certificates
  • Advanced Fire Fighting and Medical First Aid where your administration requires them (both are commonly required at management level)
  • security certificates
  • a valid medical fitness certificate
  • any tanker, gas or IGF certificates for the ship type
  • a flag state endorsement if the ship's flag differs from your issuing administration

Under Regulation I/11, a CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years.

How engineers reach 2nd Engineer and what comes next

The usual path looks like this:

  1. Engine cadet or trainee: structured onboard training plus college or academy study.
  2. OICEW CoC (Regulation III/1): sail as 4th or 3rd Engineer, often both.
  3. Approved sea service at operational level, then the management-level courses and exams required by your administration.
  4. First contract as 2nd Engineer. Many companies promote their own 3rd Engineers. They do this after a good appraisal and sometimes after a familiarisation trip or a period of supernumerary sailing.

The next step is Chief Engineer. For III/2 ships, STCW sets a minimum of 36 months' approved seagoing service for Chief Engineer certification. This can be reduced to not less than 24 months if at least 12 months of it was served as Second Engineer Officer. Your administration may add exams, courses or extra requirements on top.

Holding the certificate is not the same as being promoted. Companies usually want to see:

  • several contracts as a confident 2nd
  • good Chief Engineer appraisals
  • experience with the company's engine types before they hand over a Chief's job

Some 2nd Engineers move ashore instead. Common roles are technical superintendent, surveyor or trainer. Others move into specialist sectors such as gas carriers, offshore DP vessels or cruise ships.

Worked scenario: crankcase oil mist alarm at sea

It is 0300 on a UMS ship. The duty alarm wakes you: main engine oil mist detector high. Here is how a competent 2nd handles it. Your company procedure and the engine maker's instructions always take priority.

  1. Inform the bridge immediately. Ask the OOW to reduce engine load or slow down as the maker's instructions require, and inform the Chief Engineer.
  2. Keep people away from the crankcase doors and relief valves. A crankcase explosion can blow doors and relief devices open with flame. Do not stand in line with them.
  3. Do not open the crankcase until it has cooled for the period the maker specifies. Opening it hot lets in air and can trigger a secondary explosion.
  4. Investigate from outside first. Check:
    • bearing and main bearing temperature trends
    • lube oil pressure and temperature
    • cooling water and piston cooling
    • the oil mist detector readings per unit
  5. Once the crankcase is safe to open, isolate as required and inspect. Look for hot spots, bearing white metal, scuffed liners or a blowby source.
  6. Record everything: alarm time, actions, readings and findings. Report through the SMS. The Chief will decide on further reporting to the office.

Examiners like this scenario because it tests technical knowledge, the decision to slow down, and leadership at night with a small team.

Oral exam style questions for aspiring 2nd Engineers

Management-level orals look past the textbook to see if you can run a department. Expect questions like these:

  • "Your 3rd Engineer wants to start a fuel pump overhaul without a permit because 'it's a quick job'. What do you do?" Stop the job. The SMS applies to quick jobs too. Isolate, issue the permit, then proceed.
  • "How do you plan a main engine unit overhaul in a 12-hour port stay?" Cover:
    • spares check before arrival
    • immobilisation approval from the Master and port
    • team briefing and isolations
    • lifting gear checks
    • rest-hour planning
    • a realistic fallback if the job overruns
  • "What would you check after a blackout on restoring power?" Cover:
    • the cause
    • generator and switchboard status
    • restarting essential services in order
    • steering gear and main engine readiness
    • log entries
  • "How do you know your PMS reflects reality?" Spot checks, comparing running hours with records, and making sure jobs are not signed off without evidence.
  • "What pollution-prevention records does the engine room keep?" Under MARPOL Annex I, ships keep an Oil Record Book. Machinery space operations are recorded in Part I. Other records depend on the equipment and annexes that apply.

Answer as the person in charge, not as the person doing the spanner work.

What crewing agencies look for in a 2nd Engineer CV

Crewing staff screen fast. Make the important facts easy to find:

  • CoC details: grade, issuing administration, power limit (unlimited or restricted), expiry date and flag endorsements held.
  • Sea service by rank: separate time as 3rd and 2nd Engineer, with vessel type, GT, kW and dates.
  • Engine makes and models: for example MAN B&W or WinGD, and whether conventional camshaft or electronically controlled. Electronic engine experience is often a deciding factor.
  • Ship-type certificates: tanker, gas, IGF, high voltage, DP or Polar Code, where relevant.
  • Specialised equipment: scrubbers, ballast water treatment systems, dual-fuel plant and shaft generators.
  • Continuity: repeat contracts with the same company and explained gaps. Agents notice frequent short contracts.
  • References and appraisals: Chief Engineer appraisals carry weight.

Keep certificate copies scanned, clear and up to date. Expired documents found during screening can cost you a joining date.

Practical tips for doing the job well

  • Read the Chief's standing orders on day one. Agree who owns which machinery and who signs which records.
  • Hold a short morning meeting every working day. Cover jobs, risks, permits and who is resting.
  • Walk the engine room yourself. Do not rely only on alarm screens and reports.
  • Keep the PMS honest. An inspector will compare it with what they see.
  • Teach as you go. Your 3rd Engineer is your future relief, and cadets remember who trained them properly.
  • Prepare your handover early. Include outstanding jobs, defects, spares on order and anything still under observation.
  • Keep studying for Chief. Use your time as 2nd to learn budgets, reporting, class surveys and dealing with the office.
Key points
  • The 2nd Engineer runs the engine room day to day and reports to the Chief Engineer.
  • It is a management-level rank under STCW: Regulation III/2 for ships of 3,000 kW or more, III/3 for 750–3,000 kW.
  • STCW sets minimum sea service, but each administration sets its own exams, courses and certificate titles.
  • Core duties are planned maintenance, work allocation, safe work permits, main machinery care and training juniors.
  • On UMS ships the 2nd usually does day work plus duty rota; on manned engine rooms they often keep the 4–8 watch.
  • Chief Engineer is the next step, with STCW crediting sea time served as 2nd Engineer.
  • A strong CV shows CoC limits, sea time by rank, engine makes and models, and Chief Engineer appraisals.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 2nd Engineer keep a watch?

It depends on the engine room. On ships with periodically unattended machinery spaces, the 2nd usually does day work and shares the duty engineer rota. On continuously manned engine rooms, the 2nd often keeps a watch, traditionally the 4–8. The Chief's standing orders and company SMS decide the exact arrangement.

What certificate do I need to sail as 2nd Engineer?

You need a management-level engineering CoC: STCW Regulation III/2 for ships of 3,000 kW or more, or III/3 for 750–3,000 kW. The certificate name, exams and extra courses vary by administration, so check your own administration's current requirements. A flag state endorsement is needed if the ship's flag differs from your issuing administration.

How much sea time do I need before getting a 2nd Engineer CoC?

For III/2 ships, STCW sets a minimum of 12 months' approved seagoing service as an assistant engineer officer or engineer officer, on top of meeting the OICEW requirements. Many administrations add their own requirements, such as specific courses or service on particular ships. Treat the STCW figure as a minimum, not the full answer.

Can a 2nd Engineer take over as Chief Engineer?

In an emergency, such as the Chief becoming incapacitated, the 2nd takes charge of the engine department. Being formally appointed as Chief Engineer needs a Chief Engineer CoC for that ship's power. It also needs any flag dispensation or endorsement the administration requires.

Who is responsible for the Oil Record Book: the Chief or the 2nd Engineer?

Entries are made by the officers in charge of the operations, and each completed page is signed by the Master. Within the engine department, the Chief Engineer usually holds overall responsibility, and the company SMS defines who makes the entries. Many ships delegate day-to-day entries to the 2nd or 3rd Engineer under the Chief's supervision.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers certification of Second Engineer Officers on ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more?

Regulation III/2 covers Chief and Second Engineer Officers on ships of 3,000 kW or more. III/1 is the OICEW, III/3 covers 750–3,000 kW and III/6 is the ETO. (STCW Convention, Regulation III/2)

On a typical merchant ship, who does the 2nd Engineer report to?

The 2nd Engineer is the Chief Engineer's deputy. The Chief reports to the Master and deals with the office. (Company SMS under the ISM Code)

Under STCW, at what maximum interval must a certificate of competency be revalidated?

Regulation I/11 requires revalidation at intervals not exceeding five years, by proving continued competence and medical fitness. (STCW Convention, Regulation I/11)

The main engine oil mist detector alarms at sea. What is the correct first approach?

Reducing load and keeping people clear lowers the risk of a crankcase explosion. Opening a hot crankcase can admit air and cause a secondary explosion. (Engine maker's instructions and company SMS)

What is the STCW minimum seagoing service for certification as Second Engineer Officer under Regulation III/2?

STCW requires at least 12 months' approved seagoing service as an assistant engineer officer or engineer officer. 36 months is the Chief Engineer figure, and administrations may require more. (STCW Convention, Regulation III/2)
