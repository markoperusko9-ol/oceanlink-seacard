2nd Officer on a Merchant Ship: Duties, Certificates and Career Path

Quick answer

On most merchant ships the 2nd Officer is the navigating officer. They prepare passage plans, keep charts, ECDIS and publications corrected, and traditionally keep the 12-4 watch under the Master's authority. You need a Certificate of Competency as Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (STCW Regulation II/1) plus the supporting STCW courses. The usual next step is Chief Officer, after the approved sea time and a Chief Mate CoC.

What a 2nd Officer actually does

On a typical deep-sea ship the 2nd Officer (2/O, 'Second Mate') is the navigating officer. The exact split of duties is set by the company SMS and the Master's standing orders, but the core is much the same across fleets.

Navigation duties - Preparing the berth-to-berth passage plan for the Master's approval. This covers appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring. - Keeping ENCs, paper charts (where carried) and nautical publications adequate and up to date. That means weekly Notices to Mariners, ENC updates, T&P notices, and the correction logs and chart catalogue. - Routine checks and records for bridge equipment: gyro and magnetic compass errors, ECDIS, radar/ARPA, AIS, echo sounder, speed log, BNWAS and VDR checks. Defects go to the Master and are reported through the SMS. - Keeping the navigational watch as OOW.

Duties that vary by ship and company - GMDSS. Many ships designate the 2/O as the person with primary responsibility for radiocommunications during distress. On those ships the 2/O also handles the GMDSS log, tests and battery checks. - Medical. On some ships the 2/O looks after the hospital and medical stores. On others this falls to the Chief Officer or the Master. - Port and cargo work. The 2/O keeps a port or cargo watch under the Chief Officer. On tankers, gas carriers and bulkers this can be a large part of the job. - Safety equipment. On some ships the 2/O covers some LSA or FFA upkeep. On others the 3/O does all of it.

Watch pattern and the working day

The traditional pattern at sea on a three-watch ship is:

Watch Hours
2nd Officer 0000-0400 and 1200-1600
Chief Officer 0400-0800 and 1600-2000
3rd Officer 0800-1200 and 2000-2400

This is custom, not law. Some ships rotate the watches, some run 6-on/6-off on short-sea trades, and some put the Chief Officer on day work with a different rota. Expect the 'graveyard' 12-4 watch on most ocean-going ships.

Chart corrections, equipment checks and passage planning usually happen in the morning before the afternoon watch. In port you join the cargo or gangway watch rota set by the Chief Officer.

Whatever the rota, hours of rest apply. STCW Section A-VIII/1 sets minimum rest for watchkeepers in any 24-hour period and any 7-day period, and MLC 2006 sets limits on hours of work or rest. Records are inspected by PSC. If your passage-planning workload is eating your rest hours, tell the Master. Don't hide it in the records.

Who you report to and who reports to you

You report to: - The Master, for all navigation matters, passage plans, standing orders and night orders. On watch you are the Master's representative until relieved, and you call the Master whenever in doubt. - The Chief Officer, for deck, cargo, port watches and, usually, safety rounds and maintenance planning.

Who works under you: - On watch, the lookout or helmsman (AB or rating forming part of a navigational watch). - Deck cadets assigned to you for navigation training. Many companies make the 2/O responsible for signing off the navigation tasks in the cadet's training record book. - The 3rd or 4th Officer, informally, for chart and equipment work. You often guide junior officers, but the formal line still runs to the Master and the Chief Officer.

Certificates and competencies required

The 2/O normally holds the same Certificate of Competency as a 3/O: Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch on ships of 500 GT or more under STCW Regulation II/1. Some administrations label it 'Second Mate'. Others use 'OOW' and leave the shipboard title to the company. The competence standard is in STCW Code Table A-II/1.

On ships under 500 GT the watchkeeping CoC may be issued under STCW Regulation II/3 instead, depending on the administration and trading area.

Typical supporting certificates (check your own administration and the ship's flag):

Area Typical requirement
Basic safety Basic Training (A-VI/1), with refreshers where required
Survival craft PSCRB (A-VI/2-1)
Fire fighting Advanced Fire Fighting (A-VI/3)
Medical Medical First Aid (A-VI/4-1); Medical Care (A-VI/4-2) if you are designated to take charge of medical care
Security Designated Security Duties (A-VI/6)
Radio GMDSS GOC (Regulation IV/2) where required for watchkeeping on GMDSS ships
Navigation Radar/ARPA, ECDIS generic training and BRM, often built into the OOW course; ECDIS type-specific familiarisation for each ECDIS make you sail with
Ship type Tanker or gas endorsements under STCW Chapter V where applicable
Fitness Medical certificate under Regulation I/9

You also need a flag state endorsement if the ship's flag differs from your issuing administration. Course names, refresher intervals and whether the GOC is a separate certificate all differ by administration. Check with yours rather than relying on a crewing agent's verbal list.

How you get there and what comes next

Getting to 2/O 1. Complete approved pre-sea training and sea service as a deck cadet. 2. Pass the OOW CoC examinations (written and oral, per your administration). 3. Sail as 3rd Officer (or 4th Officer on fleets that carry one). Promotion to 2/O depends on company policy, appraisals and time in rank. There is no single STCW requirement for this step.

On some ship types, such as smaller general cargo ships, there is no 3/O. A newly certificated OOW may then join directly as 2/O. On ships under 500 GT the watchkeeping CoC may be issued under STCW Regulation II/3 instead, depending on the administration and trading area.

Next step: Chief Officer - You need a Chief Mate CoC (STCW Regulation II/2 for ships of 3,000 GT or more). - STCW sets a minimum of 12 months' approved seagoing service as OOW for this certificate. Many administrations add their own sea-time rules, approved courses and written and oral exams. The management-level competences in STCW Table A-II/2, such as leadership and managerial skills and stability, must also be covered. - After certification, companies usually want you to have sailed as 2/O on the same ship type before promoting you. On tankers and gas carriers they will also want the advanced cargo endorsement.

Plan your CoC study leave early. Exam slots and course availability can delay you more than the sea time does.

What crewing agencies look for in a 2nd Officer CV

Crewing managers screen hundreds of CVs. Make theirs an easy decision.

  • Valid documents at the top. List CoC, GOC, flag endorsements, passport, seaman's book, medical and any crew visa (e.g. US C1/D), each with its expiry date. Expiring documents get CVs rejected first.
  • Sea service table. Give vessel name, type, GT, flag, engine or propulsion, rank, sign-on and sign-off dates, and company. Highlight time in the rank of 2/O and on the ship type they are hiring for.
  • ECDIS makes. List every type-specific ECDIS you hold familiarisation for. This is often a filter.
  • Ship-type endorsements. Include tanker or gas certificates, DP, Polar Code and any others that match the vacancy.
  • Trading areas. Mention ECA, ice, high-risk areas or heavy port rotations if relevant.
  • References and appraisals from recent Masters. Explain any gaps honestly.
  • Your national seafarer ID where your country requires one (e.g. INDoS for Indian seafarers).

Keep it to two pages, use a clear PDF, and match your name exactly to your passport.

Worked scenario: your first passage plan as 2/O

You join at a discharge port. The Master tells you the next voyage is to a port you have never visited, departing in 36 hours.

  1. Handover. Before the off-signer leaves, get the chart and ENC status, the outstanding corrections, the equipment defects, and the location of the passage planning folder and checklists.
  2. Appraisal. Gather the relevant information:
    • ENC coverage and licences, or chart folios
    • Sailing directions, list of lights, list of radio signals and tide tables
    • Navigational warnings, weather routeing and load line zones
    • Ship's draught, squat and UKC policy
    • Reporting points and VTS, pilotage, ECA and anchorage information
  3. Planning. Lay the track berth to berth in ECDIS. Set safety contour and depth according to company UKC policy. Then mark:
    • Wheel-over points
    • Parallel indexes
    • No-go areas
    • Abort points and contingency anchorages
    • Reporting points and points where you call the Master or go to manual steering
  4. Check. Run the ECDIS route check and resolve every alarm, not just acknowledge it. Then do a visual check of the whole route at an appropriate scale.
  5. Approval and brief. Present the plan to the Master. Once approved, brief the other OOWs. Everyone on the bridge should know the plan before departure.
  6. Monitoring. On passage, check the ship stays on the plan. Amend the plan formally if routeing or weather changes it.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners ask about the 2/O role

Practise answering these out loud. You may meet them in OOW or Chief Mate orals.

  • 'What are the four stages of passage planning?' Appraisal, planning, execution, monitoring. Be ready to give an example of each.
  • 'When will you call the Master?' Cover restricted visibility, traffic concerns, difficulty keeping course, failure to sight land or a mark when expected, unexpected soundings, equipment failure, heavy weather and any doubt at all. Mention the standing and night orders.
  • 'How do you know your ENCs are up to date?' Explain the update status check, permits and licences, T&P notices and the correction log.
  • 'How do you check gyro error?' Use azimuth or amplitude observations, or a transit. Then explain how you log and apply the error.
  • 'Your ECDIS fails mid-passage. What do you do?' Call the Master, switch to the backup arrangement in the SMS, fix the position by independent means, and report the failure.

Examiners want to hear sound reasoning and SMS awareness, not memorised paragraphs.

Key points
  • The 2nd Officer is normally the ship's navigating officer and traditionally keeps the 0000-0400 and 1200-1600 watches.
  • Core duties are passage planning, chart and ENC corrections, publications and bridge equipment checks.
  • The required CoC is Officer in Charge of a Navigational Watch (STCW Regulation II/1); titles and supporting courses vary by administration.
  • You report to the Master on navigation and to the Chief Officer on deck, cargo and port work.
  • GMDSS, medical and LSA duties are often given to the 2/O, but this depends on the ship and company SMS.
  • The next step is Chief Officer, which needs a Chief Mate CoC under STCW Regulation II/2 plus your administration's own requirements.
  • A strong CV shows valid documents with expiry dates, time in rank, ship-type experience and ECDIS makes held.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 2nd Officer CoC different from a 3rd Officer CoC?

Usually not. Both ranks normally sail on the same OOW certificate under STCW Regulation II/1. Promotion from 3/O to 2/O is a company decision based on experience and appraisals. A few administrations use the title 'Second Mate' for the OOW certificate itself, so check how yours names it.

Why does the 2nd Officer keep the 12-4 watch?

It is a long-standing custom. It leaves the 2/O daylight morning hours for chart corrections and passage planning. It is not a legal requirement, and many ships rotate watches or use other patterns under the Master's and company's arrangements.

Do I need a GMDSS GOC to sail as 2nd Officer?

On most ocean-going ships, yes. Officers keeping a navigational watch on ships fitted with GMDSS are generally required to hold a GOC under STCW Regulation IV/2. Some administrations issue it separately and others include it in the OOW process, so confirm with your own administration.

How long must I sail as 2nd Officer before becoming Chief Officer?

STCW sets a minimum of 12 months' approved seagoing service as OOW for the Chief Mate CoC. Your administration may add its own sea-time and course requirements. Companies also set their own time-in-rank and ship-type criteria before promotion.

Can I join a new company directly as 2nd Officer?

Yes, if you hold a valid OOW CoC and can show relevant experience. Many companies prefer previous time as 2/O or a solid record as 3/O on the same ship type. Valid documents, the right flag endorsement and type-specific ECDIS familiarisation make it much easier.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers the CoC normally held by a 2nd Officer on a ship of 500 GT or more?

Regulation II/1 covers officers in charge of a navigational watch on ships of 500 GT or more. II/2 is Master and Chief Mate, II/3 covers ships under 500 GT, and VI/1 is basic safety training. (STCW Convention, Regulation II/1)

What is the first stage of passage planning in the IMO guidelines?

Appraisal comes first: gathering and assessing all relevant information before any track is laid. (IMO Resolution A.893(21), Guidelines for Voyage Planning)

What is the minimum rest an OOW must have in any 24-hour period?

STCW sets a minimum of 10 hours' rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; see also MLC 2006, Standard A2.3)

How often must an OOW Certificate of Competency be revalidated under STCW?

CoC holders must show continued competence and medical fitness at intervals not exceeding five years. (STCW Convention, Regulation I/11)

Under STCW, when should the OOW notify the Master?

The OOW must notify the Master immediately in listed situations, including restricted visibility and any doubt about safety. Calling early is expected, not a failure. (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1)
