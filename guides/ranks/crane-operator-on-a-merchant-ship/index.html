Crane Operator on a Merchant Ship

Quick answer

A shipboard crane operator runs the vessel's cranes during cargo, stores and project lifts, mainly on heavy-lift, project cargo, offshore and some geared bulk and general cargo ships. You need STCW basic training and a medical like any seafarer, plus crane competence certified through company, manufacturer, national or industry schemes. Most people come up from AB, fitter or offshore deck crew, and the usual next steps are senior crane operator, bosun or lifting supervisor.

What a shipboard crane operator actually does

On most merchant ships the cranes are worked by ABs or by shore stevedores. A dedicated Crane Operator rank appears where lifting is the core of the ship's business and the risk is high enough to justify a specialist.

Typical duties:

  • Operating ship's cranes for cargo, project cargo, stores, spares and provisions. On heavy-lift ships this includes tandem lifts with two cranes.
  • Pre-use checks of wire ropes, hooks, sheaves, limit switches, the overload or load moment indicator, brakes, hydraulics, emergency stops and communications.
  • Working to the lift plan for engineered or critical lifts, and checking that the load, rigging and radius stay inside the crane's load chart.
  • Basic maintenance with the engine department, such as greasing, wire lubrication, filter changes and hydraulic leak checks. Major electrical and hydraulic faults go to the engineers or the ETO.
  • Recording operating hours, defects and inspections in the crane logbook or the planned maintenance system.
  • Stopping the job when wind, sea state, visibility or communications fall outside the limits in the crane manual or the company procedure.

The operator does not own the lift on their own. The signaller or banksman, the riggers and the officer in charge all have defined roles. A good operator keeps that team tight.

Where you find the rank

The title, the duties and whether the post is officer, petty officer or rating level all vary by company and by vessel type. Read the job description rather than assuming.

Chain of command and working pattern

Reports to:

  • the Chief Officer for cargo and deck lifting, with the Master holding overall responsibility
  • on heavy-lift and offshore projects, often also the lifting or cargo superintendent, or the client's lift supervisor during the operation
  • the 2nd Engineer or ETO for maintenance and defects, depending on the company's planned maintenance split

Works with or directs during a lift: the signaller or banksman, the riggers or slingers, and the deck crew handling taglines.

Working pattern. Crane operators are usually day workers at sea. They spend that time on maintenance, inspections and preparing for the next port or field operation. In port or on location, hours follow the cargo or project schedule. That often means a two-operator rotation so cranes can work around the clock.

Whatever the schedule, your hours must stay within the limits your flag applies. The minimum-rest standard is at least 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days. MLC 2006 lets the flag state set either minimum rest hours (10/77) or maximum working hours (14 in 24, 72 in 7 days). STCW rest-hour rules apply where you have watchkeeping or designated safety, security or pollution-prevention duties. Flag states may allow limited exceptions under their own rules. Fatigue and crane work are a bad mix, so record your hours honestly.

Certificates and competence

There is no STCW certificate of competency called 'crane operator'. STCW sets the baseline for every seafarer, and crane competence comes from other routes.

STCW and statutory baseline (all seafarers):

  • STCW Basic Safety Training: PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR
  • Security awareness training, or designated security duties training if your role needs it
  • A valid seafarer medical certificate
  • A seaman's book or continuous discharge certificate and a passport
  • Rating Forming Part of a Navigational Watch, or Able Seafarer Deck, if you are also rostered on watch or signed on as AB

Crane competence (varies by flag, company and sector):

  • Company or manufacturer type training on the specific crane model
  • National or industry crane operator certification. Offshore work usually requires recognised industry standards, and the oil major or charterer may specify which
  • Banksman, slinger or rigger training, which many employers want even for operators
  • Lift planning familiarisation for heavy-lift and offshore roles

The ship's lifting appliances themselves must be examined, load tested and certified at intervals set by the applicable rules, as applied by flag and class. Typically that means a thorough examination at least every 12 months, and a load test at least every 5 years and after major repairs. The results go in the register of lifting appliances or the cargo gear register.

SOLAS regulation II-1/3-13 on lifting appliances (SWL 1 tonne and above) and anchor-handling winches applies from 1 January 2026. Design and installation requirements apply mainly to appliances installed on or after that date. Operation, inspection, testing and maintenance requirements apply more widely. Check your company SMS and flag guidance on how it applies to your ship.

If a requirement is not listed in your company's documents, ask the crewing office in writing. Certification rules are not uniform worldwide.

How people get the job and where it leads

Common routes in:

  1. AB or OS on geared ships. Build crane hours on cargo and stores work, then get formal type training.
  2. Fitter or motorman. Your hydraulic and mechanical knowledge suits maintenance-heavy crane roles, especially on heavy-lift ships.
  3. Offshore deck crew. The usual path is deckhand or roustabout, then banksman or rigger, then assistant crane operator, then crane operator.
  4. Shore crane operators moving to sea. You will need full STCW basic training, a seafarer medical and seagoing documents first. Shipboard motion is a new skill.

Where it leads:

  • Senior or lead crane operator on heavy-lift or construction vessels
  • Bosun or deck foreman
  • Lifting supervisor or rigging supervisor, ashore or offshore
  • Crane technician or service engineer with a manufacturer
  • Officer of the watch, if you follow the deck CoC route with approved sea service and training as your administration requires

Sea service as a crane operator may or may not count towards a deck CoC. That depends on how it is recorded and on your administration's rules. Check before you plan around it.

What crewing agencies look for in your CV

Agencies screen crane operators on specifics. 'Crane experience' on its own tells them nothing. Include:

  • Crane types you have operated: knuckle boom, telescopic boom, fixed jib, offshore pedestal, gantry, heavy-lift mast cranes
  • Makers and models, and the safe working load range
  • Operations: tandem lifts, blind lifts, subsea lifts, ship-to-platform or ship-to-ship, grab work
  • Vessel types, flags and operators, with sign-on and sign-off dates
  • Certificates with numbers and expiry dates, both STCW and crane-specific
  • Maintenance experience: wire changes, hydraulic work, limit switch and indicator checks
  • Safety record and references, ideally from a Chief Officer or lifting superintendent

Keep copies of your crane logbook entries or signed experience letters. Some offshore clients ask for documented operating hours.

Interview and oral-style questions

Superintendents and offshore clients often test candidates verbally. Expect questions like these.

Q: What do you check before the first lift of the day? Walk through it in order:

  • crane logbook and any open defects
  • wire rope condition and spooling
  • hook, safety latch and swivel
  • sheaves
  • hydraulic oil level and leaks
  • function test of all limit switches and the emergency stop
  • overload or load moment indicator working
  • radio or signal check with the banksman
  • wind reading against the crane manual
  • area under the load clear

Q: The load moment indicator trips during a lift. What do you do? - Stop and land the load safely if you can, or hold it. - Never bypass or override the indicator to finish the job. - Check the actual weight, the radius and the rigging against the lift plan. - Report to the officer in charge and record it.

Scenario: tandem lift, wind rising. The lift plan sets a wind limit and the reading is approaching it. - Raise it before you start. Do not wait until mid-lift. - Agree a hold point with the lift supervisor. - Confirm you have one signaller giving instructions to both cranes. - If the limit is reached, stop. Stop-work authority applies to you as much as anyone.

Q: Who can give you signals? Only the designated signaller, except for an emergency stop, which anyone may give and which you must obey.

Practical tips from the deck

  • Learn the load chart for your crane. Capacity drops sharply with radius and changes with the vessel's heel and motion.
  • Treat side-loading, side-lead and shock loading as the real enemies of wires, sheaves and booms. Use taglines to control the load, never the crane.
  • Never let anyone stand or work under a suspended load. Know your company's permit to work and toolbox talk rules for lifting.
  • Get friendly with the engineers. Operators who understand the hydraulic circuit diagnose faults faster and get trusted with more.
  • Keep your type training certificates for every crane model. Agencies and clients check them.
  • Log every defect, however small. An unrecorded fault becomes your problem after the next incident or port state control inspection.
Key points
  • Crane Operator is a specialist rank found mainly on heavy-lift, project, offshore and some geared cargo ships.
  • There is no STCW CoC for crane operators. STCW basic training and a medical are the baseline, and crane competence is certified separately.
  • Crane certification requirements vary by flag, company and sector, especially offshore.
  • The operator usually reports to the Chief Officer, and to the lifting superintendent on project work.
  • Never bypass a load moment indicator or limit switch, and obey an emergency stop from anyone.
  • A strong CV lists crane types, makers, SWL range, lift types and certificate expiry dates.
  • Common next steps are senior crane operator, bosun, lifting supervisor or the deck officer route.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an STCW certificate for crane operators on ships?

No. STCW does not define a crane operator certificate of competency. You need STCW basic training and a medical like any seafarer. Crane competence comes from company, manufacturer, national or industry training, depending on the flag, the employer and the sector.

Can an AB become a crane operator?

Yes, it is one of the most common routes. ABs on geared bulk carriers and general cargo ships build practical hours. They then complete formal type training for specific cranes. Offshore and heavy-lift employers usually also want documented operating experience and recognised crane certification.

Who does a crane operator report to on board?

Normally the Chief Officer for deck and cargo lifting, with the Master in overall command. During project or offshore lifts, the operator also works under the lifting or cargo superintendent and the agreed lift plan. Maintenance defects usually go to the 2nd Engineer or ETO, depending on the company.

Does crane operator sea time count towards a deck officer CoC?

It depends on your administration and on how the service is recorded. Some administrations accept certain deck rating service, and others need specific watchkeeping service. Check with your administration or an approved training institute before relying on it.

What working hours does a ship's crane operator have?

At sea it is usually day work on maintenance. In port or on location, hours follow the cargo or project schedule, often with two operators rotating. Under MLC 2006 your flag sets either minimum rest hours (10 in any 24 and 77 in any 7 days) or maximum working hours (14 in any 24 and 72 in any 7 days). STCW rest-hour rules also apply if you have watchkeeping or designated safety, security or pollution-prevention duties.

Test yourself

Which STCW certificate of competency is specifically issued for shipboard crane operators?

STCW sets training and certification for seafarers in general, but it does not define a crane operator CoC. Crane competence is certified through company, national or industry schemes (STCW Convention and Code).

During a lift the load moment indicator trips. What is the correct action?

Safety devices must never be bypassed to complete a lift. The cause must be found and reported under the company SMS (ISM Code, Section 10 on maintenance and reporting of non-conformities).

Under the minimum-rest standard in STCW and MLC 2006, what are the minimum hours of rest?

The minimum-rest standard in both instruments is at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period, with limited flag-approved exceptions. MLC 2006 also lets the flag state use maximum working hours (14 in 24, 72 in 7 days) instead (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3).

During a crane lift, whose signal must the operator always obey, even if it does not come from the designated signaller?

Normal signals come only from the designated signaller. An emergency stop from any person must be obeyed (Company lifting procedures and recognised lifting codes of practice, e.g. ILO Code of Practice on safety and health in ports / national codes of safe working practices).

Where are the examinations and load tests of a ship's cranes and lifting gear recorded?

Examinations and tests of lifting appliances are recorded in the ship's register of lifting appliances or cargo gear register. Typical intervals are a thorough examination at least every 12 months and a load test at least every 5 years and after major repairs, as applied by flag and class (ILO Convention No. 152; SOLAS regulation II-1/3-13).
