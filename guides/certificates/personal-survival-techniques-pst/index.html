Personal Survival Techniques (PST): What It Is, Who Needs It and How to Keep It Valid

Quick answer

Personal Survival Techniques (PST) is one of the four modules of STCW basic training. Its competence standard is set in Table A-VI/1-1 of the STCW Code under Regulation VI/1. Anyone with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties on board must hold it, and every five years you must show you have kept that competence, usually through a refresher course. The exact certificate format, validity marking and refresher route are set by your administration.

What PST is and where the requirement comes from

PST teaches you how to survive if you have to leave the ship. That means:

  • getting into a lifejacket and immersion suit,
  • going into the water safely,
  • getting into and managing survival craft, and
  • staying alive until rescue.

The requirement sits in STCW Regulation VI/1. The detail is in Section A-VI/1 of the STCW Code. Basic training has four parts, each with its own competence table:

Module STCW Code table
Personal Survival Techniques (PST) A-VI/1-1
Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting (FPFF) A-VI/1-2
Elementary First Aid (EFA) A-VI/1-3
Personal Safety and Social Responsibilities (PSSR) A-VI/1-4

Most training centres build their PST course on the IMO model course for personal survival techniques. The administration approves the course and the institute. A certificate from an unapproved centre is worthless at the gangway, however good the training was.

The STCW Code lets an administration grant limited exemptions for certain smaller ships or short voyages. Do not assume one applies to you. Your manning agent and flag will expect the full certificate.

Who needs PST

STCW separates two levels of training.

Safety familiarisation is for all persons employed or engaged on board, other than passengers. This includes contractors and other non-crew personnel working on board. It happens on board before any duties are assigned. It covers:

  • how to raise the alarm,
  • your muster station,
  • what to do in an emergency, and
  • where the lifejackets are.

Basic training (including PST) is for seafarers employed or engaged in any capacity on board, as part of the complement, with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties. In practice this covers:

  • every officer and rating on a muster list,
  • cadets before their first ship, and
  • catering crew, who almost always have emergency duties.

If you are on the muster list with a job to do, you need PST.

How the qualification is issued depends on your administration:

  • If basic training is not already covered by your CoC, a certificate of proficiency must be issued showing you have completed it.
  • Some administrations issue a separate certificate per module.
  • Others issue one combined basic training certificate.

Both approaches are acceptable if the issuing administration and institute are approved.

What the course covers

Table A-VI/1-1 sets out theory and practical demonstrations. Expect classroom work plus pool or sea sessions.

Knowledge and understanding:

  • types of emergency (collision, fire, foundering) and types of survival craft and their equipment,
  • where personal life-saving appliances are stowed on board,
  • survival principles: the value of drills, protective clothing and being ready for any emergency,
  • what to do when called to survival craft stations and when ordered to abandon ship,
  • what to do in the water and aboard survival craft, and
  • the main dangers to survivors: cold, drowning, dehydration, seasickness and loss of morale.

Practical demonstrations you must pass:

  • don a lifejacket correctly,
  • don and use an immersion suit,
  • jump safely from a height into the water,
  • right an inverted liferaft while wearing a lifejacket,
  • swim while wearing a lifejacket,
  • keep afloat without a lifejacket,
  • board a survival craft from the ship and from the water while wearing a lifejacket,
  • take initial actions on boarding survival craft to improve the chance of survival,
  • stream a drogue or sea-anchor,
  • operate survival craft equipment, and
  • operate location devices, including radio equipment.

If you cannot swim, say so early. Some centres can arrange extra pool time. The assessment itself cannot be waived.

Validity, refreshers and revalidation

Under Section A-VI/1, seafarers must provide evidence every five years that they have kept the standard of competence. This applies to PST and FPFF. EFA and PSSR are not covered by the five-yearly rule in the Convention, although some administrations or companies may still ask for them.

The Code allows parties to accept onboard training and experience for some PST elements, such as:

  • donning a lifejacket,
  • boarding survival craft,
  • initial actions in survival craft,
  • streaming a drogue, and
  • operating survival craft equipment and location devices.

The in-water elements, such as righting a liferaft, swimming and jumping, are not on that list. In practice most administrations want a short refresher course at an approved centre that includes the pool work.

What varies by administration:

  • whether the certificate shows an expiry date or only a completion date,
  • whether a refresher is a separate course or the full course repeated,
  • how long before expiry you may revalidate, and
  • whether foreign refresher certificates are accepted.

Check your own administration's current circular rather than relying on what a shipmate did. Rules on recognition have tightened over the years, and online verification of certificates is now common.

Why crew get stopped at joining because of PST

Real reasons crew get held at the airport, the agent's office or the gangway:

  • Expires during the contract. Many companies need certificates valid for the whole contract plus a margin. A PST that runs out in month four of a six-month contract may get you replaced.
  • Refresher missing from the folder. Some checks need both the original certificate and the refresher. Carry both.
  • Name or date-of-birth mismatch. The spelling must match your passport and seaman's book. A missing middle name is enough to trigger a query.
  • Not verifiable. If the certificate number does not appear on the administration's or institute's verification system, the company treats it as invalid until proven otherwise.
  • Unapproved or blacklisted institute. Courses bought from unapproved centres, or certificates issued without attendance, are rejected and can lead to further action against the seafarer.
  • Combined versus separate certificates. A company checklist may list four modules separately while you hold one combined certificate. Bring the course content or the administration's notice showing it covers PST.
  • Damaged or illegible originals. Lamination over security features, missing photos or torn pages cause problems.

Flag endorsement under Regulation I/10 is mainly about CoCs and some tanker and gas certificates of proficiency. Basic training certificates are not normally endorsed, but confirm with your manning office if the flag is unfamiliar.

Worked scenario: planning your refresher

An AB's PST and FPFF refreshers both expire in eight months. He expects a nine-month contract offer next month.

  1. Check expiry against the likely contract. Eight months is less than a nine-month contract, so most companies will flag it.
  2. Check your administration's revalidation window. Find out how early you can refresh without losing time on the five-year cycle. This varies, so read the current rules.
  3. Book PST and FPFF refreshers together. Many approved centres run them back-to-back, which saves a trip.
  4. Confirm the institute is approved. Check that it appears on the administration's approved list for refresher courses specifically, not just full courses.
  5. After the course, verify online. Make sure the new certificates appear on the verification system before you hand copies to the manning agent.
  6. Update your documents. Make scans and update the seafarer's document file. Keep originals in your document folder, not your checked bag.

The main lesson: do the refresher at home, between contracts. Nobody can do a pool session from the middle of the Indian Ocean.

Oral exam style: what examiners and auditors ask

Q: How do you jump from the ship's side? Check below for wreckage, survival craft and people. Hold the lifejacket down with one arm across the chest. Cover your nose and mouth with the other hand. Keep your feet together and look straight ahead. Jump feet first and keep the body upright. Lifejackets meeting the LSA Code must let the wearer jump from at least 4.5 m without injury, but always use the lowest practical height.

Q: How do you right an inverted liferaft? Go to the side with the gas cylinder, ideally with the wind behind you. Climb onto the cylinder and grip the righting strap. Lean back and use your body weight to pull the raft over. The wind helps lift the raft as it comes up. Clear yourself from under it if it lands on you.

Q: What are your first actions once in the liferaft? - Cut or release the painter and get clear of the ship. - Stream the drogue. - Look for and pick up other survivors. - Close the entrances and keep the raft ventilated. - Bail out water and dry the floor. - Give out seasickness tablets. - Check for injuries. - Set up a lookout rota. - Prepare location devices: SART, EPIRB if carried in the craft, and pyrotechnics.

Q: You are in cold water without a raft. What do you do? The first minute or so brings cold shock, with gasping and fast breathing. Keep your airway clear and do not panic-swim. Then use the HELP position (knees up, arms crossed) or huddle with others to reduce heat loss. Swimming speeds up cooling, so swim only towards something you can actually reach.

Q: Why should you never drink seawater? It increases dehydration and can kill. Ration fresh water as instructed by the person in charge of the craft.

Key points
  • PST is one of four STCW basic training modules; its competence standard is Table A-VI/1-1 of the STCW Code.
  • Anyone with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties on board needs PST; familiarisation alone is not enough.
  • Every five years you must show maintained competence in PST and FPFF, usually by an approved refresher course.
  • Certificate format, expiry marking and revalidation windows are set by your administration, so check its current rules.
  • Most joining refusals come from expiry during the contract, name mismatches, missing refreshers or unverifiable certificates.
  • Only train at institutes approved by your administration, and verify the certificate online before you travel.
  • Do refreshers between contracts; the in-water elements cannot be done on board.

Frequently asked questions

Is PST the same as STCW basic training?

No. PST is one of the four modules of basic training, alongside FPFF, EFA and PSSR. Some administrations issue one combined basic training certificate and others issue separate certificates per module. You need all four to have full basic training.

How long is a PST certificate valid?

STCW requires you to provide evidence of maintained competence in PST every five years. How that shows on the certificate depends on the administration: some print an expiry date, others record only the completion date. In both cases the five-year cycle applies.

Can I do my PST refresher on board?

The STCW Code allows some PST elements to be maintained through onboard training and experience. The in-water elements, such as righting a liferaft, swimming and jumping, need a proper facility. In practice most administrations expect a refresher course at an approved centre, so check your administration's rules.

Do I need PST if I work in the galley or as a steward?

Almost certainly yes. Catering crew are normally on the muster list with emergency duties, which brings them under the basic training requirement. Only people with no designated safety or pollution-prevention duties, such as some supernumeraries, can rely on familiarisation alone.

My PST will expire halfway through my next contract. Will the company accept me?

Many companies and manning agents will not, because they want certificates valid for the full contract. Some may allow you to refresh in a port where an approved centre exists, but this is rare and not guaranteed. Do the refresher before you sign on.

Test yourself

Which table of the STCW Code sets the minimum standard of competence in personal survival techniques?

Table A-VI/1-1 covers PST. A-VI/1-2 is fire prevention and fire fighting, A-VI/1-4 is PSSR, and A-VI/2-1 is survival craft and rescue boats (PSCRB). Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

Under Section A-VI/1, which basic training modules require evidence of maintained competence every five years?

The five-yearly evidence requirement applies to the standards in Tables A-VI/1-1 (PST) and A-VI/1-2 (FPFF). Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

A newly joined contractor with no designated safety duties sails for a short project. What does STCW require before they are assigned shipboard duties?

All persons employed or engaged on board, other than passengers, need safety familiarisation before being assigned shipboard duties. Basic training is required for those with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties. Reference: STCW Regulation VI/1 and Section A-VI/1.

When righting an inverted liferaft in the water, where should you position yourself?

Standing on the cylinder side lets you use your weight on the righting strap, and having the wind behind you helps lift the raft over. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-VI/1-1 (righting an inverted liferaft).

Once the survivors are in an inflated liferaft beside a sinking ship, what should be done first?

Getting clear of the ship avoids being dragged down or damaged; drogue, survivor pickup and ventilation follow. Water is rationed, and pyrotechnics are used only when rescue is likely to see them. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-VI/1-1 (initial actions on boarding survival craft).
