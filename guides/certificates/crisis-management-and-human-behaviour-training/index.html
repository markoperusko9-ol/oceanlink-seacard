Crisis Management and Human Behaviour Training: A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

Crisis Management and Human Behaviour training is one of the STCW passenger ship courses, required under Regulation V/2 and Section A-V/2 (Table A-V/2) of the STCW Code. On passenger ships engaged on international voyages, it is required for masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and anyone the muster list makes responsible for passenger safety in an emergency. Applicability on domestic voyages is set by the Administration. Refresher training or evidence of maintained competence is needed at intervals not exceeding five years, and your administration sets the certificate format, approved courses and revalidation process.

What the training is and why it exists

Passenger ships are a different problem from cargo ships when things go wrong. On a bulk carrier you are managing 20-odd trained crew. On a ferry or cruise ship you may be managing hundreds or thousands of people who have never done a drill, do not speak your language and are frightened.

Crisis Management and Human Behaviour training prepares senior officers and key personnel to lead in those conditions. It covers how people actually behave under stress, how to organise and control an emergency response, and how to keep communications working when everything is noisy and confused.

It is one of a family of passenger ship courses under STCW Chapter V. The others include:

  • Crowd management training, for crew designated to assist passengers in an emergency.
  • Safety training for crew providing direct service to passengers in passenger spaces.
  • On ro-ro passenger ships, passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training.

Crisis management is the senior-level one. It sits above crowd management and assumes you will be making decisions, not just following them.

Where the requirement comes from

The legal basis is the STCW Convention 1978, as amended:

  • Regulation V/2 sets the mandatory minimum training requirements for masters, officers, ratings and other personnel on passenger ships.
  • STCW Code Section A-V/2 gives the detail.
  • Table A-V/2 sets out the minimum standard of competence in crisis management and human behaviour.

The requirement is written for passenger ships on international voyages. For ships on domestic voyages, the flag administration decides whether and how it applies. Some apply the same standard, others have their own national rules.

The training links directly to two other instruments:

  • SOLAS Chapter III, which requires the muster list and emergency instructions.
  • The ISM Code, which requires the company to have procedures for emergency preparedness.

The STCW course gives you the competence. The SOLAS muster list and the company SMS tell you exactly what your role is on that particular ship.

Who needs it

Under Regulation V/2, crisis management and human behaviour training applies on passenger ships engaged on international voyages to:

  • Masters
  • Chief engineer officers
  • Chief mates
  • Second engineer officers
  • Any person designated on the muster list as having responsibility for the safety of passengers in emergency situations

For domestic voyages, the Administration sets applicability.

The last category on the list is the one that catches people out. On a cruise ship or large ferry, the muster list may give passenger safety responsibility to roles that are not senior deck or engine officers. Examples might include certain hotel department managers, staff officers or safety officers, depending on how the company has organised its emergency structure.

If your muster list role makes you responsible for passenger safety, you need the course, whatever your rank.

The practical rule: check the muster list and the company's training matrix for the ship you are joining. Do not assume that because you never needed it on tankers, you will not need it on a Ro-Pax.

The course is generally not required for officers and ratings serving only on cargo ships. Junior officers moving to passenger ships may still be required to hold it by company policy, especially if they are on a promotion track.

What the course covers

Table A-V/2 is built around five areas of competence. Approved courses vary in delivery, but all must address these:

Competence What it means in practice
Organise shipboard emergency procedures Knowing the ship's layout, safety equipment, emergency plans and muster arrangements; preparing contingency plans and drills
Optimise the use of resources Using the people and equipment you have, and improvising when some are lost
Control response to emergencies Initial assessment, decision-making, leadership and organising the response
Control passengers and other personnel during emergencies Recognising and managing panic, stress and abnormal behaviour; directing people effectively
Establish and maintain effective communications Briefing passengers and crew, keeping information accurate and timely, managing communication with shore and other parties

The course content typically includes:

  • Human reactions to stress and danger, including panic, freezing and denial.
  • How stress, fatigue and workload affect the crew's own decision-making.
  • Leadership styles in emergencies and when to switch between them.
  • Case studies of real passenger ship casualties.
  • Table-top or simulator exercises where you run an incident with incomplete information.

Course length and assessment method depend on the approving administration and the institute. So does whether the course is combined with other passenger ship modules. Some administrations accept combined passenger ship packages; others want each element certified separately. Check before you book.

Validity and revalidation

Regulation V/2 requires seafarers holding this training to do one of two things at intervals not exceeding five years:

  • Undertake appropriate refresher training, or
  • Provide evidence of having achieved the required standard of competence within the previous five years.

How that works in practice depends on your administration:

  • Format of evidence. Some issue a separate certificate of proficiency. Others record it as part of another document, or accept the training institute's certificate.
  • What counts as maintaining competence. Some accept recent passenger ship sea service in a relevant role. Others insist on a formal refresher course. Do not assume sea service alone is enough.
  • Expiry dates. Some certificates show an explicit expiry date. Others show only the issue date, and the five-year period is calculated from it.

Indian seafarers should check current DG Shipping requirements, and Filipino seafarers current MARINA requirements, for approved institutes and revalidation rules. Also check what the flag state of the ship requires, as that is what the inspector will look at.

Where you serve on a ship under a different flag, you may also need the flag state endorsement covering your CoC. The company's crewing department should confirm whether your passenger ship training is accepted as issued.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Common problems when a document check fails at the crewing office, the gangway or a port state control inspection:

  1. Missing altogether. An officer transfers from cargo ships to a Ro-Pax or cruise ship as chief mate or second engineer and only has crowd management, or nothing.
  2. Expired, or past five years with no evidence of refresher training. This is common after a long spell on cargo ships with the certificate sitting in the folder.
  3. Institute or course not approved. The course was done at a centre not approved by the issuing administration, or the certificate does not show approval details.
  4. Flag not satisfied. The ship's flag state or the company does not accept the format, or wants a specific type of certificate of proficiency.
  5. Muster list role mismatch. You are designated with passenger safety duties on the new ship but were not told, and no one checked your training against the muster list.
  6. Document discrepancies. Name spelling, date of birth or passport number differs from your other documents, or there is no English translation where one is needed.
  7. Combined course not recognised. A combined package certificate does not clearly show that crisis management and human behaviour was covered to the required standard.

Any of these can mean a delayed sign-on, a replacement being flown out, or a deficiency on a port state control report.

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask

If you are sitting for chief mate, master or second engineer with passenger ship service, expect questions like these.

Q: Which STCW regulation requires this training, and who must hold it? Regulation V/2, with competence detailed in Section A-V/2 and Table A-V/2. On passenger ships engaged on international voyages, it applies to:

  • Masters
  • Chief engineers
  • Chief mates
  • Second engineers
  • Anyone designated on the muster list with responsibility for passenger safety in emergencies

Q: Fire is reported on a passenger deck at night and passengers are already in the corridors. What are your priorities as master? A good answer covers:

  • Raise the alarm and muster the emergency teams.
  • Get an initial assessment, then fight the fire and contain it with boundary cooling and closing fire doors.
  • Make clear, calm and frequent announcements to passengers.
  • Deploy crowd control crew to guide passengers to assembly stations away from the affected zone.
  • Keep the bridge team focused on navigation.
  • Start communications with shore and the company.
  • Think ahead to escalation and abandonment.

Q: How do you recognise panic developing, and what do you do about it? Watch for people running, pushing, ignoring instructions or clustering at exits. Respond with:

  • Visible, uniformed crew giving short, direct instructions.
  • Information delivered regularly, even when there is nothing new.
  • Removing people from the source of fear.
  • Giving people something to do.

Q: How does stress affect your own crew? It causes tunnel vision, poor communication, fixation on one task and fatigue. The leader's job is to spot it, reassign tasks and keep briefings short and structured.

Tips for keeping it in date

  • Track the five-year date alongside your CoC and other STCW certificates in one list or app, with a reminder at least six months out.
  • Ask the crewing office early what the flag and company require for a passenger ship contract. Do this before you sign, not at the airport.
  • Use only administration-approved institutes. Keep the approval reference if one is shown.
  • Keep evidence of passenger ship sea service and muster list roles: discharge book entries and sea service testimonials. Some administrations accept these towards revalidation.
  • Refresh together. If you also hold crowd management or ro-ro passenger safety training, revalidating them together avoids staggered expiry dates.
  • Read the muster list in your first days aboard as part of familiarisation. Raise it with the master or safety officer if your role demands training you do not hold.
Key points
  • Crisis Management and Human Behaviour training is required by STCW Regulation V/2, with competence set out in Table A-V/2.
  • It applies on passenger ships on international voyages to masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and anyone with passenger safety responsibility on the muster list.
  • Refresher training or evidence of maintained competence is required at intervals not exceeding five years.
  • Certificate format, approved institutes and what counts for revalidation depend on the administration and flag.
  • Moving from cargo ships to passenger ships without it is a common cause of delayed sign-on.
  • Your muster list role, not just your rank, decides whether you need it.

Frequently asked questions

Is Crisis Management and Human Behaviour training the same as crowd management?

No. Crowd management is for crew designated to assist passengers in emergencies, such as guiding them to assembly stations. Crisis management and human behaviour is the senior-level training for those who lead and control the overall emergency response. Senior officers on passenger ships commonly hold both, as most are also designated on the muster list to assist passengers; check the muster list and your administration's requirements.

Do I need this course to work on a cargo ship?

Generally no. The requirement under STCW Regulation V/2 applies to passenger ships, including ro-ro passenger ships. Some companies with mixed fleets may still require it for officers on promotion tracks, so check your company's training matrix.

How long is the certificate valid?

STCW requires refresher training, or evidence of having achieved the required standard, at intervals not exceeding five years. Whether your certificate shows an expiry date, and whether sea service can count towards revalidation, depends on the issuing administration.

I am a hotel department manager on a cruise ship. Do I need it?

You need it if the muster list designates you as having responsibility for the safety of passengers in emergency situations. Rank or department does not decide it; your emergency role does. Ask the safety officer or check the muster list and company training matrix.

Will a certificate from any training centre be accepted?

Not necessarily. The course must be approved by the administration that issues your certificate, and the ship's flag state must accept it. Indian seafarers should use DG Shipping-approved institutes and Filipino seafarers MARINA-approved ones, then confirm flag acceptance with the company.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the requirement for Crisis Management and Human Behaviour training?

Regulation V/2 covers training for personnel on passenger ships. VI/1 is basic safety training, V/1-1 is oil and chemical tankers, and II/2 is master and chief mate CoC. Ref: STCW 1978 as amended, Regulation V/2.

Which of the following is NOT automatically required to hold this training on a passenger ship?

Regulation V/2 names masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and persons designated with passenger safety responsibility in emergencies. A third officer is not automatically included unless the muster list gives that role. Ref: STCW Regulation V/2.

What does STCW require to keep this training valid?

Regulation V/2 requires refresher training or evidence of achieving the required standard within the previous five years. The detailed process is set by the administration. Ref: STCW Regulation V/2.

Where is the minimum standard of competence for this training specified?

Table A-V/2 sets the competences. SOLAS III/37 covers the muster list, ISM section 8 covers emergency preparedness, and MLC A1.3 covers training and qualifications generally. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-V/2.

A chief mate moves from a bulk carrier to a Ro-Pax. Which statement is correct?

Ro-ro passenger ships are passenger ships, and Regulation V/2 requires chief mates on passenger ships on international voyages to hold this training; cargo ship service gives no exemption. Ref: STCW Regulation V/2.
