US C1/D Crew Visa: A Seafarer's Guide to Applying, Validity and Avoiding Joining Problems

Quick answer

The C1/D is a combined US transit (C-1) and crewmember (D) visa issued by US embassies and consulates. Most non-US seafarers need it to transit the US to join a ship and to serve on board while the vessel is in US waters (some nationalities and special cases are treated differently). It comes from US immigration law, not STCW or MLC, and its validity depends on your nationality, so check the expiry date long before you are assigned to a ship trading to the US.

What the C1/D actually is

The C1/D is two US non-immigrant visa classes printed as one visa foil in your passport:

  • C-1 (transit): lets you pass through the United States in immediate and continuous transit. Examples are flying into a US airport to join a ship in a US port, or connecting through the US to join a vessel elsewhere.
  • D (crewmember): covers you while you serve on board a vessel (or aircraft) in a capacity needed for its normal operation. It also lets you depart on that vessel or another one.

You are not 'employed in the US'. You are a crewmember on a foreign vessel that happens to be in US waters. That is why the visa is tied to your function on board, not to any American employer.

Two landing-permit categories that CBP may give you on arrival (these are not separate visas):

  • D-1: a crewmember who leaves the US on the same vessel they arrived on.
  • D-2: a crewmember who leaves on a different vessel, or by other means, for example a planned crew change.

The visa only lets you arrive at a US port of entry and ask for admission. US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) decides on arrival whether you can go ashore and for how long. The visa does not guarantee shore leave.

Which law requires it, and which doesn't

Candidates often get this wrong in oral exams and at the manning office.

  • It is not an STCW certificate. No STCW regulation or Code section requires a C1/D. It is not a certificate of competency or proficiency, and the flag state does not issue it.
  • It is not an MLC requirement. MLC 2006 covers seafarers' employment and living conditions. It does not oblige the US, or any port state, to issue visas.
  • It comes from US immigration law. The transit and crewmember classes are defined in the US Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The US Department of State issues the visas. CBP handles landing permits and shore leave for crew under the INA provisions on alien crewmen.

In practice, your company or charterer requires it because a crew member without a valid visa can be refused landing, kept on board, or removed. The operator can also face penalties.

Who needs it: in general, every non-US national serving on a foreign vessel that calls at US ports or transits the US to join. This includes Indian and Filipino officers, ratings and cadets.

Some cases are handled differently. Check them with the consulate rather than assume:

  • certain nationalities that are generally visa-exempt (e.g. Canadian citizens)
  • crew on fishing vessels based in the US
  • work connected with the US Outer Continental Shelf
  • crew on private yachts

You cannot use ESTA or the Visa Waiver Program to work as crew. A B1/B2 visitor visa is not a substitute for C1/D for normal ship crew either.

The application process, step by step

The US embassy or consulate in your country sets the process. Details change, so always follow the current instructions on the official embassy visa website. The usual sequence is:

  1. Get an employment letter. Your manning agent or shipowner provides a letter confirming your rank, the company and, where known, the vessel. Many agents arrange C1/D applications for their crew and know the local requirements.
  2. Complete the DS-160. This is the online non-immigrant visa application. Select the crewmember/transit category. Answer every question truthfully and keep the confirmation page.
  3. Pay the application fee. This is the machine-readable visa (MRV) fee. Some nationalities also pay a reciprocity fee when the visa is issued. The Department of State publishes the fees and they vary, so check the current amount.
  4. Book the appointment. This usually means a biometrics appointment and a consular interview. Interview waivers for some renewals are sometimes available, but eligibility changes, so do not count on one.
  5. Attend the interview. Bring:
    • your passport
    • the DS-160 confirmation and appointment confirmation
    • your photo, if required
    • the employment letter
    • your seaman's book/CDC
    • your CoC or other certificates
    • any previous passports holding US visas
  6. Wait for the result. If approved, the visa is printed in your passport. If you are refused under section 221(g) of the INA, your case needs further 'administrative processing' or more documents. This can take time, so do not apply just days before joining.

Plan for the whole process to take weeks, not days, especially in peak seasons for crew applications.

Questions the consular officer may ask

Interviews are short. The officer wants to confirm you are a genuine seafarer who will work on board and leave, not someone using a crew visa to enter the US and stay. Typical questions include:

  • What is your rank and how long have you been sailing? Answer clearly, in line with your CDC.
  • Which company and vessel will you join? Know the name, the ship type and, if known, the trading area.
  • What are your duties on board? Give a short, practical description, for example 'I keep the 4-8 watch' or 'I'm a motorman, I assist with machinery maintenance'.
  • Have you been to the US before? Did you go ashore?
  • Have you ever been refused a visa or overstayed anywhere? Answer honestly. Answers that do not match your DS-160 are a common cause of trouble.

Tips:

  • Cadets and first-trippers should be ready to explain their training and sponsorship, and to show their documents.
  • Do not memorise scripted answers. Officers can tell, and it raises doubts.
  • Never present altered or fake documents. Misrepresentation can make you permanently ineligible for US visas, with waivers possible only in limited cases. That can end a career on many trades.

Validity, permitted stay and 'revalidation'

Crew often confuse three different time periods:

Item What it means Who decides
Visa validity Period during which you can use the visa to seek entry Department of State. Depends on your nationality under the US reciprocity schedule
Number of entries Single or multiple entries during that period Department of State, by reciprocity
Permitted stay / shore leave How long you may be ashore or remain in the US CBP, at the port. Crew landing permits under the INA are limited to a maximum of 29 days

Points to remember:

  • Validity depends on nationality. Check the reciprocity schedule for your country and the dates printed on your own visa. Do not rely on what a colleague of another nationality has.
  • There is no 'revalidation' as with STCW certificates. When the visa expires you apply for a new one. There is no refresher course.
  • A new passport does not cancel a valid visa. A valid visa in an expired passport normally remains usable if it is undamaged and your personal details are unchanged. Travel with both passports.
  • The visa must be valid on the day you seek entry. That means the date you land at a US airport to join, or the date the ship arrives at a US port. It is not enough for it to be valid when you sign on elsewhere.
  • CBP can refuse shore leave for operational or security reasons, even with a valid visa. In that case crew are 'detained on board'.

Why crew get stopped at joining, and a worked scenario

Common reasons seafarers are offloaded, refused boarding at the airport, or held on board:

  • Visa expires mid-contract. The ship is fixed for a US port three months into your contract, after your visa runs out.
  • Name mismatch. Your name differs between the passport, visa, CDC and the crew list or flight ticket. Examples are a missing middle name or a changed surname after marriage.
  • Passport validity. Airlines and immigration may require the passport to be valid for a minimum period beyond your stay. The rules vary by nationality.
  • Old passport left at home. Your valid visa is in the expired passport and you travelled with only the new one.
  • Damaged visa foil. It is stained, torn or laminated over.
  • Wrong visa type. You hold B1/B2, or tried to travel on ESTA.
  • Pending administrative processing. You were assigned to a US-bound ship before the visa was issued.
  • Previous overstay or desertion record. Any past breach of crew landing conditions can lead to refusal.

Worked scenario. A 3rd Officer is lined up for a nine-month contract on a product tanker trading in the US Gulf. Joining is by flight to Houston. The visa expires four months into the contract.

  1. Check the joining. The visa is valid on the joining date, so the C-1 transit and joining are fine.
  2. Check the end of the contract. Any US port calls after the expiry date are a problem. The officer may be detained on board or need relief before arrival.
  3. Choose an action. Apply for a new visa before joining, if the consulate's rules and timescales allow. Otherwise the manning agent must plan a shorter contract or a vessel that does not trade to the US.
  4. Inform the company. Tell the manning agent at the planning stage, not after sign-on.

Keeping your C1/D in date

  • Track it like your CoC. Keep the expiry date of your visa in the same list as your CoC, medical, endorsements and STCW courses.
  • Use a working rule. Apply well before expiry if there is any chance of US trading. Many companies set their own minimum remaining validity at joining, so check your company's requirement.
  • Keep your documents consistent. If you change your passport or name, make sure your CDC, crew records and tickets match. Carry the old passport if your visa is in it.
  • Protect the foil. No stamps, stickers or lamination over it.
  • Keep copies. Hold a scan of the visa page and DS-160 confirmation for the manning agent.
  • Be truthful every time. Each DS-160 is compared with your previous applications.
  • Respect landing conditions. Return on board on time and never overstay shore leave. One breach can follow you for the rest of your career.
Key points
  • The C1/D combines a US transit visa (C-1) and a crewmember visa (D) for non-US seafarers.
  • It is required by US immigration law, not by STCW or MLC 2006.
  • Visa validity and entries depend on your nationality's reciprocity schedule; CBP decides shore leave on arrival.
  • Crew landing permits are limited to a maximum of 29 days; a valid visa does not guarantee shore leave.
  • There is no revalidation course: when the visa expires you apply again via DS-160 and interview.
  • ESTA and B1/B2 visas are not substitutes for C1/D for normal ship crew.
  • Most joining problems come from expiry mid-contract, name mismatches or leaving the old passport at home.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join a ship in the US with a B1/B2 visa instead of C1/D?

For normal merchant ship crew, no. The C1/D is the correct class for transiting to join and serving on board, and the Visa Waiver Program (ESTA) cannot be used for crew either. A few special cases, such as some private yacht crew, are handled differently, so confirm with the consulate.

How long is a C1/D visa valid for Indian or Filipino seafarers?

It depends on the US reciprocity schedule for your nationality. The Department of State publishes this schedule and can change it. Check the official schedule and the dates printed on your own visa rather than relying on others' experience.

My passport expired but my C1/D is still valid. Do I need a new visa?

Generally no. A valid, undamaged visa in an expired passport normally remains usable if your personal details have not changed. Carry both the old and new passports when travelling and joining.

Does a valid C1/D guarantee shore leave in US ports?

No. CBP decides on arrival whether each crew member may land and for how long, up to a maximum of 29 days. Crew can be detained on board even with a valid visa.

What happens if I get a 221(g) refusal?

It means your application needs further administrative processing or more documents. Follow the consulate's instructions exactly and tell your manning agent immediately. Processing time is not predictable, so you may need to be replaced for that joining.

Test yourself

Which instrument is the legal basis for the C1/D crew visa?

The C-1 and D classes are defined in US immigration law (INA section 101(a)(15)(C) and (D)), not in IMO or ILO instruments.

A Filipino AB holds a valid ESTA approval. Can he fly to New Orleans to sign on a bulk carrier?

Crew working on board cannot use the Visa Waiver Program; they need a crewmember visa such as the C1/D under the INA.

What is the maximum period of a crewman's landing permit in the US?

Under the INA provisions on alien crewmen (section 252), a conditional landing permit cannot exceed 29 days.

A 2nd Engineer's C1/D is in an expired passport and is otherwise valid and undamaged. What should he do?

Per US Department of State guidance, a valid visa in an expired passport normally remains usable if it is undamaged and the holder's details are unchanged; travel with both passports.

Who decides whether a crew member with a valid C1/D may go ashore at a US port?

The visa only allows you to seek admission; CBP grants or refuses landing under INA section 252 and 8 CFR Part 252.
