Ship Security Officer (SSO) Certificate: What It Is, Who Needs It and How to Keep It Valid

Quick answer

The Ship Security Officer certificate is a certificate of proficiency issued under STCW Regulation VI/5. It is held by the person the company designates as SSO under the ISPS Code, often the Master or Chief Officer. The STCW Convention sets no five-yearly refresher for it, but some administrations put an expiry or extra conditions on it, so check what your issuing authority and the ship's flag require.

What the SSO certificate is and where it comes from

Two instruments work together here.

  • SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and the ISPS Code require every ship they apply to to have a Ship Security Officer. The company designates the SSO by name for each ship. ISPS Code Part A, section 12 sets out what the SSO must do. Section 13 requires the SSO to have the knowledge and training for the job.
  • STCW Regulation VI/5 sets the minimum requirements for the SSO certificate of proficiency (CoP). The competence standard is in STCW Code Section A-VI/5 and its table. Guidance is in Section B-VI/5.

So the ISPS Code says the ship must have an SSO. STCW says what qualification that person must hold.

The usual training reference is IMO Model Course 3.19 (Ship Security Officer). Approved institutes build their courses around it. Course length, assessment method and fees are set by each administration and provider, so check with your own maritime authority. Do not rely on what a colleague paid elsewhere.

Do not confuse the SSO with two other people:

  • The Company Security Officer (CSO) is ashore. The CSO is responsible for the ship security assessment and for getting the Ship Security Plan (SSP) approved.
  • The Port Facility Security Officer (PFSO) is responsible for the terminal.

Who needs it and the entry requirements

Under Regulation VI/5, a candidate must:

  • have approved seagoing service of not less than 12 months, or appropriate seagoing service and knowledge of ship operations; and
  • meet the standard of competence in Section A-VI/5.

Your administration decides how it applies the "or appropriate seagoing service" option. Some cadets and junior officers are refused enrolment until they can show the service.

In practice, the people who need the SSO certificate are:

  • Masters. Many companies designate the Master as SSO, especially on smaller crews.
  • Chief Officers. This is a common SSO designation, depending on company policy.
  • Second Officers on some fleets. This applies where company policy allows a junior officer to be designated.
  • Anyone the company might designate. Many manning agents treat the SSO CoP as a standard requirement for senior deck officers, even when you will not hold the role on your next ship.

Ratings and junior officers with security duties need the lower-level CoPs instead:

  • Seafarers with Designated Security Duties (STCW VI/6) for crew assigned specific security tasks.
  • Ship Security Awareness (STCW VI/6) for all other crew.

Company policy and the SSP decide who is designated. Your rank does not decide it.

What the course covers

The competences in Section A-VI/5 follow the SSO's duties under ISPS Part A/12. Expect the course to cover:

  • Maintaining and supervising the Ship Security Plan. This includes amendments and proposing changes to the CSO.
  • Security risks and threats. This covers piracy and armed robbery, stowaways, smuggling, terrorism, and cargo and stores tampering.
  • Security inspections of the ship. You learn to identify restricted areas, access points and weaknesses.
  • Security levels 1, 2 and 3. You learn what changes at each level and how to put it in place.
  • Declaration of Security. You learn when one may be requested and how it is agreed with a port facility or another ship.
  • Searches. This covers searching people, baggage, stores and the ship, including non-intrusive methods.
  • Security equipment and systems. This includes testing, calibration and maintenance, and the Ship Security Alert System (SSAS).
  • Drills and exercises, training of crew, and record keeping.
  • Reporting. This covers security incidents and non-conformities found in internal audits, reviews and inspections, which go to the CSO.
  • Coordination with the CSO and PFSO. This includes coordinating the security side of cargo and stores handling.

Validity and revalidation

There is a common misunderstanding about validity.

  • STCW does not set a revalidation cycle for the SSO CoP. Some basic training elements (PST and FPFF) need evidence of maintained competence every five years. The SSO certificate has no equivalent requirement in the Convention.
  • Administrations can and do add conditions. Some issue SSO certificates without an expiry date. Others print an expiry or link the certificate to an STCW document that does expire. Your issuing authority's rules govern your certificate.
  • Old certificates may need checking. The SSO certificate of proficiency was introduced into STCW (Regulation VI/5) by the 2006 amendments, in force from 1 January 2008, with transitional provisions for existing SSOs until 1 July 2009. If your SSO training pre-dates these arrangements, a flag or company may ask whether it was recognised or reissued as an STCW VI/5 CoP. Check with your administration.
  • The flag may have its own expectations. Some flags or charterers expect particular documentation. Check the flag's circulars or ask the company before joining. Do not assume.

Your certificate of proficiency is a different thing from your designation as SSO. The designation comes from the company, in the SSP or a company letter, for a specific ship. It ends when you sign off.

Why crew get stopped at joining

These are the problems that most often hold up a sign-on, document check or port State control inspection:

  1. Unapproved course or provider. The certificate comes from an institute not approved by your national administration. Examples are DG Shipping in India and MARINA in the Philippines. Agents and auditors now verify certificates online. If the administration's database cannot confirm it, the company or auditor may refuse to accept it until it is verified.
  2. Name or identity mismatch. A spelling difference, a missing middle name, or a different date of birth from the passport or CDC triggers a hold. Get corrections made before you travel.
  3. Pre-STCW VI/5 certificate never recognised. The certificate was issued before the STCW VI/5 requirements took effect (1 January 2008, transitional to 1 July 2009) and does not appear in the administration's system as an STCW VI/5 CoP.
  4. Expiry printed by the issuing authority. The Convention may set no expiry, but the date printed on your document is what counts.
  5. Designation paperwork missing. You hold the CoP, but nothing on board names you as SSO after the crew change. The previous SSO's name is still in the SSP or on the bridge.
  6. Wrong certificate presented. A DSD or awareness certificate is shown where the company needed an SSO CoP. Office staff and officers can both make this mistake.
  7. Copies only. Many flags and auditors want originals, or at least verified copies, on board.

Worked scenario: taking over as SSO at a crew change

You join as Chief Officer and the company letter designates you as SSO. You have one day of handover before the outgoing Chief Officer leaves.

Before the outgoing officer leaves:

  1. Confirm your designation in writing. Make sure the SSP record or company notification is updated, and inform the CSO.
  2. Locate the SSP and check the copy is the current approved version. Note who has access. The SSP is confidential and certain sections are not open to inspection without the flag's consent.
  3. Review the records. The ISPS Code requires these records to be kept, and the administration specifies how long:
    • training, drills and exercises
    • security threats and incidents
    • breaches of security
    • changes in security level
    • security communications
    • internal audits and reviews
    • equipment maintenance and calibration
    • DoS records
  4. Learn the SSAS. Find out where the activation points are and how the test is arranged with the CSO and flag. A wrongly managed test can generate a real alert ashore.
  5. Walk the ship. Check restricted areas, locks, lighting, gangway control and the visitor log.
  6. Check the drill schedule. ISPS Part B guidance (B/13.6) recommends:
    • security drills at least every three months;
    • an extra drill within a week when more than a quarter of the crew has changed and the new crew have not drilled on that ship in the past three months.

Flags, companies and some port States treat this guidance as a firm requirement. If a large crew change has just happened, plan the drill now.

At sea: brief the crew on the current security level and on duties at level 2 and level 3. If the passage passes through a high-risk area, cross-check the SSP against the current industry guidance.

Oral exam: questions examiners and auditors actually ask

Q: Who carries out the Ship Security Assessment? The CSO is responsible for ensuring it is carried out. The SSO contributes but does not own it.

Q: Can a PSC officer read the whole SSP? No. Certain confidential provisions are not subject to inspection unless the flag State agrees. The officer can verify that the plan exists and is being implemented.

Q: Where can the SSAS be activated? From the navigation bridge and from at least one other location. It sends a covert alert ashore. It does not sound an alarm on board or alert other ships.

Q: What do you do if the port is at a higher security level than the ship? The ship must come up to at least the port's level before interfacing with the facility. Inform the CSO and record the change.

Q: What is a Declaration of Security? An agreement on who does which security measures during a ship/port or ship/ship interface. The ship or the facility can request one.

Q: What are your duties as SSO? They follow ISPS A/12.2:

  • inspect the ship;
  • maintain and supervise the SSP;
  • coordinate security of cargo and stores handling;
  • propose plan amendments;
  • report deficiencies and incidents to the CSO;
  • raise crew awareness and make sure the crew are trained;
  • coordinate with the CSO and PFSO;
  • keep security equipment operated, tested, calibrated and maintained.

Keeping your SSO certificate in date and usable

  • Verify it online yourself before applying for a contract. If it is not found, contact the institute and administration now, not at the airport.
  • Read the face of the certificate. Check for an expiry date, its STCW reference (VI/5), and that your name and date of birth match your passport and CDC exactly.
  • Keep it with your other STCW documents. Carry a scanned copy, and the original if the company requires it.
  • Check the flag before a new company or flag. Ask the manning office whether the flag or charterer wants anything beyond the CoP.
  • Get an updated designation each time you join. The CoP alone does not make you the SSO of that ship.
  • Keep up to date. Read company security circulars and current industry guidance for high-risk areas. Examiners and auditors expect you to know current practice, not just the course material.
Key points
  • The SSO certificate is a certificate of proficiency under STCW Regulation VI/5, with competences in Section A-VI/5.
  • The ISPS Code (Part A, sections 12 and 13) requires a designated SSO on each ship and sets the SSO's duties and training.
  • Entry requires at least 12 months' approved seagoing service, or appropriate seagoing service and knowledge of ship operations.
  • STCW sets no five-yearly refresher for the SSO CoP, but your issuing administration's printed conditions and expiry apply.
  • Holding the CoP is not the same as being designated SSO; the company designates you for each ship.
  • Most joining problems come from unverifiable certificates, name mismatches, old documents issued before the STCW VI/5 requirements took effect, and missing designation paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

Does the SSO certificate expire?

The STCW Convention does not set a revalidation cycle for the SSO certificate of proficiency, unlike PST and FPFF basic training. However, some administrations print an expiry date or attach conditions. The document issued to you and your administration's rules decide it, so read the certificate and check with your issuing authority.

Can a Second Officer or cadet do the SSO course?

STCW Regulation VI/5 requires at least 12 months of approved seagoing service, or appropriate seagoing service and knowledge of ship operations. Your administration decides how it applies this. Cadets are often refused until they have the service, and holding the certificate does not mean a company will designate you as SSO.

Is the SSO the same as the Company Security Officer?

No. The SSO is on board and is designated for a specific ship. The CSO is ashore and is responsible for the ship security assessment and for getting the Ship Security Plan approved. They have separate duties and separate training under the ISPS Code.

I did my SSO course before 2008. Is it still valid?

The STCW VI/5 certificate of proficiency was introduced by the 2006 STCW amendments, in force from 1 January 2008, with transitional arrangements for existing SSOs until 1 July 2009, applied by each administration. If your certificate does not appear in your administration's online verification as an STCW VI/5 CoP, contact them before applying for a contract.

Do I need a flag State endorsement for my SSO certificate?

STCW's endorsement scheme under Regulation I/10 is aimed mainly at certificates of competency and certain other qualifications. Some flags or charterers may still ask for specific documentation. Ask the company or check the flag's published requirements before joining, rather than assuming.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the requirements for the Ship Security Officer certificate of proficiency?

STCW Regulation VI/5 covers the SSO certificate of proficiency. VI/6 covers security awareness and designated security duties, VI/1 covers basic training, and I/11 covers revalidation of certificates of competency.

Who is responsible for ensuring the Ship Security Assessment is carried out?

Under ISPS Code Part A, section 11, the CSO ensures the Ship Security Assessment is carried out. The SSO contributes to it but does not own it.

When the SSAS is activated, what happens?

SOLAS Regulation XI-2/6 requires the SSAS alert to be sent ashore without being transmitted to other ships and without raising an alarm on board.

Under STCW, what is the minimum approved seagoing service for an SSO candidate, unless other appropriate sea service and knowledge of ship operations is accepted?

STCW Regulation VI/5 requires not less than 12 months of approved seagoing service, or appropriate seagoing service and knowledge of ship operations.

What does ISPS Part B guidance recommend on security drills?

ISPS Code Part B, paragraph 13.6 recommends drills at least every three months. It also recommends a drill within a week when more than 25% of the crew has changed and the new crew have not drilled on that ship in the last three months.
