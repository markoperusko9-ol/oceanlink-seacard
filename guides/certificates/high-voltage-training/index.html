High Voltage Training for Seafarers

Quick answer

High voltage (HV) training prepares engineer officers and ETOs to operate and work safely on shipboard electrical systems above 1,000 volts. The STCW Code (as amended in 2010) includes HV knowledge in the competence tables for operational- and management-level engineers and ETOs. Whether you need a separate HV course certificate, and how often it must be renewed, is decided by your administration and your company.

What high voltage training is

On ships, "high voltage" means electrical power systems in excess of 1,000 volts. This is the threshold the STCW Code uses. Typical shipboard HV systems run at 3.3 kV, 6.6 kV or 11 kV. You will find them on:

  • diesel-electric ships such as cruise ships, ferries and many offshore units
  • LNG carriers and other gas carriers with large cargo machinery
  • large container ships with heavy reefer and bow thruster loads
  • drillships, DP vessels and ships fitted for shore power connection

HV training covers the theory, the switchgear and above all the safety rules for working on these systems. A mistake at 6.6 kV does not give you a warning tingle. The arc flash alone can kill or blind you without any contact being made. That is why companies treat HV qualification as a hard requirement, and why examiners probe it in orals.

What STCW requires, and where it is left to the administration

The 2010 Manila amendments brought high voltage into the STCW Code competence tables:

  • Section A-III/1 (officer in charge of an engineering watch) includes the basic configuration and operation principles of high-voltage installations. This sits under operating electrical, electronic and control systems.
  • Section A-III/2 (chief engineer and second engineer, 3,000 kW or more) includes knowledge of the design features of high-voltage installations. This sits under the management of electrical and electronic control equipment.
  • Section A-III/6 (electro-technical officer) includes the operation and maintenance of power systems in excess of 1,000 volts.
  • Section B-III/2 (non-mandatory guidance) gives detailed recommendations for training engineering personnel who have management responsibilities for electrical power plant above 1,000 volts.

STCW does not create a stand-alone "HV certificate of proficiency" with its own fixed format the way it does for basic training or advanced fire fighting. Each administration decides how candidates prove the competence:

  • Some administrations require a separately approved HV course as a prerequisite for the management-level engineer or ETO CoC. These courses are often split into operational-level and management-level versions.
  • Others build HV into the CoC syllabus and the written and oral examinations. In that case no separate certificate is issued.
  • Many companies and charterers add their own requirement for an HV course before you join an HV ship. This applies even where the flag does not demand it.

Check the current notices from your own administration, such as DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines, and your company's training matrix. Do not rely on what a colleague needed five years ago.

Who needs it

Rank Usual position
Chief and second engineer Required by most administrations for the management-level CoC. Expected by companies operating HV ships.
ETO Core competence for the ETO CoC. HV training is expected in practice.
Third and fourth engineers Basic HV knowledge is part of the operational-level CoC (A-III/1). Companies or the administration often require a separate operational-level course before joining HV ships.
Engine cadets and electrical ratings Not usually certified for HV work. They must still follow HV safety rules and stay outside restricted areas unless authorised.

On board, holding an HV course certificate does not by itself authorise you to switch or work on HV equipment. Most company safety management systems (SMS) have their own categories of authorised and competent persons, appointed in writing by the chief engineer or master. Only those persons may issue or receive HV permits.

What the course covers

Content and duration vary by approved provider and administration. A course built around the B-III/2 guidance typically covers:

  • HV system design: generation and distribution layouts, insulated versus earthed neutral systems, neutral earthing resistors, transformers and HV motors.
  • Switchgear: vacuum and SF6 circuit breakers, contactors, interlocks, racking breakers in and out, and earthing switches.
  • Protection: overcurrent, earth fault, differential and under-voltage protection, and how relays coordinate.
  • Hazards: electric shock, arc flash, and induced and residual charge on cables and capacitors.
  • Safety documents: permit to work, sanction-to-test, limitation of access and isolation certificates, depending on what the SMS calls them.
  • Safe isolation procedure: isolate, lock and tag, prove dead with an approved HV tester, apply earths, then issue the permit.
  • Testing: insulation resistance testing and polarisation index on HV machines and cables, and safe discharge after tests.
  • Practical sessions: switching on a training panel, using HV testers and earthing equipment, and PPE selection.

Management-level versions add planning, risk assessment, supervising others and managing HV maintenance.

Worked scenario: isolating an HV motor for maintenance

Suppose a 6.6 kV ballast pump motor needs a bearing inspection. A sound sequence under a typical SMS runs as follows:

  1. Plan and assess. The authorised person reviews the single-line diagram, identifies every point of supply including any heater circuits, and completes the risk assessment.
  2. Isolate. Open the breaker, rack it to the isolated position and secure it with a personal lock. Isolate the LV anti-condensation heater supply as well.
  3. Lock and tag. Place locks and danger tags on every isolation point and record them on the isolation certificate.
  4. Prove the tester, prove dead, prove the tester. Check the HV voltage detector on a known live source or proving unit, test the circuit, then check the detector again.
  5. Apply earths. Close the earthing switch or apply approved portable earths at the point of work.
  6. Issue the permit. Hand over the permit to the competent person doing the work and brief them on the limits of the safe zone.
  7. Testing. If an insulation resistance test is needed, earths may have to be removed. That needs a sanction-to-test or equivalent, with the area controlled.
  8. Restore. Cancel the permit, remove earths, remove locks and tags in the documented order, and return to service.

The most common error is forgetting secondary supplies such as heaters, control circuits and back-feeds through transformers. The second most common is skipping the tester proving step.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • "What do we mean by high voltage on board?" Systems in excess of 1,000 V. Give your ship's actual voltage.
  • "Why use a high-resistance earthed neutral?" It limits earth fault current, which reduces damage and arc energy. Depending on the design, the fault may be alarmed and located, or tripped selectively, rather than causing a heavy fault current.
  • "Walk me through isolating an HV breaker." Use the sequence above and name the documents.
  • "Why apply earths after proving dead?" They protect against accidental re-energisation, induced voltage and residual charge.
  • "What is a sanction-to-test?" A document allowing tests that need earths removed, under controlled conditions.
  • "What PPE for HV switching?" Arc-rated clothing and face protection, and insulating gloves rated for the voltage, as specified by your SMS and the risk assessment.

Examiners want to hear your ship's procedure, not a textbook paragraph. Be ready to sketch a simple HV single-line diagram.

Why crew get stopped at joining, and how to stay in date

Common joining problems:

  • No HV certificate when the company matrix demands one for that ship. This often surfaces when an engineer moves from a conventional bulk carrier to a diesel-electric or gas ship.
  • Course not approved by the administration that issued the CoC, or not accepted by the flag.
  • Wrong level: an operational-level course held by someone promoted to second engineer, where the company requires the management-level version.
  • Company refresher interval exceeded. Some companies and charterers set their own recertification period even where the administration sets none.
  • Document errors: name spelling, passport number or INDoS/SIRB details that do not match other certificates.

Tips:

  • Ask for the ship-specific training matrix before signing the contract.
  • Keep scanned copies of every certificate. Check approval status on your administration's online verification where available.
  • Plan the HV course alongside your management-level CoC preparation. Some administrations require it before you sit the exam.
  • On joining an HV ship, insist on ship-specific HV familiarisation before you are named on any permit.
Key points
  • On ships, high voltage means power systems in excess of 1,000 V.
  • STCW Sections A-III/1, A-III/2 and A-III/6 include HV knowledge. Section B-III/2 gives the training guidance.
  • STCW sets no separate HV certificate format or validity. Administrations and companies decide.
  • A course certificate does not by itself authorise HV work on board. Authorisation normally comes from written appointment under the company SMS.
  • Safe isolation means isolate, lock and tag, prove dead with a proved tester, earth, then issue the permit.
  • Check your company's training matrix before joining any diesel-electric, gas or offshore ship.

Frequently asked questions

Is high voltage training mandatory under STCW?

STCW includes HV competence in the tables for operational-level engineers (A-III/1), management-level engineers (A-III/2) and ETOs (A-III/6), so the knowledge is mandatory for those CoCs. Whether you must also hold a separate approved HV course certificate depends on your administration. Many companies require one regardless.

How long is a high voltage certificate valid?

STCW does not set a stand-alone validity period for an HV course. Some administrations, companies or charterers set refresher requirements. Check your administration's current notices and your company's training matrix.

Do junior engineers need high voltage training?

Basic HV knowledge is part of the STCW operational-level engineer competence (Table A-III/1). Whether you also need a separate approved HV course depends on your administration, and many companies require an operational-level HV course before a third or fourth engineer joins an HV ship. Even with the course, you may only work on HV equipment when appointed under the SMS.

What voltage counts as high voltage on a ship?

Anything in excess of 1,000 V, which is the figure STCW uses. Common shipboard HV systems run at 3.3 kV, 6.6 kV or 11 kV.

Can I do the HV course at any training institute?

Normally only a course approved by your administration, or accepted by the flag of your ship, will satisfy CoC and company requirements. Verify the institute's approval before paying. Keep the certificate details consistent with your other documents.

Test yourself

Under the STCW Code, shipboard power systems are treated as high voltage when they are:

STCW refers to power systems in excess of 1,000 volts. 440 V and 690 V are common low-voltage ship systems (STCW Code, Section A-III/6).

Which part of the STCW Code gives guidance on training engineers with management responsibility for electrical plant above 1,000 V?

Section B-III/2 contains this non-mandatory guidance. A-VI/1 covers basic safety training, B-VIII/2 covers watchkeeping and A-II/1 covers the OOW deck CoC.

What validity period does STCW set for a stand-alone high voltage course certificate?

STCW includes HV competence in the CoC tables but does not define a stand-alone HV certificate validity. Administrations and company SMS set any refresher requirements.

After isolating and locking an HV breaker, what is the correct next step before applying earths?

The circuit must be proved dead with a proved HV tester before earths are applied, and the permit comes after earthing (typical SMS/HV safety rules procedure; see also STCW Code, Section B-III/2 general guidance).

A second engineer is refused joining an LNG carrier despite holding an HV certificate. Which reason is most likely?

Level mismatch is a common joining stop, and so is a course from a provider not approved by the administration. IMO does not issue endorsements, and HV competence applies to engineers as well as ETOs (STCW Code, Section A-III/2).
