Ship Security Awareness: The STCW VI/6 Certificate for Seafarers on ISPS Ships

Quick answer

Ship Security Awareness training is required under STCW Regulation VI/6 for every seafarer working in any capacity on a ship that must comply with the ISPS Code. It covers security threats, security levels, reporting and vigilance. STCW sets no refresher interval for it, but your flag state's rules and your company's SMS decide what is accepted at joining.

What Ship Security Awareness is and where it comes from

Ship Security Awareness (SSA) is a certificate of proficiency. It shows you have completed approved basic security training. It was added to STCW by the 2010 Manila Amendments, which entered into force on 1 January 2012. It sits alongside the ISPS Code, which became mandatory through SOLAS Chapter XI-2.

The legal chain works like this:

  • SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and the ISPS Code set the security framework for ships and port facilities. This includes the Ship Security Plan, security levels and the Ship Security Officer.
  • STCW Regulation VI/6 sets the mandatory minimum training and certification requirements for security-related training.
  • STCW Code Section A-VI/6 gives the detail. Table A-VI/6-1 lists the minimum standard of competence for security awareness.

STCW has three tiers of shipboard security training. Do not mix them up:

Tier Who Evidence
Security-related familiarisation Everyone on board except passengers, before taking up duties Ship-specific, given on board and recorded under the company system
Security awareness All seafarers employed or engaged in any capacity Certificate of proficiency (SSA)
Designated security duties Seafarers given security duties under the Ship Security Plan Certificate of proficiency (DSD)

The Ship Security Officer (SSO) qualification sits above these and is a separate certificate of proficiency under STCW Regulation VI/5 (Section A-VI/5).

Who needs it

The requirement covers all seafarers employed or engaged in any capacity on board a seagoing ship that must comply with the ISPS Code. In practice that means virtually every SOLAS cargo ship of 500 GT and above on international voyages, and passenger ships on international voyages.

"Any capacity" means exactly that. It includes:

  • deck and engine officers and ratings
  • cooks, stewards and messmen
  • cadets and trainees
  • ETOs, fitters and riding crew employed as part of the ship's complement

Passengers are not included.

If you hold a DSD or SSO certificate, many administrations and companies treat it as covering the awareness competences. Not all paperwork systems work that way, though. Before you join, check what your flag state and your manning agent actually accept. Do not assume.

Ships outside the ISPS Code are a different case. These include many domestic-trade vessels, some offshore units and some smaller craft. The STCW VI/6 requirement is tied to ISPS ships, but the flag state, the coastal state or the company may still require the certificate. Offshore and charterer requirements often add their own layer.

What the course covers

The course content follows Table A-VI/6-1. The competences are:

  1. Contributing to the enhancement of maritime security through heightened awareness.
  2. Recognising security threats.
  3. Understanding the need for, and methods of, maintaining security awareness and vigilance.

In practical terms, a good course covers:

  • Basic terms and definitions. This includes piracy and armed robbery against ships.
  • The international framework. What governments, companies and individual seafarers are each responsible for, including the roles of the SSO, CSO and PFSO.
  • Security levels 1, 2 and 3. What changes on board and in port at each level.
  • Reporting procedures. Who you tell, and how quickly.
  • Security-related contingency plans. Where you fit in them.
  • Recognising weapons, dangerous substances and devices. Also an awareness of the damage they can cause.
  • Handling security-related information. Knowing what is sensitive and how security-related communications are handled.
  • Techniques used to get around security measures. For example, stowaways hiding in stores deliveries, tailgating at the gangway, and false identities.
  • Drills and exercises. Why they matter and what is expected of you in them.

Assessment is based on evidence from the approved course. Many providers use a written or oral test at the end, but the format depends on the administration and provider. Course duration, delivery format (classroom or approved online) and fees are set by the administration and the training provider. They are not fixed by STCW, so check locally.

The SSA certificate does not replace ship-specific familiarisation. When you join, you still need to be shown the security plan arrangements that apply to you. These include restricted areas, access control points, how to raise the alarm, and your duties at each security level.

Validity and revalidation

STCW does not set a refresher or revalidation interval for security awareness. This is different from the basic safety training elements under Section A-VI/1, such as PST and FPFF, which require evidence of maintained competence every five years.

Many SSA certificates are therefore issued without an expiry date. Even so, confirm three things:

  • Flag state rules. Some administrations add conditions or require their own approved course.
  • Company SMS. Some companies set their own refresher policy, especially after a change of trading area or a high-risk transit.
  • Charterer or terminal requirements. These apply particularly in offshore and tanker trades.

If you started sea service before 2012, you may remember transitional arrangements. These let existing seafarers qualify through recent approved sea service or other routes. Those arrangements ended on 1 January 2014. Anyone without a valid certificate today needs to complete an approved course.

Why crew get stopped at joining

These are the common problems seen at manning agencies, at the gangway and during Port State Control checks of crew certification:

  • No certificate at all. The seafarer assumed the ISPS familiarisation done on a previous ship, or a company e-learning module, was enough. It is not.
  • Provider or course not approved. The certificate comes from a training centre the issuing administration has not approved, or the flag state does not recognise it.
  • Holding only DSD without checking. Usually fine, but some company checklists insist on an SSA line item. Clarify this before travelling, not at the airport.
  • Name or document mismatch. The spelling, date of birth or ID number differs from the passport or CDC. Officials treat this as a document problem, not a minor typo.
  • Doubtful or fake certificates. Cheap "no attendance" certificates are a real problem. Many administrations publish online verification. If your certificate cannot be verified, expect to be refused.
  • Copies only. Many companies and flag states want the original or a verifiable certificate on board.
  • Assigned security duties without DSD. Access control at the gangway and searching stores or baggage commonly count when the Ship Security Plan assigns them as security duties. If the plan gives you these duties, awareness alone is not enough.

Port State Control officers can check that seafarers hold the certificates STCW requires. A missing certificate reflects on the ship as well as on you.

Worked scenario: gangway watch on joining day

You join as an OS at an anchorage port. Within hours you are put on gangway watch. The ship is at security level 1, and the port has just raised to level 2.

Step by step, this is what a properly trained rating does:

  1. Check what you hold. Security awareness covers general vigilance. Controlling access as a designated duty under the Ship Security Plan is a DSD function. Raise this with the duty officer, politely, if you only hold SSA.
  2. Confirm the security level and what it means. The ship's plan sets the extra measures at level 2. These may include more frequent ID checks, limiting access points or searching more stores.
  3. Control access as instructed. Check ID against the expected visitor list. Log every person on and off. Escort visitors where the plan requires it.
  4. Watch for warning signs. Examples are a launch coming alongside unannounced, a stores delivery with unexplained extra packages, or a "surveyor" with no appointment.
  5. Report, do not confront. Inform the OOW or SSO immediately by the method in the plan. Do not search a suspicious package yourself.
  6. Record. Make the log entry. Accurate records support the SSO and protect you.

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask

At CoC orals, security questions are usually short and practical. These are typical, with the shape of a good answer:

  • "Which STCW regulation covers security training?" Regulation VI/6, with detail in Section A-VI/6. Awareness competences are in Table A-VI/6-1.
  • "Difference between familiarisation, awareness and DSD?" Familiarisation is ship-specific and given on board before duties. Awareness is a certificate for all seafarers. DSD is a certificate for those with duties under the Ship Security Plan.
  • "Who sets the security level for a ship?" The contracting government: the flag state for the ship, or the port state when the ship is in its port. The ship can never operate at a lower level than the port facility it is interfacing with.
  • "You find a stowaway in a hold. Actions?" Raise the alarm per the plan. Inform the OOW, Master and SSO. Secure the person safely and humanely. Search for others. Follow the company procedure for reporting to authorities.
  • "Does SSA expire?" STCW sets no refresher interval. Then mention the flag state and company caveat. Examiners like candidates who know the difference between the Convention and local rules.

Tips for keeping it in order

  • Check approval before paying. Confirm that your administration approves the training centre for that specific course.
  • Verify your certificate online where your administration offers verification. Save a screenshot of the result with your documents.
  • Check every personal detail against your passport and CDC on the day you receive the certificate.
  • Scan everything. Keep a clear scan in cloud storage, and keep the original with your other certificates of proficiency.
  • Consider DSD from the start if you are a rating or junior officer. You are likely to be given gangway or access control duties at some point.
  • Ask your agent early whether the flag of your next ship has extra requirements. Do not find out at the airport.
  • Keep your on-board familiarisation records. They are separate evidence and may be asked for during audits.
Key points
  • Ship Security Awareness is required by STCW Regulation VI/6, with competences in Table A-VI/6-1 of the STCW Code.
  • It applies to all seafarers in any capacity on ships that must comply with the ISPS Code, not only deck crew.
  • It is different from ship-specific security familiarisation and from Designated Security Duties (DSD).
  • STCW sets no refresher interval for SSA, but flag state, company or charterer rules can add requirements.
  • If the Ship Security Plan assigns you designated security duties such as access control, you need DSD, not just awareness.
  • Most joining refusals come from unapproved providers, unverifiable certificates or name mismatches.
  • Always verify your certificate with the issuing administration and keep originals with your documents.

Frequently asked questions

Is Ship Security Awareness mandatory for cooks and stewards?

Yes. STCW Regulation VI/6 covers seafarers employed or engaged in any capacity on board ships that must comply with the ISPS Code. Galley and catering crew are included, as are cadets and trainees.

Does the Ship Security Awareness certificate expire?

STCW does not set a refresher or revalidation period for security awareness. Many certificates are therefore issued without an expiry date. Your flag state, company SMS or charterer can impose their own requirements, so check before joining.

Do I need SSA if I already have DSD or SSO?

Many administrations and companies accept DSD or SSO as covering the awareness competences, because those courses include them. Acceptance is not universal in every document checklist. Confirm with your manning agent and the flag state of your next ship.

Can I do Ship Security Awareness online?

Some administrations approve online or blended delivery and others require classroom attendance. Whether the certificate is valid depends on the course being approved by the administration that issues it. Make sure the flag state of your ship recognises it too.

Is the ISPS familiarisation I received on my last ship enough?

No. Security-related familiarisation is ship-specific and is given on board before you take up duties. It does not replace the security awareness certificate of proficiency, and you need both.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the requirement for security awareness training?

Security training and certification is in Regulation VI/6. VI/1 is basic safety training and VI/3 is advanced fire fighting. (STCW Convention, Regulation VI/6)

Who must hold security awareness training under STCW?

The requirement covers every seafarer working in any capacity on ships that must comply with the ISPS Code. Seafarers given designated security duties under the Ship Security Plan (for example, access control) need DSD as well. (STCW Regulation VI/6; Section A-VI/6)

What does STCW say about refresher training for security awareness?

The five-yearly evidence of maintained competence applies to basic safety elements under Section A-VI/1, not to security awareness. (STCW Code, Sections A-VI/1 and A-VI/6)

A rating holding only SSA is given access control duties under the Ship Security Plan. What does STCW expect?

Seafarers with designated security duties under the Ship Security Plan need DSD training. SSO is only for the officer appointed as SSO. (STCW Code, Section A-VI/6, Table A-VI/6-2)

How does ship-specific security familiarisation relate to the SSA certificate?

STCW requires security-related familiarisation before duties are assigned, in addition to security awareness training for all seafarers. (STCW Code, Section A-VI/6)
