Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations: Requirements, Route and Revalidation

Quick answer

Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations is the STCW certificate of proficiency required by Regulation V/1-1. Masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations on an oil tanker must hold it. You need the basic oil and chemical tanker certificate, documented oil tanker sea time or supernumerary training, and an approved advanced course. Officers must keep it valid at least every five years by tanker service or an approved course.

What the certificate is and where it comes from

Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations is a certificate of proficiency (CoP) under the STCW Convention. In India it is still commonly called the oil tanker DCE (Dangerous Cargo Endorsement). On a flag endorsement you may see it described as a tanker endorsement. Whatever the label, the underlying requirement is the same.

  • STCW Regulation V/1-1 sets out who must hold it and how a seafarer qualifies.
  • STCW Code Section A-V/1-1 and its Table A-V/1-1-2 set out the standard of competence the course and assessment must meet.
  • Section B-V/1 gives non-mandatory guidance, including on supernumerary onboard training.

It sits one level above Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations. It is separate from the advanced chemical tanker certificate and the liquefied gas tanker certificates. Each advanced certificate covers only its own ship type.

"Oil tanker" here follows the STCW definition in Regulation I/1: a ship constructed and used for the carriage of petroleum and petroleum products in bulk. That covers crude carriers and product tankers alike. Many product/chemical tankers are certified for both oil and chemicals. Officers on those ships often need both advanced certificates.

Who needs it on board

Regulation V/1-1 requires the advanced oil tanker certificate for:

  • Master
  • Chief engineer officer
  • Chief mate
  • Second engineer officer
  • Any other person with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit, handling of cargo, tank cleaning or other cargo-related operations.

The last category is where people get caught out. Who counts as having "immediate responsibility" depends on how the ship is manned and how the company SMS assigns duties. On some ships the pumpman or a junior officer runs part of the cargo operation and falls into this group. On other ships the same rank does not. The company and flag state decide, not the rank title alone.

Other officers and ratings with specific cargo duties need at least the basic oil and chemical tanker certificate. Charterers and oil majors often go further. Under vetting systems such as officer matrix checks, they may expect the 2nd and 3rd officers to hold the advanced certificate, or expect tanker experience in rank. That is a commercial requirement, not an STCW one, but it can still decide whether you join.

How to qualify: the route step by step

Under Regulation V/1-1 you qualify in three steps:

  1. Hold the basic certificate. You must meet the requirements for Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations.
  2. Gain qualifying experience on oil tankers while holding basic. Either route works:
    • at least three months of approved seagoing service on oil tankers, or
    • at least one month of approved onboard training on oil tankers in a supernumerary capacity. This must include at least three loading and three unloading operations and be documented in an approved training record book.
  3. Complete an approved advanced course for oil tankers that meets Section A-V/1-1 and Table A-V/1-1-2.

The administration then issues the CoP.

The order and timing matter. Sea time served before you held basic does not satisfy step 2. Chemical tanker or gas carrier time is not oil tanker time either.

Administrations differ on:

  • whether you can attend the advanced course before your sea time is complete;
  • the documents they accept as proof of service, such as discharge book entries, sea service testimonials or company letters;
  • course length and approved institutes.

Check your own administration's current circular before you book anything.

What the course covers

The standard of competence in Table A-V/1-1-2 is built around these areas:

  • Safely perform and monitor all cargo operations. This covers tanker design, piping, pumping and venting systems, and instrumentation. It also covers loading and discharge planning and cargo calculations. Other topics are inerting, crude oil washing, tank cleaning, purging and gas freeing, ballasting, and ship-to-ship transfer. The course also includes stability, trim and hull stress during cargo work.
  • Physical and chemical properties of oil cargoes. This covers vapour pressure, flash point, flammable limits and static electricity. It also covers the behaviour of cargoes containing hydrogen sulphide or benzene.
  • Take precautions to prevent hazards. This covers explosion and fire hazards, toxicity, and gas measurement and instruments. It also covers hot work, and enclosed space entry and permits.
  • Occupational health and safety. Safety data sheets, personal protective equipment and working practices are covered here.
  • Respond to emergencies. Topics include the emergency organisation, cargo system failures, pump room incidents, fire and collision response, and first aid for cargo exposure.
  • Prevent pollution. This covers spill prevention and response, oil record keeping, and the shipboard oil pollution emergency plan.
  • Monitor and control compliance with legislation. The relevant parts of MARPOL, SOLAS and other instruments are covered, along with industry guidance on safe tanker operation.

Good centres use a liquid cargo handling simulator for planning, inerting and COW exercises. Treat those sessions as the most valuable part of the course.

Validity and revalidation

This is the area where practice differs most between administrations. Be clear about what STCW itself says.

For masters and officers, Regulation I/11 says that to keep serving on tankers you must establish continued professional competence for tankers at intervals not exceeding five years. Section A-I/11 allows either of these routes:

  • at least three months in total of approved seagoing service on tankers in the preceding five years, performing duties appropriate to the tanker certificate held, or
  • successful completion of a relevant approved training course.

How this shows on paper varies:

  • Some administrations print an expiry date on the tanker CoP.
  • Some align it with the CoC revalidation.
  • Some revalidate it as a separate application.

For ratings or other personnel holding the advanced certificate, some administrations issue it without an expiry. Others apply their own revalidation rule. Check your issuing administration's rules. Do not assume they match a colleague's from another country.

If you serve on a foreign flag, the tanker qualification usually needs to appear on, or be recognised by, the flag state endorsement. When you renew the national CoP, the flag endorsement may also need updating.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Common problems found at document checks by manning agents, superintendents, masters and port state control officers:

  • Basic only. The seafarer is promoted to chief mate or 2nd engineer while holding only the basic certificate.
  • Wrong advanced certificate. The seafarer holds advanced chemical but is joining a crude or product carrier on oil cargo duties, or the other way round.
  • Lapsed tanker competence. An officer has spent more than five years on dry cargo or offshore without the three months' tanker service, and has not done a refresher course.
  • Flag endorsement mismatch. The national CoP is valid, but the flag endorsement does not show the tanker qualification, or shows an earlier expiry.
  • Unverifiable certificate. The administration's online verification does not return the certificate. Name, date of birth or passport details differ between documents.
  • Copies instead of originals, or the training record book is left at home when it is needed as evidence.
  • Vetting matrix shortfall. STCW requirements are met, but the charterer's experience criteria for that rank on that ship are not.

Worked example: a 2nd engineer, ex-bulk carriers, holds basic oil and chemical training. He is offered a product tanker contract. He needs advanced oil, and advanced chemical as well if the ship is also certified and trading as a chemical tanker. Before he can do the advanced course, he needs qualifying service or supernumerary training. He has to plan the route months ahead, not the week before joining.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners and company superintendents commonly ask questions such as these. Practise answering aloud.

  • Who on board must hold advanced oil tanker training, and why does "immediate responsibility" matter? Name the four senior ranks. Explain that anyone else directly running cargo operations is also included.
  • What oxygen limits apply to inert gas? Under the FSS Code, the inert gas system must be able to deliver gas with no more than 5% oxygen by volume. It must also keep cargo tank atmospheres at no more than 8% oxygen by volume, under positive pressure.
  • Why purge before gas freeing? Purging brings the hydrocarbon content down first. Then, when air is introduced, no part of the tank atmosphere passes through the flammable range. Quote the figure your company SMS and industry guidance use.
  • What do you check before tank entry? Permit, isolation, ventilation, and atmosphere testing for oxygen, hydrocarbons and toxic gases with calibrated instruments. Also stand-by person, communications, rescue equipment and readiness to evacuate. Limits come from the SMS and enclosed space entry guidance.
  • How do you keep your tanker competence valid? Three months of tanker service in the preceding five years, or an approved course.

Keeping it in date: practical tips

  • Track two dates: your national CoP or CoC tanker validity and your flag endorsements. Put both in your phone calendar with a reminder six months ahead.
  • Keep proof of tanker service: discharge book entries, sea service testimonials and ship particulars. These matter most if you move between tanker and non-tanker contracts.
  • Going to dry cargo for a few years? Plan a tanker contract or a refresher course before the five-year window closes.
  • Keep the training record book safe if you qualified by the supernumerary route. Some administrations ask for it again.
  • Check online verification of every certificate before travelling. Fix any name or date mismatch with the issuing authority, not at the gangway.
  • Carry originals plus a scanned set in your email or cloud storage.
Key points
  • Advanced oil tanker training is an STCW certificate of proficiency under Regulation V/1-1.
  • Masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and anyone with immediate cargo responsibility on oil tankers need it.
  • Prerequisites: basic oil and chemical tanker training, then three months' oil tanker service or one month of documented supernumerary training.
  • Advanced chemical and gas certificates do not cover oil tankers, and the oil certificate does not cover them.
  • Masters and officers must show continued tanker competence at least every five years by three months' tanker service or an approved course.
  • How expiry and revalidation appear on documents depends on the issuing administration and the flag state.
  • Most joining problems are missing endorsements, wrong certificate type, lapsed competence or unverifiable records.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do the advanced oil tanker course without tanker sea time?

Some administrations let you attend the course first. However, the CoP is only issued once you have three months of approved oil tanker service, or one month of documented supernumerary training, gained while holding basic tanker training. Check your administration's current rules before booking.

Does advanced chemical tanker training cover oil tankers?

No. STCW treats advanced oil and advanced chemical as separate certificates with separate experience requirements. Officers on combined product/chemical tankers often need both.

How do I revalidate my oil tanker certificate?

Masters and officers must show continued tanker competence at intervals not exceeding five years. The routes are at least three months of tanker service in the preceding five years, or an approved course. The paperwork and whether an expiry date is printed depend on the issuing administration.

Does a 3rd officer on an oil tanker need advanced training?

Under STCW, only if they have immediate responsibility for cargo operations. Otherwise basic training covers officers with specific cargo duties. Many charterers' vetting criteria and company policies expect more, so check the company requirement.

Do I need a flag state endorsement for my tanker certificate?

Often yes. When you serve on a foreign flag, many flag states record the tanker qualification on their endorsement or require it to be recognised. Confirm with your manning agent or the flag administration before joining.

Test yourself

Under STCW Regulation V/1-1, who must hold advanced oil tanker training?

The regulation names the four senior ranks plus any person with immediate responsibility for cargo operations. Reference: STCW Regulation V/1-1.

Which experience meets the requirement for advanced oil tanker certification?

The service must be on oil tankers and gained while qualified in basic tanker training. Supernumerary training is the alternative route. Reference: STCW Regulation V/1-1.

How does a master or officer establish continued professional competence for tankers?

Tanker competence must be shown at intervals not exceeding five years. Reference: STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11.

A chief mate holds advanced chemical tanker training only and is offered a crude carrier. What applies?

Advanced oil and advanced chemical are separate certificates, and the chief mate is a named rank. Reference: STCW Regulation V/1-1.

What is the maximum oxygen content to be maintained in inerted cargo tanks?

The inert gas system delivers gas at no more than 5% oxygen, while the cargo tank atmosphere is kept at no more than 8% oxygen by volume. Reference: FSS Code, Chapter 15.
