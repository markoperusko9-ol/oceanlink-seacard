Polar Code Advanced Training: A Guide for Masters and Chief Mates

Quick answer

Polar Code Advanced Training is the STCW Regulation V/4 qualification that masters and chief mates need when their ship operates in polar waters other than ice-free or open water. To get it you must already hold the basic polar certificate, have completed approved advanced training, and have at least two months of qualifying seagoing service in polar waters or equivalent approved service. It must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years if you keep serving on ships operating in polar waters.

What the advanced polar certificate is and where the requirement comes from

Two linked instruments create the requirement:

  • The Polar Code (International Code for Ships Operating in Polar Waters), made mandatory through SOLAS Chapter XIV and the MARPOL annexes. Part I-A, Chapter 12 (Manning and training) states which officers need basic or advanced training. This depends on ship type and ice conditions.
  • STCW Regulation V/4 and Section A-V/4 of the STCW Code. These set the certification rules and the standard of competence for basic training (Table A-V/4-1) and advanced training (Table A-V/4-2). The V/4 amendments came into force on 1 July 2018.

The Polar Code tells the ship who must be trained. STCW tells the administration how to certify them.

"Polar waters" means Arctic waters and the Antarctic area as defined in SOLAS Chapter XIV. The Antarctic area is south of 60°S. The Arctic boundary is a defined line, not a simple latitude, so check it on the chart rather than assuming.

Ice in the Baltic, the Gulf of St Lawrence, the Sea of Okhotsk or northern Japan does not put you in Polar Code waters. Those areas have their own national or regional ice rules.

Who needs advanced and who needs basic

Polar Code Chapter 12 applies to ships certified under SOLAS Chapter XIV. In general terms it requires the following:

Ice conditions Tankers Passenger ships Other ships
Ice-free waters Not required Not required Not required
Open waters Basic for master, chief mate and OOWs Basic for master, chief mate and OOWs Not required
Other waters (more ice) Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOWs Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOWs Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOWs

The Polar Code defines "open water" as a large area of freely navigable water in which sea ice is present in concentrations of less than 1/10 and no ice of land origin is present. It defines "ice-free waters" as waters with no ice present.

Key practical points:

  • Engineers, ETOs and ratings are not covered by STCW V/4. Polar Code Chapter 12 does require every crew member to be made familiar with the procedures and equipment in the PWOM relevant to their duties. This is shipboard familiarisation, not an STCW certificate. Some companies and charterers also send non-deck crew on polar familiarisation or basic courses. That is a company requirement, not an STCW one.
  • The Polar Code lets the administration accept a suitably qualified person, other than the master or OOW, to meet the training requirement, subject to conditions. In practice this is often an ice navigator or ice adviser. Whether your flag allows this, and on what terms, is a flag question.
  • Plan for the worst ice you may meet, not the ice in the brochure. If the voyage plan or Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM) shows a credible chance of leaving open water, the master and chief mate need advanced.
  • Ships outside SOLAS application, such as most fishing vessels, are not covered by Chapter 12. They may still face national rules.

Entry requirements and what the course covers

Under STCW Regulation V/4, a candidate for the advanced certificate must:

  1. Meet the requirements for the basic polar certificate.
  2. Have at least two months of approved seagoing service in the deck department in polar waters. This can be at management level, or while performing watchkeeping duties at operational level. Alternatively, it can be other equivalent seagoing service approved by the administration.
  3. Complete approved advanced training and meet the standard of competence in Section A-V/4.

What counts as "equivalent" service, such as winter Baltic trading or icebreaker escort elsewhere, is decided by your administration, not the training centre. Ask the flag or your certifying authority before assuming.

The advanced competences in Table A-V/4-2 sit at management level. Approved courses typically cover:

  • Voyage planning in polar waters. This includes ice information sources and ice charts, WMO sea-ice nomenclature, routeing and the limits of hydrographic data.
  • Operational risk assessment. This means using the ship's ice class and operational limits, and the POLARIS methodology (IMO guidance MSC.1/Circ.1519) for deciding whether to proceed in given ice.
  • Ship handling in ice. This covers speed, ramming, besetting, working with icebreakers and convoy operations, and towing in ice.
  • The PWOM and the Polar Ship Certificate. This includes their limitations and how they shape decisions on board.
  • Equipment and systems in low temperatures. Topics include high-latitude effects on gyro, GNSS and communications, icing and its effect on stability, and winterisation of deck and life-saving equipment.
  • Crew safety and survival. This covers cold exposure, the extended time to rescue, and emergency response in remote areas.
  • Environmental protection. This means the Polar Code pollution-prevention requirements under MARPOL.

Course length and assessment methods are set by the approved course and the administration. Ask the centre for its approval reference.

Validity and revalidation

STCW Regulation I/11 was amended alongside V/4. Masters and officers who continue serving on ships operating in polar waters must re-establish continued professional competence at intervals not exceeding five years.

Section A-I/11 gives several routes. Your administration decides which it accepts and how to evidence them:

  • approved seagoing service in polar waters, performing duties appropriate to the certificate, of at least two months in total in the preceding five years
  • having performed functions the administration considers equivalent
  • passing an approved test
  • successfully completing an approved training course

Things that vary by administration:

  • whether the polar qualification is issued as a separate certificate of proficiency or as an endorsement
  • whether the expiry date is printed on the document
  • how polar service must be proved, for example discharge book entries, a master's testimonial or company service letters
  • whether a refresher course is mandatory or service alone is enough

When V/4 came in there were transitional arrangements that let some officers be certificated on the strength of earlier polar service. Those arrangements have closed. Anyone without the certificate today goes through the normal route.

Common reasons officers get stopped at joining

  • Basic held instead of advanced. This often happens when an OOW is promoted to chief mate on board, or a relief chief mate is sent at short notice for an ice-season voyage.
  • Expired or unrevalidated certificate. The five years ran out during a long spell on non-polar trades.
  • Course certificate but no STCW document. The training-centre certificate is not always the document the flag or port state control will accept. Some administrations issue the certificate of proficiency separately after the course.
  • Service evidence missing. The two months' polar service needed for issue was never recorded properly, so the certificate was not issued.
  • Flag recognition gap. The certificate was issued by an administration whose certificates the ship's flag has not recognised. The flag may need to issue its own document or endorsement.
  • Company or charterer rules stricter than the Code. Examples include insisting on advanced even for open-water voyages, or requiring documented ice experience. This is legitimate, and the crewing office will enforce it.
  • Name or document mismatch between passport, CoC and polar certificate. This is a general problem, but it shows up at document checks before joining.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Product tanker, Arctic summer voyage. The voyage plan shows open water all the way and the PWOM supports it.

  • Under Chapter 12, master, chief mate and OOWs need basic as a minimum.
  • If ice forecasts change and the ship may meet sea ice concentrations of 1/10 or more, or any ice of land origin, the master and chief mate need advanced. That is not something you can obtain mid-voyage.
  • Most operators therefore crew such voyages with advanced holders from the start.

Scenario 2: Bulk carrier chief mate with Baltic winter experience. The officer has three winters of Baltic ice navigation but no polar service.

  • Baltic service is not polar-waters service.
  • Whether the administration accepts it as "equivalent approved seagoing service" for the advanced certificate is the administration's decision.
  • Write to the flag or certifying authority with service evidence before booking the course.

Scenario 3: Master renewing. The certificate was issued five years ago, and the master has had one polar voyage of about six weeks since.

  • Six weeks does not reach two months.
  • The master will likely need another accepted route, such as an approved course or test, depending on what the administration allows.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • Who needs advanced polar training, and when? The master and chief mate on ships to which Chapter 12 applies, when operating in waters other than ice-free or open water. OOWs need basic.
  • What is open water? Sea ice concentration of less than 1/10, with no ice of land origin present.
  • What documents tell you the ship's polar limitations? The Polar Ship Certificate, which states the category, ice class and operational limits, and the Polar Water Operational Manual.
  • What is POLARIS for? It is an IMO-accepted method for assessing operational capability and limits in given ice conditions, based on ice class and ice regime.
  • How would you prepare for a polar passage? Use the full appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring cycle. Pay particular attention to the following:
    • ice information and chart reliability
    • communications coverage at high latitude
    • compass and positioning limitations
    • icing and stability
    • winterisation
    • extended rescue times
    • environmental restrictions
  • How do you keep the certificate valid? By establishing continued competence at intervals not exceeding five years under Regulation I/11, through service, equivalent functions, a test or a course, as your administration accepts.

Keeping it in date

  • Store the expiry date (or five-year point) alongside your CoC and medical in your document tracker. Set a reminder at least a year ahead.
  • Get polar service recorded as you do it. This means discharge book entries where your administration uses them, and a master's or company letter stating dates, ship and polar area.
  • Keep copies of ice charts or voyage records only if your company allows. The evidence your administration accepts (for example discharge book entries or company service letters) is what counts; ice charts and voyage records are only supporting material.
  • Before a polar contract, confirm three things with crewing: the flag, the trading area, and whether the flag needs its own recognition or endorsement of your certificate.
  • If you are an OOW aiming for chief mate on ice-class ships, start building polar watchkeeping service now. It counts towards the two months.
Key points
  • Advanced polar training comes from STCW Regulation V/4 and Section A-V/4, triggered by Polar Code Part I-A Chapter 12.
  • It is needed by the master and chief mate when operating in polar waters other than ice-free or open water; OOWs need basic.
  • Entry requires the basic polar certificate, approved advanced training and at least two months of qualifying polar (or approved equivalent) deck service.
  • Baltic or St Lawrence ice is not Polar Code waters; whether it counts as equivalent service is the administration's call.
  • Revalidation is at intervals not exceeding five years under Regulation I/11, by service, equivalent functions, a test or a course.
  • Certificate format, evidence of service and flag recognition all vary by administration, so check before joining.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need Polar Code Advanced Training as a second or third officer?

Under the Polar Code, officers in charge of a navigational watch need basic polar training, not advanced. Advanced is required for the master and chief mate when the ship operates outside ice-free or open water. Some companies may still prefer OOWs with advanced, especially those being lined up for promotion.

Can I do the advanced polar course without polar sea time?

Many centres will let you attend the course. However, STCW V/4 requires at least two months of approved polar (or equivalent) deck service before the advanced certificate is issued. Check with your administration whether your service qualifies before you pay for the course.

Does Baltic winter ice experience count for the advanced polar certificate?

Baltic waters are not polar waters under SOLAS Chapter XIV. The administration may accept other ice service as equivalent approved seagoing service, but that is its decision, not the training centre's. Ask your certifying authority in writing.

How long is the Polar Code Advanced certificate valid?

Regulation I/11 requires continued competence for polar service to be established at intervals not exceeding five years. You can do this through qualifying polar service, equivalent functions, an approved test or an approved course, depending on what your administration accepts. Whether an expiry date is printed on the document also depends on the issuing administration.

Do engineers need Polar Code training?

STCW Regulation V/4 covers masters and deck officers only, so there is no STCW polar certificate requirement for engineers. Polar Code Chapter 12 does require every crew member to be made familiar with the procedures and equipment in the PWOM relevant to their duties. This is shipboard familiarisation, not an STCW certificate. Some companies and charterers also require engineers to attend polar familiarisation or basic training as a company rule.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the training requirements for masters and deck officers on ships operating in polar waters?

STCW Regulation V/4, with Section A-V/4 of the Code, covers polar basic and advanced training. V/1-1 covers tankers, VI/1 basic safety and II/2 master and chief mate CoCs.

On a cargo ship (not tanker or passenger ship) operating in polar waters outside open water, who must hold advanced polar training?

Polar Code Part I-A Chapter 12 requires advanced for the master and chief mate in other waters. OOWs need basic, and engineers are outside STCW V/4.

What sea ice concentration defines 'open water' in the Polar Code?

The Polar Code defines open water as sea ice concentration of less than 1/10 with no ice of land origin present. 'No ice present' is the definition of ice-free waters.

What minimum qualifying seagoing service does STCW V/4 require for the advanced polar certificate?

STCW Regulation V/4 requires at least two months of approved deck service in polar waters, at management level or watchkeeping at operational level, or approved equivalent service.

How often must continued competence for polar service be re-established under STCW?

STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 require continued professional competence for polar service at intervals not exceeding five years.
