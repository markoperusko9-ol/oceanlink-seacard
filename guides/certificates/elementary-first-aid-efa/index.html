Elementary First Aid (EFA): What Every Seafarer Needs to Know

Quick answer

Elementary First Aid is one of the four mandatory elements of STCW basic training under Regulation VI/1, with its minimum standard set out in Table A-VI/1-3 of the STCW Code. Every seafarer with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties needs it. STCW does not itself require a five-yearly refresher for EFA, but some administrations and companies have their own rules, so check before you assume your old certificate is fine.

What EFA is and where the requirement comes from

Elementary First Aid (EFA) is the entry-level first aid qualification for seafarers. It is one of the four components of STCW basic training:

  • Personal Survival Techniques (Table A-VI/1-1)
  • Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting (Table A-VI/1-2)
  • Elementary First Aid (Table A-VI/1-3)
  • Personal Safety and Social Responsibilities (Table A-VI/1-4)

The requirement sits in STCW Regulation VI/1, with the detail in Section A-VI/1 of the STCW Code. Table A-VI/1-3 sets out one competence: taking immediate action when you come across an accident or other medical emergency. The knowledge, methods of demonstrating competence and assessment criteria are all listed in that table, and the course you attend must be approved by your administration to meet it. IMO publishes a model course for EFA that many approved training centres base their syllabus on, but the administration's approval is what matters.

The MLC 2006 also comes into it. Regulation 1.3 says seafarers must not work on a ship unless they are trained or certified as competent, or otherwise qualified, for their duties, and that they must have completed training for personal safety on board. In practice, flag and port state inspectors check your STCW basic training certificates as evidence of this.

One point often goes wrong at the start of a career. EFA is not the same as Medical First Aid or Medical Care. Those are separate, higher-level qualifications under Section A-VI/4, covered further down.

Who needs it

Under Regulation VI/1, seafarers employed or engaged on board as part of the ship's complement with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties must complete approved basic training, which includes EFA. On a merchant ship that covers practically everyone on the muster list: officers, ratings, cadets, catering staff and ETOs.

Other people on board, such as some passenger-ship staff without safety duties, need safety familiarisation rather than full basic training. The STCW Code also lets administrations exempt certain ships from parts of the requirement in limited circumstances. Don't assume an exemption applies to you. Manning agents and companies will expect the certificate regardless.

Officer CoC programmes usually build Medical First Aid, and for senior deck officers Medical Care, into the route to the certificate. The exact combination depends on the administration, so check your own CoC requirements.

What the course covers

Table A-VI/1-3 covers three broad areas of knowledge.

  1. Assessing the situation. This means working out what the casualty needs and what threatens your own safety. You don't rush into a space with a collapsed man until you know why he collapsed.
  2. Basic body structure and functions. You need enough anatomy to understand what you are doing and why.
  3. Immediate measures in an emergency, including:
    • positioning the casualty
    • applying resuscitation techniques
    • controlling bleeding
    • basic management of shock
    • dealing with burns and scalds, including those caused by electric current
    • rescuing and transporting a casualty
    • improvising bandages and using the materials in the emergency kit

Assessment is mostly practical. You show you can do CPR on a manikin, apply dressings, put someone in the recovery position and run a casualty assessment. The exact course length and format are set by each administration's approval, so go by what your approved training centre states.

The resuscitation techniques taught are based on the approved syllabus and generally reflect recognised resuscitation guidelines, which are revised from time to time. That is one good reason to refresh your skills even when the certificate doesn't demand it.

Validity and revalidation: where people get confused

Since the 2010 Manila amendments, Section A-VI/1 requires seafarers to show every five years that they have kept up the standard of competence for Personal Survival Techniques and Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting only. That is why refresher courses exist for PST and FPFF.

EFA and PSSR are not included in that five-yearly requirement under STCW. At convention level, an EFA certificate does not expire.

There are three caveats:

  • Administration rules. Some administrations print an expiry or validity period on basic training documents, or package refreshers in a way that covers more than STCW strictly requires. Your national rules apply to certificates they issue. Check the current circulars from your own administration (for example DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines) rather than relying on what a friend did five years ago.
  • Company and charterer requirements. Some companies' SMS, or oil-major and offshore clients, want first aid training more recent than STCW requires. That is a contractual requirement, not a convention one, but it will still stop you joining.
  • Combined certificates. Some administrations issue one basic training certificate covering all four elements. Others issue four separate ones. If your PST or FPFF refresher is overdue, a combined document may be treated as invalid as a whole, even though the EFA part is not time-limited.

Why crew get stopped at joining over EFA

These are the problems that most often hold seafarers up at the agent's office, the airport or the gangway:

  • Training centre not approved. The course was done at an institute not approved by the issuing administration for that course, or the approval had lapsed. The certificate then fails online verification.
  • Certificate not traceable online. Many administrations and manning agents verify certificates through an online system. If the training centre never uploaded your record, or uploaded it with errors, the paper in your hand won't help much. In India, for example, check that the course shows correctly against your INDoS record.
  • Name or date-of-birth mismatch. The spelling differs from your passport or CDC (seaman's book). It sounds minor, but it is a common reason documents are rejected.
  • Confusing EFA with Medical First Aid. An officer turns up with EFA when the position or flag requires Medical First Aid, or the reverse.
  • Overdue PST/FPFF refresher. The seafarer believes "basic training is fine" because EFA doesn't expire, but the refresher-dependent elements have lapsed.
  • Company requirement not met. The SMS or charterer wants first aid training within a set period, and the seafarer's course is older.
  • Originals missing or damaged. Scans are accepted at some stages but not all.

Worked scenario: steam scald in the engine room

A motorman is scalded on the forearm by a steam leak at the boiler. You are the oiler on watch. A typical EFA-level response runs like this:

  1. Make it safe. Don't walk into the leak. Raise the alarm and inform the engineer on watch so the leak can be isolated.
  2. Remove the casualty from danger once you can do so without becoming a second casualty.
  3. Assess the casualty. Check that he is responsive and breathing normally, and look for other injuries such as a fall from the platform.
  4. Cool the burn with cool running water for a sustained period, as taught on your course. Do not use ice.
  5. Remove jewellery, watch and loose clothing near the burn before swelling starts. Leave anything that is stuck to the skin.
  6. Cover the burn loosely with a clean, non-fluffy dressing or cling film from the kit. Don't burst blisters or apply creams.
  7. Watch for shock. Lie him down, keep him warm, reassure him and give nothing by mouth if further treatment may be needed.
  8. Hand over to the officer in charge of medical care, who will decide on radio medical advice. Report through the ship's accident reporting procedure.

The EFA skill being tested here is not complex medicine. It is doing the right things in the right order without making the situation worse.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners at CoC orals, and senior officers during onboard familiarisation, often probe first aid basics. Be ready for questions like these.

  • "You find a man collapsed in the pump room. What do you do?" Do not enter. Raise the alarm and follow the enclosed space rescue procedure with breathing apparatus. Many enclosed-space deaths are would-be rescuers.
  • "A crew member has received an electric shock at a distribution board. First action?" Isolate the supply, or separate the casualty using non-conducting material, before touching them. Then assess breathing and treat burns at the entry and exit points.
  • "What is the difference between EFA, Medical First Aid and Medical Care?" Give the three levels and their STCW tables: A-VI/1-3, A-VI/4-1 and A-VI/4-2.
  • "How do you recognise shock?" Look for pale, cold, clammy skin, a rapid weak pulse, rapid shallow breathing, and anxiety or confusion. Explain the basic management.
  • "How do you control severe bleeding?" Apply direct pressure and dress the wound. Know your company's guidance on tourniquets and where the kit is stowed.

Answer in a logical sequence that starts with your own safety and the alarm. Examiners notice when a candidate starts with treatment and forgets the danger.

Keeping it in date and keeping your skills real

  • Verify your certificate online as soon as you receive it, not the night before you fly.
  • Track the PST and FPFF refresher dates alongside EFA. Most joining problems involving "basic training" are really refresher problems.
  • Check your administration's current rules on basic training validity whenever you renew documents. Circulars change.
  • Ask your manning agent whether the company or charterer has stricter first aid requirements before you accept a contract.
  • Keep originals plus clear scans, and make sure your name matches your passport and CDC exactly.
  • Take part properly in onboard drills. Casualty handling, stretcher work and CPR practice keep the skill alive between courses.
  • Know where the kit is on every new ship during safety familiarisation: first aid boxes, stretchers, the medical locker and the AED if fitted.
Key points
  • EFA is one of four STCW basic training elements, with its standard set out in Table A-VI/1-3 of the STCW Code.
  • Everyone with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties needs basic training, including EFA.
  • STCW requires five-yearly evidence of competence only for PST and FPFF, not EFA.
  • Some administrations and companies impose their own validity or refresher rules, so always check.
  • EFA is not Medical First Aid (A-VI/4-1) or Medical Care (A-VI/4-2).
  • Most joining refusals come from unverifiable certificates, name mismatches or lapsed PST/FPFF refreshers.
  • In any casualty situation, your own safety and raising the alarm come first.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Elementary First Aid certificate expire?

Under STCW, EFA has no five-yearly revalidation requirement. Only PST and FPFF need evidence of maintained competence every five years. However, your administration or company may set its own validity rules, so check the current national circulars and your company's requirements.

Is EFA the same as Medical First Aid?

No. EFA (Table A-VI/1-3) is basic training for all crew with safety duties. Medical First Aid (Table A-VI/4-1) is for seafarers designated to provide medical first aid on board, and Medical Care (Table A-VI/4-2) is for those designated to take charge of medical care.

Do I need to redo EFA when I do my basic training refresher?

STCW only requires refreshers for PST and FPFF. Some administrations or training centres package refreshers differently, and some companies want more recent first aid training. Check with your administration and manning agent before booking.

Will a foreign ship accept my EFA certificate from my home country?

Generally yes. Basic training certificates issued by an STCW-compliant administration through an approved training centre are widely accepted, and flag endorsement under Regulation I/10 applies to CoCs and certain tanker proficiency certificates rather than basic training. Some flags or companies may still ask for verification, so make sure your certificate can be checked online.

Why was my EFA certificate rejected by the manning agent?

Common reasons are a training centre that was not approved for the course, a certificate that cannot be verified online, or a name that doesn't match your passport or CDC. The agent may also have flagged a lapsed PST or FPFF refresher, or a company requirement for more recent training. Ask exactly which check failed so you fix the right problem.

Test yourself

Which table of the STCW Code sets the minimum standard of competence for Elementary First Aid?

EFA is Table A-VI/1-3 (Section A-VI/1). A-VI/1-1 is PST (Section A-VI/1), and A-VI/4-1 is Medical First Aid (Section A-VI/4).

Who is required by STCW Regulation VI/1 to complete basic training, including EFA?

Regulation VI/1 applies basic training to seafarers with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties, regardless of rank, subject only to limited exemptions an administration may grant under Section A-VI/1.

A seafarer designated to take charge of medical care on board must meet the standard in:

Section A-VI/4 and Table A-VI/4-2 cover seafarers designated to take charge of medical care. EFA is the basic level for all crew.

A crew member is lying in contact with a live distribution board. What is your first action?

Table A-VI/1-3 starts with assessing threats to your own safety. Touching a casualty who is still live makes you a second casualty.
