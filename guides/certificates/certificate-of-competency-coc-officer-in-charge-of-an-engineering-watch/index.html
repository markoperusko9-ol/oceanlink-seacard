Certificate of Competency (CoC) – Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch

Quick answer

The Officer in Charge of an Engineering Watch (OICEW) CoC is the operational-level engineer certificate required by STCW Regulation III/1 to keep an engine-room watch, or act as designated duty engineer, on seagoing ships with 750 kW propulsion power or more. It is issued by your national administration after approved training, seagoing service and examination. It must be revalidated at intervals of no more than five years, with the details set by each administration.

What the OICEW certificate is

The OICEW CoC is the first engineer officer certificate under STCW. It sits at the operational level and allows you to take charge of an engine-room watch in a manned engine room. In a periodically unmanned engine room (UMS), it allows you to act as the designated duty engineer.

The certificate's name changes by country:

  • India: the DG Shipping certificate is commonly called MEO Class IV (Marine Engineer Officer Class IV).
  • Philippines: MARINA issues it as an OIC-EW certificate.

The rank you sail in also varies. With an OICEW you will usually sign on as 4th or 3rd engineer, depending on the company and the ship's manning. The certificate's capacity is what matters legally, not the rank title on the crew list.

The certificate is not valid for 2nd engineer or chief engineer posts. Those need the management-level certificates under Regulations III/2 or III/3.

Check your certificate for limitations, because some administrations restrict it, for example:

  • to motor ships only
  • to near-coastal voyages
  • to a power range

A limitation you never noticed in your own country can stop you at a new company.

Which rules require it

Instrument What it does
STCW Regulation III/1 Minimum requirements for certification as OICEW or designated duty engineer, for ships of 750 kW propulsion power or more
STCW Code, Section A-III/1 The standard of competence: the competences, knowledge and assessment methods
STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2 Watchkeeping principles, including taking over the watch and when to call the chief engineer
STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 Revalidation and continued professional competence
STCW Regulation I/10 Recognition (endorsement) of your CoC by another flag
MLC 2006, Regulation 1.3 Seafarers must be trained or certified as competent for their duties

On ships below 750 kW, the national administration sets its own certification rules. Those certificates are often not interchangeable with a full III/1 CoC.

The safe manning document issued by the flag decides how many certificated engineers a ship must carry, and at what level.

Who needs it and the route to getting it

You need an OICEW CoC if you will stand an engine-room watch or be on the UMS duty roster as an engineer officer.

Under Regulation III/1, a candidate must meet these minimums:

  • Age: at least 18 years old.
  • Training and sea service: workshop skills training plus approved seagoing service. The service required is shorter if done within an approved training programme with on-board training recorded in a training record book, and longer by the alternative route. Your administration publishes the exact figures and how they count.
  • Supervised watchkeeping: at least six months of engine-room watchkeeping duties under the supervision of the chief engineer or a qualified engineer officer, as part of that sea service.
  • Competence: meet the Section A-III/1 standard, usually proved through approved education and written and oral examinations.

You will also need safety training: basic training (Reg. VI/1), plus the survival craft, advanced fire fighting and medical first aid standards (A-VI/2, A-VI/3, A-VI/4) that form part of the A-III/1 competences. Most administrations require the separate certificates.

On top of this, administrations add their own requirements, such as:

  • pre-sea course structure
  • exam formats and attempts
  • extra courses
  • documents such as India's INDoS number

Always work from your own administration's current circulars, not from what a batchmate did years ago.

What the syllabus covers

Section A-III/1 groups the competences under four functions:

  • Marine engineering:
    • keeping a safe engineering watch
    • using English in written and oral form
    • using internal communication systems
    • operating main and auxiliary machinery
    • operating pumping systems
    • operating fuel, lubrication and ballast systems
  • Electrical, electronic and control engineering:
    • operating electrical, electronic and control systems
    • maintaining and repairing them
  • Maintenance and repair:
    • using hand tools, machine tools and measuring instruments
    • maintaining and repairing shipboard machinery and equipment
  • Controlling the operation of the ship and care for persons on board:
    • pollution prevention (MARPOL)
    • seaworthiness and stability basics
    • fire prevention and fighting
    • life-saving appliances
    • medical first aid
    • legislation
    • leadership and teamworking skills

Expect the examiners to concentrate on practical watchkeeping:

  • what you check on a round
  • how you take over and hand over
  • how you respond to alarms
  • what you record, and where

Oral exam: what examiners actually ask

Examiners typically look for a safe, logical sequence, not a textbook recital. Typical questions, with what a good answer contains:

"You are taking over the watch. What do you check?" - standing orders and the chief engineer's special instructions - machinery running and on standby - any work in progress and isolations in force - tank levels, including bilges and sludge - fuel and lube oil condition - alarms outstanding - any operation in progress that should be completed before handover, and any abnormal conditions

Section A-VIII/2 says the engineer being relieved must not hand over if they have reason to believe the relieving officer is obviously not capable of carrying out the watch duties effectively, and must inform the chief engineer. Any important operation in progress at handover should be concluded by the officer being relieved unless the chief engineer orders otherwise.

"Blackout at sea. Go." 1. Inform the bridge and the chief engineer. 2. Check the standby generator has started and connected. If not, start it manually. 3. Confirm the emergency generator is supplying the emergency switchboard. 4. Check for an obvious fault (e.g. on the switchboard or a tripped breaker) before reconnecting, so you do not close onto a fault. Investigate the root cause once essential services are restored. 5. Restore essential services in sequence: steering, lubrication and cooling pumps, then the main engine.

"Oil mist detector alarm on the main engine." 1. Treat it as a crankcase explosion risk. 2. Inform the bridge and reduce speed or stop as per company and maker instructions. 3. Keep people away from the crankcase doors and relief valves. 4. Do not open the crankcase until the maker's cooling period has passed. 5. Find the hot spot before restarting.

"When do you call the chief engineer?" - engine damage or malfunction that may endanger safe operation - anything that could cause propulsion, auxiliary or steering failure - any emergency - when in doubt

The company's standing orders define the details.

"How do you discharge bilges?" - only through approved 15 ppm oil filtering equipment, with the 15 ppm alarm and automatic stopping device (required on ships of 10,000 GT and above and for any discharge within a special area) - with the ship en route and the effluent not exceeding 15 ppm - inside special areas the same conditions apply, and there is no discharge at all in the Antarctic area - otherwise retain on board and land to a reception facility - every operation recorded in the Oil Record Book

If you say "I'd just pump it," the oral ends quickly.

Validity and revalidation

Under Regulation I/11, you must revalidate at intervals of no more than five years. To do so you must:

  • hold a valid medical certificate
  • prove continued professional competence

Section A-I/11 lists ways to prove competence:

  • approved sea service in the certificate's functions of at least 12 months in total in the preceding five years, or
  • functions considered equivalent to that sea service, or
  • passing an approved test, or completing an approved training course, or
  • at least three months in total of approved sea service in a supernumerary capacity or in a lower officer rank immediately before taking up the rank for which the certificate is valid

Some administrations accept additional options, so check your administration's rules.

Your safety certificates also need keeping up:

  • Five-yearly refresher required: personal survival techniques, fire prevention and fire fighting, PSCRB and advanced fire fighting.
  • No STCW refresher requirement: elementary first aid, medical first aid and PSSR. Some flags or companies may still ask for one, so check.

The following are set by your administration, not by STCW, so check its rules:

  • documents accepted as evidence of sea service
  • whether discharge book entries or company sea-service letters are needed
  • how far ahead you can apply
  • fees and processing times
  • whether online verification is available

A flag endorsement (Reg. I/10) cannot outlive the CoC it endorses. Renew the CoC, then renew the endorsement.

Why engineers get stopped at joining

Common reasons include:

  • CoC or endorsement expiring mid-contract. Many companies will not sign you on unless the CoC covers the whole contract plus a margin. That is a company rule, not STCW, but it is common.
  • No flag endorsement, and no proof of application. Some flags let you serve for up to three months on proof that an endorsement application has been submitted. You need that proof in hand.
  • Refresher certificates expired. Basic safety training, PSCRB or AFF out of date makes the CoC set incomplete.
  • Medical certificate expired or wrong format for the flag.
  • Name or date of birth mismatch between passport, CoC, CDC and endorsement.
  • Copies only. Regulation I/2 expects original certificates on board while you serve.
  • Limitations on the CoC, such as motor only or near-coastal, that do not suit the ship.
  • Missing ship-type training, such as oil, chemical or gas tanker basic training, or IGF Code training on gas-fuelled ships. Some companies also require high voltage training.
  • Certificate cannot be verified online or through the administration, so the company holds you until it is confirmed.

Port State Control can check your certificates under Regulation I/4. A certificate problem found on board becomes the master's and the company's problem too.

Keeping it in date: a simple system

  1. Keep one expiry list covering CoC, flag endorsements, medical, refreshers, passport, CDC and visas. Set reminders well ahead, because processing times vary by administration.
  2. Plan revalidation before your last contract of the five-year cycle, not after it. Sea-service documents are easier to get while you are still with the company.
  3. Collect sea-service proof every contract: sign-off entries, the company sea-service letter and the training record book where applicable.
  4. Book refreshers ashore between contracts so they do not fall due on board.
  5. Ask crewing which flag the next ship flies and start the endorsement early.
  6. Scan everything and carry the originals in a waterproof folder.
  7. Start your management-level plan early. Your OICEW sea service and watchkeeping records feed into the 2nd engineer certificate.
Key points
  • The OICEW CoC is required by STCW Regulation III/1 for engine-room watchkeepers on ships of 750 kW propulsion power or more.
  • The standard of competence is in STCW Code Section A-III/1; watchkeeping principles are in Section A-VIII/2.
  • Candidates must be at least 18 and have at least six months of supervised engine-room watchkeeping within approved sea service.
  • Revalidate at intervals of no more than five years under Regulation I/11; the evidence accepted is set by your administration.
  • PST, FPFF, PSCRB and AFF need five-yearly refreshers; EFA, Medical First Aid and PSSR do not under STCW.
  • Flag endorsements under Regulation I/10 cannot outlast the underlying CoC.
  • Most joining stoppages are about expiry dates, missing endorsements, name mismatches and copies instead of originals.

Frequently asked questions

Is MEO Class IV the same as the OICEW certificate?

Yes. MEO Class IV is the Indian DG Shipping certificate issued for the STCW Regulation III/1 operational level. Other countries use different names for the same STCW capacity. What matters internationally is the STCW regulation and capacity stated on the certificate.

Can I sail as 2nd engineer with an OICEW certificate?

No. The 2nd engineer post needs a management-level certificate under Regulation III/2, or III/3 for ships within the lower power range. A company cannot get round this by changing the rank title.

How much sea time do I need to revalidate my CoC?

Section A-I/11 accepts approved sea service of at least 12 months in total in the preceding five years. Alternatives include equivalent functions, an approved test or course, or three months' supernumerary or lower-rank service immediately before taking up the rank. Your administration decides what documents prove that service and may accept additional options.

Do I need a flag endorsement if my CoC is from India or the Philippines and the ship is under another flag?

Yes. Serving on a ship of a different flag requires that flag's endorsement recognising your CoC under Regulation I/10. Some flags allow you to serve for up to three months while the application is processed, but only with documentary proof that it was submitted.

Does my OICEW CoC cover high voltage systems?

Not automatically. High voltage competence is addressed in STCW at management level and in separate training. Many companies and some administrations require a high voltage course for engineers on ships with HV plant, so check the company's requirements before joining.

Test yourself

STCW Regulation III/1 applies to engineer officers on seagoing ships with main propulsion machinery of what power?

Regulation III/1 covers ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 750 kW or more; 3,000 kW is the threshold used for the management-level split in III/2 and III/3. Ref: STCW Regulation III/1.

Which of these meets the continued-competence sea service option for revalidating a CoC?

Section A-I/11 accepts at least 12 months in total in the preceding five years. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-I/11.

What minimum period of supervised engine-room watchkeeping must an OICEW candidate have performed during the required sea service?

Regulation III/1 requires at least six months of engine-room watchkeeping under the supervision of the chief engineer or a qualified engineer officer. Ref: STCW Regulation III/1.

Which of these STCW trainings requires evidence of maintained competence every five years?

The Manila amendments require five-yearly evidence of competence for PST, FPFF, PSCRB and Advanced Fire Fighting; EFA, PSSR and security awareness carry no STCW refresher requirement. Ref: STCW Regulations VI/1, VI/2, VI/3 and Sections A-VI/1, A-VI/2, A-VI/3.

You hold a valid Philippine OIC-EW CoC and are joining a ship under a different flag. What does STCW require?

Regulation I/10 requires the flag's endorsement of recognition; the flag may permit service of up to three months while the application is pending, with documentary proof. Ref: STCW Regulation I/10.
