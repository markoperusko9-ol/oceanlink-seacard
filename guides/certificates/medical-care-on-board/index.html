Medical Care on Board: The STCW VI/4 Certificate Explained

Quick answer

Medical Care on board is the STCW proficiency required under Regulation VI/4 for any seafarer designated to take charge of medical care on board, typically on ships that do not carry a doctor (MLC Standard A4.1). In practice it is held by masters and chief mates, and many administrations require it before issuing a management-level deck CoC. STCW does not set a fixed five-year refresher for it as it does for basic training, but some flag states and companies do, so check your administration's rules and the ship's flag.

What the certificate is and where it comes from

Medical Care is the higher of the two STCW medical proficiencies. Medical First Aid trains you to give immediate help. Medical Care trains you to take charge of a patient on board for days at a time, with no doctor available and only the ship's medicine chest and radio medical advice to work with.

The legal basis sits in two instruments:

  • STCW Regulation VI/4 and Section A-VI/4. These set the minimum requirements for medical first aid and medical care. The competence standard for Medical Care is in Table A-VI/4-2.
  • MLC 2006, Regulation 4.1 and Standard A4.1. A ship that does not carry a doctor must have either at least one seafarer in charge of medical care and administering medicines as part of their regular duties, or at least one seafarer competent to provide medical first aid. National law decides which, taking into account factors such as voyage length and crew size. A person in charge of medical care who is not a doctor must have completed medical care training that meets STCW. A person designated for medical first aid must have completed STCW medical first aid training.

The competence also appears at management level in the deck department. Table A-II/2 includes organising and managing the provision of medical care on board. That is why most administrations link Medical Care to the Chief Mate and Master CoC.

MLC also requires a qualified doctor on ships carrying 100 or more persons and ordinarily engaged on international voyages of more than three days' duration. On those ships the Medical Care holder is not the primary medical provider, but the certificate may still be required for the officer's CoC.

Who needs it

Regulation VI/4 places the requirement on the seafarer designated to take charge of medical care on board. The convention does not name a rank. Rank requirements come from your administration and your company.

Who Typical position
Master Usually required. Commonly a prerequisite for the Master CoC
Chief Officer Usually required. Commonly a prerequisite for the Chief Mate CoC
2nd Officer On many ships the 2nd Officer handles the medicine chest and records. Some companies want this person to hold Medical Care, others accept Medical First Aid with the master in overall charge
Other officers and ratings Medical First Aid or Elementary First Aid, depending on their duties and the administration

If the ship's documents name you as the person in charge of medical care, you need Medical Care. Medical First Aid alone does not meet that duty.

Most training centres and administrations expect you to hold Medical First Aid before you start Medical Care. Check the entry requirements of the approved centre you are booking with.

What the course covers

The content follows Table A-VI/4-2 and builds on the medical first aid knowledge in Table A-VI/4-1. IMO Model Course 1.15 is the usual reference for training centres. Course length and the split between theory and practicals are set by each administration's approval, so durations vary.

Expect to cover:

  • Care of casualties with head and spinal injuries, fractures, dislocations, burns, scalds, frostbite and serious bleeding.
  • Emergencies: cardiac arrest, drowning, asphyxia and hypothermia.
  • Toxic hazards on board, including use of the Medical First Aid Guide that accompanies the IMDG Code for dangerous goods incidents.
  • Examining a patient: taking a history, recording vital signs and describing findings clearly.
  • Obtaining radio medical advice. You learn what information the doctor ashore needs and how to pass it accurately.
  • Pharmacology and the medicine chest: dosages, routes, storage and records.
  • Clinical skills: sterilisation, wound care, suturing or wound closure, injections, catheterisation and basic nursing.
  • Medical conditions: communicable diseases and emergencies such as appendicitis, alcohol and drug abuse, dental problems, and gynaecology, pregnancy and childbirth.
  • Care of rescued persons, and handling a death on board.
  • Hygiene, disease prevention and medical records.
  • Using the medical guide carried on board, such as the International Medical Guide for Ships or the flag's equivalent.

A good course is heavily practical. If your centre only lectured you, practise injections and dressings on a training mannequin before you need them at sea.

Validity and revalidation: why it depends on the flag

This is where most confusion comes from.

  • STCW itself sets a five-year refresher rule for basic training and some other proficiencies. It does not set a fixed five-year refresher for Medical Care in the same way.
  • Some administrations still put an expiry date on the certificate or require periodic refresher training. Some do this as a national rule, and some do it as a condition of revalidating a management-level CoC.
  • Some companies and charterers set their own refresher interval in the SMS or crewing policy, even when the flag does not.

The practical rule:

  1. Read your certificate. If it shows an expiry date, that date applies.
  2. Check the current requirements of your issuing administration (for example your national maritime authority).
  3. Check the requirements of the flag of the ship you are joining. A flag endorsement can only reflect what the flag accepts.
  4. Check your company's crewing matrix.

If any of these four sources requires a refresher, you need the refresher. Do not rely on someone telling you "medical care never expires".

Why crew get stopped at joining

These are the common reasons a manning agent, the company or a port state inspector flags a problem:

  • Medical First Aid held where Medical Care is needed. This happens when an officer is promoted, or is joining as Chief Officer, without the higher course.
  • Mixing it up with the medical fitness certificate. Medical Care is a training proficiency. The seafarer medical fitness certificate (MLC Regulation 1.2 and STCW I/9) is about your own health. They are different documents and both are checked.
  • Certificate from a centre the administration has not approved. The certificate cannot be verified online, or the flag will not accept it for endorsement.
  • Expired under national or company rules, even though STCW sets no fixed expiry.
  • Name or date-of-birth mismatch with the passport, CDC or CoC.
  • Original not carried. Many companies and inspectors want originals on board, not only scans.
  • Flag endorsement issues. For example, the CoC endorsement was issued before the Medical Care certificate was presented, or the flag has a specific requirement not met.

Under an MLC inspection, port state control can ask who the designated medical care person is and check that their training matches.

Worked scenario: crew member with abdominal pain, five days from port

You are Chief Officer and the designated medical care officer. An AB reports increasing pain in the lower right abdomen, nausea and a fever.

  1. Examine and record. Take the history, temperature, pulse, respiration and blood pressure. Note the location and nature of the pain, and record the time of each observation.
  2. Inform the master.
  3. Contact radio medical advice (TMAS). MLC requires that medical advice by radio or satellite is available at any hour, free of charge. Use the company procedure or the TMAS your flag or the coast station directs you to.
  4. Give clear facts. Have the medical report form and your observations ready. Give ship position, ETA at nearest ports, the medicine chest contents and the patient's allergies.
  5. Treat as advised. Use only drugs from the chest as directed. Record every dose in the medicine log.
  6. Monitor and re-report. Repeat observations at the interval the doctor gives, and call back if the patient gets worse.
  7. Discuss a diversion or evacuation. The doctor's advice feeds the master's decision; the master decides. Record everything in the log book and medical records.

The course exists for exactly this situation: sound observations and clear reporting, so the doctor ashore can treat the patient through you.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners for management-level orals often probe medical care. Be ready for questions like these.

  • Who on board is responsible for medical care, and where is that stated? The designated person under the SMS and MLC Standard A4.1, under the master's overall authority.
  • What do you prepare before calling for radio medical advice? Patient details, vital signs, symptoms and their timeline, medications already given, allergies, ship's position and nearest ports.
  • How do you control drugs in the medicine chest? Locked storage, a controlled-drugs register, expiry checks and replenishment to the flag's required scale.
  • Where do you find treatment for exposure to a dangerous cargo? The MFAG, using the information on the dangerous goods documents.
  • What records do you keep? The medical log, the medicine chest log, the medical report form, and log book entries.

Examiners want to see that you know the procedure and the chain of responsibility, not that you can recite drug names.

Keeping it in date

  • Keep a document matrix with every certificate's issue and expiry date, including national refresher dates that are not printed on the certificate.
  • Before your next contract, confirm the joining ship's flag requirements with your crewing office. Do not assume your home administration's rules apply.
  • If you are heading for promotion, do Medical Care before applying for the Chief Mate exam. It is a common missing item on applications.
  • Keep originals in your document folder and certified copies with the company.
  • Use online verification where your administration offers it, so the company can confirm the certificate quickly.
  • On board, check the medicine chest regularly and keep up hands-on practice. A certificate is not the same as current skill.
Key points
  • Medical Care is required by STCW Regulation VI/4 for the seafarer designated to take charge of medical care on board.
  • MLC 2006 Standard A4.1 requires ships without a doctor to have either a seafarer in charge of medical care or one competent in medical first aid, as national law specifies. Anyone in charge of medical care who is not a doctor must be trained to the STCW medical care standard.
  • Masters and chief mates usually need it, and many administrations make it a prerequisite for the management-level deck CoC.
  • STCW sets no fixed five-year refresher for it, but some administrations and companies do, so always check.
  • Medical Care training is not the same as the seafarer medical fitness certificate.
  • Holding only Medical First Aid when designated for medical care is a common reason for being stopped at joining.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Medical First Aid and Medical Care?

Medical First Aid trains you to take immediate action at an accident or illness. Medical Care trains the person in charge of medical care to manage patients over a longer period, use the medicine chest and work with radio medical advice. Both come under STCW Regulation VI/4. Medical Care is the higher level.

Does the Medical Care certificate expire?

STCW does not impose a fixed five-year refresher for Medical Care as it does for basic training. However, some administrations put an expiry date on the certificate or require refreshers, and some companies have their own intervals. Follow whatever is printed on your certificate and the rules of your administration, the ship's flag and your company.

Do I need Medical Care for the Chief Mate CoC?

In most administrations, yes. Medical Care is required before a Chief Mate or Master CoC is issued, because Table A-II/2 includes organising medical care on board. Check your own administration's current exam eligibility list to be sure.

Can a 2nd Officer be the medical officer with only Medical First Aid?

It depends on how duties are designated. The person formally designated to take charge of medical care must hold Medical Care. Many ships designate the master or chief officer and have the 2nd Officer assist with the chest and records. Some companies require the 2nd Officer to hold Medical Care as well.

Is Medical Care the same as my seafarer medical certificate?

No. Medical Care is a training proficiency in treating others. The seafarer medical fitness certificate under MLC Regulation 1.2 and STCW Regulation I/9 confirms your own fitness for duty. You need each one separately where it applies.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the requirements for Medical Care on board?

Regulation VI/4 covers medical first aid and medical care. VI/1 is basic training, VI/3 is advanced fire fighting, and I/9 is medical fitness standards. Reference: STCW Regulation VI/4.

Under MLC 2006, a seafarer in charge of medical care on a ship without a doctor, who is not a doctor, must have:

Standard A4.1 para 4(c) requires persons in charge of medical care who are not doctors to have completed training in medical care meeting STCW. Depending on national law, a ship without a doctor may instead designate a seafarer competent in medical first aid. Reference: MLC 2006, Standard A4.1.

Which statement about Medical Care validity is correct?

Unlike basic training, STCW does not lay down a fixed five-year refresher for Medical Care, but national and company rules may. Reference: STCW Regulation VI/4 and national requirements.

Where does the competence table for Medical Care appear in the STCW Code?

Table A-VI/4-2 specifies the minimum standard for medical care. A-VI/4-1 covers medical first aid. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/4.

The Chief Officer is designated in the SMS to take charge of medical care but holds only Medical First Aid. What is the position?

The seafarer designated to take charge of medical care must meet the Medical Care standard. Either the designation or the training must change. Reference: STCW Regulation VI/4; MLC Standard A4.1.
