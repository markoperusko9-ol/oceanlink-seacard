IGF Code Basic Training: A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

IGF Code basic training is the STCW certificate of proficiency required under Regulation V/3. Every seafarer with designated safety duties for the care, use or emergency response to the fuel on a ship using gas or other low-flashpoint fuel must hold it, for example on an LNG- or methanol-fuelled ship. You must complete it before you are assigned those duties. Your administration sets how the certificate is issued, how long it is valid and how it is revalidated, so check those rules before you join.

What IGF Code basic training is and where it comes from

The IGF Code is the International Code of Safety for Ships using Gases or other Low-flashpoint Fuels. It became mandatory through SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part G and entered into force on 1 January 2017. It covers the design, equipment and operation of ships that burn fuels such as LNG. Through its alternative design route it also covers other low-flashpoint fuels, such as methanol.

The crew training requirements are not in the IGF Code itself. They are in STCW:

  • Regulation V/3 sets the minimum training and qualification requirements for masters, officers, ratings and other personnel on ships subject to the IGF Code.
  • STCW Code Section A-V/3 and Table A-V/3-1 list the competences for basic training.
  • Table A-V/3-2 covers the separate advanced training.

The IGF basic certificate is a certificate of proficiency, not a certificate of competency. It sits alongside your CoC and your other STCW courses.

One point catches people out. The IGF Code does not apply to gas carriers that use their cargo as fuel and comply with the IGC Code, such as an LNG carrier burning cargo boil-off. Crew on those ships are covered by the liquefied gas tanker training under Regulation V/1-2. A gas carrier burning a low-flashpoint fuel other than its cargo may still be subject to the IGF Code, so check with the company.

Who needs it

Regulation V/3 requires the basic certificate for seafarers responsible for designated safety duties associated with the care, use or emergency response to the fuel. The training must be completed before you are assigned those duties, not during your first contract.

In practice the company decides who has designated safety duties, through its SMS and muster list. On many gas-fuelled ships this covers most of the crew, because fuel-related duties appear in emergency response and bunkering. Crew commonly required to hold it include:

  • Master and all deck officers
  • All engineer officers and ETOs
  • Engine ratings, and deck ratings with bunkering or emergency party duties
  • Any other crew whose muster list duties involve the fuel system or a gas emergency

The advanced certificate is a separate requirement. It applies to masters, engineer officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for the care and use of the fuel and fuel systems. Holding basic does not give you advanced.

Gas tanker certificates: STCW treats holders of the liquefied gas tanker training certificates under V/1-2 as meeting the IGF basic requirement. How this is recorded differs by administration. Some issue a separate IGF certificate, others accept the gas tanker certificate as it stands. Confirm with your flag or manning office rather than assuming.

What the course covers

Table A-V/3-1 groups the basic competences broadly as follows:

  • Contributing to safe operation of a ship subject to the IGF Code. This covers the fuel system layout, fuel containment, gas-safe and gas-dangerous spaces, and the safety systems.
  • Taking precautions to prevent hazards. This covers the properties of the fuels, flammability, ignition sources, gas detection and the shutdown arrangements.
  • Occupational health and safety. This covers cryogenic burns, asphyxiation, toxicity (relevant to methanol), PPE and enclosed space awareness.
  • Fire-fighting on a ship using gas or low-flashpoint fuel. This covers the fixed systems and why some fuel fires are handled differently from oil fires.
  • Emergency response. This covers leaks, bunkering incidents and the ship's emergency procedures.
  • Pollution prevention in relation to releases of fuel.

Some practical points every candidate should understand:

  • LNG is carried at around −162 °C. Contact with the liquid, or with uninsulated pipework, causes severe cold burns and can embrittle ordinary steel.
  • Cold methane vapour stays low at first. Once it warms it becomes lighter than air and rises.
  • Methanol burns with a flame that is very hard to see in daylight. A thermal camera or a heat-sensing approach matters.
  • Gas detection, ventilation and automatic shutdowns are the main defences. Do not bypass or isolate them.

Course length, the practical content and the assessment method are set by your administration and the approved training centre. Check the course is approved by the administration that will issue your certificate.

Validity and revalidation

This is where practice varies most, so treat this section as general guidance only.

  • For masters and officers, STCW Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 require evidence of continued competence for IGF ships at intervals not exceeding five years. This means approved relevant sea service (at least 3 months in the last five years) or an approved relevant training course. How this applies to ratings and how certificates are dated depends on the administration.
  • Many administrations issue IGF certificates with a validity period, often aligned to the five-year cycle used for other STCW certificates.
  • Revalidation normally needs evidence of continued competence. That evidence is usually either relevant sea service on ships subject to the IGF Code (or on gas tankers, where accepted) within the validity period, or a refresher or revalidation course.
  • Some administrations issue the certificate without an expiry date but apply their own rules for continued service. Others tie the revalidation date to your CoC.

What to do:

  1. Read the validity date on the certificate itself, not on the course completion letter.
  2. Ask your administration, or your manning agent, what counts as qualifying sea service and how much is needed.
  3. Keep sea service proof (discharge book entries, sea service testimonials) that clearly names the vessel and shows it is gas- or methanol-fuelled.

Indian seafarers should make sure the course is DG Shipping-approved and appears against their INDoS record. Filipino seafarers should follow MARINA's certificate of proficiency process. Each system has its own rules on issue and renewal.

Why crew get stopped at joining

The common reasons are:

  • No certificate at all. The crew member was told the ship was conventional, but it is dual-fuel. This is often noticed only at the gangway or at a company document check.
  • Gas tanker certificate assumed to cover it without the flag or company confirming. Some companies want the IGF certificate itself shown.
  • Course from a centre not approved by your administration. The certificate may not be recognised.
  • Certificate not uploaded or verified on the national database, so the company cannot verify it online.
  • Expired certificate, or a revalidation date missed because it differs from the CoC date.
  • Basic held, advanced needed. An officer is promoted or moved to a rank with immediate responsibility for the fuel system.
  • Name or date of birth mismatch between the certificate, passport and seaman's book.

Worked scenario: A 3rd Engineer holding basic and advanced oil tanker certificates is sent to a dual-fuel container ship. Two days before travel, the crewing office spots that the ship burns LNG. He needs IGF basic as a minimum. As an engineer officer, he also needs IGF advanced under Regulation V/3. Advanced has its own entry requirements: the basic certificate, approved advanced training, and approved seagoing service that includes bunkering operations on IGF ships (part of which may be replaced by approved simulator training), unless he qualifies through the gas tanker equivalence route. He cannot get advanced in a weekend, so the realistic outcome is a delayed joining or a change of ship. The lesson is to ask what fuel the ship burns as soon as you are offered a contract.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners and superintendents often check basic understanding at joining or during audits. Be ready for questions like these.

Q: Which STCW regulation requires IGF training? Regulation V/3. The competences are in Section A-V/3 of the STCW Code.

Q: Does the IGF Code apply to an LNG carrier burning boil-off? No. A gas carrier using its cargo as fuel is covered by the IGC Code. The crew need liquefied gas tanker training under V/1-2.

Q: What is the first action if gas detection alarms in the fuel preparation room? Follow the ship's emergency procedure. Typically this means raising the alarm and confirming any automatic shutdown has operated. Do not enter without authorisation and proper protection, and keep ignition sources away. Your answer should refer to the ship's own procedures.

Q: Why is methanol fire-fighting different? The flame can be almost invisible in daylight. Methanol also mixes with water, which affects how foam and water perform. Detection by heat or a thermal camera matters.

Q: Who decides which crew have designated safety duties for the fuel? The company, through the SMS and muster list. This must be consistent with STCW and the flag's requirements.

Tips for keeping it in date

  • Ask about the fuel before accepting a contract. Dual-fuel ships are now common in container, car carrier, tanker and offshore fleets.
  • Keep one document list. Record each certificate's issue and expiry date, then set a reminder well ahead of any expiry.
  • Log your IGF sea service. Get testimonials that name the vessel and its fuel type. This helps with revalidation and with the sea service needed for advanced training.
  • Plan for advanced early if you are an engineer or are heading towards command.
  • Check recognition if you sail under a foreign flag. Some flags have their own recognition or endorsement process.
  • Do the ship-specific familiarisation properly. The certificate covers general knowledge. The company must still familiarise you with that ship's fuel system and emergency procedures before you take up duties.
Key points
  • IGF basic training is required by STCW Regulation V/3, with competences in Table A-V/3-1.
  • It applies to seafarers with designated safety duties for the fuel on ships subject to the IGF Code, such as LNG- or methanol-fuelled ships.
  • It must be completed before you are assigned those duties.
  • Gas carriers using their cargo as fuel under the IGC Code are not covered by the IGF Code; their crew need V/1-2 gas tanker training.
  • Validity, revalidation and recognition of gas tanker certificates depend on your administration.
  • Advanced training is a separate certificate for masters, engineer officers and those immediately responsible for the fuel.
  • Ask what fuel a ship burns before you accept a contract to avoid being stopped at joining.

Frequently asked questions

Is IGF Code basic training mandatory?

Yes, for seafarers with designated safety duties for the care, use or emergency response to the fuel on ships subject to the IGF Code. The requirement is in STCW Regulation V/3. The company's SMS and muster list define who holds those duties on each ship.

Do I need IGF training to work on an LNG carrier?

Not if the carrier uses its cargo as fuel. In that case it is covered by the IGC Code, even when it burns boil-off gas, and you need the liquefied gas tanker training certificates under STCW Regulation V/1-2. A gas carrier burning a low-flashpoint fuel other than its cargo may still be subject to the IGF Code, so check with the company.

Does my basic gas tanker certificate cover IGF basic?

STCW treats holders of the liquefied gas tanker training certificates as meeting the IGF basic requirement. How this is documented varies by administration, and some companies still want a separate IGF certificate. Check with your flag administration and manning office before joining.

How long is an IGF Code basic training certificate valid?

It depends on the issuing administration. For masters and officers, STCW Regulation I/11 requires evidence of continued competence for IGF ships at intervals not exceeding five years, through relevant sea service or an approved course. Always read the date on your certificate and confirm the renewal rules with your administration.

Do I need IGF training for a methanol-fuelled ship?

Methanol is a low-flashpoint fuel, and ships using it fall under the IGF Code framework, so the V/3 training requirement applies. Companies may also require extra methanol-specific training and ship familiarisation. Confirm the exact requirements with your company and flag.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the training requirements for seafarers on ships subject to the IGF Code?

Regulation V/3 covers ships subject to the IGF Code. V/1-1 covers oil and chemical tankers, and V/1-2 covers liquefied gas tankers. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/3.

An LNG carrier built to the IGC Code burns boil-off gas in its engines. Which training covers its crew?

Gas carriers using their cargo as fuel under the IGC Code are not covered by the IGF Code, so V/1-2 gas tanker training applies. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part G; STCW Regulation V/1-2.

When must a seafarer hold IGF basic training?

Regulation V/3 requires seafarers responsible for designated safety duties associated with the care, use or emergency response to the fuel to hold the basic certificate before taking up those duties. Separate ship-specific familiarization is also required before any shipboard duties. Reference: STCW Regulation V/3.

Why is a methanol fire particularly hazardous for fire-fighting teams?

Methanol burns with a flame that is very hard to see in daylight, so heat detection or a thermal camera is important. Fuel hazards are part of the basic competences. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-V/3-1.

Who holds the IGF advanced certificate requirement, in addition to basic?

Advanced training is required for masters, engineer officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for the care and use of the fuel and fuel systems. Reference: STCW Regulation V/3; Table A-V/3-2.
