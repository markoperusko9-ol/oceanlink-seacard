Seaman's Book (Continuous Discharge Certificate): A Practical Guide

Quick answer

A seaman's book, called a Continuous Discharge Certificate (CDC) in India and some other administrations and a Seafarer's Identification and Record Book (SIRB) in the Philippines, is a national document that identifies you as a seafarer and records each sign-on and sign-off. It is not an STCW certificate. Its legal basis comes from national law and the MLC 2006 requirement for a record of employment. The ILO seafarers' identity document conventions apply where the book doubles as, or sits alongside, the national Seafarer's Identity Document (SID). Validity, renewal and acceptance by flag states all depend on the issuing administration, so check your own rules and keep the book clean, current and consistent with your passport.

What a seaman's book actually is

The seaman's book is your working identity at sea. It does two jobs:

  • Identity. It shows that you are a professional seafarer, which matters to immigration, port authorities and agents when you travel to join or leave a ship.
  • Record of service. Each time you sign on or off, the master (or a shipping master or consular official, depending on the system) enters the ship's name, IMO or official number, flag, your capacity and the dates and ports. The entry is signed and stamped.

Different administrations use different names:

Name you will hear Typical usage
CDC (Continuous Discharge Certificate) India (issued under the Directorate General of Shipping) and some other South Asian administrations
SIRB (Seafarer's Identification and Record Book) Philippines, issued by MARINA
Seaman's book / discharge book / seafarer's record book Many other national administrations and some open registries

Keep it separate in your mind from three other documents that are often confused with it:

  • Passport. This is your travel document. The seaman's book usually supports it and does not replace it, although some states accept it alone in limited cases.
  • Certificate of Competency (CoC) and STCW certificates. These prove what you are qualified to do. The book proves where and when you served.
  • Training Record Book for cadets, which records onboard training tasks. The seaman's book does not.

The legal basis: MLC, ILO identity conventions and national law

There is no single global "seaman's book regulation". The requirement is built up in layers:

  • MLC 2006, Regulation 2.1 and Standard A2.1. Seafarers must be given a document recording their employment on board. The convention leaves the form to national law, and it states that this record must not contain any statement about the quality of your work or your wages. That is why a genuine discharge entry never carries a conduct grade or pay figure.
  • ILO Convention No. 108 (1958) and No. 185 (2003, revised). These cover the Seafarers' Identity Document (SID), meaning recognition of seafarer status for shore leave, transit and transfer. C185 introduced a biometric document. In many states the SID is a separate document from the discharge or record book. These conventions apply to your seaman's book only where it also serves as, or is issued alongside, the national SID. Which convention applies depends on what your state has ratified and implemented. In India, the CDC and the biometric Seafarer's Identity Document (BSID) are separate documents issued under rules made under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.
  • National merchant shipping legislation. This sets who may hold a book, the issue process, validity, fees, replacement and the format of entries.
  • Flag state requirements. Some flags accept your national book. Others require you to hold their own seafarer book or identification document while serving on their ships. Your manning agent should confirm this before you travel.

The STCW Convention does not require a seaman's book. STCW does require proof of approved seagoing service for CoCs, though. Many administrations accept discharge entries, together with sea service testimonials, as part of that proof.

Who needs one and how you typically get it

Anyone who will serve on board as part of the crew normally needs a seaman's book from their national administration. That includes officers, ratings, cadets, galley and hotel staff. Exact eligibility rules vary, but the general sequence looks like this:

  1. Complete the qualifying training. This is usually an approved pre-sea course or the STCW basic training modules. Some administrations link the book to completion of a specific approved pre-sea programme.
  2. Get registered where required. In India, for example, you need an INDoS number before applying for the CDC.
  3. Pass a medical by an approved doctor, if your administration asks for it at issue.
  4. Gather identity documents. You will need a valid passport, photographs and any proof of address or nationality that is asked for.
  5. Apply through the official channel. Many administrations now run online applications with an appointment for biometrics or verification. Some require sponsorship or a request from a licensed manning agency or company.
  6. Check the book on collection. Your name, date of birth and passport details must match exactly.

There is no "seaman's book course". If anyone offers to get you a book without the qualifying training and the official process, walk away. Fake or irregularly issued books are a known problem. They are checked at immigration, by port state control and by employers, and they can end a career.

Validity, renewal and replacement

Validity periods are set by each administration, and some have changed theirs over the years, so do not rely on a colleague's book from another country or another decade. Check the expiry printed in your own book and your administration's current rules.

In general terms:

  • Expiry. Most books carry an expiry date. Many employers and some immigration authorities want it valid beyond the full expected contract, not just on the joining date.
  • Renewal. This usually needs the old book, an application, current identity documents and sometimes evidence of recent sea service or a valid medical. Some administrations cancel the old book and return it so you keep the history. Others transfer the record.
  • Book full. When the service pages run out, you will need a continuation or new book even if the old one has not expired. Watch the remaining pages before a long contract with several vessel changes.
  • Loss or damage. Report a loss promptly, usually with a police or official report, and apply for a duplicate. Recovering old sea service entries can be slow, so keep copies.
  • Name changes. After marriage or a legal change of name, update the passport and the seaman's book so they match before you travel.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Most problems happen at the airport, at immigration or at the gangway. Agents and masters are personally exposed if they let someone through with defective papers. The usual causes:

Problem Why it stops you
Book expired, or expiring during the contract The company or immigration will not accept it. Some flags will not sign you on.
No sign-off entry from the last ship It looks as if you are still serving elsewhere, and it creates a sea service gap.
Name or date of birth differs from the passport Immigration and visa systems flag the mismatch.
Missing master's signature or ship's stamp on an entry The entry may be treated as invalid.
Pages damaged, altered, or entries overwritten Suspected tampering. The book may be refused or retained.
No pages left Nowhere to make the sign-on entry.
Flag requires its own book or document, not yet obtained You cannot be lawfully signed on under that flag.
Carrying only a scan or photocopy The original is normally required.
Online verification does not match the physical book Where the administration runs an online database, the agent may check it, and a mismatch stops you.

Almost all of these are preventable at home with a ten-minute check.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: the missing sign-off. An AB signed off in a port where the master was busy with cargo, and the discharge entry was never completed. Six months later the agent for his next ship refuses to process him because the book shows him still on the previous vessel.

Fix: Contact the previous company and ask for the entry to be completed by an authorised person, or for a formal sign-off document as your administration accepts. Do this before you are called for the next contract. Better still, never leave a ship without checking the entry is signed, stamped and dated.

Scenario 2: expiry mid-contract. A 2nd Officer's book expires four months into a planned nine-month contract. The company will not send him.

Fix: Renew as soon as your administration allows renewal before expiry. Treat the expiry date of the book like your CoC and medical: plan around the longest contract you might accept.

Scenario 3: change of flag. A cadet joins a ship that changes flag during the contract. The new flag requires its own seafarer document.

Fix: The company or manager normally arranges flag documentation. Make sure your national book also records the change correctly, either as a sign-off and sign-on or in whatever form the master and administration require, so your sea service stays continuous.

Oral exam: what examiners and surveyors ask

At CoC orals and assessments, and sometimes during PSC or flag inspections, expect questions like these:

  • "Is your seaman's book an STCW certificate?" No. It is a national identity and service record. STCW governs certificates of competency and proficiency.
  • "Which international instrument requires a record of your employment?" MLC 2006, Standard A2.1. It also bars any statement of work quality or wages in that record.
  • "Who may hold your seaman's book on board?" It is your document, and who may hold it is governed by national law. ILO Convention No. 185 provides that the Seafarers' Identity Document (SID) remains in your possession at all times, except when held for safekeeping by the master with your written consent. That rule applies to your seaman's book where the book also serves as your national SID. Many company SMS procedures set out how crew documents are held and returned.
  • "How do you prove sea service for your next CoC?" Through discharge entries, sea service testimonials and any other evidence your administration specifies. Know your own administration's list.
  • "What would you do if your book was lost on board?" Inform the master, get a written statement and the ship's records, notify the issuing administration and apply for a duplicate under its procedure.

Examiners may also check your book against your application. Inconsistent dates between the book, testimonials and the application are a common reason for delay.

Key points
  • A seaman's book (CDC in India and some other administrations, SIRB in the Philippines) is a national identity and sea service record, not an STCW certificate.
  • MLC 2006 Standard A2.1 requires a record of employment and forbids any comment on work quality or wages in it.
  • Validity, renewal and replacement rules are set by your own administration, so check them rather than relying on others' experience.
  • Some flag states require their own seafarer document in addition to your national book.
  • Never leave a ship without a signed, stamped and dated discharge entry.
  • Your name and date of birth must match your passport exactly.
  • Keep scans of every page and renew well before a long contract.

Frequently asked questions

Is a seaman's book the same as a CDC?

Yes, in practice. CDC (Continuous Discharge Certificate) is the name used in India and some other administrations for the seaman's book. The Philippine equivalent is the SIRB. All of them record your seafarer identity and your sign-on and sign-off entries under national law.

Can I travel to join a ship with only my seaman's book?

Usually you need your passport as well, plus any visa and the agent's documents the destination requires. Some states give limited recognition to seafarer identity documents for transit or shore leave, but this varies. Confirm with your manning agent before you fly.

How long is a seaman's book valid?

It depends on the issuing administration, and periods have changed over time. Check the expiry date printed in your book and your administration's current rules. Many companies want it valid for the whole contract.

What should I do if the master forgot to sign me off in my CDC?

Contact the company or manager of that ship promptly and ask for the entry to be completed, or for whatever formal sign-off evidence your administration accepts. Do not try to fill in or alter the entry yourself, because that can make the book invalid.

Can the company keep my seaman's book?

The book is your document, and who may hold it is governed by national law. Where your book also serves as your national Seafarer's Identity Document, ILO Convention No. 185 applies. Under that convention the document stays in your possession, except when the master holds it for safekeeping with your written consent. Companies often hold crew documents in the ship's office under their SMS, but they must be returned to you when you need them and when you sign off.

Test yourself

Which instrument requires that seafarers be given a record of their employment on board?

MLC 2006 Standard A2.1 requires a document recording employment on board. STCW A-VI/1 covers basic safety training, not service records.

According to MLC 2006, what must the record of employment NOT contain?

MLC 2006 Standard A2.1 prohibits statements about work quality or wages in the record. The other items are normal entries.

A seafarer's CDC expires two months into a planned eight-month contract. What is the most likely outcome?

Many employers and authorities require validity over the full contract. Only the issuing administration can renew the book, under its national rules.

Which statement about the seaman's book and STCW is correct?

The book is a national document. STCW (e.g. Regulation I/2 and Chapters II and III) governs certificates and their sea service requirements, and the evidence accepted is set by the administration.

Under the ILO seafarers' identity document framework, who should normally hold the seafarer's identity document?

ILO Convention No. 185 provides that the SID remains in the seafarer's possession at all times, except when held for safekeeping by the master with the seafarer's written consent.
