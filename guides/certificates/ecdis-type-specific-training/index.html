ECDIS Type-Specific Training: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

ECDIS type-specific training makes you proficient on the particular make and model of ECDIS fitted on your ship, on top of the generic ECDIS competence required by STCW. It is driven mainly by the ISM Code and STCW Regulation I/14 familiarisation duties. Whether a shore certificate, a manufacturer course or documented onboard familiarisation is accepted depends on the flag, the company SMS and often the charterer.

Generic vs type-specific: what the difference actually is

There are two layers of ECDIS training, and people mix them up all the time.

  • Generic ECDIS training teaches the principles. That covers ENC data, chart presentation, safety settings, alarms, route planning and monitoring, sensor inputs, the limits of the system and the risk of over-reliance. It is part of the STCW competence for officers in charge of a navigational watch and above. Your administration recognises it, usually through an approved course based on IMO Model Course 1.27 or through the CoC syllabus itself.
  • Type-specific training (familiarisation) teaches you to work the actual equipment on your ship. Every manufacturer lays out its menus, alarm handling, route tools, chart loading and update procedures differently. Knowing what a safety contour is does not mean you can find and set it quickly on an unfamiliar console at 0300 with a crossing vessel on the bow.

In short, generic training proves you understand ECDIS. Type-specific training proves you can operate this ECDIS. You need both before you take a watch on a ship that uses ECDIS as its primary means of navigation.

Where the requirement comes from

No single regulation says "hold a type-specific ECDIS certificate". The obligation is assembled from several instruments:

  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 sets the carriage requirement for ECDIS. It was phased in by ship type, size and build date. Once ECDIS is the primary means of navigation, the officers using it must be competent on it.
  • STCW Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring seafarers are familiarised with their specific duties and with the ship's arrangements, installations and equipment before they are assigned those duties.
  • ISM Code, Section 6 requires the company to make sure the master and new personnel are properly qualified and familiarised with their duties, and to identify and provide the training needed to support the SMS.
  • IMO guidance on good ECDIS practice (MSC.1/Circ.1503 as revised) explains how generic and type-specific training fit together. It treats type-specific familiarisation as a company responsibility under the ISM Code.

Because the duty falls on the company, the method is set by the company SMS within whatever the flag accepts. Some administrations specify what they will accept as evidence, and many charterers and vetting inspectors have their own expectations. Always check your flag's requirements and your company's SMS rather than assuming one fixed rule.

Who needs it and when

In practice, type-specific training is expected for:

  • The master
  • Chief officer, 2nd officer and 3rd officer (and 4th officer where carried) who keep a navigational watch on ECDIS
  • Any officer who plans passages on ECDIS. On most ships that is the 2nd officer.

Deck cadets are not normally required to hold it. Many companies still let cadets do the onboard familiarisation package under supervision, which is good preparation.

Timing: the training must be done before you are relied on to keep a watch with the equipment. Companies handle this in different ways:

Approach Typical use
Shore course by the manufacturer or an approved provider, before joining Common where charterers or vetting expect a certificate
Computer-based or simulator familiarisation module Often accepted by companies with a documented assessment
Structured onboard familiarisation with a checklist signed off by the master Accepted by some companies and flags, often combined with the above

Which of these is enough for your ship is decided by the SMS and the flag, not by the crewing agent's preference.

What a type-specific course typically covers

Content varies by manufacturer and provider, but a sound course or familiarisation package should leave you able to do the following on that model without hesitation:

  1. Start-up, shutdown and failure handling: switching between the main unit and the backup, and what happens to the route and alarms when a sensor drops out.
  2. Chart management: installing ENC permits, loading base and update cells, checking that updates have been applied, and viewing the update history.
  3. Display and safety settings: safety contour, safety depth, shallow and deep contours, display base, standard display and all-other-data, and day and night palettes.
  4. Route planning: building a route, setting cross-track limits, running the route check, and resolving the warnings and alarms it raises.
  5. Route monitoring: look-ahead settings, the alarm list, acknowledging alarms, and using radar overlay and AIS targets.
  6. Sensor configuration: selecting the primary and secondary position sources, checking gyro and log inputs, and spotting a sensor offset or failure.
  7. User-defined features: manual updates, mariner's notes, clearing lines and parallel index lines (where the model supports them).
  8. Logbook, playback and data recording functions.

If a course spends most of its time re-teaching generic theory and very little on the actual console, ask whether it will satisfy your company's SMS.

Validity, revalidation and software updates

STCW sets no expiry date for type-specific ECDIS familiarisation. It is not a certificate of proficiency that you revalidate every five years like some STCW courses. Still, "it never expires" is a dangerous assumption:

  • Model and software version matter. If the ship's ECDIS gets a major software upgrade, a new user interface or a different model from the same maker, your old certificate may no longer reflect the equipment. Many companies then require refresher or difference training.
  • Company and charterer policies may set their own refresh intervals or call for evidence of recent training. This is common in tanker and gas trades subject to vetting.
  • Flag requirements differ. Some administrations publish specific guidance on what they accept, and others leave it entirely to the ISM system.
  • Chart standards change. Updates to presentation and data standards can alter how the display behaves. Manufacturers usually issue software updates to keep up, and the bridge team needs to know what has changed.

Treat your type-specific certificate as valid for a specific model and software generation, subject to your company's policy. Do not treat it as a lifetime ticket.

Why crew get stopped at joining

These are the problems that most often hold officers back at the crewing office or the gangway:

  • Wrong make or model. The certificate is for one manufacturer, but the ship has another. In some cases it is the same maker but a different model or interface that the company treats as different equipment.
  • No generic ECDIS on record. Some administrations endorse a limitation on a CoC where the holder has no recognised ECDIS training, barring service on ECDIS-fitted ships. Check your CoC for any such limitation.
  • Provider not accepted. The course provider is not recognised by the manufacturer, the company or the charterer.
  • Name or document mismatch. Spelling, date of birth or passport number on the certificate does not match your CoC or passport.
  • Doubtful certificates. Companies increasingly verify with the issuing centre. A certificate that cannot be verified is treated as no certificate, and presenting a fraudulent one can end a career.
  • Last-minute ship change. You were cleared for one vessel, then reassigned to a sister ship fitted with different equipment.

Worked scenario: a 2nd officer holds a type-specific certificate for a common maker's ECDIS. Two days before joining, the agent switches him to another ship in the fleet. That ship received a different model during a refit. The company's SMS requires either the manufacturer's course or the approved CBT module plus a master's sign-off before he takes a solo watch. The fix is to complete the CBT before travelling and plan an overlap with the outgoing officer. Joining and hoping for the best is not the answer.

Oral exam and PSC: what you will be asked

Examiners, masters, vetting inspectors and port state control officers all test ECDIS the same way: show me. Be ready for these:

  • "Who is responsible for your type-specific familiarisation?" The company, under the ISM Code and STCW Regulation I/14. The master verifies it on board.
  • "Set the safety contour for our current draught and show me the safety depth." Know where the setting is on this model and how you work out the value under the SMS, including squat and UKC policy.
  • "If the value you enter isn't in the ENC, what does the system use?" The next deeper contour available in the data.
  • "Show me the last chart update and the permit status." You should be able to find both quickly.
  • "What is your backup if this unit fails?" Describe the ship's approved backup arrangement and the procedure for switching over.
  • "Show me the alarm settings and explain what's switched off and why." Disabled or muted alarms with no justification are a frequent finding.
  • "Which position source is primary, and how would you know it had failed?"

PSC officers can raise deficiencies where officers cannot show proficiency on the fitted equipment. In serious cases, a lack of demonstrated proficiency on the fitted ECDIS can be treated as a detainable deficiency, at the PSC officer's discretion. A certificate on file helps, but your hands on the trackball are what gets checked.

Tips for keeping it in date

  • Ask the crewing office for the ECDIS make, model and software version of your next ship when you get your joining instructions. Do not wait until the airport.
  • Keep a list of the models you are trained on, with certificate numbers and issuing centres, alongside your CoC and other documents.
  • Use the onboard familiarisation package even if you hold a shore certificate. Make sure the master signs off your checklist, because that record is what an auditor asks for.
  • After any software update, read the release notes, run through the changes with the bridge team, and make sure any training the company requires is recorded.
  • Practise between watches: route checks, switching sensors, finding the alarm list. Speed comes from repetition.
  • Get originals verified. Keep scanned copies, and make sure the issuing centre can confirm your certificate if a company asks.
Key points
  • Generic ECDIS training is an STCW competence; type-specific training covers the exact make and model on your ship.
  • The type-specific duty rests on the company through the ISM Code Section 6 and STCW Regulation I/14.
  • Shore course, CBT or onboard familiarisation may all be accepted, depending on flag, company SMS and charterer.
  • STCW sets no expiry, but a model change or major software update can make your training out of date.
  • The commonest joining problems are a wrong-model certificate, a CoC ECDIS limitation and unverifiable certificates.
  • Inspectors test proficiency hands-on: safety settings, alarms, updates, sensors and backup arrangements.

Frequently asked questions

Is ECDIS type-specific training an STCW certificate?

Not in the same way as a CoC or certificate of proficiency. The generic ECDIS competence comes from STCW. Type-specific familiarisation is a company duty under STCW Regulation I/14 and the ISM Code. It is usually evidenced by a manufacturer or approved-provider certificate or a documented onboard familiarisation, depending on flag and SMS.

Does ECDIS type-specific training expire?

STCW sets no fixed validity period for it. However, it relates to a particular model and software generation. Companies, charterers or flags may require refresher or difference training after upgrades or at set intervals, so check your company's policy.

I have a type-specific certificate for one make. Can I sail on a ship with another make?

Not without familiarisation on the new equipment. Menus, alarm handling and chart tools differ between manufacturers, and often between models from the same maker. Your company will tell you whether a shore course, CBT or onboard sign-off is required before you keep a watch.

Can type-specific ECDIS familiarisation be done on board?

Some companies and administrations accept a structured onboard familiarisation with an assessment and the master's sign-off, often combined with a computer-based module. Others, and many charterers, expect a shore certificate. The SMS and the flag's guidance decide.

Do I need generic ECDIS training before the type-specific course?

Yes. Generic competence is the foundation, and type-specific training assumes you already understand ECDIS principles. Some administrations endorse a limitation on the CoC of officers without recognised ECDIS training, which bars service on ECDIS-fitted ships.

Test yourself

Under which instrument does the company's duty to provide type-specific ECDIS familiarisation mainly arise?

SOLAS V/19 sets the carriage requirement. The duty to ensure personnel are familiar with ship equipment comes from ISM Code Section 6 and STCW Regulation I/14.

An officer enters a safety contour value that does not exist in the ENC. What does the ECDIS use?

Under ECDIS performance standards, the system defaults to the next deeper contour in the data, which keeps the error on the safe side.

A ship's ECDIS receives a major software upgrade with a new user interface. What is the correct view of the officers' existing type-specific training?

Type-specific familiarisation relates to the actual equipment fitted. Under ISM Code Section 6 and STCW Regulation I/14, the company must ensure officers are familiar with the ship's equipment and must provide any training needed, so a major upgrade may require refresher or difference training as the company and flag require.

Which document gives IMO guidance on good ECDIS practice, including training?

MSC.1/Circ.1503 (as revised) is IMO's ECDIS guidance for good practice and describes generic and type-specific training.

Who verifies on board that a newly joined OOW is familiar with the fitted ECDIS before a solo watch?

The company is responsible under ISM Code Section 6 and STCW Regulation I/14. The master normally verifies and signs off familiarisation on board.
