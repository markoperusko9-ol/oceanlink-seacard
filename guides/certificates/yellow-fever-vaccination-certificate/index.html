Yellow Fever Vaccination Certificate: A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

The yellow fever certificate is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP), the 'yellow card', issued under the WHO International Health Regulations (2005). It is not an STCW or MLC certificate. It becomes valid 10 days after a single dose given at a designated vaccination centre and, under the 2016 amendment, it stays valid for life. Manning agents routinely demand it, and crew are most often stopped because the card is missing, unstamped, from a non-designated clinic, or does not match the passport.

What the yellow fever certificate actually is

The yellow fever certificate is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP). Most seafarers call it the 'yellow card' or 'yellow book'. Its legal basis is the World Health Organization's International Health Regulations (2005), usually shortened to IHR:

  • Annex 6 sets the model format of the certificate and the rules for completing it.
  • Annex 7 sets the specific requirements for yellow fever vaccination.

Keep this clear in your head, because candidates often get it wrong:

  • It is not an STCW certificate. You will not find it in the STCW Convention or Code, and no training course is attached to it.
  • It is not issued under MLC 2006. The MLC medical certificate (Regulation 1.2) is a separate document from a recognised medical practitioner. It covers your fitness for duty, not your vaccination status.
  • It is a public health travel document. Port health authorities, immigration officers and airlines can check it, just like a passport or visa.

The same ICVP booklet can record other vaccinations or prophylaxis that a country requires under the IHR. Yellow fever is the one seafarers deal with most.

Who needs it, and when

Under the IHR, each country decides whether to require proof of yellow fever vaccination on arrival. Requirements generally fall into two groups:

  • Countries with risk of yellow fever transmission. These are mainly parts of sub-Saharan Africa and tropical South America. Some of them require the certificate from every arriving traveller.
  • Countries without transmission but where the mosquito vector is present. These require the certificate from travellers arriving from, or transiting through, risk areas. India is the example most Indian seafarers meet. If you arrive from a yellow fever risk country without a valid certificate, you can face quarantine on arrival.

Country lists and transit rules change. Check the current WHO country list and the destination's health authority before travel. Do not rely on what worked on your last contract.

In practice, most seafarers need the card for one of these reasons:

  1. Company or manning agent policy. Ships on worldwide trade can be fixed to West Africa or Brazil at short notice. Many companies therefore make the yellow card a standard pre-joining document for all ranks, whatever the current trading pattern.
  2. Joining or signing off in a risk country. You fly in to join a ship in Lagos, Tema or a Brazilian port, or you fly home from one.
  3. Repatriation through or into a country that checks arrivals from risk areas. India is the obvious case.
  4. Port health inspection on arrival. In some ports the authorities may check crew vaccination records during clearance.

The rule for your career is simple. If you plan to sail deep-sea, get vaccinated once, properly, and keep the card with your passport.

Getting vaccinated: step by step

  1. Find a designated yellow fever vaccination centre. Only centres designated by the national health administration can issue a valid ICVP for yellow fever. In the Philippines, the Bureau of Quarantine issues the ICVP. In India, use a centre on the health ministry's list of designated centres, which includes some port and airport health organisations. A private clinic that is not on the list can give you a vaccine but not a certificate that will be accepted.
  2. Carry your passport. Your name on the certificate should match your passport exactly.
  3. Medical screening. The clinician will ask about allergies, immune conditions, medications and, for women, pregnancy. They decide whether you can safely take the vaccine.
  4. Single dose. WHO advice is that one dose gives lifelong protection for most people.
  5. Check the certificate before you leave the counter. It must show:
    • your full name as in the passport
    • the date of vaccination
    • the vaccine name and batch details
    • the clinician's signature
    • the official stamp of the vaccination centre

The certificate is completed in English or French. Another language can be added. 6. Note the 10-day rule. For a first vaccination, the certificate becomes valid only 10 days after the vaccination date. If you are vaccinated on a Monday and fly to a requiring country the following Tuesday, you are not covered.

Fees, appointment systems and documents required vary by country and centre. Ask the centre directly.

Validity: the 2016 change and older cards

For many years the certificate was valid for 10 years. The World Health Assembly then amended IHR Annex 7 through resolution WHA67.13. From 11 July 2016, a yellow fever certificate is valid for the life of the person vaccinated.

What this means for you:

Situation Status under the IHR
Vaccinated after the amendment came into force Valid for life, from 10 days after the dose
Older card printed or stamped 'valid 10 years', now past that date Still valid for life under the amended IHR; States party should not require revaccination or a new certificate on the grounds of the old 10-year date

Practical caution:

  • A few officials, agents or company checklists may still be working from old rules. If someone questions an old card, politely point to the IHR amendment and the WHO country list, which states the lifetime validity.
  • Some administrations, companies or medical advisers may recommend a booster for particular groups on medical grounds. That is a clinical decision, not a certificate expiry.

There is no 'revalidation' in the STCW sense. You do not renew a yellow card. You only need a new one if the original is lost, damaged, or was never valid in the first place.

Medical exemptions

Some people should not receive the vaccine for medical reasons. Examples include certain immune conditions or treatments, and some allergies. The decision belongs to the clinician, not the manning agent.

Where vaccination is contraindicated, IHR Annex 6 requires the clinician to give written reasons for the opinion, in English or French (and, where appropriate, another language), which the competent authorities on arrival should take into account. Understand what this written statement does and does not do:

  • The receiving country takes it into account, but it can still apply health measures on arrival.
  • It does not guarantee that every company, charterer or port will accept you for a contract that trades to risk areas.
  • Medical fitness standards for seafarers are set under each flag state's or administration's rules. How a contraindication affects your employability can vary.

If you hold a waiver, tell your manning agent early, carry the original with your passport, and expect questions.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Common reasons crew are stopped include:

  • No card at all. It was left at home, or the seafarer assumed it was not needed for this vessel.
  • No official stamp. The signature is there, but the centre stamp is missing or illegible.
  • Non-designated issuer. A general clinic or hospital card that is not an ICVP from a designated centre.
  • Name mismatch. Spelling differs from the passport, the name order is reversed, a middle name is missing, or the name was changed after marriage with no supporting link.
  • Corrections and overwriting. Handwritten alterations without a stamp or initials make officials suspicious.
  • Vaccinated too recently. Less than 10 days before arrival in a country that requires the card.
  • Fake or bought certificates. Some agents sell 'yellow cards' without a vaccination. Port health and airline staff do spot them. A forged health document can end a career and can be a criminal matter. It also leaves you unprotected against a disease that kills.
  • Damaged card. Water-soaked or torn so the key entries cannot be read.
  • An old card queried as 'expired'. Covered above. Know the lifetime-validity rule so you can explain it calmly.

Worked scenarios: questions from the crewing office and port health

Scenario 1. You are an Indian 3rd Officer signing off in Brazil, flying home via a Gulf hub. Your card is from 2012 and says 'valid 10 years'. Answer: Under the amended IHR Annex 7 your certificate is valid for life. Carry it in your hand luggage. India checks arrivals from risk areas, so expect it to be checked. If an officer queries the date, refer to the lifetime-validity rule.

Scenario 2. A Filipino AB gets vaccinated on the 1st and flies to join a ship in a West African port on the 8th. Answer: This is a problem. A first certificate is valid only from 10 days after vaccination, so on the 8th it is not yet valid. Tell the agent. The joining date or route may need to change.

Scenario 3. A cadet shows a card from a city clinic with a doctor's signature but no centre stamp. Answer: This is likely to be rejected. Get vaccinated or certified at a designated centre before joining.

Scenario 4 (oral-style). 'Is the yellow fever certificate an STCW requirement?' Answer: No. It is an ICVP under the WHO International Health Regulations (2005), Annexes 6 and 7. Countries require it for entry. Companies often require it as policy.

Keeping it safe and in order

  • Keep it with your passport and CDC. It is a travel document. Do not pack it in checked baggage.
  • Scan and photograph it. Keep a copy on your phone and in email. A copy will not replace the original at a border, but it helps a centre trace your record.
  • Check the name the day you get it. Fixing a mismatch years later is harder.
  • If you change your passport name, carry evidence that links the two names. Ask the issuing authority how it handles amendments.
  • If you lose it, contact the issuing centre first. Whether it can reissue from its records, or you need a new vaccination, depends on the national authority and the centre.
  • Before every contract, check the trading area and your route home against current WHO and destination requirements. Your card may be fine; your transit plan may not be.
Key points
  • The yellow card is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP) under WHO IHR (2005), not an STCW or MLC certificate.
  • A first certificate becomes valid 10 days after the single vaccination dose.
  • Since 11 July 2016 the certificate is valid for life, including older cards that showed a 10-year limit.
  • It must be issued by a designated vaccination centre, signed by the clinician and carry the centre's official stamp.
  • Entry rules differ by country, and many companies require the card for all crew regardless of trading area.
  • Name mismatches, missing stamps, non-designated issuers and fake cards are the usual reasons crew are stopped.
  • Medical waivers exist under IHR (2005) Annex 6, but a receiving country or company may still apply measures.

Frequently asked questions

Does my yellow fever certificate expire after 10 years?

No. Following the amendment to IHR Annex 7, yellow fever certificates have been valid for the life of the person vaccinated since 11 July 2016. This includes older cards that were printed with a 10-year validity. Countries should not demand revaccination on the grounds of the old date.

Can I join a ship the day after getting vaccinated?

For a first vaccination, the certificate is valid only from 10 days after the vaccination date. You can travel earlier if neither your route nor the joining port requires the card. If any country on the route does require it, you are not covered until day 10. Plan vaccination well ahead of any contract that may touch risk areas.

Where can Indian and Filipino seafarers get the yellow fever certificate?

Only at centres designated by the national health authority. In the Philippines, the ICVP is issued by the Bureau of Quarantine. In India, use a centre on the health ministry's list of designated yellow fever vaccination centres. Check current locations and procedures directly with the authority.

Is the yellow fever certificate required by STCW or MLC 2006?

No. It comes from the WHO International Health Regulations (2005), Annexes 6 and 7. Countries apply it at their borders, and companies and manning agents often make it a standard joining document. Your MLC medical certificate is a separate document.

What happens if I arrive in India from a yellow fever country without the certificate?

You can be placed in quarantine on arrival under India's health rules. This is why crew signing off in West Africa or South America must carry the original card in hand luggage. Check the current requirements before you travel, because transit rules and country lists change.

Test yourself

Which instrument provides the legal basis for the yellow fever vaccination certificate?

The ICVP and the yellow fever requirements are set by IHR (2005), Annex 6 (certificate format) and Annex 7 (yellow fever). STCW and MLC deal with medical fitness, not vaccination certificates.

When does a certificate for a first yellow fever vaccination become valid?

Under IHR (2005) Annex 7, a primary vaccination certificate takes effect 10 days after vaccination.

A seafarer holds a properly issued 2010 card marked 'valid 10 years'. What is its status today under the IHR?

Resolution WHA67.13 amended IHR Annex 7 so that, from 11 July 2016, certificates are valid for life, including existing ones.

Which defect is a common reason for a yellow card to be rejected at joining?

IHR (2005) Annex 6 requires the clinician's signature and the official stamp of the administering centre. English is an accepted language, and the issuing country does not need to match the passport.

What does a medical waiver for yellow fever vaccination do?

IHR (2005) Annex 6 requires the clinician to give written reasons, which authorities on arrival should take into account, but this does not guarantee entry without further measures.
