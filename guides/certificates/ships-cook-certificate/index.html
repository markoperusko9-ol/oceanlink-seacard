Ship's Cook Certificate: What It Is, Who Needs It and How to Keep It Valid

Quick answer

Any seafarer engaged as a ship's cook with responsibility for food preparation must be trained and qualified under MLC 2006 Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2, normally through a course approved or recognised by the competent authority. It is a Maritime Labour Convention requirement, not an STCW one. Validity, revalidation and recognition of foreign certificates are set by the flag administration and your national authority, so check with both before signing on.

What the ship's cook certificate is, and where it comes from

The ship's cook certificate is your proof that you are trained and qualified to run a ship's galley. Many people assume it is an STCW certificate. It is not. The requirement comes from the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 (MLC 2006), Title 3, Regulation 3.2 (Food and catering) and its mandatory Standard A3.2.

The main points of Standard A3.2 are:

  • Seafarers engaged as ship's cooks with responsibility for food preparation must be trained and qualified for the position.
  • That training includes completing a course approved or recognised by the competent authority. The course covers practical cookery, food and personal hygiene, food storage, stock control, environmental protection, and catering health and safety.
  • Nobody under 18 may be employed, engaged or work as a ship's cook.

Before MLC 2006, cook certification fell under the ILO Certification of Ship's Cooks Convention, 1946 (No. 69). MLC 2006 consolidated that convention and replaced it for ratifying states. The non-mandatory Guideline B3.2.2 gives further guidance on examination and certification of cooks, and on states recognising certificates issued by other countries.

Because this is an MLC requirement, it is checked under the ship's Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance (DMLC). Port State Control officers can also inspect it.

Who needs it, and the two exceptions

Who needs it: anyone signed on as the ship's cook with responsibility for food preparation. On most deep-sea ships that means the Chief Cook. Whether a second cook or cook-steward must also hold a full certificate depends on the flag's rules, the company SMS and the manning arrangement. Ask the manning office in writing rather than assuming.

Standard A3.2 allows two exceptions.

1. Small crews. A ship may operate with a prescribed manning of fewer than ten, and its size of crew or trading pattern may mean the competent authority does not require a fully qualified cook. In that case, anyone processing food in the galley must still be trained or instructed in:

  • food and personal hygiene
  • handling and storage of food on board

2. Dispensation in exceptional necessity. The competent authority may issue a dispensation allowing a cook who is not fully qualified to serve on a specified ship for a limited period. That period runs either until the next convenient port of call or for no more than one month. The person must still be trained or instructed in food and personal hygiene and in handling and storage of food.

A dispensation is issued by the administration, not by the master or the manning agent. It is a stop-gap and cannot replace the certificate.

Remember the basics too. A certified cook is still a seafarer, so you also need everything every other crew member needs:

  • STCW basic training (STCW Code Section A-VI/1)
  • security awareness training
  • a valid medical fitness certificate
  • any flag or company-specific documents

What the course and assessment cover

MLC 2006 lists the subject areas but leaves course length, structure and examination to each administration. Course duration, the practical cooking test and any sea-service requirement therefore differ between countries and between approved institutes. Do not trust anyone who quotes you a single "international" syllabus length.

Expect training built around the subjects named in Standard A3.2:

Area What it means in the galley
Practical cookery Menu planning, balanced and varied meals, cooking for a multinational crew
Food and personal hygiene Hand washing, illness reporting, cross-contamination control, clean uniform
Food storage Correct temperatures for provision rooms, chillers and freezers, and first-in-first-out stock rotation
Stock control Ordering, consumption records, avoiding waste and shortages on long passages
Environmental protection Garbage segregation and handling of food waste in line with MARPOL Annex V and the ship's garbage management plan
Catering health and safety Galley fire risks (deep-fat fryers, hoods and ducts), burns, cuts, slips, safe use of equipment in a seaway

Regulation 3.2 requires that food is provided free of charge. Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2 also require food of suitable quality, quantity and variety, taking into account religious requirements and cultural practices. On mixed Indian and Filipino crews, that is part of the job, not an extra.

Where to check approval: if you trained in India, confirm the institute and course are approved by the Directorate General of Shipping. In the Philippines, confirm with MARINA and the relevant national training authority. Some companies and flags also ask for a separate food hygiene certificate, so check your contract requirements.

Validity and revalidation: it depends on the administration

MLC 2006 sets no expiry period for the ship's cook certificate. There is no MLC or STCW five-year rule for cooks.

The rules depend on who issued the certificate and on the flag:

  • Issuing administration. Some national certificates have no printed expiry. Others may be time-limited or tied to refresher training. Read what is printed on your certificate and check your national authority's current rules.
  • Flag state. The flag of the ship you join decides whether it accepts your national certificate as it is, or wants some form of recognition or endorsement first. Guideline B3.2.2 encourages states to recognise each other's cook certificates, but recognition is not automatic everywhere.
  • Company SMS and charterers. Some companies require periodic food safety refreshers or in-house galley assessments on top of the statutory minimum.

Your certificate does not "stop counting" because you were ashore for a while. But your other documents do expire, and an expired medical, passport or STCW basic training certificate will stop you joining just as surely.

Common reasons cooks get stopped at joining

Most refusals at the manning office, the gangway or a PSC inspection come down to a handful of problems:

  1. Certificate not recognised by the flag. You hold a national certificate, but the flag requires recognition that was never arranged.
  2. Institute or course not approved. The certificate came from a training centre the administration does not recognise, or from a general hotel or culinary course that is not an approved ship's cook course.
  3. Name or date of birth mismatch. Spelling differs between the certificate, passport and seaman's book. Fix this before applying, not at the airport.
  4. Originals not available. Only a photocopy or phone photo is carried when the company or master needs to sight the original.
  5. Rank and certificate do not match. You are signed on as Chief Cook, but the document held covers a lower catering grade under the national scheme.
  6. Under 18. Not permitted as a ship's cook under Standard A3.2, regardless of training.
  7. Other documents expired. Medical fitness, STCW basic training or security training lapsed. The cook certificate is fine, but you still cannot sail.

Worked scenario: a cook joins in a foreign port. During the familiarisation check the master finds the certificate is from an institute he cannot verify, and the flag's approval list does not show it.

The master informs the company. The company may ask the flag for a dispensation, but the flag will only grant one in exceptional necessity, for that ship only, and for no more than one month or until the next convenient port.

Meanwhile a PSC officer could record a deficiency against Regulation 3.2. Whether that leads to detention depends on the officer's assessment.

The cook may be signed off and replaced. Repatriation costs are normally the shipowner's responsibility under MLC Regulation 2.5, unless national law or the agreement finds the seafarer in serious default, so check your contract. All of this would have been caught by a two-minute verification before travel.

Oral-exam style: what a master, auditor or PSC officer may ask

Being certified is only half of it. Inspectors check that the galley actually runs to the standard. Be ready for questions like these.

  • "Which convention requires your certificate?" MLC 2006, Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2. Not STCW.
  • "How do you prevent cross-contamination?" Separate boards and utensils for raw and cooked food, raw meat stored below ready-to-eat food, hand washing between tasks.
  • "How do you check your provision rooms?" Daily temperature checks recorded in the log, stock rotation, and removal and proper disposal of spoiled stock.
  • "Who inspects the galley and stores?" Standard A3.2 requires frequent documented inspections by, or under the authority of, the master. These cover food and drinking water supplies, storage and handling spaces, and the galley and its equipment. Know where the records are kept.
  • "What do you do with food waste?" Follow the ship's garbage management plan, segregation and the record-keeping procedure. Never improvise.
  • "A crew member keeps a religious diet. What do you do?" Plan the menu to respect it. Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2 require food supplies to take into account religious requirements and cultural practices.
  • "There is a fat fire in the fryer. Action?" Isolate the heat and use the correct fixed system or extinguisher, or a fire blanket. Never use water. Raise the alarm according to the muster list.

Tips for keeping your certificate in order

  • Verify before you pay for a course. Confirm the institute is approved by your national administration (DG Shipping in India, MARINA in the Philippines). Ask whether the certificate is recognised by the flags you usually sail under.
  • Ask about flag recognition at every new contract. Different flag, different rules. Get the manning office to confirm in writing.
  • Keep originals plus certified copies. Carry originals in a waterproof folder and keep scans in secure cloud storage.
  • Track every expiry date in one list. Include medical, passport, seaman's book, STCW basic training, security training, visas and the cook certificate itself if it is time-limited. Start renewals early.
  • Make sure names match exactly across every document.
  • Keep evidence of your galley work. Sea-service testimonials and appraisals signed by masters help if an administration or employer asks for proof of experience.
  • Re-read your certificate's conditions when national rules change. Administrations do update their schemes.
Key points
  • The ship's cook certificate is required by MLC 2006 Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2, not by STCW.
  • Training must be through a course approved or recognised by the competent authority, covering cookery, hygiene, storage, stock control, environmental protection and galley safety.
  • No one under 18 may work as a ship's cook.
  • Ships with prescribed manning under ten may be exempted from carrying a fully qualified cook, but galley staff must still be trained in hygiene and food handling.
  • A dispensation is issued only by the administration, in exceptional necessity, for a specified ship and no longer than one month or until the next convenient port.
  • MLC sets no expiry; validity, refreshers and recognition depend on the issuing administration and the flag.
  • Cooks still need STCW basic training, security training and a valid medical certificate to join.

Frequently asked questions

Is the ship's cook certificate an STCW certificate?

No. It is required under the Maritime Labour Convention 2006, Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2. STCW does not cover cook qualifications. As a seafarer, though, a cook still needs STCW basic training like everyone else on board.

Does the ship's cook certificate expire?

MLC 2006 does not set an expiry period. Some national certificates have no printed expiry, while others may be time-limited or tied to refresher training. Check the certificate itself and your issuing administration's current rules, and ask the flag of your next ship whether it needs any recognition.

Can I sail as cook without the certificate?

Only in limited cases. On ships with prescribed manning of fewer than ten, the authority may not require a fully qualified cook, but galley staff must be trained in hygiene and food handling. Otherwise, the administration can issue a dispensation only in exceptional necessity, for a specified ship and no longer than one month or until the next convenient port.

Will a hotel or culinary diploma be accepted instead?

Not automatically. The requirement is for a course approved or recognised by the competent authority that covers the subjects listed in Standard A3.2. Check with your national administration and the flag before relying on a shore-side catering qualification.

What is the minimum age to work as a ship's cook?

18. Standard A3.2 states that no seafarer under 18 may be employed, engaged or work as a ship's cook, regardless of training or certificates held.

Test yourself

Which instrument contains the mandatory requirement for ship's cooks to be trained and qualified?

Cook qualification is a food and catering requirement under MLC 2006 Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2. STCW VI/1 covers basic safety training, not cooks.

What is the minimum age for a seafarer working as a ship's cook?

MLC 2006 Standard A3.2 prohibits anyone under 18 from being employed, engaged or working as a ship's cook.

A dispensation for a cook who is not fully qualified can last for how long at most?

Under MLC 2006 Standard A3.2, a dispensation is for a specified ship and limited to the next convenient port of call or a period not exceeding one month.

On a ship with prescribed manning of fewer than ten where the authority does not require a fully qualified cook, what applies to anyone preparing food?

MLC 2006 Standard A3.2 still requires training or instruction in hygiene and in the handling and storage of food on these smaller ships.

Who determines the validity period and any refresher requirement for a ship's cook certificate?

MLC 2006 Regulation 3.2 sets no expiry, so validity and revalidation are matters for the issuing administration. Acceptance on a particular ship depends on the flag.
