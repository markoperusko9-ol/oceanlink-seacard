Radar Navigation and ARPA: The Deck Officer's Certificate Guide

Quick answer

Radar Navigation and ARPA training is the simulator-based course that proves a deck officer can use radar and target tracking to navigate safely and avoid collision. It underpins the competences in STCW Section A-II/1 (operational level) and A-II/2 (management level), and you need it before your administration will issue a CoC. Whether the course certificate itself expires depends on your administration and company, not STCW directly.

What the Radar Navigation and ARPA course is

This is the course that teaches you to use radar and automatic target tracking for navigation and collision avoidance. It also covers how these tools fail and mislead you. It is taught on an approved radar simulator, with classroom theory alongside.

The name varies. You will see:

  • Radar Navigation, Radar Plotting and Use of ARPA, at operational level, for the OOW / 3rd and 2nd Officer
  • Radar Navigation at Management Level, for the Chief Mate and Master
  • Local names. Indian cadets usually hear the operational course called RANSCO, and other administrations have their own titles.

IMO has published model courses for both levels: Model Course 1.07 for operational level and 1.08 for management level. These are guidance for training institutes, not law. The actual course content, duration and assessment are approved by your national administration, so do not assume a course from one country is automatically accepted by another.

On newer equipment you will see "TT" (target tracking) rather than "ARPA". This reflects the current IMO performance standards for radar. The skills are the same: acquiring targets, reading CPA/TCPA, using trial manoeuvre and understanding the errors.

Where the requirement comes from

There is no stand-alone "radar certificate" regulation in the way there is for, say, Basic Safety Training. The requirement sits inside the deck CoC standards:

  • STCW Regulation II/1 and Section A-II/1. The OOW competence table includes using radar and ARPA to maintain safety of navigation. That covers radar principles, plotting, target tracking, its limitations, and applying the COLREGs.
  • STCW Regulation II/2 and Section A-II/2. At management level, you are expected to use information from navigation equipment, radar included, to support command decisions.
  • STCW Regulation I/12 and Section A-I/12. These set performance standards for simulators used in mandatory simulator-based training, with specific provisions for radar and ARPA simulation. Section B-I/12 gives further guidance.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19. This sets which ships must carry radar and automatic plotting or tracking aids, depending on size. It is the reason the skill matters on almost every ship you will join.

Because the competence is mandatory for the CoC, administrations require proof of approved radar/ARPA training before they will issue the CoC. Many also want it before you sit the oral exam; check your administration's requirements.

Who needs it: operational vs management level

Level Who Typical point you need it
Operational Deck cadets moving up to OOW; 3rd and 2nd Officers Before the OOW CoC exam or issue (STCW II/1)
Management 2nd Officers preparing for Chief Mate / Master Before the Chief Mate CoC exam or issue (STCW II/2)

Engine officers, ETOs and ratings do not need it. Ratings forming part of a navigational watch keep a look-out by sight and hearing and report what they see and hear, but they are not trained in radar/ARPA and this course does not apply to them.

Some administrations treat the management-level course as building on the operational one. Others combine elements differently. Check your administration's current requirements for the CoC you are applying for, because the order and prerequisites differ between countries.

What the course covers, with a worked plot

Expect roughly these blocks, with the exact syllabus set by your approved institute:

  • Radar theory. Pulse length, beamwidth, X-band vs S-band, and range and bearing discrimination.
  • Setting up. Gain, sea and rain clutter, tuning, and performance monitoring.
  • False and missed echoes. Shadow sectors, side lobes, multiple and indirect echoes, and small targets in clutter.
  • Plotting. The relative motion triangle, relative and true vectors, CPA/TCPA, and target course and speed.
  • ARPA/TT. Acquisition, tracking, vector modes, trial manoeuvre, target swap, and the errors that come from wrong gyro or speed log input.
  • Ground vs sea stabilisation. When to use each, and why a ground-stabilised display misleads you for collision avoidance in a tidal stream.
  • COLREG application. Rules 6, 7, 8 and 19 in simulator exercises, often in restricted visibility.

Worked scenario. Own ship is steering 000° at 12 knots in fog.

  1. At 1200 you have a target bearing 045°, range 8.0 nm.
  2. At 1206 the target is still bearing 045°, range 6.8 nm.
  3. The bearing is steady and the range is closing, so the target's relative track runs straight down the bearing line towards you. CPA is effectively zero, and risk of collision exists (Rule 7(d)).
  4. Relative speed is 1.2 nm in 6 minutes, which is 12 knots. TCPA is 6.8 ÷ 12 hours, about 34 minutes, so roughly 1240.
  5. The target is forward of your beam, on the starboard bow. Rule 19(d) says to avoid altering to port. Make a substantial alteration to starboard, reduce speed if needed, and keep plotting to confirm the new CPA.

Examiners want to see you work this both ways: by manual plot and with ARPA. Then they want you to explain which ARPA outputs could mislead you. For example, if speed input is from GPS (over the ground) in a tidal stream, the target's true vector shows course and speed over the ground rather than her heading through the water, so her aspect can be wrong. Relative-motion CPA/TCPA are largely unaffected by own-ship speed input errors.

Validity and revalidation

This is where seafarers get confused, so be precise:

  • STCW does not give the radar/ARPA course certificate its own fixed validity period. The training feeds into your CoC.
  • Your CoC must be revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years (STCW Regulation I/11). Each administration decides what evidence it wants for revalidation, such as sea service or a refresher.
  • Some administrations and many companies set their own rules. For example, they may ask for a refresher if you have been ashore for a long time, or require a management-level course before endorsing a higher CoC.
  • Charterer and oil major vetting may look at simulator and navigation training records as part of officer matrix checks. Requirements vary by charterer.

So the honest answer to "does my radar certificate expire?" is: check the certificate itself, your administration's current notices, and your company's training matrix. If any of them sets a date, that date applies to you.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Common problems seen at manning offices, during document checks and at flag endorsement:

  • Wrong level for the rank. An officer is joining as Chief Officer with only the operational-level course on file, when the administration or company requires management level.
  • Institute or course not approved by the administration that issued the CoC. The certificate is then not accepted for a flag state endorsement application.
  • Name or date-of-birth mismatch between the course certificate, passport and CDC. A single spelling difference can hold up verification.
  • Certificate not verifiable on the administration's or institute's online database, or a scanned copy that is unclear.
  • Confusing ECDIS with radar. ECDIS generic training does not replace radar/ARPA training, and the reverse is also true.
  • Company refresher out of date where the SMS or training matrix requires periodic refreshers.

Port State Control normally checks your CoC and endorsements, not the course certificate. But under STCW Regulation I/4 an inspector with clear grounds, for example after a collision or grounding, or where the ship has been navigated in an erratic or unsafe manner, can assess whether watchkeepers can actually do the job. Being able to use the radar properly is part of that.

Oral exam: what examiners ask

Questions that come up again and again in deck orals:

  • "What does Rule 7 say about radar?" You must use radar properly, including long-range scanning and systematic observation. Do not make assumptions on scanty information, especially scanty radar information.
  • "You detect a vessel by radar alone, forward of your beam. What do you avoid?" Altering to port, unless she is being overtaken.
  • "Sea or ground stabilised for collision avoidance?" Sea stabilised. It shows the target's heading through the water, so it shows her aspect.
  • "Name three ARPA errors." Wrong speed or heading input, target swap, and lost targets in clutter. Also the time lag before a newly acquired target's vector settles.
  • "How do you check radar performance?" Use the performance monitor, check against known targets, and check heading marker alignment and range accuracy against a fixed object.
  • "Small craft not on radar: what does Rule 6 say?" It lists radar limitations, including small vessels and ice not being detected, as factors in setting safe speed.

Answer by stating the rule in your own words, then saying what you would actually do on the bridge.

Keeping it in date and keeping sharp

  • Keep originals and clear scans of both operational and management-level certificates with your CoC documents.
  • Check verification online if your administration offers it, before you go to the manning office.
  • Read your company training matrix at each contract. Refresher rules are often there, not in STCW.
  • Plan the management-level course early in your 2nd Officer time, so it does not delay your Chief Mate exam.
  • Practise manual plotting on watch in clear weather, then compare your result with the ARPA. It is the best preparation for orals and for the night the ARPA lies to you.
  • Learn each new ship's radar during familiarisation: menus, TT settings, speed input source and alarms.
Key points
  • Radar/ARPA training is a mandatory competence for deck CoCs under STCW Sections A-II/1 and A-II/2.
  • Training must be on simulators meeting STCW Regulation I/12 and Section A-I/12 standards.
  • Operational level is for the OOW CoC; management level is for Chief Mate and Master.
  • STCW sets no fixed expiry for the course certificate; administration and company rules decide refreshers.
  • The CoC itself is revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years under STCW Regulation I/11.
  • ECDIS training and radar/ARPA training are separate and do not substitute for each other.
  • Wrong level, unapproved institutes and name mismatches are the usual joining problems.

Frequently asked questions

Is Radar Navigation and ARPA mandatory under STCW?

Yes, as part of the deck officer CoC. The OOW and management-level competence tables in STCW Sections A-II/1 and A-II/2 include radar and ARPA use. Administrations therefore require approved training before issuing the CoC.

Does the radar/ARPA certificate expire?

STCW does not give the course certificate its own validity period. Some administrations, companies or charterers set refresher requirements, so check the certificate, your administration's notices and your company training matrix.

Do I need a separate radar course for Chief Mate?

Generally yes. Administrations require management-level radar navigation training for the Chief Mate and Master CoC, building on the operational-level course. The exact prerequisites and order differ by administration.

Does ECDIS training cover radar and ARPA?

No. ECDIS generic training covers the electronic chart system, while radar/ARPA training covers radar, plotting and target tracking. Radar/ARPA training is required for the deck CoC. Without ECDIS training your CoC will normally carry a limitation preventing service on ships fitted with ECDIS, so in practice you need both.

Will another country accept my radar/ARPA certificate?

Not automatically. When you apply for a flag state endorsement, the endorsing administration looks at the training behind your CoC, and it must have been approved by the issuing administration. Check acceptance before you join a ship under a new flag.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation covers the use of simulators, including radar and ARPA simulators, in mandatory training?

Regulation I/12, with Section A-I/12, sets performance standards for simulators, including radar and ARPA simulation. I/11 covers revalidation and VI/1 covers basic safety familiarisation and training.

Under COLREG Rule 7, what must you avoid when assessing risk of collision?

Rule 7(c) warns against assumptions based on scanty information, especially scanty radar information. Rule 7 also calls for long-range scanning and radar plotting, and Rule 7(d) treats a compass bearing that does not appreciably change as indicating risk of collision.

A target's bearing stays steady while its range decreases. What does this indicate?

Rule 7(d) treats a steady compass bearing with decreasing range as risk of collision. Even an appreciable bearing change does not always rule out risk, especially with large vessels or at close range.

In restricted visibility, a vessel detected by radar alone is forward of your beam and is not being overtaken. What should you avoid?

Rule 19(d)(i) says to avoid an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken. Reducing speed and sounding fog signals are both expected in restricted visibility.

Under STCW, what is the maximum interval for revalidating a deck officer's CoC?

STCW Regulation I/11 requires CoC revalidation at intervals not exceeding five years. The evidence required for revalidation is set by each administration.
