Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations

Quick answer

Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations is the STCW certificate of proficiency, under Regulation V/1-1, that officers and ratings with cargo or cargo-equipment duties on oil or chemical tankers must hold. You qualify by holding STCW basic training and then either completing an approved course or doing three months of approved seagoing service on oil or chemical tankers. For masters and officers, STCW requires continued tanker competence to be shown at least every five years (Regulation I/11.3); beyond that, validity and revalidation practice depends on the issuing administration.

What the certificate is and where it comes from

This is the entry-level tanker qualification under the STCW Convention. It sits in Chapter V (special training requirements for personnel on certain types of ship), in Regulation V/1-1. The minimum standard of competence is set out in the STCW Code, Section A-V/1-1, paragraph 1, and Table A-V/1-1-1.

The current wording came in with the 2010 Manila amendments. Before that, the same level of training was called tanker familiarisation. You will still hear older hands, and see older certificates, using that name. If your certificate pre-dates the Manila amendments, check with your administration whether it has been reissued or converted.

The certificate covers oil tankers and chemical tankers together. It does not cover liquefied gas tankers. Gas carriers have their own basic and advanced certificates under Regulation V/1-2.

This basic certificate is also the first step towards the advanced oil tanker or advanced chemical tanker certificates. Those are needed by senior officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations.

Who needs it, and who companies insist on anyway

STCW requires the certificate for officers and ratings assigned specific duties and responsibilities related to cargo or cargo equipment on oil or chemical tankers. In practice that usually includes:

  • Deck officers who keep cargo watches or take part in loading, discharging, tank cleaning or inerting
  • Engineers who run or maintain cargo pumps, the inert gas plant or cargo-related machinery
  • Pumpmen, bosuns, ABs and OSs who connect hoses, handle manifolds, take ullages or samples, or work on deck during cargo operations
  • ETOs who maintain cargo-related electrical and control systems

The legal minimum is not the whole story. Many tanker operators require every crew member to hold the basic tanker certificate, including catering staff, because of the company's own SMS and charterer or terminal expectations. Many manning agents will not process you for a tanker without it, whatever your rank.

Never assume your role is exempt. Ask the company in writing before you sign on.

Advanced certificates build on this one. Masters, chief engineers, chief mates, second engineers and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations need the relevant advanced certificate. To get it you need:

  • the basic certificate,
  • the qualifying tanker service set out in Regulation V/1-1: three months on the relevant tanker type, or one month's approved supernumerary onboard training with at least three loading and three unloading operations, and
  • an approved advanced training course for oil or chemical tankers.

Two routes to qualify

Regulation V/1-1 gives two ways to obtain the basic certificate. Both require you to hold STCW basic training (Section A-VI/1, often called BST) first.

Route What you need
Sea service At least three months of approved seagoing service on oil or chemical tankers, meeting the standard in Section A-V/1-1 paragraph 1
Course An approved basic training course for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations, meeting the same standard

The course route is by far the most common, because you cannot easily get tanker sea service without the certificate. How the administration assesses "approved" service varies. It depends on what your discharge book, testimonials and training record show.

Worked scenario: an AB moving from bulk carriers to a product tanker

  1. Check that your BST modules are all in date. Most institutes and administrations will not enrol or certify you otherwise.
  2. Book the basic oil and chemical tanker course only at an institute approved by your administration for that course. Check the approval yourself; don't rely on the institute's say-so.
  3. Find out whether your administration issues a separate certificate of proficiency after the course, or whether the institute's certificate is the final document.
  4. If a separate certificate is needed, apply and wait for it before you join. A course-completion slip may not satisfy the company, the flag or a port State inspector.
  5. Send a scan to the manning office early so any problem surfaces ashore, not at the gangway.

What the course covers

Table A-V/1-1-1 groups the required competences roughly as follows:

  • Contribute to the safe cargo operation of oil and chemical tankers. This covers:
    • basic tanker design and types
    • cargo systems: piping, pumps, valves and venting
    • the main stages of loading, discharging, tank cleaning and inerting
  • Take precautions to prevent hazards. This covers:
    • the physical and chemical properties of oil and chemical cargoes
    • flammability and explosion hazards
    • toxicity
    • static electricity
    • ignition sources
    • reactivity
    • the use of safety data sheets
  • Apply occupational health and safety precautions. This covers:
    • gas detection and measuring instruments
    • enclosed space entry
    • protective equipment
    • permits to work
    • first aid for cargo exposure
  • Carry out fire-fighting operations. This covers tanker fire-fighting systems and the hazards of fighting cargo fires.
  • Respond to emergencies. This covers basic emergency procedures, including emergency shutdown.
  • Take precautions to prevent pollution from the release of oil or chemicals. This covers:
    • the basic MARPOL Annex I and Annex II picture
    • shipboard pollution response

IMO publishes a model course that many institutes follow. However, course length, structure and assessment are approved by each administration and are not the same everywhere.

Treat the course as a foundation, not as permission to operate cargo systems unsupervised. Your ship-specific familiarisation and the company's cargo procedures still apply in full.

Validity and revalidation – the STCW rule and where administrations differ

This is where seafarers get caught out. STCW contains a general framework for revalidation and continued competence in Regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11, and it includes a specific rule for tankers.

For masters and officers, STCW Regulation I/11 paragraph 3 requires continued professional competence for tankers to be established at intervals not exceeding five years. Section A-I/11 paragraph 3 says this is done in one of two ways:

  • at least three months in total of approved tanker sea service in the previous five years, or
  • completing the relevant approved training course.

How administrations apply this, and whether ratings' certificates carry an expiry, varies.

What you may see in practice:

  • Some administrations issue the basic certificate with a fixed validity, often five years. They revalidate it on proof of recent tanker sea service, or require a refresher or a repeat course where service is lacking.
  • For ratings, some administrations issue it without an expiry date. The company or charterer may still look for recent tanker service. For masters and officers, a certificate with no printed expiry does not remove the five-year continued-competence requirement under Regulation I/11.3.
  • Officers may find the tanker qualification needs to appear on, or be recognised in, their flag State endorsement, depending on the flag. Flag State endorsements recognising an officer's tanker qualification will generally also depend on the officer meeting the Regulation I/11.3 continued-competence requirement.

Because the rules differ, do not take another seafarer's word for your situation. Check:

  1. The expiry date, if any, printed on your certificate.
  2. If you are a master or officer, whether you have three months' tanker service in the last five years or need an approved course.
  3. Your administration's current circular or notice on revalidating tanker certificates.
  4. The flag State's requirements for recognition, if you are an officer serving under a foreign flag.
  5. The company's own policy on recent tanker experience.

Tips for keeping it in date

  • Keep sea service evidence for every tanker contract: discharge book entries, sign-off testimonials and the vessel type stated clearly.
  • Diary the expiry at least six months ahead. Processing times at administrations and institutes can be long.
  • If you have been off tankers for a while, ask early whether you need a refresher before you are offered a contract.

Why crew get stopped at joining

The common reasons, roughly in the order a Master or superintendent sees them:

  • Wrong certificate. Gas tanker basic training held for a chemical tanker, or the reverse.
  • Course slip only. The institute's completion certificate is presented where the administration issues a separate certificate of proficiency.
  • Expired certificate, or no evidence of the tanker service needed to revalidate it.
  • Unapproved institute. The course was done somewhere the administration or flag does not recognise.
  • BST prerequisite out of date. Expired basic training modules can undermine the whole document set.
  • Name or date-of-birth mismatch between the tanker certificate, passport and seaman's book.
  • No flag recognition for officers whose flag requires the tanker qualification to be recognised.
  • Copies only. Originals are not available for checking at joining or during a port State control inspection.

A document problem found at the gangway usually means you are sent home and the ship sails short-handed or late. Fixing it ashore before travel costs far less time and goodwill.

Oral exam: questions you may be asked

Masters, chief officers and CoC examiners often test basic tanker knowledge informally. Be ready for questions like these:

  • Q: What does an inert atmosphere protect against? A: It keeps the oxygen content in the cargo tanks too low for a hydrocarbon atmosphere to burn or explode. It also creates a serious asphyxiation hazard for anyone entering.
  • Q: Why is static electricity a concern during loading and tank cleaning? A: Some cargoes and operations generate static charge. A discharge in a flammable atmosphere can ignite it. That is why initial loading rates, sampling and dipping are controlled.
  • Q: Before entering a cargo tank, what must be confirmed? A: Several things:
    • a permit to work is issued under the company's enclosed space procedures
    • the tank is ventilated
    • tests show adequate oxygen and acceptable levels of flammable and toxic gases
    • communication and a standby person are in place
    • rescue equipment is ready
  • Q: Where do you find the hazards of a particular chemical cargo? A: In the safety data sheet and the cargo documentation, read together with the ship's procedures.
  • Q: Does this certificate let you serve on an LPG carrier? A: No. Gas carriers need the separate basic training under Regulation V/1-2.

Keep answers practical. Examiners want to see that you understand the hazard and the control measure, not that you can recite the table.

Key points
  • The certificate is required by STCW Regulation V/1-1, with the standard set in Section A-V/1-1 and Table A-V/1-1-1.
  • STCW requires it for officers and ratings with specific cargo or cargo-equipment duties on oil or chemical tankers.
  • You must hold STCW basic training first, then either complete an approved course or do three months of approved tanker service.
  • It covers oil and chemical tankers only; gas carriers need separate training under Regulation V/1-2.
  • Masters and officers must show continued tanker competence at least every five years (Reg. I/11.3, A-I/11.3); other validity rules depend on the administration.
  • Many companies require every crew member on a tanker to hold it, beyond the legal minimum.
  • Most joining refusals come from wrong, expired, unrecognised or mismatched documents, all of which are fixable ashore.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need basic tanker training if I am a cook or steward on a tanker?

STCW requires it for those with specific cargo or cargo-equipment duties, which normally does not include catering staff. However, many tanker companies require all crew to hold it under their own policies. Ask the company before joining.

Is basic oil and chemical tanker training valid on an LNG or LPG carrier?

No. Liquefied gas tankers come under a separate STCW regulation, V/1-2, with their own basic and advanced certificates. You need the gas tanker basic training to serve with cargo duties on a gas carrier.

How long is the basic tanker certificate valid?

For masters and officers, STCW Regulation I/11.3 requires continued professional competence for tankers at intervals not exceeding five years. This is shown by three months' tanker service in the last five years or by an approved course. For ratings, and for how the certificate itself is dated, it depends on the administration. Check your certificate and your administration's current rules.

Can I get the certificate without doing the course?

STCW allows an alternative route: at least three months of approved seagoing service on oil or chemical tankers, together with STCW basic training and meeting the required standard. In practice most seafarers take the approved course, because tanker service is hard to obtain without the certificate.

Is the old tanker familiarisation certificate still accepted?

Tanker familiarisation was the pre-2010 name for this level of training. Whether an older certificate is still accepted, or must be converted or reissued, is decided by your administration. Confirm before your next tanker contract.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the requirement for basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations?

Oil and chemical tanker training is in Regulation V/1-1. V/1-2 covers gas tankers, VI/1 covers basic safety training and II/4 covers navigational watch ratings. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-1.

Which training must a candidate already hold before obtaining the basic oil and chemical tanker certificate?

Regulation V/1-1 requires completion of basic training under Section A-VI/1 as a prerequisite. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-1; STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

A seafarer holding basic oil and chemical tanker training is offered a deck job with cargo duties on an LPG carrier. Is the certificate sufficient?

Gas carriers have separate training requirements under Regulation V/1-2. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulations V/1-1 and V/1-2.

Apart from completing an approved course, what route does STCW allow to the basic oil and chemical tanker certificate?

Regulation V/1-1 accepts at least three months of approved seagoing service on oil or chemical tankers as an alternative to the approved course. Ship-specific familiarisation is a separate requirement. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-1.

Under STCW, who must hold basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations?

The requirement applies to officers and ratings with specific cargo or cargo-equipment duties on oil or chemical tankers. Senior officers additionally need advanced training. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-1.
