Polar Code Basic Training: A Seafarer's Guide to the STCW V/4 Certificate

Quick answer

Polar Code basic training is the certificate of proficiency required under STCW regulation V/4 for masters, chief mates and officers in charge of a navigational watch on ships operating in polar waters. Whether you need it depends on the ship type and the ice conditions expected on the voyage. You must show continued competence at intervals of not more than five years, and your issuing administration sets the exact process.

What Polar Code basic training is

The International Code for Ships Operating in Polar Waters, known as the Polar Code, is mandatory through two instruments:

  • SOLAS chapter XIV makes the safety provisions mandatory. This is Part I-A of the Code. Part I-B is recommendatory guidance.
  • MARPOL (Annexes I, II, IV and V) makes the pollution prevention provisions mandatory. This is Part II-A of the Code. Part II-B is recommendatory.

Chapter 12 of the Code deals with manning and training. It points to STCW for the qualifications that deck officers must hold.

The STCW requirement sits in regulation V/4. The detailed standard of competence is in Section A-V/4 of the STCW Code. That section sets out two levels:

  • Basic training, with the standard of competence in Table A-V/4-1.
  • Advanced training, with the standard of competence in Table A-V/4-2. You must already be qualified at basic level before you can take advanced.

After you complete an approved basic course and meet the standard, your administration issues a certificate of proficiency (CoP). A course attendance slip from the training centre is not the same document. Ports, flags and companies expect to see the CoP itself.

Who needs it: the Polar Code manning table

Polar waters means the Antarctic area and the Arctic waters. Each is defined geographically in SOLAS chapter XIV. Inside those areas, the training requirement turns on two things: ice conditions and ship type.

The Code uses three ice categories:

  • Ice-free waters means no ice is present at all.
  • Open waters means sea ice concentration is below one tenth and there is no ice of land origin.
  • Other waters means anything more severe than open waters.

Ice-free Not required Not required Not required
Open waters Basic for master, chief mate, OOW Basic for master, chief mate, OOW Not required
Other waters Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOW Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOW Advanced for master and chief mate; basic for OOW

The table only states the convention minimum. In practice the bar is often set higher:

  • Company SMS, charterers and the Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM) often require basic training for all deck officers on any polar voyage, even where the Code does not.
  • Engineers and ratings are not covered by regulation V/4. However, the Polar Code requires every crew member to be familiar with the PWOM procedures and equipment relevant to their duties. Some companies also send engineers on the course.
  • Flag administrations may add their own requirements. Always check the flag's circulars for the ship you are joining.

What the course covers

Table A-V/4-1 is built around four broad competences. In plain terms, they are:

  1. Contribute to the safe operation of ships in polar waters. This covers:
    • ice terminology and ice types;
    • where different ice can be expected;
    • reading ice charts and ice information;
    • how ice and low temperature affect a ship's performance.
  2. Monitor and ensure compliance with legislative requirements. This covers:
    • the Polar Code itself;
    • the Polar Ship Certificate and PWOM;
    • other regional rules that apply in polar areas.
  3. Apply safe working practices and respond to emergencies. This covers:
    • crew performance and fatigue in the cold;
    • cold-weather PPE;
    • survival in low temperatures;
    • how emergencies such as fire, damage and abandoning ship differ when it is cold and remote;
    • limited search and rescue support.
  4. Ensure compliance with pollution prevention requirements and prevent environmental hazards. This covers:
    • the stricter discharge rules in polar waters;
    • the sensitivity of the polar environment and its wildlife.

On a basic course, expect:

  • classroom sessions;
  • ice chart exercises;
  • often a simulator element.

The course approval sets the course length, the assessment method and whether simulator time is included. These vary between countries and training centres. Only attend a course approved by an administration whose CoP your flag will accept.

Validity and revalidation

STCW regulation I/11 requires masters and officers serving on ships in polar waters to establish continued professional competence for polar operations at intervals not exceeding five years.

Section A-I/11 lists the routes to do this:

  • approved seagoing service performing duties appropriate to the certificate held, for at least two months in total during the preceding five years;
  • having performed functions considered equivalent to that seagoing service;
  • passing an approved test;
  • completing approved training.

The administration sets the practical detail. This includes:

  • how seagoing service is evidenced and whether the administration requires it to be in polar waters;
  • whether the CoP shows an expiry date at all;
  • whether a refresher course is mandatory.

Some administrations print a five-year expiry on the CoP. Others may align polar revalidation with CoC revalidation. Check your own administration's rules and current notices. Do not rely on what a shipmate's certificate from a different country says.

Worked scenarios: does the officer need it?

Scenario 1. A product tanker is fixed for a summer cargo into an Arctic port. The ice forecast shows open water along the route.

  • Answer: The master, the chief mate and every OOW need basic training. A tanker in open waters triggers the basic requirement.

Scenario 2. A bulk carrier is on the same route under the same open-water forecast.

  • Answer: The Code does not require it. Check the PWOM and company instructions, though, because many operators require basic regardless.

Scenario 3. A general cargo ship is routed through waters where the ice forecast is above open-water concentration.

  • Answer: The master and chief mate need advanced. The OOWs need basic.

Scenario 4. A 2nd officer holds basic training. Mid-contract, the ship's next fixture is into heavier ice.

  • Answer: For the 2nd officer, nothing changes, because basic is enough for an OOW in other waters. The master and chief mate must hold advanced. If they do not, the company must resolve this before the ship enters those waters.

Why crew get stopped at joining

These are the issues that hold up sign-on or bring questions from port state control:

  • Course certificate but no CoP. You finished the course, but the administration has not yet issued the certificate of proficiency.
  • Unapproved or unrecognised provider. Your administration did not approve the course, or the flag does not accept it.
  • Out of date. The certificate has passed the revalidation date set by the issuing administration.
  • Wrong level. A master or chief mate holds only basic, but the voyage plan includes ice beyond open waters.
  • Flag documentation gaps. Some flags want polar qualifications reflected in their endorsement or recognition paperwork. The officer has not applied, or the endorsement does not show it.
  • Name or document mismatch. The name, date of birth or CDC/passport details on the CoP do not match your other papers.
  • Late voyage change. The ship was not trading to polar waters when you were contracted, so nobody checked. Then the fixture changed.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like short, precise answers. Practise these:

  • Where is the requirement for polar training in STCW? Regulation V/4, with the standard of competence in Section A-V/4 of the Code.
  • Which SOLAS chapter makes the Polar Code mandatory? Chapter XIV.
  • Define open waters. Sea ice concentration below one tenth, with no ice of land origin present.
  • Does a container ship in open water need polar-trained officers? Not under the Code's table, but it may under the PWOM, company or flag requirements.
  • What document on board tells you the ship's polar category and limitations? The Polar Ship Certificate, supported by the PWOM.
  • What are the extra challenges of an emergency in polar waters? Expect follow-up questions here. Good points include:
    • hypothermia and cold-water survival times;
    • icing of life-saving appliances and deck equipment;
    • battery and equipment performance in low temperature;
    • long distances to SAR assets;
    • extended survival periods.

Keeping it in date: practical tips

  • Scan everything. Keep a scan of the CoP with your CoC and other STCW documents. Check its expiry date whenever you check the others.
  • Track it as a five-year cycle. Treat the revalidation interval as five years at most, unless your administration's notice says otherwise.
  • Ask before signing. Before you sign a contract, ask the manning agent where the ship trades. Polar voyages are often fixed at short notice.
  • Plan the next step early. If you are heading for chief mate or master on ice-trading ships, plan the advanced course. Advanced has its own sea service requirement.
  • Check recognition across administrations. If you hold certificates from more than one administration, confirm which one issued the polar CoP and that your flag accepts it.
  • Re-verify after an upgrade. After a CoC upgrade or renewal, check whether your administration needs the polar CoP re-issued or linked to the new certificate.
Key points
  • Polar Code basic training is an STCW regulation V/4 certificate of proficiency; the standard of competence is in Table A-V/4-1.
  • The Polar Code is mandatory through SOLAS chapter XIV (Part I-A, safety) and MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V (Part II-A, pollution prevention).
  • Tankers and passenger ships in open waters need basic training for the master, chief mate and OOWs.
  • In waters beyond open-water conditions, all ship types need advanced for the master and chief mate and basic for OOWs.
  • Continued competence must be shown at intervals not exceeding five years; the method is set by the administration.
  • Company SMS, PWOM and charterers often require polar training beyond the convention minimum.
  • A course attendance certificate is not the same as the CoP issued by the administration.

Frequently asked questions

Do ratings and engineers need Polar Code basic training?

STCW regulation V/4 applies to masters, chief mates and officers in charge of a navigational watch, not to engineers or ratings. All crew must still be familiar with the PWOM procedures and equipment relevant to their duties. Some companies ask engineers to take the course anyway. Individual flags may also set additional requirements, so check the flag's circulars.

Is Polar Code basic training required on every voyage to the Arctic or Antarctic?

No. Under the Polar Code manning table, it is not required in ice-free waters. In open waters it is required only on tankers and passenger ships. In more severe ice it is required for all ship types, and the master and chief mate must hold advanced.

How long is the Polar Code basic certificate valid?

STCW requires continued competence for polar service to be shown at intervals not exceeding five years. Whether your CoP carries a printed expiry date depends on the issuing administration. The exact revalidation process also depends on the administration, so check its current notices.

Can I do the advanced course without basic training?

No. Under STCW Section A-V/4, a candidate for advanced training must already meet the basic requirements. Advanced also has its own seagoing service requirement and an approved advanced course.

Will a polar certificate from one country be accepted by another flag?

Not automatically. Acceptance depends on the flag administration and how it treats certificates of proficiency issued by other STCW parties. Confirm with the flag or your manning agent before joining.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the training requirement for officers on ships in polar waters?

Regulation V/4 covers masters and deck officers on ships operating in polar waters, with competence standards in Section A-V/4.

A bulk carrier will operate in Arctic open waters. Under the Polar Code manning table, what training must the OOWs hold?

Polar Code Part I-A chapter 12 requires basic training in open waters only for tankers and passenger ships, although company or flag rules may go further.

Which SOLAS chapter makes the safety part of the Polar Code mandatory?

SOLAS chapter XIV gives mandatory effect to Part I-A (safety measures) of the Polar Code.

A general cargo ship will enter waters with ice beyond open-water conditions. What must the chief mate hold?

In other waters, the Polar Code chapter 12 table requires advanced training for the master and chief mate, and basic for OOWs.

At what maximum interval must officers serving in polar waters establish continued professional competence for polar operations?

STCW regulation I/11 and Section A-I/11 require polar continued competence to be established at intervals not exceeding five years.
