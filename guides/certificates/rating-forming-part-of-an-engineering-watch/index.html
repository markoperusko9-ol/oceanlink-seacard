Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch: The STCW III/4 Certificate Explained

Quick answer

The Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate is the STCW Regulation III/4 qualification. An engine rating needs it to keep a watch in a manned engine room, or to do duties in a periodically unmanned one, on a seagoing ship of 750 kW propulsion power or more. You qualify through approved seagoing service, or approved special training plus sea time, and an assessment against Section A-III/4 of the STCW Code. STCW does not set a five-year revalidation for it, but your administration may set its own validity rules, and your basic training refreshers and medical must stay in date.

What the certificate is and where it comes from

The certificate comes from STCW Regulation III/4. It covers ratings who form part of a watch in a manned engine room or who are designated to perform duties in a periodically unmanned engine room. The standard of competence is in Section A-III/4 of the STCW Code. The function is marine engineering at the support level.

In plain terms, it proves you can be trusted as the second pair of eyes and hands on an engine-room watch. You make rounds, take readings, report abnormalities, understand and act on the engineer's orders, and respond correctly in an emergency.

MLC 2006, Regulation 1.3, adds a general labour-law principle. Seafarers must not work on board unless they are trained, or certified as competent or otherwise qualified, for their duties. STCW is where the actual watchkeeping standard is set.

The document has different names depending on who issues it. Some administrations call it a Certificate of Proficiency. Others call it a watchkeeping certificate or a rating's certificate. What matters is that an STCW Party issued or recognised it under Regulation III/4.

Who needs it and who does not

Regulation III/4 applies to ratings on seagoing ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 750 kW propulsion power or more who either:

  • form part of the watch in a manned engine room, or
  • are designated to perform duties in a periodically unmanned engine room (UMS rounds, duty rating on call, and so on).

The regulation itself makes two exceptions:

  • Ratings under training, who work under supervision while building their sea time.
  • Ratings whose duties are of an unskilled nature.

In practice, an oiler or motorman on watch needs it. A fitter who takes a watch or UMS duty needs it too. A wiper doing cleaning and general labour usually does not. However, the moment the company puts a wiper on the watch bill as the watch rating, the requirement applies.

Your Safe Manning Document and the company's watch arrangements decide which positions must be filled by certified ratings. If you are unsure, ask the Chief Engineer what your designated duties are, not just what your rank is called.

Entry requirements and how you qualify

Under Regulation III/4, a candidate must:

  1. Be not less than 16 years of age.
  2. Have completed one of the following:
    • approved seagoing service, including not less than six months of training and experience, or
    • special training, either pre-sea or on board, including an approved period of seagoing service of not less than two months.
  3. Meet the standard of competence in Section A-III/4 of the STCW Code.

The administration decides what counts as "approved" service and training, including which courses, which record books and which ship types. Many ratings come through an approved pre-sea engine rating course followed by documented sea time. Others build up the longer service route on board.

You will also need the usual STCW and MLC baseline documents:

  • Basic training under Regulation VI/1 (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR)
  • Security awareness training
  • A valid medical fitness certificate

What you must submit depends on the administration. It usually includes:

  • a completed training record book
  • sea service testimonials signed by the Master or Chief Engineer
  • discharge book entries
  • course certificates

Check the current list with your administration or its approved training institute before you apply. Requirements and forms change, and an incomplete file is a frequent cause of delay.

What the competence covers

Section A-III/4 sets out what a watch rating must be able to do. In summary:

  • Carry out a watch routine. You must know the terms used in machinery spaces and the names of machinery and equipment. You must follow engine-room watchkeeping procedures and keep safe working practices. This also covers basic environmental protection procedures, using the internal communication systems, and knowing the engine-room alarm systems well enough to tell them apart. The fire-extinguishing media alarm is especially important.
  • Understand orders and be understood in matters relevant to your watch duties.
  • For keeping a boiler watch, maintain correct water levels and steam pressures. This covers safe operation of the boiler, checking gauge glass levels, and spotting abnormal conditions.
  • Operate emergency equipment and apply emergency procedures. You must know your emergency duties, the alarm signals, the escape routes from machinery spaces, and the location and use of firefighting equipment.

On the job, your rounds are your main product:

  • reading pressures and temperatures and comparing them with normal values
  • checking levels in sumps, tanks and the boiler gauge glass
  • looking for leaks, unusual noise, vibration or smell
  • reporting clearly to the engineer of the watch

The principles for the engineering watch as a whole are in Section A-VIII/2. The officer in charge carries the responsibility, but you are expected to support that watch properly.

Oral and practical assessment: what examiners ask

How competence is demonstrated varies. It may be:

  • an onboard assessment recorded in a training record book
  • a practical or oral check at an approved institute
  • an examination by the administration

Whatever the format, a rating should be able to answer these kinds of questions confidently:

  • "Walk me through your rounds." Give your actual route, what you check at each machine, and what normal values look like on this ship.
  • "The boiler gauge glass shows no water. What do you do?" Do not top up blindly. Report immediately to the engineer of the watch and follow their orders and the boiler instructions. A missing level may mean dangerously low water, and the response is an engineer's decision.
  • "How do you recognise the CO2 or fixed-system release alarm, and what do you do?" Leave the space immediately by the nearest escape route. Never wait to see what is happening.
  • "Show me the emergency escape trunk from the bottom platform." You should be able to show this on any ship you serve on.
  • "You find a fuel oil leak spraying near a hot surface." Raise the alarm and report to the engineer of the watch. Do not attempt anything you have not been ordered to do or trained for.
  • "How do you call the duty engineer during UMS?" Know the call system and the dead-man alarm, if fitted. Also know the rule that you do not enter the machinery space alone in UMS without informing the bridge, as your SMS requires.
  • "What is the engine-room bilge alarm telling you?" Explain the possible causes and what you must not do, such as pumping overboard without authority. MARPOL Annex I applies.

Worked scenario. During a night UMS round you hear a high-pitched noise from a generator. The LO pressure reading is lower than on your last round.

  1. Do not start shutting things down on your own unless you have been trained and instructed to. If the situation becomes dangerous, get to a safe position or leave the space and report.
  2. Call the duty engineer through the agreed system and give a clear report: which generator, what reading, what change, what noise.
  3. Stay at a safe position and follow instructions.
  4. Log what you reported and when.

This sequence is exactly what Section A-III/4 is testing: observe, report clearly, act on orders, and record.

Validity and revalidation

This is where many ratings get confused.

  • STCW Regulation I/11 sets the five-year revalidation rule for masters, officers and radio operators. It does not impose the same rule on a Regulation III/4 rating's certificate.
  • Your administration may still set its own rules. Some issue the rating's certificate without an expiry date. Others print an expiry date, link it to other documents, or require re-issue in a new format after national rule changes. Read what is printed on your certificate and check the issuing authority's current guidance. Do not rely on what someone else's certificate says.
  • The documents around it do expire. Under Regulation VI/1 and Section A-VI/1, seafarers must show evidence of maintaining their basic training competence every five years. In particular, that means refreshers for PST and FPFF. Your medical certificate also has a fixed validity.

In practice, a "no expiry" watch certificate is worthless at joining if your basic training refresher or medical has lapsed.

Why ratings get stopped at joining

These are the usual problems found at the crewing office, at the gangway or during a Port State Control check under Regulation I/4:

Problem What happens
Holding only the pre-sea course certificate, not the III/4 certificate itself Not qualified for a watch position; may only join as a trainee or in an unskilled role
Basic training refreshers out of date Cannot sign on in most companies; a PSC deficiency if found on board
Medical expired or expiring during the contract Often refused at the agency, or the contract is shortened
Name or date-of-birth mismatch between certificate, CDC and passport Document held for verification; you may miss the ship
Certificate cannot be verified online with the issuing administration Company or flag refuses it until confirmed
Crew list shows "Oiler" or "Motorman" but the documents only support "Wiper" Safe manning and PSC problem; you may be re-rated or signed off
Flag-specific documentation for ratings not arranged Depends on the flag; some flags have their own requirements, so ask the company early

A certified watch rating is often part of meeting the minimum safe manning. A missing or invalid certificate is therefore the ship's problem as well as yours, and companies treat it seriously.

Tips for keeping it in date and moving up

  • Make a single expiry list covering your basic training refreshers, medical, passport, CDC and visas. Set reminders at least six months ahead, because course seats and medical appointments fill up.
  • Carry originals plus clear copies of your certificates, and keep scans in a safe place.
  • Get every sea service testimonial signed and stamped before you sign off. Missing service records slow down every future application.
  • Use your administration's online verification to confirm your certificate shows correctly before you go to the agency.
  • Plan your next step. Able Seafarer Engine under Regulation III/5 requires you to meet the III/4 requirements first. You then need engine department sea service while qualified as a watch rating: generally 12 months, or six months plus approved training. Your III/4 service and records are the foundation for it.
  • Keep learning the ship you are on. On each new vessel, learn the escape routes, alarms and fixed-system release points during your familiarisation. That knowledge keeps you and the watch safe.
Key points
  • The certificate is required by STCW Regulation III/4, with the competence standard in Section A-III/4 of the STCW Code.
  • It applies to engine-room watch ratings and UMS duty ratings on seagoing ships of 750 kW propulsion power or more.
  • Ratings under training and ratings doing purely unskilled duties are exempt.
  • Entry requires a minimum age of 16 and either six months' approved service or special training plus at least two months' approved sea service.
  • STCW sets no five-year revalidation for it, but your administration's own validity rules apply.
  • Basic training refreshers and your medical do expire, and letting them lapse is a frequent reason ratings are stopped at joining.
  • It is the stepping stone to Able Seafarer Engine under Regulation III/5.

Frequently asked questions

Does a wiper need the Rating Forming Part of an Engineering Watch certificate?

Not if the wiper's duties are genuinely unskilled, such as cleaning and general labour. Regulation III/4 exempts ratings whose duties are of an unskilled nature, and also ratings under training. If the wiper is placed on the watch bill or given UMS duties, the requirement applies.

Does the engine watch rating certificate expire after five years?

STCW Regulation I/11's five-year revalidation applies to masters, officers and radio operators, not to III/4 ratings. However, some administrations print a validity date or impose their own rules, so check your certificate and the issuing authority. Your basic training refreshers and medical certificate have their own expiry dates regardless.

Is my pre-sea engine rating course certificate the same as the III/4 certificate?

Usually not. The course is normally the approved special training, and you still need the required sea service and the competence assessment. After that, the administration issues the actual III/4 certificate. Joining as a watch rating with only a course certificate is a common reason crew get re-rated or refused.

How much sea time do I need for the III/4 certificate?

Under Regulation III/4, you need one of two routes. The first is approved seagoing service that includes at least six months of training and experience. The second is approved special training that includes at least two months of approved seagoing service. Your administration decides what training and service it approves and how it must be documented.

What is the difference between this certificate and Able Seafarer Engine?

The III/4 certificate is the basic watchkeeping qualification for engine ratings. Able Seafarer Engine under Regulation III/5 is a higher qualification that builds on it. It requires you to meet the III/4 requirements and then complete further engine department service, generally 12 months, or six months with approved training.

Test yourself

Regulation III/4 applies to watch ratings on seagoing ships with main propulsion machinery of what power?

Regulation III/4 applies to ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 750 kW propulsion power or more (STCW Regulation III/4). The 3,000 kW figure is the threshold for chief engineer and second engineer officer certificates under Regulation III/2.

What is the minimum age for a III/4 engine watch rating certificate?

Regulation III/4 requires the candidate to be not less than 16 years of age. National or company rules may set higher ages for particular work (STCW Regulation III/4).

Which of the following is exempt from holding the III/4 certificate?

Ratings under training and ratings whose duties are unskilled are excluded. Anyone keeping a watch or on UMS duty on a ship of 750 kW or more needs the certificate (STCW Regulation III/4).

Which statement about validity is correct?

Regulation I/11 revalidation applies to masters, officers and radio operators. Basic training competence must still be evidenced every five years (STCW Regulation I/11, Regulation VI/1 and Section A-VI/1).

On a round you find the boiler gauge glass showing no water level. What is the correct action for the watch rating?

A missing level may indicate dangerously low water, and adding feed blindly can be hazardous. The rating's competence is to recognise abnormal conditions and report them (STCW Code, Section A-III/4).
