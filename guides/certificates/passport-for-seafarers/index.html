Passport for seafarers: what it is, what governs it and why crew get stopped at joining

Quick answer

Your passport is a national travel document issued by your own government. It is not an STCW certificate and no course is involved. Its validity, blank pages, visas and name details are checked at nearly every stage of joining a ship, and a fault in any of them is one of the most common reasons a seafarer is offloaded at the airport.

What the passport is, and what it is not

A passport is issued by your own state, through the passport authority of India, the Philippines or wherever you hold citizenship. It proves your identity and nationality for international travel. It is the document that carries your visas.

It is easy to file it with your sea papers, but it sits apart from them:

  • It is not an STCW certificate. No STCW regulation requires it, and there is no training course or assessment.
  • It is not issued by the maritime administration. DG Shipping in India and MARINA in the Philippines do not issue passports.
  • It is not the same as your Seaman's Book (CDC) or a Seafarer's Identity Document (SID). Those are seafarer-specific documents. The passport is a general citizen's travel document.

In practice it is still the one document without which nothing else works. Your CoC, endorsements, medical and CDC are useless if you cannot board the flight to the ship.

Which instruments actually apply

No maritime convention requires you to hold a passport in the way STCW requires a CoC. Several instruments still touch on seafarer identity and travel:

Instrument What it deals with
ILO Seafarers' Identity Documents Convention (Revised), 2003 (No. 185), as amended A biometric Seafarer's Identity Document (SID) to facilitate shore leave, transit and transfer. For joining a ship, transit and transfer, the Convention contemplates the SID being supplemented by a passport, so it does not replace one.
ILO Seafarers' Identity Documents Convention, 1958 (No. 108) The older SID convention, still in force for some states that have not moved to No. 185
FAL Convention 1965, as amended Standardises arrival and departure paperwork. This includes the crew list, which records each crew member's identity document details.
MLC 2006, Regulation 2.5 Repatriation. A valid travel document is what makes repatriation possible in practice.

Whether your state issues an SID, and which convention it has ratified, depends on the administration. Even where you hold an SID, ports and immigration authorities can still require a passport and visa. Carry both.

The rules on passport validity, blank pages and visas come from national immigration law: that of the country you fly into, transit through and join the ship in. They do not come from IMO or ILO. This is why the requirement changes with every joining port.

Who needs one and where it is checked

Every seafarer travelling internationally needs a valid passport. This covers every rank from deck and engine cadet to master, catering crew, ETOs, riding crew and superintendents.

The passport is typically checked at these points:

  1. Manning agent or crewing office. They check it during document verification before your contract is processed. They also use it to apply for visas and OK-to-board letters.
  2. Your own country's departure immigration. For example, the Bureau of Immigration in Manila checks outbound seafarers along with their contract-related documents.
  3. Airline check-in. Airlines are liable if they carry a passenger who is refused entry, so check-in staff will refuse boarding if your passport does not meet the destination's rules.
  4. Arrival or transit immigration. Even when entering as crew on a joining letter, you need a passport the authorities will accept.
  5. On board. The master or the officer handling ship's documents enters the details in the crew list. Many companies hold passports in the ship's safe. Check your company's SMS and the applicable national practice on who holds them and when they are returned to you.
  6. At sign-off. You need it to travel home, often through a different country than the one you joined in.

Getting and renewing it: the process in general terms

There is no course. The process is an application to your national passport authority. Fees, documents, appointment systems and processing times differ by country and change often. Use the official passport authority website, not agents' estimates.

General points that hold almost everywhere:

  • Adult passports in India and the Philippines are currently issued with a ten-year validity. Minors' passports are shorter. Confirm the current rule with your issuing authority.
  • A passport cannot be "revalidated" like a CoC. When it nears expiry or runs out of pages, you apply for a new one.
  • Your new passport has a new number. Any document that records your passport number must then be updated. This can include your CDC, INDoS record, flag state applications, visa records and company crew database.
  • Keep the old cancelled passport if it contains a valid visa. For example, US practice generally allows an unexpired, undamaged visa in an expired passport to be used together with a new valid passport of the same nationality. Check the current rule for the specific visa before relying on it.
  • Apply while ashore. Renewal from abroad through an embassy or consulate is possible for many nationalities, but it is slower and harder to arrange around port calls.

If the passport is lost or stolen on board or abroad, report it to the master and the local police. Then contact your country's nearest embassy or consulate. The master and agent can support the paperwork, but only your own government can issue a replacement or emergency travel document.

Why crew get stopped at joining

Most offloadings caused by passports come down to a handful of avoidable faults.

  • Too little validity left. Many countries and airlines require the passport to stay valid for a set period beyond your intended stay. Six months is a common figure, but it varies; the Schengen area, for example, applies its own rules. For seafarers the "intended stay" can mean the whole contract, so companies often set a stricter internal minimum. Some crewing offices will not deploy you if the passport expires during the contract.
  • No blank visa pages. Some countries require one or two blank pages for the visa or entry stamp.
  • Name mismatch. The passport, CDC, CoC, INDoS record, visa, air ticket and OK-to-board letter must show the same name, in the same order and with the same spelling. Problems often come from initials, missing surnames or a change of name after marriage.
  • Damage. A torn data page, a water-damaged chip or a detached cover can lead to refusal at check-in or immigration.
  • Visa issues. The visa may be in an old passport that was left at home. It may be the wrong category, such as a tourist visa instead of a crew or transit visa. It may also have expired while you were waiting to join.
  • Details not updated. The visa or OK-to-board letter may have been issued against an old passport number.

Worked scenario. A 3rd Engineer is due to join a bulk carrier in Rotterdam on a six-month contract. His passport expires in five months, and he holds a valid Schengen visa in it. At the crewing office the documentation officer flags it, because the passport expires part-way through the contract. The company's policy will not allow deployment on that basis, and the visa would have to be reissued in a new passport anyway. He loses the slot to a reliever. Had he renewed the passport a year earlier, the visa application would have been made in the new passport from the start.

Crewing office and gangway: questions you should be able to answer

These questions come up at the crewing office, from documentation officers and sometimes in CoC orals as part of ship's documents and port entry:

  • "What is the expiry date of your passport and how does it compare with your contract end date?" Know it without looking. If the passport expires during the contract, say so before the office finds it.
  • "Which document records your identity details for port clearance?" The crew list, standardised under the FAL Convention. It carries each crew member's identity document details.
  • "Is an SID a substitute for a passport?" No. Under ILO C185, entry for joining, transit or transfer is based on the SID supplemented by a passport, and immigration authorities can still require a passport and visa.
  • "Who holds crew passports on board?" Practice varies with company SMS and port requirements. The master is often responsible for ship's and crew documents, but the seafarer must be able to get the passport back for travel and repatriation.
  • "Your visa is in your old passport. Can you travel?" That depends on the issuing country's rules for that visa. If it is allowed, you carry both passports.

Keeping it in date: a practical routine

  • Track three dates: passport expiry, visa expiries and your expected contract end. Store them in the same place as your CoC and medical expiry dates.
  • Renew early. As a working rule, renew when the remaining validity would not comfortably cover your next full contract plus travel. Your company's minimum may be stricter.
  • Watch the pages, not just the date. A heavily stamped passport can be full long before it expires.
  • Fix name issues once. Make sure every document matches the passport exactly. Get corrections made at the issuing authority, not by explaining at the airport.
  • Update everyone after renewal: your manning agent, the company crew database, the INDoS record (Indian seafarers, through the applicable process), and any pending visa or flag applications.
  • Keep copies. Keep a scan of the data page and visas in personal cloud storage, plus a paper copy separate from the original. Copies do not replace the passport, but they speed up a replacement.
  • Protect it on board. Use a waterproof pouch. Never use it as a bookmark, and never let it travel loose in a sea bag.
Key points
  • The passport is a national travel document. It is not an STCW certificate and there is no course or revalidation.
  • Validity, blank-page and visa rules come from national immigration law, so they change with each joining and transit country.
  • A Seafarer's Identity Document under ILO C185 is used alongside a passport, not instead of it. Carry both.
  • Too little remaining validity, name mismatches and visas in old or wrong passports are the commonest reasons for offloading.
  • Renew while ashore, well before the passport would expire during your next contract.
  • After renewal, update the passport number with your manning agent, company and every linked record.
  • Keep an old passport if it holds a valid visa, and check the issuing country's rule before relying on it.

Frequently asked questions

How much passport validity do I need to join a ship?

There is no single international figure. Each country's immigration law sets its own rule, and airlines enforce it at check-in. Many countries ask for a set period beyond your stay, often six months. Companies frequently require validity covering the whole contract, so check with your crewing office.

Is a Seaman's Book or SID enough to travel without a passport?

Generally no. ILO C185 treats the SID as a facilitation document that is supplemented by a passport for joining, transit and transfer. A CDC is a record of sea service and identity, not a travel document for international flights. Some limited facilitation exists for shore leave and transit, but you should always travel with a valid passport.

My visa is in my expired passport. Can I still use it?

It depends on the country that issued the visa. US practice, for example, generally allows an unexpired, undamaged visa in an old passport to be used alongside a new passport of the same nationality. Other countries may require a transfer or a new visa, so confirm before you fly and carry both passports.

Can I renew my passport while I am on board?

Often you can, through your country's embassy or consulate in a port of call. It takes planning around port stays and is usually slower than applying at home. It is far easier to renew before you sign on.

Does the company keep my passport on board, and is that allowed?

Many ships hold crew passports in the master's safe for port clearance and security. The exact practice depends on the company SMS and the ports visited. You should be able to get it back when you need it, especially for shore leave, medical treatment or repatriation.

Test yourself

Which instrument requires a seafarer to hold a national passport?

Passports are national travel documents governed by national immigration law. STCW, MLC and SOLAS do not require them. ILO C185 deals with the separate Seafarer's Identity Document.

Under ILO C185, what is the status of a Seafarer's Identity Document?

ILO Convention No. 185 (as amended) provides for a biometric SID to facilitate shore leave, transit and transfer. For joining, transit and transfer it is used together with a passport, so it does not replace one.

A seafarer's passport expires during a planned six-month contract. What is the most likely outcome at the crewing office?

Entry and airline rules often require validity beyond the stay, and companies commonly require cover for the full contract. Only the seafarer's own state can issue or extend a passport.

Which document, standardised under the FAL Convention, records crew identity document details for port clearance?

The FAL Convention standardises the crew list, which records each crew member's identity document details. The Safe Manning Document sets minimum manning, not identity.

After receiving a new passport, what should a seafarer do?

A new passport carries a new number, so linked records and visa applications must be updated. Some countries allow a valid visa in an old passport to be used with the new one.
