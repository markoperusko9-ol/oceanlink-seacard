MLC 2006 (Maritime Labour Convention): A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

The Maritime Labour Convention 2006 is the ILO convention that sets minimum standards for seafarers' employment and living conditions, including contracts, wages, hours of rest, leave, repatriation, accommodation, food and medical care. It entered into force on 20 August 2013. Ships of 500 GT and above engaged in international voyages, or operating from a port or between ports in another country, carry a Maritime Labour Certificate and a Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance, and port state control inspectors check these along with your contract and rest-hour records.

What MLC 2006 is and when it came into force

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 (MLC 2006) was adopted by the International Labour Organization (ILO) in Geneva in February 2006. It brought dozens of older ILO maritime conventions and recommendations together into a single instrument. People often call it the seafarers' bill of rights. It is also described as the fourth pillar of international shipping regulation, alongside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW.

It entered into force on 20 August 2013. That was twelve months after the ratification threshold was reached. The threshold required at least 30 member states holding at least 33% of world gross tonnage, and it was met in August 2012. Both the Philippines and India have ratified it.

Since then the convention has been amended. Two amendments matter most to crew:

  • 2014 amendments (in force 18 January 2017): shipowners must have mandatory financial security to cover two things:
    • repatriation and abandonment of seafarers
    • contractual claims for death or long-term disability
  • 2018 amendments (in force 26 December 2020): a seafarer held captive on or off the ship as a result of piracy or armed robbery keeps their employment agreement. Wages and other entitlements continue during captivity.

The convention sets minimums. Your flag state's national law, a collective bargaining agreement (CBA) or your company may give you better terms. They cannot give you worse ones.

How the convention is structured

MLC 2006 has three layers:

  • Articles and Regulations: the core obligations, which are binding.
  • The Code, Part A (Standards): detailed requirements, which are mandatory.
  • The Code, Part B (Guidelines): guidance on how to implement Part A. These are not mandatory, but flag states must give them due consideration.

The Regulations and the Code are grouped into five Titles:

Title Subject Examples
1 Minimum requirements to work on a ship Minimum age, medical certificate, training, recruitment and placement
2 Conditions of employment Seafarers' employment agreement, wages, hours of work and rest, leave, repatriation, manning, career development
3 Accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering Cabins, sanitary facilities, galley standards, ship's cook
4 Health protection, medical care, welfare and social security Medical care on board, shipowners' liability, safety and accident prevention, shore welfare
5 Compliance and enforcement Flag state inspection and certification, complaint procedures, port state control

Numbering follows the Title. For example, Regulation 2.3 is hours of work and rest, and its matching Standard is A2.3. If you remember the Title, you can find the topic quickly.

What it means for you in daily work

Before you join

  • Minimum age is 16. Seafarers under 18 must not do night work or work likely to endanger their health or safety, apart from limited exceptions set by the flag state.
  • You need a valid medical certificate. The convention allows a maximum validity of two years, or one year for seafarers under 18. Colour vision is valid for up to six years. Your flag state may set shorter periods.
  • Recruitment and placement services must not charge you fees for finding you a job. In India this means DG Shipping-licensed RPSL agencies. In the Philippines it means DMW-licensed manning agencies. Limited exceptions are allowed, such as the cost of your national medical certificate, seafarer's book, passport or similar personal documents. Visa costs are borne by the shipowner.

Your contract

  • You must have a signed Seafarers' Employment Agreement (SEA).
  • You must be given the chance to review it and take advice before you sign.
  • You and the shipowner each keep a signed original, and a copy is held on board.
  • Your record of employment must not contain any statement on the quality of your work or your wages.

Wages and leave

  • You are paid at intervals of no more than one month.
  • You receive a monthly account showing payments and deductions.
  • You can arrange allotments to your family.
  • Paid annual leave is calculated on a minimum of 2.5 calendar days per month of employment.

Hours of work and rest

The flag state sets either maximum work hours or minimum rest hours. Most flags use rest hours, which also lines up with STCW.

  • Maximum work: 14 hours in any 24 hours, and 72 hours in any 7 days.
  • Minimum rest: 10 hours in any 24 hours, and 77 hours in any 7 days.
  • Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours.
  • The gap between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.
  • Musters and drills should disturb rest as little as possible.
  • If you are called out during rest, you get compensatory rest.
  • The master may suspend these limits for the immediate safety of the ship, people or cargo, or to help others in distress. Once the situation is normal, you must get adequate rest.

Repatriation

  • You are entitled to repatriation at no cost to you. This applies when your contract ends, if you are sick or injured, and in other listed cases.
  • The maximum service period before you are entitled to repatriation must be less than 12 months.
  • The shipowner cannot demand an advance payment towards repatriation. Costs can be recovered only if national law or the CBA finds you in serious default of your obligations.

Living conditions and health

  • Accommodation, food and drinking water must meet set standards.
  • The ship's cook must be trained and qualified, and must be at least 18 years old.
  • You are entitled to medical care on board and ashore, free of charge as far as possible.
  • The shipowner is liable for sickness and injury costs connected with your employment.
  • Ships carrying 100 or more persons on international voyages of more than three days must carry a qualified medical doctor.

Certificates and records the convention creates on board

Maritime Labour Certificate (MLC certificate)

This certificate is required for ships of 500 GT and above that either:

  • are engaged in international voyages, or
  • operate from a port, or between ports, in another country.

The flag state or its recognised organisation issues it for a maximum of five years. There is an intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates. A flag state may also certify other ships at the shipowner's request.

Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance (DMLC)

  • Part I is drawn up by the flag state. It lists the national requirements that implement the convention.
  • Part II is drawn up by the shipowner. It sets out the measures adopted to ensure ongoing compliance, and the flag state then certifies it.

The certificate and DMLC cover 16 areas listed in Appendix A5-I of the convention, including:

  • minimum age and medical certification
  • qualifications
  • the SEA
  • use of a licensed recruitment service
  • hours of work or rest
  • manning levels
  • accommodation and recreational facilities
  • food and catering
  • health and safety
  • medical care
  • on-board complaint procedures
  • payment of wages
  • the two financial security items

Other documents you should see on board

  • Copies of the MLC certificate and DMLC posted in a conspicuous place where crew can see them.
  • A copy of the convention itself, available to seafarers.
  • The table of shipboard working arrangements, posted in an easily accessible place. It must be in the working language of the ship and in English.
  • Hours of rest records for each seafarer. You are entitled to a copy of the records about you, endorsed by the master or an authorised person and by you.
  • Financial security certificates from the insurer or P&I club for repatriation/abandonment and for death/disability claims, also posted.
  • The on-board complaint procedure. Every seafarer gets a copy when joining.
  • Records of food and water supply inspections, which the master or a delegate must carry out at frequent intervals.
  • Wage accounts and copies of SEAs.

Complaints: on board and ashore

On board

Regulation 5.1.5 requires an on-board complaint procedure. It normally works like this:

  1. Raise the issue with your head of department.
  2. If it is not resolved, take it to the master.
  3. If it is still not resolved, take it to the company, and to the flag state if necessary.

The convention says you may also complain directly to the master and to external authorities. You must not be victimised for filing a complaint. The procedure must give contact details for the flag state competent authority. It must also name a person on board who can advise you confidentially.

Ashore

Under Regulation 5.2.2 you can complain to a port state control officer in port. The officer will normally:

  • check whether the on-board procedure has been used, where appropriate
  • protect your confidentiality
  • investigate

The complaint can lead to a more detailed inspection. You can also contact your union or seafarers' welfare organisation.

Worked scenario

An AB's rest-hour record has been filled in to show compliance, but he actually worked through cargo operations with only 4 hours' rest. He should:

  1. Raise the issue through the on-board procedure.
  2. Keep his own notes.
  3. Refuse to sign a record he knows is false.

Falsified rest records are a common PSC finding. A ship that keeps records which do not match the log book, port times and cargo timings exposes the master and the company, not just the crew member.

What port state control typically checks

Under Regulation 5.2.1, a port state inspector treats the MLC certificate and DMLC as prima facie evidence of compliance. A more detailed inspection follows if:

  • the documents are missing, invalid or falsified
  • there are clear grounds that conditions do not meet the convention
  • the ship has changed flag to avoid compliance
  • a complaint has been made

Typical items checked

  • Validity of the MLC certificate, DMLC Parts I and II, and the posted financial security certificates.
  • SEAs: signed by both parties, a copy on board, and terms consistent with the CBA and national law.
  • Wage accounts, and evidence that wages are paid on time.
  • Hours of rest records, compared against deck and engine logs, port times and cargo operations.
  • The posted table of working arrangements.
  • Medical certificates and STCW certificates for crew.
  • Minimum age.
  • Galley hygiene and provision stores, food quality, drinking water, and food inspection records.
  • Condition of accommodation, sanitary spaces and laundry.
  • Health and safety arrangements and accident records.
  • The medical chest and medical care arrangements.
  • Crew interviews.

Detention

PSC can detain a ship if conditions on board are clearly hazardous to seafarers' safety, health or security, or if a deficiency is a serious or repeated breach of the convention.

Non-ratifying flags

Under the principle of no more favourable treatment, ports in ratifying states can inspect ships flying the flag of non-ratifying states against MLC standards too. Each regional PSC regime (Paris MoU, Tokyo MoU, Indian Ocean MoU and others) applies its own procedures and campaigns.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: Name the five Titles of MLC 2006.

A: Minimum requirements to work on a ship; conditions of employment; accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering; health protection, medical care, welfare and social security; compliance and enforcement.

Q: Which parts of the Code are mandatory?

A: Part A (Standards) is mandatory. Part B (Guidelines) is not mandatory, but flag states must give it due consideration.

Q: Who drafts DMLC Part I and Part II?

A: The flag state drafts Part I. The shipowner drafts Part II, and the flag state certifies it.

Q: As master, how do you ensure rest-hour compliance during a busy port call?

A:

  • Plan watches and cargo duties in advance.
  • Use shore labour or extra hands where available.
  • Keep honest records.
  • Give compensatory rest after any call-out.
  • Use the suspension power only for genuine emergencies, never for commercial pressure.

Q: What must be posted for the crew?

A:

  • The MLC certificate and DMLC.
  • The financial security certificates.
  • The table of shipboard working arrangements in the working language and English.

The complaint procedure must also be made available, with a copy given to each seafarer.

Q: When can PSC go beyond checking the certificate?

A: When documents are not valid, when there are clear grounds of non-compliance, when the ship has reflagged to avoid compliance, or when there is a complaint.

Exam weighting and the exact questions depend on your administration's syllabus for the CoC you are sitting.

Key points
  • MLC 2006 is the ILO convention setting minimum living and working standards for seafarers; it entered into force on 20 August 2013.
  • It has five Titles; Code Part A is mandatory, Part B is guidance that flag states must consider.
  • Rest limits: at least 10 hours in any 24 and 77 in any 7 days, in no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours.
  • Ships of 500 GT and above engaged in international voyages, or operating from a port or between ports in another country, carry a Maritime Labour Certificate (up to 5 years) and a DMLC Parts I and II.
  • You must have a signed SEA, monthly wage accounts, fee-free recruitment and repatriation at no cost to you.
  • Financial security certificates for abandonment and for death or disability claims must be posted on board.
  • PSC treats the MLC certificate as prima facie evidence but will inspect in detail on clear grounds or after a complaint.

Frequently asked questions

When did MLC 2006 come into force?

It was adopted by the ILO in 2006 and entered into force on 20 August 2013. That was twelve months after the required 30 ratifications covering at least 33% of world gross tonnage were reached in August 2012. Later amendments, such as the financial security requirements, came into force on their own dates.

What are the MLC rest hour rules?

Under MLC, the flag state sets either maximum work hours (14 in any 24 hours and 72 in any 7 days) or minimum rest hours (10 in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days). Rest can be split into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, and the gap between rest periods cannot exceed 14 hours. STCW sets matching rest-hour requirements for watchkeepers.

Can a manning agency charge me a fee under MLC?

No. Recruitment and placement services must not charge seafarers, directly or indirectly, for finding them employment. Limited exceptions are allowed for the cost of personal documents such as the national medical certificate, seafarer's book and passport. Your national administration's licensing rules for agencies also apply.

What is the difference between DMLC Part I and Part II?

Part I is drawn up by the flag state and lists the national laws that implement the convention's requirements. Part II is drawn up by the shipowner and describes how the ship complies on an ongoing basis. Part II is then certified by the flag state or its recognised organisation.

How long can my contract on board last under MLC?

The convention requires the maximum service period on board, after which you are entitled to repatriation, to be less than 12 months. The exact maximum and normal contract lengths are set by the flag state's law, your CBA and your SEA. Many are well below the MLC limit.

Test yourself

On what date did MLC 2006 enter into force?

MLC 2006 was adopted in 2006 but entered into force on 20 August 2013, twelve months after the ratification threshold was met. 18 January 2017 is when the financial security amendments took effect (MLC 2006, Article VIII; 2014 amendments to the Code).

What is the status of Part B of the MLC Code?

Part A contains mandatory standards; Part B contains non-mandatory guidelines that members must give due consideration when implementing (MLC 2006, Article VI).

Who draws up Part II of the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance?

The flag state prepares DMLC Part I; the shipowner prepares Part II describing ongoing compliance measures, which is then certified (MLC 2006, Regulation 5.1.3 and Standard A5.1.3).

Under the maximum hours of work option, what is the limit in any 24-hour period?

Maximum hours of work must not exceed 14 hours in any 24 hours and 72 hours in any 7 days. Ten hours is the minimum rest figure, not a work limit (MLC 2006, Standard A2.3).

Which ships must carry a Maritime Labour Certificate?

Certification is required for ships of 500 GT and above engaged in international voyages or operating from or between foreign ports; flags may certify other ships on request (MLC 2006, Regulation 5.1.3).
