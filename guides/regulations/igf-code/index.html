IGF Code: A Seafarer's Guide to Gas and Low-Flashpoint Fuel Safety

Quick answer

The IGF Code is the International Code of Safety for Ships using Gases or other Low-flashpoint Fuels. It became mandatory under SOLAS on 1 January 2017 and covers the design, bunkering, operation and crew training of ships that burn fuels such as LNG. If you have designated safety duties related to the fuel, you need STCW basic IGF training; the master, engineer officers and those with immediate responsibility for the fuel need advanced training. Port state control will check your certificates, your gas detection and shutdown systems, and whether you actually know the emergency procedures.

What the IGF Code is and when it came in

The IGF Code is the IMO's safety code for ships that use gases or other low-flashpoint fuels as fuel. A low-flashpoint fuel is one whose flashpoint is below the limit SOLAS normally allows for ships' fuel oil, which is 60°C. LNG is the fuel the Code was built around.

  • Adopted: 2015, by IMO resolution MSC.391(95).
  • Made mandatory: through amendments to SOLAS Chapter II-1. These added Part G, which applies the Code.
  • Entry into force: 1 January 2017.

The STCW training requirements for crew on these ships (Regulation V/3) entered into force on the same date.

The Code is goal-based. Each chapter states a goal and functional requirements, and then gives detailed prescriptive rules. At present the detailed rules are written for natural gas. Ships burning other low-flashpoint fuels, such as methyl/ethyl alcohol, ammonia or hydrogen, generally have to be approved through the alternative design route. That route requires an engineering analysis showing a level of safety equivalent to the Code. IMO has issued interim guidelines for some of these fuels, and the detail is evolving, so check the current position with your company or class.

Which ships it applies to

In broad terms, the Code applies to SOLAS ships that use low-flashpoint fuels. This means passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and above, built on or after the entry-into-force date. It also applies to existing ships converted to use such fuels after that date.

The precise construction-date definitions are in SOLAS, and your flag administration applies them.

The main exclusion to remember: gas carriers that burn their cargo (boil-off gas) in line with the IGC Code come under the IGC Code, not the IGF Code. This is a classic exam trap. An LNG carrier burning boil-off is an IGC ship. An LNG-fuelled container ship, ferry or bulker is an IGF ship.

Non-SOLAS craft, such as some domestic ferries and harbour tugs, may still be required to follow the IGF Code by their national administration. Some administrations do this and others do not.

How the Code is structured

You do not need to memorise chapter numbers, but you should know the layout:

Part What it covers
Part A General: goal and functional requirements, general requirements, risk assessment, limiting the consequences of an explosion
Part A-1 Specific requirements for natural gas fuel: ship arrangement, fuel containment (tanks), piping and materials, bunkering, fuel supply to consumers, power generation, fire safety, explosion prevention, hazardous area zones, ventilation, electrical installations, control, monitoring and safety systems
Part B-1 Manufacture, workmanship and testing
Part C-1 Drills and emergency exercises, and operation
Part D Training (which points to STCW)

Crew live mostly in Parts C-1 and D. Designers live in Parts A and A-1, but the A-1 concepts below are what you meet on every watch.

What it means in daily work on board

Concepts you must understand:

  • Hazardous area zones. Spaces and deck areas are classified by how likely a flammable gas atmosphere is (Zones 0, 1 and 2). This decides which electrical equipment is allowed there. Do not take a non-certified radio, phone or torch into these areas.
  • Machinery space concepts. A gas-safe machinery space has all gas piping enclosed (double-walled or in ducts), so the space is treated as gas-safe in all conditions. An ESD-protected machinery space has single-walled gas piping. In that design, a gas leak automatically shuts off and isolates the fuel supply (emergency shutdown).
  • Key spaces and valves. You will meet the tank connection space, the fuel preparation room, the bunkering station and the vent mast. The master gas fuel valve is located outside the machinery space and isolates the gas supply to the consumers in that machinery space. It does not isolate the ship's whole fuel supply.
  • Gas detection and ventilation. Fixed detectors alarm at a low percentage of the lower explosive limit (LEL) and trigger shutdown at a higher level, as the Code sets out. The ventilation of ducts and hazardous spaces is monitored, and loss of ventilation is itself an alarm.

Worked scenario: gas alarm in an ESD-protected engine room

  1. The detector alarms at the first threshold. Alert the engineer on watch and the bridge, and follow the company emergency procedure.
  2. If concentration reaches the shutdown level, the ESD isolates the gas supply automatically. Do not override it to keep the engine running.
  3. Expect any non-certified electrical equipment in the space to be de-energised as designed.
  4. On a dual-fuel plant, the engine changes over to oil fuel. Verify that the changeover has happened and that the fuel supply really is isolated.
  5. Do not enter to investigate without the risk assessment, gas testing and PPE your SMS requires.
  6. Afterwards, find and repair the leak, test the system, record the event and report it under the ISM Code.

Training and certificates for crew

Part D of the Code refers to STCW Regulation V/3 and STCW Code Section A-V/3. The result is certificates of proficiency (CoPs) issued or recognised by the administration.

  • Basic training. For seafarers with designated safety duties related to the care, use of, or emergency response to the fuel. In practice, many companies require it for all crew on the ship.
  • Advanced training. For the master, engineer officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for the care and use of the fuel and fuel systems. Before it is issued you need:
    • basic training;
    • a period of approved sea service on an IGF ship that includes bunkering operations;
    • the advanced course itself.

Part of the bunkering experience may be replaced by approved simulator training. - Equivalence with gas tanker training. STCW allows certain liquefied gas tanker qualifications to be counted towards IGF requirements. How your administration applies this varies, so check before assuming your gas tanker CoP covers you. - Revalidation. Administrations require these CoPs to be kept valid. The method and interval are set nationally, so check with your administration.

Ship-specific familiarisation is also required before you take up duties. This is separate from the CoP.

The Code also requires drills and emergency exercises covering the gas fuel system. These are on top of the SOLAS drills. Frequency and format, such as practical drills or tabletop exercises, are set out in the Code and your company SMS, so follow your SMS schedule.

Records and documents you will find on board

There is no separate stand-alone 'IGF certificate'. Compliance is surveyed under the ship's SOLAS statutory certificates, and usually reflected in a class notation for gas-fuelled ships. Class notations differ between societies.

On board, expect to find and maintain:

  • Crew CoPs: IGF basic and advanced, as required for each person.
  • Familiarisation records for the ship's fuel system.
  • Drill and exercise records for gas-fuel emergencies.
  • Fuel handling and bunkering procedures as required by the Code. Expect pre-bunkering and bunkering checklists agreed with the supplier.
  • Bunker delivery documentation from the supplier for fuel delivered for combustion. MARPOL Annex VI sets the documentation requirements, and these differ for gas fuels such as LNG, so check what your flag administration expects.
  • Maintenance and test records for gas detection calibration, ESD function tests, ventilation, safety valves and pressure relief, and double-wall or duct monitoring.
  • Alarm and event logs from the gas management and safety systems.
  • Approved documentation such as the hazardous area plan and, where relevant, the alternative design approval.

What port state control typically checks

PSC officers will usually look at:

  • Certificates. Valid IGF basic and advanced CoPs for the right people, matching the crew list and your duties.
  • Competence. Can the engineer on watch explain the ESD logic, the location of the master gas fuel valve and the response to a gas alarm?
  • Gas detection. Is it working? When was it last calibrated? Are there any inhibited or overridden alarms?
  • Ventilation. Are fans running, and are low-flow alarms functional?
  • Records. Bunkering procedures and completed checklists, drill records, maintenance logs and bunker delivery documentation.
  • Hazardous areas. The condition of certified electrical equipment, no unauthorised equipment, and closed and alarmed access doors.
  • Drills. An officer may ask for a gas-leak scenario to be demonstrated.

Inspectors often test crew knowledge and drill performance as well as equipment condition.

Oral exam style: questions an examiner asks

  • What does IGF stand for and when did it become mandatory? International Code of Safety for Ships using Gases or other Low-flashpoint Fuels; mandatory under SOLAS from 1 January 2017.
  • Does it apply to an LNG carrier burning boil-off? No. That ship is covered by the IGC Code.
  • What is the difference between a gas-safe and an ESD-protected machinery space? In a gas-safe space all gas piping is enclosed, so the space is safe in all conditions. In an ESD-protected space the piping is single-walled and relies on automatic shutdown and isolation if gas is detected.
  • What is the master gas fuel valve? The automatic valve outside the machinery space that isolates the gas supply to the consumers in that space.
  • What training does the second engineer need? Advanced IGF training, built on basic training plus sea service including bunkering, under STCW V/3.
  • How are fuels other than natural gas approved? Through the alternative design approach, supported by IMO interim guidelines where available.
  • What drills does the Code add? Gas-fuel-specific drills and emergency exercises, carried out as the Code and the company SMS require.
Key points
  • The IGF Code covers ships burning gas or other low-flashpoint fuels and has been mandatory under SOLAS since 1 January 2017.
  • Gas carriers burning their cargo as fuel come under the IGC Code, not the IGF Code.
  • Detailed rules exist for natural gas; other fuels such as methanol and ammonia go through alternative design approval.
  • Seafarers with designated fuel-related safety duties need STCW V/3 basic training; the master, engineer officers and those responsible for the fuel need advanced training; everyone needs ship-specific familiarisation.
  • Know the difference between gas-safe and ESD-protected machinery spaces and where the master gas fuel valve is.
  • There is no separate IGF certificate; compliance sits within SOLAS statutory certificates and class.
  • PSC focuses on crew certificates, gas detection and ventilation, bunkering records and how well crew perform in drills.

Frequently asked questions

Is IGF basic training mandatory for all crew on an LNG-fuelled ship?

STCW requires it for seafarers with designated safety duties related to the fuel. Many companies and some administrations require it for everyone on board. Check your company SMS and flag requirements before joining.

Can my gas tanker certificate replace IGF training?

STCW allows certain liquefied gas tanker qualifications to count towards the IGF requirements. Each administration applies this in its own way. Confirm with your administration, or have the CoP issued, before you sign on.

Does the IGF Code apply to methanol or ammonia-fuelled ships?

Yes, they use low-flashpoint fuels and are within the Code's scope. The detailed prescriptive rules currently cover natural gas, so these ships are approved through the alternative design route. IMO interim guidelines support that process.

What is the difference between the IGF Code and the IGC Code?

The IGC Code covers ships that carry liquefied gas as cargo, including when they burn that cargo as fuel. The IGF Code covers other ships that use gas or low-flashpoint fuels for their own propulsion or power.

Who needs IGF advanced training?

The master, engineer officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for the care and use of the fuel and fuel systems. You must first hold basic training and complete approved sea service that includes bunkering operations.

Test yourself

When did the IGF Code become mandatory under SOLAS?

The Code was adopted in 2015 and entered into force through SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part G on 1 January 2017. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part G; resolution MSC.391(95).

An LNG carrier burns boil-off gas from its cargo in its engines. Which code governs this?

Gas carriers using their cargo as fuel in line with the IGC Code are excluded from the IGF Code. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part G; IGC Code.

What best describes an ESD-protected machinery space?

The ESD-protected concept relies on emergency shutdown when gas is detected. The gas-safe concept encloses all gas piping. Reference: IGF Code, Part A-1.

Which STCW regulation sets the training for seafarers on IGF ships?

Regulation V/3 and Section A-V/3 cover basic and advanced IGF training. V/1-2 covers liquefied gas tanker cargo operations. Reference: STCW Regulation V/3.

A ship is designed to burn methanol as fuel. How is it normally approved?

The detailed rules currently cover natural gas, so other low-flashpoint fuels need alternative design approval. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part G; IGF Code, Part A.
