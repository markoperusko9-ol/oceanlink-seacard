STCW Convention: What Every Seafarer Needs to Know

Quick answer

The STCW Convention (1978, in force since 28 April 1984, heavily revised in 1995 and 2010) sets the international minimum standards for seafarer training, certification, watchkeeping and hours of rest. Your flag state applies these standards through its national rules. The Convention decides which certificates you must carry, how often they need revalidating, how much rest you must get, and what port state control can check when they board.

What the STCW Convention is and when it came in

STCW stands for the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers. IMO adopted it on 7 July 1978 and it entered into force on 28 April 1984. It was the first convention to set basic training and certification requirements for seafarers at an international level.

The original text was vague in places, so it has been overhauled twice:

  • 1995 amendments (in force 1 February 1997). These were a near-complete rewrite. They moved the technical detail into a new STCW Code and gave IMO a role in checking how Parties implement the Convention, which is the origin of the so-called "White List".
  • 2010 Manila amendments (in force 1 January 2012, with a transitional period to 1 January 2017). These brought in tighter hours-of-rest rules, an alcohol limit, security training, refresher training for basic safety, new competences such as ECDIS, BRM/ERM and leadership, and new grades such as Electro-Technical Officer and Able Seafarer Deck/Engine.

Later amendments added training for ships under the IGF Code and the Polar Code, among others. The Convention keeps evolving, so always check the current consolidated edition rather than an old course handout.

What STCW does not cover: warships, naval auxiliaries and other government ships on non-commercial service, fishing vessels (they have the separate STCW-F Convention), pleasure yachts not engaged in trade, and wooden ships of primitive build. It also sets minimum standards only. Your flag state can, and often does, demand more.

How the Convention is structured

There are three layers – Articles, Annex and the STCW Code, with the Code split into mandatory Part A and advisory Part B. Knowing which layer a requirement sits in tells you how binding it is.

Layer What it contains Binding?
Articles Legal framework: definitions, application, certificates, control (PSC), dispensations Yes
Annex (Regulations, Chapters I–VIII) The core rules, e.g. Regulation II/1 for OOW deck Yes
STCW Code Part A Detailed standards: competence tables, rest hours, medical standards Yes (mandatory)
STCW Code Part B Recommended guidance to help apply Part A No (advisory)

The Annex chapters, which the Code mirrors section by section:

  • Chapter I – General provisions (certificates, medical fitness, revalidation, recognition, company responsibilities, port state control)
  • Chapter II – Master and deck department
  • Chapter III – Engine department
  • Chapter IV – Radiocommunication and radio operators
  • Chapter V – Special training for personnel on certain ship types (tankers, passenger ships, IGF, polar)
  • Chapter VI – Emergency, occupational safety, security, medical care and survival functions (basic safety training, PSCRB, AFF, medical, security)
  • Chapter VII – Alternative certification
  • Chapter VIII – Watchkeeping (fitness for duty, rest hours, watch arrangements)

The numbering carries through. Regulation III/1 in the Annex is supported by Section A-III/1 (mandatory) and Section B-III/1 (guidance) in the Code. Examiners expect you to use this numbering.

The certificates and documents STCW creates

Your document folder is largely built on STCW. The main types:

  • Certificate of Competency (CoC). Issued to masters, officers and GMDSS radio operators, e.g. OOW deck under Regulation II/1 or Chief Engineer under III/2.
  • Certificate of Proficiency (CoP). Issued for ratings' qualifications and specialist training, e.g. basic safety training, PSCRB, tanker cargo operations or security. Depending on the regulation and the flag, some ratings' qualifications may also be issued as a CoC.
  • Endorsement attesting recognition (Regulation I/10). You need this from the flag state when your CoC was issued by a different country. Some flags allow you to serve for up to three months while the endorsement application is pending, provided you carry documentary proof that it was submitted. Check what your flag actually permits.
  • Medical certificate (Regulation I/9). Under the Convention it is valid for a maximum of two years, or one year if you are under 18. A colour vision certificate may be valid for up to six years. Your flag or national medical rules may set shorter periods.
  • Documentary evidence. Training records, record books and similar papers that support your certificates.

Revalidation (Regulation I/11). Masters and officers must revalidate their CoC at intervals not exceeding five years. You prove continued medical fitness and professional competence, usually through approved sea service, and the alternative routes are set by your administration. Under Chapter VI, holders of proficiency in survival techniques, fire-fighting, PSCRB and AFF must show they have maintained the standard every five years, which in practice means refresher training. Elementary First Aid and PSSR do not require five-yearly refresher evidence under STCW, although some flags or employers may ask for it. The exact refresher format, and whether it can be done partly on board, depends on the flag.

Originals on board. STCW requires the original certificates to be available on board while you are serving. Photocopies or scans alone will not satisfy an inspector, although some flags accept electronic certificates they have issued and that can be verified online.

What STCW means in your daily work

STCW is not just about getting the ticket. Several parts govern how you work on board.

Familiarisation. Before you are assigned shipboard duties you must receive safety familiarisation (Chapter VI). The company must also make sure you are familiar with your specific duties and with the ship's equipment and procedures (Regulation I/14). This is the joining checklist you sign.

Hours of rest (Regulation VIII/1, Section A-VIII/1). These apply to watchkeepers and to anyone with designated safety, security or pollution-prevention duties:

  • at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period, and 77 hours in any 7-day period;
  • rest may be split into no more than two periods, and one of them must be at least 6 hours long;
  • the gap between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours;
  • drills should be conducted in a way that disturbs rest as little as possible, and if you are called out during rest you should get adequate compensatory rest;
  • watch schedules must be posted where they are easily accessible.

Administrations may allow limited exceptions under strict conditions. Your flag's rules and the company SMS will say whether any exceptions apply on your ship.

Alcohol. For the purpose of preventing alcohol abuse, the Code requires a limit of not more than 0.05% blood alcohol or 0.25 mg/l in breath (or an equivalent amount). Many companies run a zero-alcohol policy, which is stricter and perfectly allowed.

Watchkeeping principles (Chapter VIII). The Code covers:

  • what a proper handover involves;
  • when the OOW must call the master;
  • how a lookout must be maintained;
  • the composition of the watch;
  • the equivalent principles for the engine room.

Much of your company's bridge and engine standing orders is built on this.

Records on board that relate to STCW

Expect an auditor or inspector to look at these:

  • Hours of rest records. These must be kept for every person covered. Each seafarer should receive a copy of their own records, endorsed by the master or an authorised person and by the seafarer. Falsified or "too perfect" records are a known PSC target.
  • Table of shipboard working arrangements. This is the posted watch schedule.
  • Crew certificate files. Original CoCs, flag endorsements, CoPs, medical certificates and seaman's book for everyone on board, matched against the crew list.
  • Safe Manning Document. Strictly speaking this is issued under SOLAS Chapter V, not STCW. However, STCW requires the company to man the ship in line with it, so inspectors check the two together.
  • Familiarisation records. Signed checklists for each new joiner.
  • Training record books. For cadets and trainees, used as evidence for their first CoC application.
  • Drill and training records. These are logged under SOLAS, but they support the competence picture.

Keeping all of this in order is formally the company's job under Regulation I/14. On board it usually falls on the master, with help from the chief officer and chief engineer.

What port state control checks under STCW

The Convention's control provisions (Article X and Regulation I/4) let an inspector:

  • verify that everyone who must be certificated holds a valid certificate, an endorsement or a valid dispensation;
  • check numbers and certificates against the Safe Manning Document;
  • assess whether seafarers can actually keep the required watchkeeping and security standards. This deeper assessment needs clear grounds, for example:
    • the ship has been in a collision, grounding or stranding;
    • there has been an illegal discharge;
    • the ship has been manoeuvred erratically or unsafely, or has not followed a routeing measure;
    • the ship is being operated in a way that poses a danger.

Regulation I/4 lists the deficiencies that can justify detention if they pose a danger. They include:

  • seafarers without the required certificate, endorsement, valid dispensation or documentary proof that an endorsement application has been submitted;
  • failure to comply with the manning requirements;
  • watch arrangements that do not meet the flag's requirements;
  • no competent person on watch for equipment essential to navigation, radiocommunication or pollution prevention;
  • failure to show professional proficiency for assigned duties;
  • inability to provide sufficiently rested people for the first watch at the start of a voyage and for later relieving watches.

Worked scenario. An inspector boards after a near-grounding in the approaches. They ask the OOW to show how the position was being monitored on ECDIS, then ask for the 2nd Officer's ECDIS training certificates and the bridge team's rest records for the past week.

The records show the 2nd Officer had only 8 hours of rest in the 24 hours before the incident, because a cargo watch was worked straight after a sea watch. That is a non-compliance with Section A-VIII/1. Combined with the incident, it gives clear grounds for a deeper assessment and possibly a detention.

The lesson is simple. Plan cargo and sea watches so they fit within the rest rules, and record actual hours honestly, not the planned schedule.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

"Where does the Convention give PSC the right to check crew certificates?" Article X and Regulation I/4. Mention the clear-grounds trigger for a competence assessment.

"Difference between Part A and Part B of the Code?" Part A is mandatory standards. Part B is recommended guidance. Give an example, such as the competence tables in A-II/1 for OOW.

"State the hours of rest requirements." Give all four parts:

  • 10 hours in 24;
  • 77 hours in 7 days;
  • no more than two periods, one of them at least 6 hours;
  • no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods.

Then add that records are kept and that a copy goes to the seafarer.

"Who is responsible for ensuring the ship is properly manned with certificated seafarers?" The company, under Regulation I/14. The master implements this on board.

"Your CoC was issued by India and you join a Marshall Islands ship. What do you need?" An endorsement attesting recognition from the flag state under Regulation I/10. Note any interim period the flag allows.

"When were the Manila amendments adopted, and name three changes." Adopted 2010, in force 1 January 2012. Suitable changes include:

  • the hours-of-rest tightening;
  • the alcohol limit;
  • ECDIS training;
  • leadership and resource management;
  • security training;
  • the ETO and Able Seafarer grades.

Examiners reward clear references and a practical example from your own sea time far more than reciting text.

Key points
  • STCW 1978 entered into force on 28 April 1984; the 1995 and 2010 (Manila) amendments reshaped it.
  • Annex regulations and STCW Code Part A are mandatory; Code Part B is guidance only.
  • Minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days, split into no more than two periods.
  • CoCs are revalidated at intervals of no more than five years; PST, FPFF, PSCRB and AFF holders must show maintained competence every five years (EFA and PSSR have no such requirement).
  • A foreign-issued CoC needs a flag state endorsement under Regulation I/10.
  • Original certificates must be on board; PSC checks them against the Safe Manning Document and rest records.
  • Flags may set stricter rules than STCW minimums, so always check your flag and company SMS.

Frequently asked questions

Is STCW the same as basic safety training?

No. STCW is the whole convention covering training, certification and watchkeeping for all grades. Basic safety training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR) is one requirement within Chapter VI of it. People often say "STCW course" when they mean basic safety training.

How often do I need to refresh my STCW basic safety courses?

Under the Convention, holders of proficiency in personal survival techniques and fire prevention and fire-fighting must show they have maintained the standard every five years. The same applies to PSCRB and advanced fire-fighting. Elementary First Aid and PSSR do not require five-yearly refresher evidence under STCW, although some flags or employers may ask for it. How this is done (full refresher, shortened course, or partly on board) is decided by your administration, so check your flag and national rules.

Does STCW apply to fishing vessels and yachts?

STCW does not apply to fishing vessels, which have a separate convention, STCW-F. It also does not apply to pleasure yachts not engaged in trade, warships and some other exempted craft. Commercially operated yachts may fall under STCW or under flag-specific schemes, depending on the flag.

Can I sail on a foreign flag ship before my flag endorsement arrives?

Regulation I/10 lets an administration allow service for up to three months if you hold documentary proof that an endorsement application has been submitted. Not every flag uses this option in the same way. Confirm with your company or the flag before joining.

What happens if my hours of rest records show a violation?

A recorded non-conformity should be investigated under the company SMS, with the cause identified and the watch arrangements corrected. At PSC, rest violations can be recorded as deficiencies. If they show the ship cannot provide sufficiently rested watchkeepers, they can contribute to detention. Never falsify the records, because that turns a correctable problem into a serious one.

Test yourself

What is the minimum hours of rest in any 7-day period required by the STCW Code?

Section A-VIII/1 requires at least 77 hours of rest in any 7-day period. 72 is the MLC maximum hours of work figure, and 70 relates to permitted exceptions.

What is the legal status of Part B of the STCW Code?

Part A of the STCW Code is mandatory, while Part B contains recommended guidance to help Parties apply the Convention (STCW Code, Parts A and B).

Under STCW, what is the maximum validity of a seafarer's medical certificate for a person aged 18 or over?

Regulation I/9 limits medical certificates to a maximum of two years (one year if under 18). Six years applies only to colour vision certificates.

Which of the following gives a PSC inspector clear grounds to assess a crew's watchkeeping competence under Regulation I/4?

Regulation I/4 lists collision, grounding or stranding among the clear grounds for an assessment. Crew nationality and dry-dock dates are not grounds.

Under STCW, hours of rest may be divided into how many periods?

Section A-VIII/1 allows rest to be split into no more than two periods, one at least 6 hours long, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods.
