MARPOL Annex I – Oil: What It Means on Board

Quick answer

MARPOL Annex I is the part of the MARPOL Convention that controls oil pollution from ships. It sets discharge limits, including 15 ppm for machinery space bilges and separate rules for tanker cargo residues, and it creates the IOPP Certificate, the Oil Record Book and the SOPEP. Port State Control inspectors compare the Oil Record Book with the oily water separator, the tanks and the crew's knowledge, and all three must agree.

What Annex I is and when it came into force

MARPOL is the IMO convention for preventing pollution from ships. It has six annexes, and Annex I deals with oil. Oil here means petroleum in any form: crude, fuel oil, sludge, oil refuse and refined products. Products covered by Annex II as noxious liquids are excluded.

Key dates you can rely on:

  • 2 October 1983: MARPOL 73/78 entered into force, and Annex I came into force with it.
  • 1 January 2007: a fully revised Annex I came into force. The regulations were renumbered at that point, so older textbooks quote different regulation numbers from current ones.

Annex I has been amended many times since. Examples include requirements on fuel tank protection, sludge tank piping, electronic record books and the Polar Code.

Some details are set by the flag Administration, such as approval of equipment and plans and the wording of national penalties. Your company SMS will add its own procedures on top. Always work to whichever is stricter.

How Annex I is structured

Knowing the chapter layout helps you find things quickly and answer oral exam questions.

Chapter Subject Examples
1 General Definitions, application, exceptions (Reg 4)
2 Surveys and certification Surveys, IOPP Certificate, port State control on operational requirements (Reg 11)
3 Machinery spaces of all ships Sludge tanks (Reg 12), fuel tank protection (Reg 12A), standard discharge connection (Reg 13), oil filtering equipment (Reg 14), discharge control (Reg 15), Oil Record Book Part I (Reg 17)
4 Cargo area of oil tankers Segregated ballast, double hulls (Reg 19), crude oil washing, discharge control (Reg 34), Oil Record Book Part II (Reg 36)
5 Oil pollution incidents Shipboard Oil Pollution Emergency Plan (Reg 37)
6 Reception facilities Port State obligations (Reg 38)
7 Fixed and floating platforms Special requirements (Reg 39)
8 Ship-to-ship transfer of oil cargo STS operations plan and notification (Regs 40–42)
9 Antarctic area Ban on carrying and using heavy grade oils (Reg 43)
10 onward Verification of compliance and Polar Code provisions III Code audits, polar requirements

The main split to remember is between Chapter 3 and Chapter 4. Chapter 3 applies to every ship's engine room. Chapter 4 applies only to the cargo area of oil tankers.

The discharge rules: machinery spaces vs cargo area

Annex I has two separate discharge regimes. Candidates often mix them up in orals.

Machinery space bilges and fuel tanks (Reg 15), ships of 400 GT and above. Outside special areas, oily mixtures may go overboard only if all of these apply:

  • the ship is proceeding en route;
  • the mixture has been processed through approved oil filtering equipment;
  • the oil content of the effluent, without dilution, does not exceed 15 ppm;
  • on tankers, the mixture does not come from cargo pump room bilges;
  • the mixture is not mixed with oil cargo residues.

Inside special areas the 15 ppm rule still applies. In addition, the equipment must have an alarm and automatic stopping device that shuts off the discharge when 15 ppm is exceeded. Ships of 10,000 GT and above must have this alarm and automatic stopping arrangement in any case (Reg 14).

There is no distance from land in the machinery space rule. Ships below 400 GT have their own provisions, which flag and port rules can tighten further.

Tanker cargo area (Reg 34). Discharge of oil from the cargo area is allowed only if all of these apply:

  • the tanker is outside special areas;
  • it is more than 50 nautical miles from the nearest land;
  • it is proceeding en route;
  • the instantaneous rate of discharge is no more than 30 litres per nautical mile;
  • the total quantity discharged is within 1/30,000 of the previous cargo for tankers delivered after 31 December 1979, or 1/15,000 for older tankers;
  • the oil discharge monitoring and control system (ODME) and the slop tank arrangement are in operation.

The Annex I special areas are:

  • Mediterranean Sea
  • Baltic Sea
  • Black Sea
  • Red Sea
  • Gulfs area
  • Gulf of Aden
  • Antarctic area
  • North West European waters
  • Oman area of the Arabian Sea
  • Southern South African waters

In the Antarctic area, oil discharge is prohibited altogether. Under the Polar Code (Annex I Chapter 11), any discharge of oil or oily mixtures is also prohibited in Arctic waters. Special-area requirements for some designated areas take effect only once reception facilities are confirmed, so check the current IMO special areas table. Your passage plan should mark special area boundaries so that the engine room knows when the rules change.

Exceptions (Reg 4). Discharges are excused only in limited cases:

  • to secure the safety of the ship or save life at sea;
  • when oil escapes because of damage to the ship or its equipment, provided all reasonable precautions were taken after the damage occurred or the discharge was discovered to prevent or minimise it. This does not apply if the owner or master acted with intent to cause damage, or recklessly and with knowledge that damage would probably result;
  • discharges of substances containing oil, approved by the Administration, when used to combat specific pollution incidents to minimise the damage.

An exception never covers a deliberate bypass.

Certificates and records Annex I creates on board

IOPP Certificate (International Oil Pollution Prevention Certificate)

  • Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above.
  • Valid for up to five years, subject to annual and intermediate surveys.
  • Comes with a Supplement listing the equipment fitted on board:
    • Form A for ships other than oil tankers;
    • Form B for oil tankers.
  • The Supplement must match what is actually fitted. A replaced oily water separator or bilge alarm that is not reflected in the Supplement is a classic deficiency.

Oil Record Book Part I (machinery space operations)

  • Required for the same ships as the IOPP: tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above.
  • Each operation is recorded under a code letter:
    • A: ballasting or cleaning of fuel oil tanks
    • B: discharge of dirty ballast or cleaning water from those tanks
    • C: collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge)
    • D: non-automatic discharge or disposal of bilge water
    • E: automatic discharge of bilge water
    • F: condition of the oil filtering equipment
    • G: accidental or other exceptional discharges
    • H: bunkering of fuel or bulk lube oil
    • I: additional operational procedures and general remarks
  • IMO guidance (MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2) gives model entries for each code. Use it.

Oil Record Book Part II (cargo and ballast operations)

  • Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above.
  • Normally kept by the Chief Officer.

Rules that apply to both Parts

  • Each entry is signed by the officer in charge of the operation.
  • Each completed page is signed by the Master.
  • The book must be kept readily available for inspection.
  • It must be retained for three years after the last entry.
  • Annex I now permits an electronic record book if it is approved by the Administration. Check what your flag accepts before relying on it.

SOPEP (Reg 37)

  • Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above.
  • Must be approved by the Administration.
  • Many ships carry a combined SMPEP covering both oil and noxious liquids.
  • Oil tankers of 5,000 DWT and above must also have prompt access to shore-based computerised damage stability and residual strength calculations.

STS operations plan (Chapter 8)

  • Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above that transfer oil cargo at sea.
  • Must be approved by the Administration.
  • Records of STS operations are kept with the plan for three years.
  • Coastal State notification requirements apply.

What it means in daily work – a worked scenario

Scenario: Your ship is crossing the Mediterranean and the bilge holding tank is near full. The Chief Engineer asks you, as 3rd Engineer, to pump it out.

  1. Confirm the position and condition.
    • The ship is in a special area.
    • It must be en route.
    • The OWS must have a working 15 ppm alarm and automatic stop.
    • Check your SMS. Many companies require the Chief Engineer's permission, or the Master's notification, before any overboard discharge. Some ban discharge in certain areas entirely.
  2. Check the equipment.
    • Confirm the 15 ppm monitor is within its accuracy-check validity.
    • Confirm the three-way valve recirculates when the alarm trips.
    • Confirm the overboard valve seal number matches the seal log.
  3. Record the start soundings of the bilge holding tank.
  4. Run the OWS and stay with it. Never leave it unattended. Never dilute the sample line with fresh water to fool the monitor.
  5. Record the stop time, ship's position and end soundings. Calculate the quantity discharged.
  6. Make the ORB entry under code D. Include:
    • quantity;
    • tank;
    • start and stop times;
    • positions;
    • method (through 15 ppm equipment).
  7. Sign the entry. The Chief Engineer reviews it.

If you make a mistake in the ORB, do not tear out pages or use correction fluid. Strike through with a single line so the original stays legible, then sign and date the struck-through entry and make the corrected entry after it, in line with MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2. Your SMS will specify the exact method.

On deck, Annex I shows up in other ways:

  • bunkering checklists and drip trays;
  • scupper plugs in place before oil transfers;
  • SOPEP lockers stocked and inventoried;
  • SOPEP drills;
  • on tankers, ODME logs and slop tank management.

Ratings matter here too. The AB plugging scuppers and the Oiler watching a sounding pipe during bunkering are both doing Annex I work.

What Port State Control typically checks

Port State officers check certificates under MARPOL Article 5. Where there are clear grounds, Reg 11 also lets them check whether the master and crew are familiar with essential oil pollution prevention procedures. In practice the inspection looks at:

  • The IOPP Certificate and Supplement: valid, endorsed for annual surveys, and matching the equipment fitted.
  • ORB Part I consistency: the inspector compares entries against:
    • actual tank soundings;
    • sludge and bilge tank capacities in the Supplement;
    • incinerator and boiler burning of sludge;
    • shore receipts for sludge landed.

Unexplained gains or losses of sludge are a red flag. - OWS demonstration: you may be asked to run it, show the 15 ppm alarm stopping the discharge, and explain the process. - Piping integrity: signs of bypasses, flexible hoses, blank flanges removed, or fresh paint over modified pipework (a "magic pipe"). Sludge tank piping must not have overboard connections other than the standard discharge connection. - Overboard valve seals and the seal log, where the company uses them. - SOPEP: approved, contact list current, equipment present. - For tankers: - ORB Part II; - ODME records and printouts; - the COW manual where applicable; - the STS plan if the ship does STS operations.

Inspectors often question the person who runs the OWS. If that engineer cannot explain the procedure, it can lead to a deficiency or detention, even with a perfect logbook. Falsified records are treated as a serious offence under the national law of many port States, and individuals as well as companies have been prosecuted.

Oral exam practice questions

Examiners for engineering and deck CoCs commonly ask questions like these. Practise answering them aloud.

  • What is the discharge criterion for machinery space bilges outside a special area? En route, through approved filtering equipment, 15 ppm or less undiluted, not from cargo pump room bilges, not mixed with cargo residues.
  • What changes inside a special area? The equipment must have an alarm and automatic stop at 15 ppm. In the Antarctic area, no discharge at all. Under the Polar Code, oil discharge is also prohibited in Arctic waters.
  • Who signs the ORB and how long is it kept? The officer in charge signs each entry and the Master signs each page. Retained for three years after the last entry.
  • What is code C used for? Collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge), including incineration and landing ashore.
  • What is the purpose of the standard discharge connection? It allows sludge and oily bilge water to be discharged to reception facilities using a common flange size.
  • When is a discharge excused? To secure safety or save life. After damage to the ship or its equipment, provided all reasonable precautions were taken after the damage or discovery of the discharge, and the owner or master did not act with intent to cause damage or recklessly with knowledge that damage would probably result. Also when Administration-approved substances containing oil are used to combat a specific pollution incident.
  • What do you do if the OWS fails? Stop discharging and retain on board. Record the failure (code F or I as appropriate). Report to the Chief Engineer and company. Land the oily water ashore if needed.
Key points
  • Annex I entered into force on 2 October 1983. The revised, renumbered Annex I applies from 1 January 2007.
  • Machinery space discharges must be en route, through approved equipment, and at no more than 15 ppm undiluted.
  • Tanker cargo-area discharge needs more than 50 nm from land, 30 litres per nm maximum rate, a working ODME, and no special area.
  • The IOPP Certificate and ORB Part I apply to oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above.
  • Oil Record Books must be retained for three years after the last entry and must match the actual soundings.
  • Oil discharge is banned in the Antarctic area, which also bans heavy grade oil, and in Arctic waters under the Polar Code.
  • PSC tests crew knowledge and OWS operation where there are clear grounds, not just the paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 15 ppm rule in MARPOL Annex I?

It is the maximum oil content, measured without dilution, allowed in machinery space bilge water discharged overboard. The water must be processed through approved oil filtering equipment while the ship is en route. Inside special areas, the equipment must also stop the discharge automatically if 15 ppm is exceeded.

How long must the Oil Record Book be kept on board?

Both Part I and Part II must be kept readily available for inspection. They must be retained for three years after the last entry. Your company may require originals to be sent ashore after that, but the three-year minimum applies on board.

Which ships need an IOPP Certificate?

Oil tankers of 150 GT and above and all other ships of 400 GT and above engaged in voyages to ports or terminals under the jurisdiction of other Parties. The Administration, or a recognised organisation on its behalf, issues it after an initial survey. It is valid for up to five years, with annual and intermediate surveys.

Can I discharge bilge water in the Mediterranean?

Yes, provided all the Reg 15 conditions are met and the filtering equipment has a 15 ppm alarm and automatic stopping device. Check your SMS first, as many companies restrict or forbid discharge in special areas. The only Annex I special area with a total ban is the Antarctic area. Oil discharge is also prohibited in Arctic waters under the Polar Code.

What is the difference between ORB Part I and Part II?

Part I records machinery space operations such as bilge, sludge, fuel tank and bunkering operations, and every ship in scope keeps it. Part II is kept only by oil tankers and records cargo and ballast operations: loading, discharging, crude oil washing, slop handling and discharges through the ODME.

Test yourself

What is the maximum oil content allowed in machinery space bilge water discharged overboard from a ship of 400 GT and above?

Machinery space discharges must not exceed 15 ppm without dilution. The 30 litres per nm rate applies only to tanker cargo-area discharges. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Reg 15.

How long must an Oil Record Book be retained on board?

The ORB must be kept readily available for inspection for three years after the last entry. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regs 17 and 36.

An oil tanker wants to discharge oily mixtures from its cargo area. How far from the nearest land must it be, among other conditions?

Cargo-area discharge requires the tanker to be more than 50 nm from land, en route, outside special areas, with the ODME and slop tanks in operation. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Reg 34.

Under which ORB Part I code would you record incinerating sludge?

Code C covers collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge). Code D is for bilge water and Code H is for bunkering. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Appendix III; MEPC.1/Circ.736/Rev.2.

Which ships must hold an IOPP Certificate?

The IOPP Certificate applies to oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regs 6 and 7.
