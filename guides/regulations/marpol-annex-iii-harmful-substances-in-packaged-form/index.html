MARPOL Annex III – Harmful substances in packaged form: a seafarer's guide

Quick answer

MARPOL Annex III prevents pollution from harmful substances (marine pollutants) carried by sea in packages, containers, portable tanks and vehicles. It sets rules on packing, marking, labelling, documentation, stowage and quantity limits, and it bans jettisoning except to save the ship or life. In practice it works through the IMDG Code. On board, it shows up as the dangerous goods manifest or stowage plan, marine pollutant marks, and checks by port state control.

What Annex III is and when it came into force

Annex III is one of the six annexes to MARPOL. Its full subject is the prevention of pollution by harmful substances carried by sea in packaged form. Annex I covers oil in bulk and Annex II covers noxious liquids in bulk. Annex III covers the drums, bags, IBCs, containers and tank containers that general cargo ships, container ships, ro-ros and multipurpose vessels carry every day.

Key dates and status:

  • Original Annex III entered into force on 1 July 1992.
  • A revised Annex III was adopted by MEPC resolution MEPC.193(61) and entered into force on 1 January 2014. The revision brought it into line with the IMDG Code.
  • Annex III is an optional annex. A State can ratify MARPOL without accepting it. In practice, most major flag and port States have accepted it.

The point to remember is that Annex III states the principles in law. The detailed technical rules sit in the IMDG Code, which has been mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII since 1 January 2004. When you apply Annex III on board, you are really applying the IMDG Code's marine pollutant provisions.

What counts as a 'harmful substance' and 'packaged form'

Harmful substances under Annex III are those identified as marine pollutants in the IMDG Code. Substances that meet the criteria in the appendix to Annex III also count. Those criteria are based on aquatic toxicity, bioaccumulation and degradability.

Packaged form means the forms of containment specified in the IMDG Code. These include:

  • Single and combination packagings (drums, jerricans, boxes, bags)
  • IBCs and large packagings
  • Freight containers and other cargo transport units
  • Portable tanks, and road and rail tank vehicles

Two practical points catch people out:

  • A marine pollutant is not always a separate class. Many marine pollutants are also Class 3, 6.1, 8 and so on. Their environmental hazard is shown by the marine pollutant mark on top of the class label. Where a substance has no other hazard, it is usually shipped under UN 3077 or UN 3082 (environmentally hazardous substance, solid or liquid, n.o.s.), Class 9.
  • Empty packagings count too. A packaging that previously held a harmful substance is treated as a harmful substance itself. This applies unless adequate precautions have been taken to ensure no harmful residue remains, such as cleaning.

How the Annex is structured

Annex III is short: eight regulations plus an appendix. In plain terms:

Topic What it requires
Application Carriage of harmful substances in packaged form is prohibited except in accordance with the Annex. The IMDG Code provides the detail.
Packing Packages must be adequate to minimise the hazard to the marine environment, given the specific contents.
Marking and labelling Packages are durably marked or labelled to show they contain a harmful substance, in accordance with the IMDG Code. The IMDG Code sets the detail: proper shipping name or technical name, marine pollutant mark, and marks that remain identifiable after at least three months' immersion in the sea.
Documentation Transport information follows the IMDG Code and must be made available to persons designated by the port State authority. Under the IMDG Code, this includes shipping documents identifying marine pollutants and a special list, manifest or detailed stowage plan on board.
Stowage Harmful substances are properly stowed and secured to minimise hazards to the marine environment without impairing ship and crew safety.
Quantity limitations Certain substances may be prohibited or limited in quantity per ship for scientific and technical reasons.
Exceptions Jettisoning is prohibited except to secure the ship's safety or save life at sea. Leakages may be washed overboard only under appropriate measures and where safety allows.
Port State control Port States may inspect operational requirements when there are clear grounds to doubt crew familiarity with procedures.

The appendix sets out the criteria used to identify harmful substances in packaged form.

What it means for crew in daily work

Who does what varies by ship type and company SMS. On a large container ship, stowage planning is largely done ashore and the ship verifies it. On a general cargo or multipurpose ship, the chief officer may plan everything. The typical tasks are:

Before loading

  • Check the dangerous goods manifest or list against the stowage plan. Marine pollutants must be identified.
  • Confirm segregation and stowage meet the IMDG Code. Under-deck or protected stowage is generally preferred for marine pollutants where the Code allows it, but follow the Code's entry for that substance.
  • Make sure the ship's Document of Compliance for dangerous goods, where required, covers the classes and spaces planned.

During loading and the voyage

  • Look for the marine pollutant mark on packages and on cargo transport units. Report missing, damaged or illegible marks, or leaking units, to the officer of the watch or chief officer.
  • Include dangerous goods and marine pollutant stows in lashing checks, especially in heavy weather.
  • Know where the EmS Guide and the Medical First Aid Guide are, and how to find the relevant spillage schedule.

If something leaks or goes overboard

  • Treat it as a dangerous goods emergency first. Personal safety and the ship come before the environment.
  • Washing a spill overboard is only acceptable where the Annex III conditions are met and the company procedures and EmS guidance allow it. It is not a default clean-up method.
  • The master must report losses or probable losses of harmful substances in packaged form under MARPOL Article 8 and Protocol I. This includes containers lost overboard.

Records and documents on board

Annex III does not create a dedicated MARPOL certificate. There is no 'Annex III certificate' equivalent to the IOPP. Its paper trail is the cargo documentation:

  • Dangerous goods transport documents from the shipper. These use the proper shipping or correct technical name and identify marine pollutants. They include a declaration that the cargo is correctly packed, marked and labelled.
  • Container or vehicle packing certificate for goods packed into a cargo transport unit, as required by the IMDG Code.
  • Special list, manifest or detailed stowage plan on board, showing the harmful substances and their location. Under the IMDG Code, a copy is also kept ashore by the owner or their representative until the goods are discharged.
  • Document of Compliance for ships carrying dangerous goods, where applicable. This is a SOLAS requirement, not MARPOL, but PSC usually checks it together with the manifest.

Retention periods, electronic formats and who signs the ship's copy of the manifest depend on the flag Administration and company SMS. Check your own SMS.

What port state control typically looks at

Annex III itself allows port State officers to inspect operational requirements where there are clear grounds that the master or crew are not familiar with essential procedures. In practice, PSC officers commonly:

  • Compare the DG manifest with the stowage plan and spot-check actual container positions.
  • Check that marine pollutant marks and class placards are present on units in view.
  • Ask the duty officer to locate a marine pollutant on the plan and explain the EmS spillage procedure for it.
  • Check that a current edition of the IMDG Code, with its supplement, is available on board.
  • Look at the DG Document of Compliance and whether the actual stowage complies with it.
  • Ask how a lost container or package would be reported.

A vague answer to the question 'where are your marine pollutants stowed?' is a classic clear ground for a deeper inspection.

Oral exam corner: typical examiner questions

Q: Is Annex III mandatory? It is optional for States when ratifying MARPOL. If your flag or the port State is a party, it applies to you. Most are.

Q: How do you know a substance is a marine pollutant? From the IMDG Code's Dangerous Goods List and the marine pollutant mark on the package or unit. The transport document must also say so.

Q: Can you dump a leaking drum of marine pollutant? Only if it is necessary to secure the safety of the ship or save life at sea. Otherwise, contain it and deal with it according to the EmS and the SMS. If anything does go overboard, it must be reported.

Q: What is the link between Annex III and SOLAS Chapter VII? Annex III covers the environmental hazard and SOLAS VII covers the safety hazard. Both are implemented through the IMDG Code.

Worked scenario: In heavy weather, you discover that a 20 ft tank container marked as a marine pollutant has shifted and is weeping at a valve. The steps are:

  1. Warn the bridge and keep people clear.
  2. Identify the UN number from the manifest.
  3. Consult the EmS fire and spillage schedules and the MFAG.
  4. Alter course or speed to reduce motion if needed.
  5. Contain the spill and resecure the unit only when it is safe to do so.
  6. Inform the company.
  7. Report any loss to the sea in line with Protocol I.
  8. Record the event as the SMS requires.
Key points
  • Annex III covers marine pollutants carried in packages, containers, portable tanks and vehicles, not in bulk.
  • The original Annex entered into force on 1 July 1992; the revised Annex has applied since 1 January 2014.
  • The detailed rules come from the IMDG Code, which also implements SOLAS Chapter VII.
  • Packages must carry the IMDG Code proper shipping name (with technical name where required) and the marine pollutant mark, and the ship must hold a manifest or stowage plan showing their location.
  • Jettisoning is banned except to save the ship or life at sea; losses must be reported under MARPOL Protocol I.
  • There is no specific Annex III certificate; PSC checks the DG documents, the actual stowage and crew familiarity.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between MARPOL Annex III and the IMDG Code?

Annex III is the MARPOL law that requires harmful substances in packaged form to be controlled. The IMDG Code contains the detailed technical rules for packing, marking, documentation, segregation and stowage. In practice, you comply with Annex III by following the IMDG Code's marine pollutant provisions.

Does MARPOL Annex III apply to bulk carriers and tankers?

It applies to any ship carrying harmful substances in packaged form, whatever the ship type. Bulk cargoes are covered elsewhere: oil under Annex I, noxious liquids under Annex II, and solid bulk cargo residues under Annex V. A tanker or bulker carrying packaged marine pollutants as cargo would still be subject to Annex III.

What does the marine pollutant mark look like?

It is the IMDG Code mark showing a fish and a tree. It is displayed on packages and on cargo transport units carrying marine pollutants, alongside any class labels or placards. The IMDG Code sets its size and the cases where small packagings are exempt.

Do I need to report a container lost overboard under MARPOL?

Yes, if it contained harmful substances. MARPOL Article 8 and Protocol I require reports of actual or probable losses of harmful substances in packaged form, including freight containers. Your SMS will set out the reporting chain to the coastal State and the company.

Is there an Annex III certificate on board?

No. Annex III does not create a certificate like the IOPP. Compliance is shown through the DG transport documents, packing certificates, the manifest or stowage plan, and the SOLAS Document of Compliance for dangerous goods where the ship needs one.

Test yourself

When did the original MARPOL Annex III enter into force?

The original Annex III entered into force on 1 July 1992. The 1983 date is when MARPOL 73/78 and Annex I entered into force, 2004 is when the IMDG Code became mandatory, and 2005 is Annex VI. (MARPOL Annex III)

How are harmful substances identified for the purposes of Annex III?

Harmful substances are marine pollutants under the IMDG Code or substances meeting the appendix criteria. Many belong to classes other than Class 9. (MARPOL Annex III, Regulation 1 and Appendix)

An empty drum that previously held a marine pollutant is shipped back uncleaned. How is it treated?

Previously used empty packagings are treated as harmful substances unless adequate precautions ensure no harmful residue. (MARPOL Annex III, Regulation 1)

When may packaged harmful substances be jettisoned?

Jettisoning is prohibited except to secure the ship's safety or save life at sea. There is no distance-from-land allowance. (MARPOL Annex III, Exceptions regulation)

Under Annex III and the IMDG Code, what must a ship carrying harmful substances have on board?

The ship must carry a special list, manifest or detailed stowage plan identifying the harmful substances and where they are stowed. No Annex III certificate or record book exists. (MARPOL Annex III, Regulation 4, and the IMDG Code documentation provisions)
