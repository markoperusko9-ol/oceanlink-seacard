ISM Code: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

The ISM Code is the international standard for the safe management of ships and pollution prevention, made mandatory through SOLAS Chapter IX. It requires the Company to run a documented Safety Management System (SMS), certified by a Document of Compliance ashore and a Safety Management Certificate on board. For crew, it shows up as procedures, checklists, drills, familiarisation, non-conformity and near-miss reports, and audits.

What the ISM Code is and where it comes from

ISM stands for the International Management Code for the Safe Operation of Ships and for Pollution Prevention. IMO adopted it in 1993 by Assembly resolution A.741(18). It became mandatory when SOLAS Chapter IX, 'Management for the Safe Operation of Ships', was brought into force.

The Code was phased in:

Date Ships covered
1 July 1998 Passenger ships (including passenger high-speed craft), and oil tankers, chemical tankers, gas carriers, bulk carriers and cargo high-speed craft of 500 GT and upwards
1 July 2002 Other cargo ships and mobile offshore drilling units of 500 GT and upwards

Government ships on non-commercial service are outside SOLAS. Some administrations apply ISM, or a reduced national version, to smaller or domestic vessels. Check what your own flag requires.

The idea behind the Code is simple. Many accidents trace back to poor management, not just poor seamanship. ISM does not tell you how to run your ship line by line. Instead, it sets out what a management system must achieve. The Company then writes its own SMS to meet those requirements, and the system is audited against both the Code and the Company's own procedures.

How the Code is structured

The Code has a preamble and two parts. Know the section headings: examiners like asking which section covers what.

Part A – Implementation

  1. General: definitions, objectives, application and the functional requirements of an SMS
  2. Safety and environmental-protection policy
  3. Company responsibilities and authority
  4. Designated person(s)
  5. Master's responsibility and authority
  6. Resources and personnel
  7. Shipboard operations
  8. Emergency preparedness
  9. Reports and analysis of non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences
  10. Maintenance of the ship and equipment
  11. Documentation
  12. Company verification, review and evaluation

Part B – Certification and verification

  1. Certification and periodical verification
  2. Interim certification
  3. Verification
  4. Forms of certificates

The Code has been amended several times since 1993. Always work from the current consolidated edition. IMO has also issued guidelines for administrations on how to audit and certify under the Code, currently the revised guidelines in resolution A.1118(30).

The key roles: Company, DPA and Master

The Company. In ISM terms, 'Company' does not just mean the registered owner. It means whichever owner, manager or bareboat charterer has taken over responsibility for operating the ship and accepted the duties of the Code. That is the organisation named on the Document of Compliance. The Company must:

  • define responsibility, authority and the lines of communication for everyone whose work affects safety and pollution prevention
  • provide enough resources and shore support for the DPA to do the job

The Designated Person Ashore (DPA). Section 4 requires the Company to designate one or more persons ashore. The DPA links the ship to the Company and has direct access to the highest level of management. The role covers:

  • monitoring the safety and pollution-prevention side of each ship's operation
  • making sure resources and shore support are actually provided

Most SMSs require the DPA's name and contact details to be posted on board, and any crew member can contact the DPA, not only the Master.

The Master. Section 5 makes the Master responsible for:

  • putting the Company policy into practice on board
  • motivating the crew
  • issuing orders and instructions in a clear and simple way
  • verifying that requirements are followed
  • periodically reviewing the SMS and reporting its deficiencies to shore management

The SMS must clearly state that the Master has overriding authority. This means the Master can take whatever decision they judge necessary for safety and pollution prevention, and can ask the Company for help.

What ISM means in your daily work

Most seafarers never open the Code itself, but they work inside the SMS every day. In practice:

  • Familiarisation. Section 6 requires the Company to give new joiners, and anyone transferred to new safety-related duties, proper familiarisation with those duties. Instructions that are essential before sailing must be identified, documented and given. That is why you sign a familiarisation checklist on joining.
  • Procedures and checklists. Section 7 covers key shipboard operations such as:
    • navigation and watchkeeping
    • cargo and bunkering
    • permit-to-work, enclosed space entry and hot work

Checklists are filled in as the job is done, not afterwards. - Working language. Instructions must be in a language the crew understand, and the crew must be able to communicate effectively when carrying out their duties. - Drills. Section 8 requires the Company to identify potential emergencies, set up procedures for them, and run drills and exercises. The SMS should also make sure the Company organisation can respond to an emergency at any time. - Reporting. Section 9 requires non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences to be reported to the Company, then investigated and analysed, with corrective action taken. Near-miss reporting usually sits here. How 'just' or 'no-blame' the culture is depends on the Company. The Code expects reporting, so use the system. - Maintenance. Section 10 requires inspections at suitable intervals, reporting and correction of defects, and records. It also requires the Company to identify equipment whose sudden failure could cause a hazardous situation, and to promote its reliability. In engine-room and bridge practice this is the 'critical equipment' list in the planned maintenance system. Testing of standby equipment that is not in continuous use is part of it.

Worked example – a defect on watch. On a night watch, the third engineer finds the standby steering pump will not start on changeover. Under a typical SMS, the steps are:

  1. Inform the bridge and the chief engineer immediately.
  2. Record the defect in the planned maintenance system or defect log.
  3. Raise a defect or non-conformity report under the SMS procedure.
  4. The chief engineer and Master assess the risk and any operational restrictions, and report to the Company.
  5. Corrective action and its verification are recorded so the report can be closed out.

If the defect is never written down, the next auditor or port state officer will find a gap between the paperwork and reality. That gap is exactly what ISM is designed to prevent.

Certificates and records the Code creates

Certificates

  • Document of Compliance (DOC). Issued to the Company for the ship types it manages. A copy is kept on board so the Master can show it on request. Its validity is subject to annual verification.
  • Safety Management Certificate (SMC). Issued to the individual ship after its SMS has been audited on board. Maximum validity is five years. At least one intermediate verification is required, normally between the second and third anniversary dates, unless the flag requires more.
  • Interim DOC / interim SMC. Used for a new Company or a newly acquired, delivered or reflagged ship. They allow the system to run long enough to be audited for full certification. The permitted durations are set out in Section 14 and the administration's rules. Your fleet department or the recognised organisation will confirm the dates.

Certification is done by the flag administration, or by a recognised organisation acting on its behalf.

Typical records on board

  • SMS manuals, kept as controlled documents
  • completed checklists and permits to work
  • familiarisation and training records
  • drill records
  • planned maintenance and defect records
  • non-conformity, accident and near-miss reports with corrective actions
  • Master's review of the SMS
  • internal audit reports
  • minutes of shipboard safety meetings

Section 11 requires documents to be controlled. Only valid, current versions should be in use, and obsolete ones should be promptly removed. An uncontrolled photocopy of an old procedure in the cargo control room is a classic audit finding.

Under Section 12, the Company must carry out internal audits on board and ashore at intervals not exceeding twelve months. The Code allows this to be extended in exceptional circumstances, by no more than three months.

What port state control and auditors look for

Port state control (PSC) officers do not carry out a full ISM audit. When they find deficiencies, though, they ask whether the SMS is actually working. Typical checks:

  • DOC copy and SMC are on board, valid and consistent: same Company, correct ship type, endorsements up to date
  • the crew know who the DPA is and how to contact them
  • drills are done and recorded, and crew can actually perform them when asked
  • critical equipment is maintained and tested, and defects are reported
  • earlier deficiencies were reported to the Company and closed out
  • the Master's review and the internal audits have taken place
  • familiarisation records exist for recent joiners

Worked scenario. A PSC officer finds three things:

  • the emergency fire pump will not deliver pressure
  • the fire drill record shows drills that the crew cannot describe
  • the pump defect does not appear in the maintenance system

Each item is a deficiency in its own right. Taken together, they suggest the SMS has failed to keep equipment and drills in order. The officer may therefore record an ISM-related deficiency as well.

Depending on how serious the findings are, the outcome may include:

  • detention
  • a requirement for corrective action before departure
  • an additional audit by the flag or recognised organisation

The exact approach and coding follow the procedures of the regional PSC regime or the national authority.

The Code defines a major non-conformity. It is an identifiable deviation that poses a serious threat to the safety of personnel or the ship, or a serious risk to the environment, and so needs immediate corrective action. Lack of effective and systematic implementation of a Code requirement also counts. A major non-conformity cannot simply be noted and left for later.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

These come up in deck and engine CoC orals. Answer in your own words and give a shipboard example.

  • What is the ISM Code and how is it made mandatory? An IMO code for safety management and pollution prevention, made mandatory by SOLAS Chapter IX.
  • What are the objectives of an SMS? Safe practices in ship operation and a safe working environment. Assessing risks and setting safeguards against them. Continuously improving the safety-management skills of personnel, including emergency preparedness.
  • Difference between DOC and SMC? The DOC belongs to the Company, with a copy on board. The SMC belongs to the ship.
  • Who is the DPA and why does the role exist? The link between ship and shore, with direct access to top management. It exists so that safety concerns do not get lost in the commercial chain.
  • What is the Master's overriding authority? The Master's right to take any decision needed for safety or pollution prevention, and to ask the Company for help.
  • What would you do if a procedure in the SMS was unsafe or unworkable? Do not quietly ignore it. Raise it through the Master as a non-conformity or SMS feedback so the Company can correct it, and in the meantime act safely.
  • What is critical equipment? Equipment and systems whose sudden failure could cause a hazardous situation. Give an example from your own ship, such as steering gear, the emergency generator or the emergency fire pump.
Key points
  • The ISM Code was adopted by IMO resolution A.741(18) and is made mandatory by SOLAS Chapter IX.
  • It became mandatory on 1 July 1998 for passenger ships, tankers, gas carriers, bulk carriers and cargo HSC, and on 1 July 2002 for other cargo ships and MODUs of 500 GT and upwards.
  • The Company holds the Document of Compliance; each ship holds a Safety Management Certificate valid for up to five years with an intermediate verification.
  • The DPA links ship and shore with direct access to the highest level of management, and any crew member may contact them.
  • The SMS must state the Master's overriding authority for safety and pollution prevention.
  • Non-conformities, accidents, hazardous occurrences and defects must be reported, investigated and closed out.
  • PSC looks for evidence that the SMS works in practice, not just that the paperwork exists.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ISM Code apply to ships under 500 GT?

Under SOLAS Chapter IX, cargo ships are covered from 500 GT and upwards, while passenger ships on international voyages are covered regardless of size. Some flag administrations apply ISM, or a simplified national safety management standard, to smaller or domestic vessels. Check your flag's rules and your Company SMS.

What is the difference between the DOC and the SMC?

The Document of Compliance is issued to the Company and shows that its management system meets the Code for the listed ship types; a copy is kept on board. The Safety Management Certificate is issued to an individual ship after its on-board SMS has been audited. A ship needs both to be valid.

Can an ordinary crew member contact the DPA directly?

Yes. The DPA is the link between the ship and the Company, with direct access to the highest level of management on safety and pollution-prevention matters. Contact details should be posted on board, and most SMSs explicitly say any crew member may use them, especially if a concern is not being addressed on board.

Is a near-miss report required by the ISM Code?

The Code requires reporting and analysis of non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences under Section 9. Near misses are normally reported through that system. The exact forms, categories and reporting culture are set by the Company SMS.

Can a ship be detained for ISM failures?

Yes. Where PSC finds deficiencies that show the SMS is not being implemented effectively, an ISM-related deficiency may be recorded. If serious, the ship can be detained or an additional audit may be required. How this is coded and followed up depends on the PSC regime and the flag administration.

Test yourself

From what date did the ISM Code become mandatory for oil tankers, bulk carriers and passenger ships?

SOLAS Chapter IX phased in ISM: 1 July 1998 for passenger ships, tankers, gas carriers, bulk carriers and cargo HSC, and 1 July 2002 for other cargo ships and mobile offshore drilling units of 500 GT and upwards. 2004 is the ISPS Code date. (SOLAS 1974 as amended, Chapter IX)

To whom is the Document of Compliance issued?

The DOC is issued to the Company that has accepted responsibility for operating the ship; the ship carries a copy. The ship itself receives the SMC. (ISM Code, Part B, Section 13)

What best describes the Designated Person Ashore?

Section 4 requires the Company to designate a person or persons ashore with direct access to top management to monitor safety and pollution prevention. The CSO is a separate ISPS role. (ISM Code, Section 4)

What is the maximum period of validity of a full-term Safety Management Certificate?

The SMC is issued for a period not exceeding five years, with at least one intermediate verification. Annual verification applies to the DOC, not the SMC. (ISM Code, Section 13)

Under the ISM Code, who must the SMS state has overriding authority to make decisions on safety and pollution prevention?

Section 5 requires the SMS to include a clear statement emphasising the Master's authority, including overriding authority and the right to request Company assistance. (ISM Code, Section 5)
