Port State Control: What Every Seafarer Needs to Know

Quick answer

Port State Control (PSC) is the right of a port's maritime authority to inspect foreign-flagged ships in its ports. Inspectors check that the ship, its crew and its operation meet the international conventions, and they can detain a ship that does not. For crew, PSC means your certificates, records, drills and equipment can be examined at any port call, and you may be asked to show you can actually operate the ship's safety and pollution-prevention equipment.

What Port State Control is, and why it exists

The flag state carries primary responsibility for its ships through surveys, certificates and its recognised organisations. Some flags and some owners do this poorly, so the conventions also allow the port state where a ship is calling to check foreign ships. That second line of defence is Port State Control.

PSC is not a single convention, so it has no single date of entry into force. The power to inspect is written into each major instrument:

  • SOLAS: control provisions in Chapter I, plus operational control in Chapter XI-1
  • MARPOL: the Articles, plus operational-requirement control regulations in individual Annexes (for example Annex I and Annex VI)
  • STCW: Article X and Regulation I/4, covering certificates and whether watchkeepers are actually competent
  • Load Lines 1966: Article 21
  • MLC, 2006: Regulation 5.2.1 for inspections and 5.2.2 for onshore complaints by seafarers
  • Other instruments, including the Tonnage, Anti-Fouling Systems and Ballast Water Management Conventions

IMO publishes harmonised guidance for port state control officers (PSCOs) in its Procedures for Port State Control. These are adopted by IMO Assembly resolution, for example Resolution A.1155(32), and are revised from time to time, so check for the latest version. These procedures cover how inspections are run, what counts as "clear grounds", detention criteria and reporting.

A key principle is "no more favourable treatment". A ship flying the flag of a state that has not ratified a convention is still inspected against that convention's standards when it calls at a port of a state that has.

How PSC is organised: regional MOUs and national regimes

Port states mostly cooperate through regional Memoranda of Understanding (MOUs). Members share inspection data so the same ship is not inspected in every port while a poor ship cannot hide.

The United States Coast Guard runs its own PSC programme and is not part of a regional MOU. Some states belong to more than one MOU.

Each regime has its own targeting system, deficiency action codes, publication of detentions and rules for refusing access to repeat offenders. The Paris MOU introduced its risk-based New Inspection Regime in 2011, and other MOUs use similar ship-risk profiling. The factors weighed are typically ship type and age, flag performance, recognised organisation performance, company performance and the ship's own deficiency and detention history.

The exact inspection intervals, ban thresholds and codes differ between regimes. Always check the current rules of the MOU you are trading in.

How an inspection runs, step by step

  1. Boarding. The PSCO shows identification and is usually met at the gangway and taken to the Master. Have the gangway log, visitor control and ISPS procedures followed properly, because this is the first thing the PSCO sees.
  2. Initial inspection. The PSCO checks the ship's certificates and documents. They also look at the general condition of the ship, its equipment and the crew's working and living conditions.
  3. Clear grounds. If something suggests the ship or crew does not substantially meet requirements, the PSCO has clear grounds for a more detailed inspection. Examples include:
    • missing or invalid certificates
    • obvious defects
    • crew who cannot communicate or perform key duties
    • a report or complaint
    • evidence from records
  4. Detailed inspection and operational checks. The PSCO may call for drills or equipment demonstrations, and may question crew on their duties.
  5. Deficiencies. Each deficiency is recorded with an action required. Typical actions are rectify before departure, rectify within a set period, rectify at the next port, or agree with the flag state. The precise codes vary by MOU.
  6. Detention. If deficiencies are clearly hazardous to safety, health or the environment, the ship is detained until they are fixed. The flag state is notified and the recognised organisation is usually involved.
  7. Report. The Master receives a copy of the inspection report.

The IMO procedures say ships must not be unduly detained or delayed, and SOLAS and MARPOL recognise a right to compensation where that happens. Each regime has a review or appeal route, normally through the flag state or the company.

What PSC typically checks

Certificates and documents. The exact list depends on ship type, size and flag. Common items are:

  • Safety certificates, Load Line, IOPP, IAPP and ISM DOC/SMC
  • ISSC, the Maritime Labour Certificate and DMLC Parts I and II
  • Ballast water certificate and management plan
  • Minimum safe manning document
  • Crew CoCs, flag endorsements and medical certificates

Records PSCOs read closely:

  • Oil Record Book and Garbage Record Book, cross-checked against equipment, sludge and bilge tank soundings and port receipts
  • Hours of rest records (STCW and MLC), compared with the watch schedule and overtime sheets
  • Drill records, maintenance records for LSA and FFA, and ISM non-conformities
  • Chart and publication corrections, passage plans and ECDIS records
  • Seafarer employment agreements and wage accounts under the MLC

Equipment and operation:

  • Emergency generator, emergency fire pump and steering gear, including emergency steering
  • Lifeboat engines, release gear and launching arrangements; liferafts and HRUs
  • Fire doors, fire dampers, quick-closing valves and remote stops
  • Oily water separator and 15 ppm alarm, ODME on tankers
  • Watertight and weathertight closures, vents and air pipes
  • GMDSS equipment, navigation lights and BNWAS
  • Accommodation, galley, food and drinking water, and medical stores

Concentrated Inspection Campaigns (CICs). These are usually run jointly by several MOUs over about three months each year. Each campaign focuses on one topic using a set questionnaire, so it is worth checking each year's announced topic before you trade into the region.

What PSC means for you in daily work

The best PSC preparation is simply running the ship properly every day. PSCOs are trained to spot the ship that "prepared for inspection".

  • Know your muster list duty. Expect a PSCO to stop a drill and ask a rating what their job is, or ask an officer to start the emergency fire pump.
  • Be honest with records. Rest hours copied from the schedule, ORB entries that do not match tank soundings and drills logged but never held are classic findings. They can also escalate into an ISM-related deficiency.
  • Keep your own documents valid. Check your CoC, endorsements, medical, STCW training certificates and passport before joining. An expired crew certificate is the ship's problem at PSC.
  • Know your equipment. Engine watchkeepers should be able to demonstrate the OWS and start the emergency generator; bridge and engine watchkeepers should know how to change over to emergency steering.
  • Communicate. The PSCO may assess whether crew can understand the working language of the ship.
  • Your complaint rights. Under the MLC, a seafarer can raise a complaint ashore with an authorised officer in port, such as a PSCO. This applies to matters like unpaid wages, rest hours or living conditions, and the complaint should be treated confidentially.

Pre-arrival PSC checklists are usually set by the company SMS. Use them as a genuine check, not a box-ticking exercise.

Worked scenario: the emergency fire pump test

A PSCO boards a bulk carrier, reviews the certificates and finds the fire drill record shows a drill every week. That is unusual, and it raises a question. The PSCO asks the duty officer to start the emergency fire pump and put water on deck.

What goes wrong. The duty officer is unsure where the pump is started from. A motorman eventually starts it locally, but the suction does not prime and no pressure reaches the hydrant.

Likely outcome. This gives the PSCO clear grounds for a more detailed inspection. A non-functioning emergency fire pump is generally regarded as grounds for detention, because the ship would have no firefighting water if the main pumps were lost. The PSCO may also record the following:

  • a deficiency on crew familiarity
  • a question over whether the drill records are genuine
  • possibly an ISM-related deficiency, because the planned maintenance and drill system did not catch the problem

What should have happened. The pump should be tested regularly under the planned maintenance system, with the priming arrangement checked. The duty officer and the engine watch should know the start procedure. Drills should be real and recorded honestly. Then the test takes a few minutes and the PSCO moves on.

Oral exam corner: PSC questions examiners ask

Q: Where does a port state get the authority to inspect a foreign ship? From the control provisions in each convention it has ratified, such as SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, Load Lines and the MLC. Procedures are harmonised by IMO resolution and regional MOUs.

Q: What are "clear grounds"? Evidence that the ship, its equipment or its crew do not substantially meet convention requirements. Clear grounds justify a more detailed inspection beyond the initial certificate and condition check.

Q: When is a ship detained? When deficiencies are serious enough to make the ship unsafe to proceed or a threat to health or the environment. The ship is held until they are rectified or until an acceptable arrangement is agreed.

Q: As Master, what do you do after a detention?

  1. Inform the company, the DPA and the flag state.
  2. Involve the recognised organisation where class or statutory items are involved.
  3. Rectify the deficiencies and request re-inspection.
  4. Raise the non-conformity under the SMS and find the root cause.
  5. Consider the appeal route if you believe the detention is unjustified.

Q: What is operational control? The PSCO checking that the crew can actually carry out essential shipboard procedures, not just that the equipment exists. This is provided for in SOLAS Chapter XI-1 and the MARPOL Annexes.

Key points
  • PSC is the right of a port state to inspect foreign ships against the conventions it has ratified; it has no single entry-into-force date.
  • IMO's Procedures for Port State Control, adopted by Assembly resolution, harmonise practice; regional MOUs and the USCG run the regimes.
  • Inspections start with certificates and general condition, and become detailed only where there are clear grounds.
  • Detention follows deficiencies clearly hazardous to safety, health or the environment; codes and ban rules vary by MOU.
  • Records such as the ORB, rest hours and drill logs are cross-checked against reality, so honesty matters.
  • Crew may be asked to demonstrate drills and equipment, so know your muster duty and your machinery.
  • Under the MLC, seafarers can complain ashore to a PSCO about wages, rest or living conditions.

Frequently asked questions

Can PSC inspect a ship whose flag state has not ratified a convention?

Yes. The 'no more favourable treatment' principle means ships of non-party flags are checked against the same standards when calling at a port of a party state. This stops owners avoiding inspection simply by choosing a non-party flag.

What is the difference between a deficiency and a detention?

A deficiency is any non-compliance found and recorded, with an action such as rectify before departure or within a set period. A detention is ordered only where deficiencies are serious enough that the ship is unsafe to sail or a threat to health or the environment. Most inspections with deficiencies do not end in detention.

Can a PSCO check my personal certificates?

Yes. Under STCW and the MLC, PSCOs can examine your CoC, flag endorsement, training certificates and medical fitness certificate. They can also assess whether you can perform your watchkeeping or emergency duties. Keep originals or acceptable copies as required by your flag and company.

Can I complain to port state control about unpaid wages or poor conditions?

Yes. The MLC provides for onshore complaints by seafarers in ports of ratifying states, and the PSCO can investigate. The complaint should be treated confidentially. Using the shipboard complaint procedure first is normal but not always compulsory.

Does a PSC detention affect my career?

A detention is recorded against the ship and company, not as a personal penalty. However, if it stems from your negligence or falsified records, the company and possibly the flag administration may take action. Honest records and real competence are your best protection.

Test yourself

What normally triggers a more detailed PSC inspection?

Detailed inspections follow clear grounds found during the initial inspection, not age alone. Reference: IMO Procedures for Port State Control (e.g. Resolution A.1155(32)).

Which of these runs its own port state control programme outside the regional MOUs?

The USCG operates a national PSC programme; India is in the Indian Ocean MOU, Japan in the Tokyo MOU and the UK in the Paris MOU.

Under which instrument can a seafarer make an onshore complaint to a port state officer about wages or living conditions?

MLC, 2006, Regulation 5.2.2 provides onshore complaint-handling procedures for seafarers.

A ship flies the flag of a state that has not ratified a convention. When calling at a party state's port, what applies?

The 'no more favourable treatment' principle ensures non-party ships meet the same standards (e.g. SOLAS 1974 Protocol 1988 and MARPOL Article 5).

What best describes 'operational control' during PSC?

Operational control assesses crew ability to perform key procedures such as drills and equipment operation. Reference: SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 4.
