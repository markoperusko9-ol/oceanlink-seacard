MARPOL Convention: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

MARPOL is the IMO convention that controls pollution from ships. Its six annexes cover oil, noxious liquids, packaged harmful substances, sewage, garbage and air emissions. On board it shows up as certificates, record books, plans and discharge rules. Port state control inspectors check that your paperwork, your tank soundings and your equipment all agree.

What MARPOL is and how it came about

MARPOL is the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships. It covers operational and accidental pollution. It does not cover dumping of waste loaded specifically for disposal at sea, which falls under a separate instrument, the London Convention.

Key dates:

  • 1973: IMO adopted the original convention. Not enough states ratified it.
  • 1978: A Protocol was adopted after a run of tanker casualties. It absorbed the 1973 text, which is why you still see "MARPOL 73/78".
  • 2 October 1983: The combined instrument entered into force.
  • 1997: A further Protocol added Annex VI (air pollution). It entered into force on 19 May 2005.

MARPOL is a living instrument. It is amended through IMO's Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC), usually by tacit acceptance. In practice, rules change every few years. Always work from the current consolidated edition and your company's latest circulars, not from memory or an old textbook.

Structure: Articles, Protocols and six Annexes

The convention has four layers:

  • Articles: general obligations, such as violations, inspection, undue delay and reporting.
  • Protocol I: reporting of incidents involving harmful substances.
  • Protocol II: arbitration.
  • Annexes: the technical regulations that drive daily work on board.
Annex Subject Entered into force
I Oil 2 October 1983
II Noxious liquid substances in bulk 2 October 1983 (in practice applied from 6 April 1987)
III Harmful substances in packaged form 1 July 1992
IV Sewage 27 September 2003
V Garbage 31 December 1988
VI Air pollution and energy efficiency 19 May 2005

A state joining MARPOL must accept Annexes I and II. Annexes III to VI are optional. Most major flag and port states have accepted all six. Even so, check the position for any particular state rather than assuming it.

Several annexes define special areas where discharges are tighter or banned outright. Examples are the Mediterranean, the Baltic and the Antarctic area. Annex VI also defines Emission Control Areas (ECAs) for sulphur and/or NOx. The lists differ between annexes, so one sea area can be special for one annex and not for another.

Certificates, record books and plans on board

Surveys follow the harmonised system: initial, annual, intermediate and renewal. Certificates are valid for a maximum of five years, subject to endorsement.

Certificates

  • IOPP: International Oil Pollution Prevention Certificate (Annex I).
    • Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above.
    • Its Supplement lists the equipment fitted: Form A for ships other than oil tankers, Form B for oil tankers.
  • NLS Certificate (Annex II): for ships carrying noxious liquid substances in bulk. Chemical tankers certified under the IBC Code normally hold a Certificate of Fitness that serves this purpose.
  • ISPP: International Sewage Pollution Prevention Certificate (Annex IV).
  • IAPP: International Air Pollution Prevention Certificate (Annex VI).
  • IEE: International Energy Efficiency Certificate (Annex VI).
  • EIAPP: Engine International Air Pollution Prevention Certificate. Issued for individual diesel engines under the NOx Technical Code, and kept with the engine's Technical File.

Annex V (garbage) has no certificate. It relies on plans, placards and the record book.

Record books and plans

  • Oil Record Book:
    • Part I covers machinery space operations.
    • Part II covers cargo and ballast operations on oil tankers.
    • Keep for three years after the last entry.
  • Cargo Record Book (Annex II), plus the Procedures and Arrangements Manual.
  • Garbage Record Book (ships of 400 GT and above, ships certified to carry 15 or more persons on voyages to ports or offshore terminals under the jurisdiction of another Party, and fixed and floating platforms). Keep for at least two years after the last entry.
  • Garbage Management Plan (100 GT and above, or 15 or more persons) and placards (12 m and over).
  • SOPEP: Shipboard Oil Pollution Emergency Plan. Required for oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above. NLS ships carry an SMPEP, often combined with the SOPEP.
  • Annex VI records:
    • Ozone-depleting substances record book, where required.
    • Fuel changeover records for ECAs.
    • Engine Technical Files and Record Books of Engine Parameters.
    • The SEEMP.
  • Bunker Delivery Notes: keep on board for three years.
  • MARPOL fuel samples: keep under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for at least 12 months.

Approved electronic record books are now permitted. Whether you use paper or electronic depends on what your flag administration has approved and what your company uses.

What MARPOL means in your daily work

Engine department

  • Machinery space bilge water may only go overboard while the ship is en route, through approved oil filtering equipment, with effluent not exceeding 15 ppm.
    • Inside Annex I special areas, the equipment must also have the 15 ppm alarm and automatic stopping device.
    • Discharge is not permitted from cargo pump-room bilges on tankers.
    • In the Antarctic area, any discharge of oil or oily mixtures is prohibited.
  • On ships of 10,000 GT and above, and for any discharge inside a special area, the 15 ppm equipment must alarm and stop the discharge automatically. Many smaller ships are also fitted this way.
  • Sludge goes ashore, to the incinerator, or to another approved method listed in the IOPP Supplement.
  • Every operation goes in the Oil Record Book without delay, under the correct code letter. Examples:
    • C: collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues.
    • D: non-automatic discharge or disposal of bilge water.
    • H: bunkering.
    • I: additional operational procedures and general remarks.
  • The officer in charge signs each entry, and the master signs each completed page.

Deck department

  • Segregate garbage as the Garbage Management Plan requires.
  • Never put plastics overboard, anywhere.
  • Check the discharge rules for food waste and cargo residues against the ship's distance from land and whether you are in a special area.
  • On tankers, Part II of the ORB and the ODME records must match the cargo and slop figures.

Fuel and air emissions

  • The global sulphur limit has been 0.50% m/m since 1 January 2020, and 0.10% m/m inside sulphur ECAs. A ship may also use an approved equivalent such as a scrubber.
  • Since 1 March 2020, ships without an approved equivalent may not carry non-compliant fuel for use.
  • ECA changeovers must be completed and logged with time, position and tank volumes.

Everyone

Never discharge anything you are unsure about. Ask first. Some coastal states prosecute individual crew members for pollution offences and false records, not just the company.

Worked scenario: the OWS fails mid-passage

You are 3rd Engineer. The bilge holding tank is filling, and the oil content meter keeps failing its self-check.

  1. Stop discharging. Do not bypass the meter or rig a temporary line. Any bypass arrangement, the so-called "magic pipe", is exactly what inspectors and prosecutors look for.
  2. Inform the 2nd Engineer and Chief Engineer, then follow the company SMS for equipment failure.
  3. Record the failure in ORB Part I under the code for the condition of oil filtering equipment. Note the time, cause if known and actions taken.
  4. Manage the tank. Reduce bilge water generation, use the spare capacity listed in the IOPP Supplement, and transfer between designated tanks with ORB entries for each transfer.
  5. Plan disposal ashore at the next port reception facility and keep the receipts.
  6. Repair and test, then record that the equipment is back in service.

Port state control will compare the ORB entries, the tank soundings and the OWS condition. If those three tell the same story, a genuine breakdown is a manageable deficiency. If they don't, you have a serious problem.

What port state control typically checks

PSC inspectors work under the port state regimes, such as the Paris and Tokyo MoUs, and their own national rules. Common MARPOL checks:

  • Certificates are valid, endorsed and match the ship. For example, the IOPP Supplement must match the equipment actually fitted.
  • ORB Part I:
    • Entries are complete, signed and consistent.
    • Sludge and bilge tank quantities agree with soundings.
    • Disposal receipts and incinerator use make sense against fuel consumption.
  • OWS demonstration:
    • The 15 ppm alarm works.
    • The automatic stop or three-way valve operates.
    • No suspicious flanges, hoses or fresh paint on overboard lines.
  • Garbage: segregation, placards, the Garbage Management Plan, Garbage Record Book entries and port reception receipts.
  • SOPEP: on board, current, with up-to-date contact lists. Crew should know where the spill kit is.
  • Annex VI:
    • Bunker Delivery Notes and retained samples.
    • Fuel changeover records.
    • Scrubber data where fitted.
    • EIAPP and Technical Files.
    • ODS record book.
  • Sewage: the treatment plant is operational and approved, and holding tank use is consistent.
  • Crew familiarity: inspectors often ask ratings and junior officers to operate equipment or explain procedures. Not knowing your own system can lead to a more detailed inspection.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners like

Q: Which MARPOL annexes are compulsory for a party? Annexes I and II. Annexes III to VI are optional.

Q: Who signs the Oil Record Book? The officer or officers in charge of each operation sign each entry. The master signs each completed page.

Q: What happens to the ORB after the last entry? It stays on board, readily available for inspection, for three years.

Q: Can garbage go overboard in a special area? Only in limited cases. Food waste may be discharged under stricter conditions than outside special areas. Plastics are prohibited everywhere. Know the Annex V table and your company's often stricter policy.

Q: What is the difference between the IAPP and the EIAPP? The IAPP covers the ship. The EIAPP covers an individual diesel engine's NOx compliance.

Q: Why do examiners keep asking about consistency? Many MARPOL detentions and prosecutions arise from records that don't match reality, rather than from an honest equipment failure that was properly logged and reported.

Key points
  • MARPOL 73/78 entered into force on 2 October 1983; Annex VI followed on 19 May 2005.
  • Six annexes cover oil, noxious liquids, packaged goods, sewage, garbage and air; only Annexes I and II are compulsory for parties.
  • Main certificates are IOPP, NLS, ISPP, IAPP and IEE, plus an EIAPP for each diesel engine; Annex V has no certificate.
  • Keep the Oil Record Book for three years and the Garbage Record Book for at least two years after the last entry.
  • Machinery space bilge water may only go overboard through approved equipment at 15 ppm or less; plastics never go overboard.
  • The fuel sulphur limit is 0.50% m/m globally and 0.10% m/m in ECAs, unless an approved equivalent is fitted.
  • PSC looks for consistency between records, tank soundings, receipts and working equipment.

Frequently asked questions

What does MARPOL 73/78 mean?

It refers to the 1973 convention as modified by the 1978 Protocol. The two are read as one instrument, which entered into force in 1983. The 1997 Protocol later added Annex VI on air pollution.

How long must the Oil Record Book be kept on board?

For three years after the last entry, readily available for inspection. Your company may require longer retention or archiving ashore, so follow the SMS as well.

Is there a MARPOL certificate for garbage?

No. Annex V works through the Garbage Management Plan, placards and the Garbage Record Book, depending on ship size and persons carried. Compliance is checked during surveys and port state control inspections.

Can a crew member be personally prosecuted for a MARPOL violation?

Yes, in some jurisdictions. Several coastal and port states have prosecuted individual engineers and officers for illegal discharges and false record book entries. Never falsify a record, and report any instruction to bypass pollution prevention equipment through the company's reporting channels.

What is the difference between a special area and an ECA?

Special areas are defined under Annexes I, II, IV and V and impose stricter discharge rules. Emission Control Areas are defined under Annex VI and impose stricter limits on sulphur and/or NOx emissions. The geographical lists are different, so check each annex separately.

Test yourself

Which MARPOL annexes must a state accept when it becomes a party?

Annexes I and II are compulsory; III to VI are optional annexes a state may accept separately. Reference: MARPOL 73/78, Articles.

What is the global fuel oil sulphur limit outside ECAs since 1 January 2020 for ships without an approved equivalent?

The global cap is 0.50% m/m. The 0.10% m/m limit applies inside sulphur ECAs, and 3.50% was the previous global limit. Reference: MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 14.

How long must the Oil Record Book Part I be retained on board after the last entry?

The ORB is kept on board for three years after the last entry, readily available for inspection. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 17.

Where may plastics be discharged into the sea?

Annex V prohibits the discharge of all plastics into the sea in all areas. Reference: MARPOL Annex V, Regulation 3.

Which MARPOL annex does not result in a statutory pollution prevention certificate for the ship?

Annex V relies on the Garbage Management Plan, placards and the Garbage Record Book rather than a certificate. Annexes I, IV and VI give the IOPP, ISPP and IAPP. Reference: MARPOL Annex V, Regulation 10.
