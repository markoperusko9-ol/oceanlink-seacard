SOLAS Convention: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

SOLAS, the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974, sets the minimum safety standards for the construction, equipment and operation of merchant ships. It entered into force on 25 May 1980 and is kept current through regular amendments. On board, it drives your drills, equipment checks, statutory certificates and much of what a port state control officer will inspect.

What SOLAS is and where it came from

SOLAS is the most important international treaty on merchant ship safety. The first version was adopted in 1914 after the Titanic disaster. Later versions followed in 1929, 1948 and 1960. The convention in force today is SOLAS 1974. It was adopted on 1 November 1974 and entered into force on 25 May 1980.

The convention is kept current in two ways:

  • Technical chapters (other than Chapter I): amendments use the tacit acceptance procedure. They enter into force on a set date unless enough Contracting Governments object.
  • The Articles and Chapter I: amendments need explicit acceptance.

The convention is amended regularly rather than rewritten. That is why you will hear "SOLAS as amended".

Two protocols also matter:

  • 1978 Protocol: added survey and tanker-related requirements.
  • 1988 Protocol: introduced the Harmonized System of Survey and Certification (HSSC). This aligned survey dates and certificate validity across conventions.

The flag state (Administration) applies SOLAS to its ships through national law. The flag either surveys ships itself or authorises recognised organisations (classification societies) to do so on its behalf.

Which ships SOLAS applies to

By default, the SOLAS chapters apply to ships on international voyages. Chapter I says that, unless a chapter expressly provides otherwise, the regulations do not apply to:

  • warships and troopships
  • cargo ships of less than 500 gross tonnage
  • ships not propelled by mechanical means
  • wooden ships of primitive build
  • pleasure yachts not engaged in trade
  • fishing vessels

Some chapters set their own scope:

  • Chapter IV (radiocommunications) applies to cargo ships of 300 GT and upwards on international voyages.
  • Chapter V (navigation) applies, with limited exceptions, to all ships on all voyages.

This is why some Chapter V requirements apply even to small coastal craft, subject to flag-state exemptions. Examples are:

  • voyage planning (V/34)
  • the obligation to assist persons in distress (V/33)
  • the safe manning provisions (V/14)

The COLREGs apply separately under their own Rule 1.

Under SOLAS, a passenger ship is one that carries more than 12 passengers. Passenger ships face stricter requirements, including more frequent drills and annual certificate renewal.

Ships below the SOLAS thresholds, or on purely domestic voyages, are covered by national rules. These may borrow heavily from SOLAS but differ from flag to flag. If you work on coastal or domestic tonnage, check your flag's own requirements rather than assuming SOLAS applies in full.

Structure: the chapters at a glance

The Articles set out the treaty mechanics, such as application and the amendment procedure. The technical content sits in the Annex, which is divided into chapters.

Chapter Subject
I General provisions: surveys, certificates, control by port states
II-1 Construction: structure, subdivision and stability, machinery and electrical installations
II-2 Construction: fire protection, detection and extinction
III Life-saving appliances and arrangements
IV Radiocommunications (GMDSS)
V Safety of navigation
VI Carriage of cargoes
VII Carriage of dangerous goods
VIII Nuclear ships
IX Management for the safe operation of ships (makes the ISM Code mandatory)
X Safety measures for high-speed craft
XI-1 Special measures to enhance maritime safety
XI-2 Special measures to enhance maritime security (makes the ISPS Code mandatory)
XII Additional safety measures for bulk carriers
XIII Verification of compliance
XIV Safety measures for ships operating in polar waters (Polar Code)
XV Safety measures for ships carrying industrial personnel

Many chapters make codes mandatory by reference. Examples:

  • the LSA Code and FSS Code for equipment standards
  • the IMDG Code for dangerous goods
  • the IGC and IBC Codes for gas and chemical tankers
  • the IGF Code for gas-fuelled ships
  • the ISM and ISPS Codes

When someone says "it's a SOLAS requirement", the detail is often in one of these codes.

What SOLAS means in your daily work

You do not need to memorise regulation numbers to work safely. You do need to recognise that much of your routine comes straight from SOLAS.

Drills and training (Chapter III)

  • Crew with emergency duties must know those duties before the voyage begins.
  • On cargo ships, every crew member takes part in at least one abandon ship drill and one fire drill every month.
  • If more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in a drill on that ship in the previous month, the drills must be held within 24 hours of leaving port.
  • Passenger ships hold these drills weekly.
  • Each lifeboat must be launched with its assigned operating crew and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months. The Administration may accept alternative arrangements for some launching systems.
  • Enclosed space entry and rescue drills are held at least every two months.
  • Onboard training in the ship's life-saving appliances must be given no later than two weeks after a crew member joins.

Maintenance and inspections

  • LSA are inspected weekly and monthly under Chapter III.
  • Maintenance follows the ship's instructions for on-board maintenance.
  • Liferafts and hydrostatic release units are serviced at the required intervals. Normally this is no more than 12 months, unless the Administration permits an extension.
  • Fire-fighting systems and appliances are maintained under Chapter II-2 and the FSS Code.

Bridge (Chapter V)

Chapter V covers:

  • equipment carriage requirements, such as ECDIS, AIS, VDR and BNWAS on the ships that need them
  • passage planning before sailing
  • pilot transfer arrangements
  • the duty to respond to persons in distress
  • steering gear tests within 12 hours before departure
  • emergency steering drills at least every three months

Engine room

  • Chapter II-1 covers machinery, electrical installations, emergency power and steering gear.
  • Chapter II-2 covers fire integrity and machinery space fire protection.

Many items on your planned maintenance and drill schedule exist because of SOLAS. Your company's SMS (under Chapter IX/ISM) turns them into procedures and checklists. Your company's frequencies may be stricter than SOLAS, but they can never be looser.

Certificates and records SOLAS creates on board

Statutory certificates (Chapter I)

  • Passenger Ship Safety Certificate: valid for no more than 12 months.
  • Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate.
  • Cargo Ship Safety Equipment Certificate, with its Record of Equipment.
  • Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificate, with its Record of Equipment.
  • Alternatively, a single combined Cargo Ship Safety Certificate.
  • Exemption Certificate, where any exemption has been granted.

Cargo ship certificates under the HSSC are valid for up to five years. They are kept valid by the required annual, intermediate and/or periodical surveys. The mix depends on the certificate. Each survey is endorsed on the certificate, and a renewal survey is carried out before expiry.

Other SOLAS-driven documents

  • Minimum Safe Manning Document
  • Safety Management Certificate and a copy of the company's Document of Compliance (ISM)
  • International Ship Security Certificate (ISPS)
  • Continuous Synopsis Record
  • Polar Ship Certificate, where applicable
  • Dangerous goods compliance documents, where applicable

Plans, manuals and records

  • muster list and emergency instructions
  • fire control plan
  • training manual and fire safety operational booklet
  • stability information and damage control information
  • cargo securing manual, where required
  • log entries for drills, training, LSA inspections and steering gear tests

The 2nd Officer, Chief Officer and Chief Engineer usually own these records. Even so, every officer's signature on a drill record means the drill actually happened as written. Inspectors cross-check records against reality.

What port state control typically checks

Chapter I allows port states to verify that a foreign ship's certificates are valid. They can inspect further where there are clear grounds to believe the ship or its equipment does not match its certificates. Chapter XI-1 extends this to operational requirements, meaning whether the crew can actually do the job.

PSC inspections are carried out under the regional MOUs (Paris, Tokyo, Indian Ocean and others) or by the USCG. A typical inspection will include:

  • Certificates: validity, survey endorsements, and safe manning against the crew actually on board.
  • Fire safety: fire doors and dampers closing properly, the fire detection panel, the emergency fire pump starting and delivering pressure, and fixed systems in order.
  • Life-saving appliances: lifeboat release gear, davits, liferaft and HRU servicing dates, immersion suits and lifejacket lights.
  • Emergency systems: starting the emergency generator, emergency lighting and the general alarm.
  • Navigation: up-to-date charts or ENCs, the passage plan, BNWAS working and the VDR annual performance test.
  • Drills witnessed: often a fire or abandon ship drill, to judge whether the crew is familiar with its duties.
  • ISM: whether non-conformities and maintenance records reflect reality.

Deficiencies may have to be rectified before departure or within a set period. Serious cases can lead to detention. A detention affects the ship, the company and its future inspection targeting. Each MOU publishes its own procedures and detention criteria.

Worked scenario. During a PSC drill, the inspector asks an AB to don an SCBA and enter a smoke-filled alleyway. The AB does not know how to check the cylinder pressure or test the low-pressure whistle.

The ship's paperwork shows monthly fire drills, so the inspector concludes the drills are not effective. This can become an operational deficiency, and in serious cases grounds for detention.

The cure is not more paperwork. It is realistic drills where everyone handles the equipment.

Oral exam: what examiners ask about SOLAS

Q: When did SOLAS 1974 enter into force, and how is it amended? A: It entered into force on 25 May 1980. Amendments to the technical chapters use tacit acceptance: they enter into force on a set date unless enough Contracting Governments object. Amendments to the Articles and Chapter I need explicit acceptance.

Q: Name the chapters and say which make the ISM and ISPS Codes mandatory. A: Chapter IX makes the ISM Code mandatory. Chapter XI-2 makes the ISPS Code mandatory. Be ready to list all the chapters in order.

Q: What certificates does SOLAS require for a cargo ship, and how are they kept valid? A: The Safety Construction, Safety Equipment and Safety Radio certificates, or a combined Cargo Ship Safety Certificate. They are kept valid by the required annual, intermediate and/or periodical surveys under the HSSC, endorsed on the certificate, with a renewal survey before expiry.

Q: Your ship's crew changed by 40% at the last port. What must you do? A: If more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on board this ship in the previous month, hold abandon ship and fire drills within 24 hours of leaving port. Make sure new joiners with emergency duties are familiar with them before the voyage begins. Give onboard LSA training within two weeks of joining.

Q: What steering gear tests and drills does SOLAS require? A: Steering gear checks and tests within 12 hours before departure, and emergency steering drills at least every three months. Both must be logged.

Q: On what basis can a PSC officer inspect beyond certificates? A: Where there are clear grounds that the ship, its equipment or its crew's operational ability does not meet the requirements.

Examiners vary between administrations. Some accept a sound practical answer. Others expect regulation references. Know the chapter structure cold and you will handle most follow-ups.

Key points
  • SOLAS 1974 entered into force on 25 May 1980 and is kept current by regular amendments.
  • It applies to ships on international voyages. Chapter V applies to almost all ships on all voyages.
  • Chapters I to XV cover surveys, construction, fire, LSA, radio, navigation, cargoes, ISM, security, bulk carriers, polar waters and industrial personnel.
  • Many SOLAS requirements live in mandatory codes such as LSA, FSS, ISM, ISPS, IMDG and the Polar Code.
  • Cargo ships hold monthly abandon ship and fire drills, with drills within 24 hours of leaving port if more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on board that ship in the previous month.
  • SOLAS certificates must carry valid survey endorsements, and records must match what is physically on board.
  • Port state control checks both certificates and whether the crew can actually operate the equipment.

Frequently asked questions

Does SOLAS apply to small or domestic ships?

Generally, SOLAS applies to ships on international voyages, and cargo ships under 500 GT are excluded from most chapters. Chapter V on navigation applies to nearly all ships, and Chapter IV applies to cargo ships from 300 GT. Domestic and smaller vessels are covered by national rules, which differ by flag state.

Is the ISM Code part of SOLAS?

The ISM Code is a separate code, but SOLAS Chapter IX makes it mandatory. In the same way, Chapter XI-2 makes the ISPS Code mandatory. That is why the Safety Management Certificate and the International Ship Security Certificate are treated as SOLAS documents.

How often do SOLAS drills have to be held on a cargo ship?

Each crew member takes part in at least one abandon ship drill and one fire drill a month. Lifeboats are launched with their crew at least every three months, and enclosed space rescue drills are held at least every two months. Your company SMS may require more frequent drills.

What happens if port state control finds a SOLAS deficiency?

Depending on severity, the deficiency may have to be rectified before departure or within a set time, often at the next port. Serious deficiencies that make the ship unsafe can lead to detention. Detention data is published by the regional MOUs and affects how often the ship is targeted.

Which edition of SOLAS should be on board?

The ship should carry an up-to-date consolidated edition, with any amendments that have since entered into force. The exact publication requirements are set by the flag state and the company's SMS. Old editions are a common audit and PSC finding.

Test yourself

When did SOLAS 1974 enter into force?

SOLAS 1974 was adopted on 1 November 1974 but entered into force on 25 May 1980. The 1998 date relates to the ISM Code's first phase of application. Ref: SOLAS 1974, Articles.

Which SOLAS chapter makes the ISM Code mandatory?

Chapter IX covers management for the safe operation of ships and makes the ISM Code mandatory. XI-2 does the same for the ISPS Code. Ref: SOLAS Chapter IX.

How often must each lifeboat be launched with its assigned operating crew and manoeuvred in the water?

Each lifeboat is to be launched with its assigned crew and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months during an abandon ship drill. The Administration may accept alternatives for some launching arrangements. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

On a cargo ship, more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on board in the previous month. When must abandon ship and fire drills be held?

In this case the drills must be held within 24 hours of leaving port. The two-week period applies to onboard LSA training, not to drills. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

What is the minimum frequency for enclosed space entry and rescue drills under SOLAS?

Enclosed space entry and rescue drills are required at least every two months. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.
