IBC Code: A Practical Guide for Seafarers on Chemical Tankers

Quick answer

The IBC Code is the IMO code for the construction and equipment of ships carrying dangerous chemicals and noxious liquids in bulk. It is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII and MARPOL Annex II, and applies to chemical tankers built on or after 1 July 1986. On board it shows up as the Certificate of Fitness, its attached product list and the Chapter 17 carriage requirements you check before loading any cargo.

What the IBC Code is and where its authority comes from

IBC stands for the International Code for the Construction and Equipment of Ships Carrying Dangerous Chemicals in Bulk. Its purpose is to set an international standard for carrying bulk liquid chemicals safely. It does this by matching each product's hazards to the design, equipment and operation of the ship that carries it.

The Code is mandatory through two conventions:

  • SOLAS Chapter VII, Part B makes it mandatory for safety. This covers fire, toxicity, reactivity and corrosivity.
  • MARPOL Annex II makes it mandatory for pollution prevention. This covers noxious liquid substances.

This dual footing explains why the product list carries both safety and pollution information. It also explains why surveyors and PSC officers look at it from both angles.

Which ships it covers:

Ship Applicable code
Chemical tanker built on or after 1 July 1986 IBC Code
Chemical tanker built before 1 July 1986 BCH Code (the older Bulk Chemical Code), as applied by the flag
Gas carriers IGC Code, not IBC

The IBC Code was substantially revised to line up with the revised MARPOL Annex II. Both applied from 1 January 2007. That revision introduced the pollution categories X, Y, Z and OS (Other Substances) that you see on today's product lists. The Code has been amended many times since. Always work from the edition and amendments your ship's documents are based on.

How the Code is structured

The chapters fall into three practical groups.

Ship design and equipment (Chapters 1–13):

  • General provisions, surveys and certification
  • Survival capability after damage and location of cargo tanks
  • Ship arrangements: segregation, accommodation, pump rooms, access
  • Cargo containment and cargo transfer (piping, hoses, valves)
  • Materials of construction and tank coatings
  • Cargo temperature control (heating and cooling)
  • Tank venting and gas-freeing
  • Environmental control (inerting, padding, drying)
  • Electrical installations, fire protection, mechanical ventilation and instrumentation

Crew safety and operations (Chapters 14–16):

  • Chapter 14 covers personnel protection: protective clothing, safety equipment, emergency escape respiratory and eye protection, decontamination showers and eyewash.
  • Chapter 15 sets special requirements for individual products.
  • Chapter 16 covers operational requirements, such as cargo information on board, personnel training and tank filling.

The product tables (Chapters 17–21):

  • Chapter 17 is the summary of minimum requirements and the heart of the Code. It holds one line per product.
  • Chapter 18 lists products to which the Code does not apply. Some of these are still regulated under MARPOL Annex II.
  • Chapter 19 is the index of product names and synonyms.
  • Chapter 20 covers the transport of liquid chemical wastes.
  • Chapter 21 sets the criteria used to assign carriage requirements to products.

A product that does not appear in the Code can still be shipped. It must first be assessed and carried under a tripartite agreement between the shipping country, the flag state and the receiving port state. IMO publishes these provisional assessments in an annual MEPC.2/Circular.

Reading a Chapter 17 entry: what each column tells you

Every officer on a chemical tanker should be able to read a Chapter 17 line without hesitating. Each entry tells you:

  • Pollution category: X, Y or Z. This drives MARPOL Annex II discharge and prewash rules.
  • Hazard: S (safety), P (pollution) or S/P (both).
  • Ship type: 1, 2 or 3. Type 1 is for the most hazardous products and needs the greatest protection against cargo escape after damage. Type 3 is for the least hazardous.
  • Tank type: independent or integral, and gravity or pressure.
  • Tank vents: open or controlled.
  • Tank environmental control: inerting, padding, drying, ventilation, or none required.
  • Electrical equipment: temperature class, apparatus group, and whether the flashpoint exceeds 60 °C.
  • Gauging: open, restricted or closed.
  • Vapour detection: flammable, toxic, both, or none.
  • Fire protection: the extinguishing media suitable for the product, such as alcohol-resistant foam, regular foam, water spray or dry chemical.
  • Emergency equipment: whether Chapter 14 escape respiratory and eye protection is required.
  • Special requirements: references to paragraphs in Chapters 15 and 16.

The special requirements column is the one people skip, and it causes incidents. It can carry many kinds of restriction, for example:

  • inhibitor requirements
  • limits on heating adjacent tanks
  • maximum cargo temperatures
  • restrictions on contact with water or air
  • bans on certain materials such as copper alloys

Always follow the paragraph reference and read the full text.

What the IBC Code means for your daily work

For deck officers, ratings and engineers on a chemical tanker, the Code turns into routine habits.

  • Before nomination is accepted: check that the exact product appears on the ship's Certificate of Fitness product list, matched to the tanks it is approved for.
  • Cargo planning: confirm the following against Chapter 17 and the shipper's information:
    • tank coating compatibility
    • segregation from reactive cargoes
    • heating limits and adjacent-tank restrictions
    • inerting or padding needs Many companies also require a compatibility chart check under their SMS.
  • PPE and emergency equipment: wear the protective equipment the cargo demands. Make sure escape sets are available where Chapter 17 requires them, and test decontamination showers and eyewash before cargo work.
  • Gauging and sampling: respect restricted or closed gauging. Do not open ullage ports on a closed-gauging cargo simply because it is quicker.
  • Gas detection: use the right detector for the vapour hazard listed, flammable, toxic or both. Check calibration and the correct sensor or tube type. Toxic detection usually needs more than a standard flammable-gas meter.
  • Engine room: cargo heating, inert gas or nitrogen supply, and pump-room or deck-house ventilation often fall to engineers. They must know the temperature limits and the ventilation requirements.
  • Tank cleaning and residues: discharge, stripping and prewash follow MARPOL Annex II and the ship's Procedures and Arrangements Manual. The IBC Code sits alongside these documents.

Training requirements come from STCW, not the IBC Code. You need basic tanker training for oil and chemical cargo operations, and advanced chemical tanker training for those with immediate responsibility for cargo. Endorsement requirements and sea-service criteria are set by your administration.

Certificates and records the Code creates on board

International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Dangerous Chemicals in Bulk (COF)

  • The flag state or a recognised organisation issues the COF after survey.
  • It is maintained by initial, annual, intermediate and renewal surveys under the harmonised system of survey and certification, as set out in Chapter 1 of the IBC Code, with a maximum validity of five years.
  • Its attachment lists the products the ship may carry, the tanks they may go in and any conditions.
  • For an IBC-certified chemical tanker, the COF is generally accepted in place of a separate MARPOL Annex II NLS certificate. Confirm the arrangement on your own ship's documents.

Documents that work alongside the COF:

Document Comes from
Cargo information / safety data sheets for each product IBC Code Chapter 16 and SOLAS
Procedures and Arrangements (P&A) Manual MARPOL Annex II
Cargo Record Book MARPOL Annex II
Shipboard Marine Pollution Emergency Plan for NLS (often combined with SOPEP) MARPOL Annex II
Loading/stability information, including damage stability IBC Code Chapter 2 as amended, and class
Medical guidance for chemical exposure SOLAS/IMO medical guidance; the specific publication required varies by flag

Since amendments that took effect on 1 January 2016, the Code requires chemical tankers to carry a stability instrument capable of verifying both intact and damage stability. Existing ships had to comply by their first renewal survey, and no later than 1 January 2021. The flag may grant waivers in specified cases.

What port state control typically checks

Chemical tankers attract close attention, particularly under expanded inspection regimes. Typical checks include:

  • COF validity and product list: are the cargoes on board actually listed, in the approved tanks?
  • Cargo documents: is the cargo information on board and available to the people handling the cargo?
  • Cargo Record Book and P&A Manual: are entries complete, signed and consistent with operations?
  • Personnel protection: the inspector may check:
    • escape respiratory equipment for the number of people on board where required
    • decontamination showers and eyewash working, including in cold weather
    • protective clothing in good condition
  • Gas detection: are suitable instruments on board for the cargo hazard, calibrated, with spare consumables?
  • Venting and overflow protection: P/V valves, high-level and overfill alarms, flame screens.
  • Fire protection: deck foam system, and whether the foam concentrate suits the cargoes carried.
  • Crew competence: inspectors may ask a duty officer to explain a cargo's hazards or to locate its Chapter 17 entry. Tanker endorsements are checked against STCW.

Detention criteria and inspection focus vary between PSC regimes. A clear certificate does not stop an inspector from detaining for a defect they can see.

Oral exam corner and a worked scenario

Scenario: The charterer nominates a product for a centre tank. What do you do?

  1. Look the name up in Chapter 19 to find the Chapter 17 entry name. Watch for synonyms.
  2. Check that the product is on the COF attachment and allowed in that tank.
  3. Read the Chapter 17 entry: ship type, tank environmental control, gauging, vapour detection, fire protection and emergency equipment.
  4. Follow every special requirement reference, for example heating limits or an inhibitor certificate.
  5. Check compatibility with adjacent cargoes and with any heating in adjacent tanks.
  6. Confirm coating compatibility with the coating maker's resistance list.
  7. Prepare PPE, detectors and the cargo information for the crew, then brief them.
  8. If the product is not on the list, do not load. Refer the matter to the company. It may need a tripartite agreement and a certificate amendment.

Typical examiner questions:

  • Difference between ship type 1 and type 3? Type 1 has the greatest protection against cargo escape after damage and is meant for the most hazardous products. Type 3 has the least.
  • Why does the Code sit under both SOLAS and MARPOL? Because the cargoes create both safety hazards and pollution hazards.
  • What is in Chapter 18? Products to which the Code does not apply. Some of them remain subject to MARPOL Annex II.
  • Pre-1986 chemical tanker? It falls under the BCH Code.
Key points
  • The IBC Code sets construction and equipment standards for ships carrying dangerous chemicals and noxious liquids in bulk.
  • It is mandatory under both SOLAS Chapter VII and MARPOL Annex II, and applies to chemical tankers built on or after 1 July 1986.
  • Chemical tankers built before 1 July 1986 fall under the BCH Code.
  • Chapter 17 holds each product's minimum carriage requirements; always follow its special requirement references.
  • The Certificate of Fitness and its product list decide what the ship may carry and in which tanks.
  • Products not listed in the Code need assessment and a tripartite agreement before carriage.
  • PSC focuses on the COF product list, cargo records, gas detection, escape equipment and crew knowledge of cargo hazards.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the IBC Code and the BCH Code?

Both cover chemical tankers. The IBC Code applies to ships built on or after 1 July 1986. The BCH Code is the earlier code that applies to chemical tankers built before that date, as implemented by the flag state.

What is the IBC Certificate of Fitness?

It is the International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Dangerous Chemicals in Bulk, issued after survey by the flag or a recognised organisation. Its attachment lists the products the ship may carry and the tanks they may go in. For IBC-certified ships it is generally accepted in place of a separate MARPOL Annex II NLS certificate.

What do ship type 1, 2 and 3 mean in the IBC Code?

They describe how well the ship's design protects against cargo escape after collision or grounding damage. Type 1 gives the greatest protection and is for the most hazardous products. Type 3 is for the least hazardous. Chapter 17 states the minimum ship type for each product.

Can a chemical tanker load a product that is not in the IBC Code?

Not on the master's or charterer's say-so. An unlisted product must first be assessed and carried under a tripartite agreement between the shipping country, the flag state and the port state, and the ship's certificate must cover it. IMO circulates provisional assessments in an annual MEPC.2/Circular.

Does the IBC Code set training requirements for chemical tanker crew?

The Code includes operational and training provisions, but the certification requirements come from STCW. These are basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations, and advanced chemical tanker training for those with immediate cargo responsibility. Sea-service and endorsement details are set by each administration.

Test yourself

Which IBC ship type is intended for products with the greatest overall hazard?

Type 1 ships have the greatest protection against cargo escape after damage and are meant for the most hazardous products. Coating does not change ship type (IBC Code, Chapter 2).

A chemical tanker built in 1983 is generally required to comply with which code?

The IBC Code applies to chemical tankers built on or after 1 July 1986. Earlier ships fall under the BCH Code, which is mandatory under MARPOL Annex II and recommendatory under SOLAS, as applied by the flag state (MARPOL Annex II; SOLAS Chapter VII).

Where do you find the minimum carriage requirements for a named product?

Chapter 17 is the summary of minimum requirements. Chapter 19 is only an index of names, and Chapter 18 lists products the Code does not apply to (IBC Code, Chapters 17–19).

Which certificate is issued to a ship complying with the IBC Code?

The IBC Certificate of Fitness, with its product list, is issued after survey. The IOPP certificate is a MARPOL Annex I document, and the gas certificate belongs to the IGC Code (IBC Code, Chapter 1).

A charterer asks you to load a liquid not listed in Chapters 17 or 18 or in the current MEPC.2/Circular. What applies?

Unlisted products need provisional assessment and a tripartite agreement between the shipping, flag and port states. The ship must also be certified for the product (IBC Code, Chapter 1; MARPOL Annex II).
