Ballast Water Management Convention: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

The Ballast Water Management (BWM) Convention 2004 is the IMO treaty that controls how ships take up, treat and discharge ballast water and sediments so that harmful organisms are not carried between regions. It entered into force on 8 September 2017. On board it requires an approved Ballast Water Management Plan, a Ballast Water Record Book and, for ships of 400 GT and above, an International Ballast Water Management Certificate. Port state control can check all three and can sample ballast.

What the BWM Convention is and why it exists

The full title is the International Convention for the Control and Management of Ships' Ballast Water and Sediments, 2004. It was adopted at IMO in February 2004.

The problem it addresses is biological rather than chemical. Ballast taken up in one port carries plankton, larvae, bacteria and pathogens. When that ballast is discharged thousands of miles away, some species survive and establish themselves where they have no natural predators. The results include damaged fisheries, fouled intakes and public health risks.

MARPOL deals with oil, chemicals, sewage, garbage and air emissions. Ballast water sits in its own separate convention. Do not confuse the two when answering exam questions or filling in PSC paperwork.

Entry into force and which ships it applies to

Two conditions had to be met before the convention could enter into force:

  • at least 30 States had to ratify it
  • those States had to represent at least 35% of world merchant tonnage.

The tonnage threshold was reached in September 2016, and the convention entered into force 12 months later, on 8 September 2017.

It applies to ships that are designed or constructed to carry ballast water and that either fly the flag of a Party or operate under a Party's authority.

Broadly excluded are:

  • ships not designed to carry ballast water
  • warships and other government ships on non-commercial service
  • ships with permanent ballast in sealed tanks that is not discharged
  • ships operating only in waters under one Party's jurisdiction, unless that Party decides otherwise.

The ship's own flag status does not change what happens in port. A Party can apply the convention's requirements to a ship in its waters even if that ship flies a non-Party flag.

Not every coastal State is a Party. The United States, for example, has not ratified the convention. It applies its own federal ballast water rules, which call for systems type-approved by the US Coast Guard. Some Parties also add local requirements, such as pre-arrival reporting. Always check port and agent guidance as well as your Ballast Water Management Plan.

How the convention is structured

The convention has two main parts:

  • Articles, which deal with general obligations, inspections, violations and sediment reception facilities
  • an Annex of regulations, grouped into five sections.
Section Content in plain terms
A – General provisions Definitions, application, exceptions (e.g. safety of ship or saving life), exemptions granted by a Party
B – Management and control requirements for ships BWM Plan (B-1), Record Book (B-2), implementation schedule (B-3), exchange (B-4), sediments (B-5), duties of officers and crew (B-6)
C – Special requirements in certain areas Additional measures a Party may impose, warnings about bad uptake areas
D – Standards D-1 exchange standard, D-2 performance standard, approval of systems
E – Survey and certification Surveys and the International Ballast Water Management Certificate

Two appendices set out the model forms for the certificate and the record book.

Technical detail sits in IMO guidelines rather than in the convention text. The best known are the G-series guidelines (for example, G2 on sampling and G4 on BWM plans). There is also the BWMS Code, which governs type approval of treatment systems and is mandatory.

D-1 and D-2: exchange versus treatment

D-1 (exchange standard). The ship swaps coastal ballast for open-ocean water. The target is at least 95% volumetric exchange.

When the pumping-through method is used, pumping three times the tank volume through the tank counts as meeting the standard. Less than three times is accepted only if the ship can demonstrate 95% exchange.

Regulation B-4 sets where exchange should be done:

  • preferably at least 200 nm from the nearest land, in water at least 200 m deep
  • if that is not possible, at least 50 nm from land, still in 200 m depth.

The ship is never required to deviate or delay the voyage just to carry out an exchange. Exchange is also not required if the master judges it would threaten the safety of the ship, considering weather, stability and stresses.

D-2 (performance standard). This sets limits on viable organisms in the ballast water discharged:

  • size-based limits for organisms of 50 micrometres and above
  • size-based limits for organisms from 10 to under 50 micrometres
  • limits for indicator microbes: toxicogenic Vibrio cholerae, E. coli and intestinal enterococci.

In practice, meeting D-2 means a type-approved ballast water management system (BWMS). Common types are filtration followed by UV treatment, or electrochlorination.

Where things stand. The implementation schedule was phased by build date and survey dates, but the end point is fixed. Ships within scope had to meet D-2 by 8 September 2024 at the latest. Ships built on or after 8 September 2017 had to comply from delivery.

Exchange alone is therefore no longer the normal way to comply for most ships. Exchange still appears in contingency arrangements and in some local regimes.

What it means for crew in daily work

Regulation B-6 requires officers and crew to know their ballast water duties and to be familiar with the ship's own BWM Plan. In practice, the work falls into four areas.

Planning ballast operations - The chief officer plans ballast operations around cargo, stability and stress limits. - Check whether the next port has extra rules, such as local reporting, no-discharge zones or freshwater ports. - Avoid uptake where possible in shallow water, near sewage outfalls, during algal blooms and at night. Note that the reason for avoiding uptake at night is the advice in the guidelines, not a legal prohibition.

Running the BWMS - Follow the manufacturer's operating manual and the BWM Plan. - Know the system's operating limits for salinity, temperature and turbidity. Electrochlorination systems can struggle in fresh or brackish water. Filters can clog in muddy rivers. - For systems that dose an active substance, neutralisation before discharge matters. So does monitoring of residual oxidant. - UV-based systems often treat on both uptake and discharge, and some have holding-time requirements.

Sediments - Remove and dispose of sediment according to the plan, typically during dry-dock or at reception facilities. - Never wash sediment overboard in coastal waters. - Log sediment removal and disposal.

Engine and deck coordination On many ships the BWMS is maintained by the engine department and operated from the CCR or the ballast control console. Your SMS will say who does what. Make sure alarms, bypass valves and failure reporting are understood by everyone involved.

Records and certificates on board

Ballast Water Management Plan (B-1). - Ship-specific and approved by the Administration. - Describes the safety procedures, the methods of management, the sediment procedures and the reporting arrangements. - Names a designated officer responsible for its implementation. On most ships this is the chief officer, but check your plan. - Must be in the working language of the ship. If that is not English, French or Spanish, a translation into one of those is required.

Ballast Water Record Book (B-2). Entries cover: - uptake, treatment, circulation and discharge - exchange operations - discharges to reception facilities - accidental or exceptional discharges, with the reasons - BWMS failures.

Each entry is signed by the officer in charge of the operation, and each completed page by the master.

The book stays on board for at least two years after the last entry. It then stays in the company's control for at least three years.

The record book format has been revised by amendment, so use the version your flag requires.

International Ballast Water Management Certificate. - Issued to ships of 400 GT and above, excluding floating platforms, FSUs and FPSOs. - Issued after an initial survey. - Valid for up to five years, subject to annual, intermediate and renewal surveys. - Smaller ships are dealt with as the flag Administration decides.

BWMS documentation. - Type approval certificate and operating manual. - Commissioning test records for newly installed systems, now required by amendment. - Self-monitoring data logs, which many systems store electronically.

What port state control typically checks

Under the convention, PSC begins with documents. Officers normally:

  • confirm a valid certificate is on board
  • inspect the Ballast Water Record Book
  • check the approved BWM Plan.

They may then take samples of ballast water, following IMO sampling guidance. Sampling should not cause undue delay.

A more detailed inspection is likely when:

  • there is no valid certificate
  • the record book is missing or inconsistent
  • the BWMS is clearly not working
  • crew cannot operate the system or explain the plan.

Typical findings include:

  • record book entries that do not match tank soundings or BWMS logs
  • discharges recorded while the BWMS shows a bypass or alarm
  • no record of a system failure
  • the designated officer unaware of their role
  • an outdated plan after BWMS installation
  • crew unable to start the system or explain neutralisation.

Worked scenario: BWMS fails at sea

You are three days from a discharge port and the UV system fails. Ballast taken up while the system was working received uptake treatment, but most UV systems must also treat on discharge to meet D-2, so you cannot assume that ballast is compliant to discharge. You still need to deballast on arrival.

  1. Record the failure, time and tanks affected in the record book. Log the troubleshooting.
  2. Inform the company per the SMS and BWM Plan. The company should involve the flag State.
  3. Notify the port State of the arrival port before arrival. Do not wait until the PSC officer boards.
  4. Agree a contingency measure acceptable to the port State. Options under IMO contingency guidance include retaining ballast, discharging to a reception facility or another ship, or an exchange if the port State permits it.
  5. Never discharge untreated ballast quietly and fix the paperwork later. Logs and system data will show it.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • When did the BWM Convention enter into force, and what were the conditions? 8 September 2017. The conditions were 30 States and 35% of world tonnage, with entry into force 12 months after both were met.
  • Difference between D-1 and D-2? D-1 is an exchange efficiency standard of 95% volumetric exchange. D-2 is a discharge quality standard setting limits on viable organisms and indicator microbes.
  • Where should exchange be carried out? Preferably 200 nm from land in 200 m depth. If that is not possible, 50 nm from land in 200 m depth. There is no requirement to deviate, and exchange is not required if it would endanger the ship.
  • What documents are required? The approved BWM Plan, the Ballast Water Record Book and, for ships of 400 GT and above, the International BWM Certificate. Add the BWMS type approval documentation.
  • How long are record books retained? Two years on board after the last entry, then three years under company control.
  • What can PSC do? Check the certificate, inspect the record book and take samples. A detailed inspection can follow if there are clear grounds.
  • Name situations where the regulations do not apply to a discharge. Examples include discharge to ensure the safety of the ship or to save life at sea, and accidental discharge resulting from damage, provided reasonable precautions were taken.
Key points
  • The BWM Convention 2004 entered into force on 8 September 2017 and is separate from MARPOL.
  • D-1 is the 95% volumetric exchange standard; D-2 is the discharge performance standard met by a type-approved BWMS.
  • Ships within scope had to meet D-2 by 8 September 2024 at the latest; newer ships complied from delivery.
  • On board you need an approved BWM Plan with a designated officer, a Ballast Water Record Book and, for 400 GT and above, the International BWM Certificate.
  • Record books stay on board two years after the last entry, then three years under company control.
  • PSC checks documents first and may sample ballast water; record book entries must match BWMS logs.
  • Some coastal States, including the USA, apply their own rules, so check port requirements every voyage.

Frequently asked questions

When did the Ballast Water Management Convention come into force?

It entered into force on 8 September 2017, twelve months after the ratification thresholds of 30 States and 35% of world tonnage were met. It was adopted at IMO in 2004.

Is ballast water exchange still allowed under the BWM Convention?

The D-1 exchange standard was a transitional arrangement. Ships within scope had to meet the D-2 standard by 8 September 2024 at the latest. Exchange may still be used as a contingency measure if the port State agrees, or where a local regime calls for it. Check your BWM Plan and the port's requirements.

Who is responsible for ballast water management on board?

The BWM Plan names a designated officer responsible for implementing it, commonly the chief officer. Every officer and crew member with ballast duties must know their tasks and be familiar with the plan. The master signs each completed page of the record book.

What happens if the ballast water treatment system breaks down?

Record the failure in the Ballast Water Record Book, inform the company, and notify the port State before arrival. Then follow the contingency measures in your BWM Plan and the IMO contingency guidance. Discharging untreated ballast without the port State's agreement is likely to be treated as a violation.

Does the BWM Convention apply in US ports?

The United States is not a Party to the convention. It applies its own federal ballast water rules, which require systems type-approved by the US Coast Guard along with its own reporting. Ships trading to the US must comply with both regimes as applicable.

Test yourself

On what date did the BWM Convention enter into force?

The convention was adopted in 2004 and the ratification threshold was met in September 2016. It entered into force 12 months later, on 8 September 2017 (BWM Convention, Article 18).

What does the D-1 standard require?

D-1 is the exchange standard requiring 95% volumetric exchange. The organism limits belong to D-2 (BWM Convention, Annex, Regulation D-1).

Where should ballast water exchange preferably be conducted under Regulation B-4?

The preferred location is 200 nm from land in 200 m depth. Where that is not possible, the minimum is 50 nm from land, still in 200 m depth (BWM Convention, Annex, Regulation B-4).

How long must the Ballast Water Record Book be kept?

The record book stays on board for at least two years after the last entry, then under company control for at least three years (BWM Convention, Annex, Regulation B-2).

Which ships are issued the International Ballast Water Management Certificate?

Survey and certification under Section E apply to ships of 400 GT and above, excluding floating platforms, FSUs and FPSOs. Smaller ships are dealt with as the Administration decides (BWM Convention, Annex, Regulations E-1 and E-2).
