IMDG Code: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

The International Maritime Dangerous Goods (IMDG) Code sets the rules for carrying dangerous goods in packaged form by sea. It covers classification, packing, marking, documentation, stowage and segregation, and it has been mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII since 1 January 2004. On board, it decides what you load, where it goes, which documents you hold, and how you respond if something leaks or catches fire.

What the IMDG Code is and where its authority comes from

The IMDG Code is the international rulebook for dangerous goods in packaged form at sea. That means drums, boxes, IBCs, portable tanks, containers and vehicles. It does not cover bulk cargo:

  • Solid bulk cargoes come under the IMSBC Code.
  • Bulk liquid chemicals come under the IBC Code.
  • Liquefied gases in bulk come under the IGC Code.

IMO first adopted the Code in 1965 as a recommendation. It became mandatory on 1 January 2004 through amendments to SOLAS Chapter VII, Part A. The Code also gives effect to MARPOL Annex III, which deals with harmful substances in packaged form. This is why marine pollutants are identified and marked under the same system.

The Code is amended every two years. Each set of amendments carries a number and year, for example '41-22'. Normally there is a period when the new amendment may be applied voluntarily before it becomes mandatory. Always check which amendment your flag and your company SMS treat as current, and make sure that edition is on board. An out-of-date copy can be raised as a deficiency.

How the Code is structured

The Code comes in two volumes plus a Supplement.

Part Content Where you use it on board
1 General provisions, definitions, training, security Scope, exemptions, high-consequence goods
2 Classification (Classes 1–9, marine pollutants) Understanding the hazard
3 Dangerous Goods List, special provisions, limited and excepted quantities The first page you open for any UN number
4 Packing and tank provisions Checking whether packaging or tank is suitable
5 Consignment procedures: marking, labelling, placarding, documents Checking boxes and paperwork
6 Construction and testing of packagings, IBCs, portable tanks Rarely used by crew directly
7 Transport operations: stowage, segregation, CTUs Chief officer's planning and checking

Volume 2 contains Part 3, the Dangerous Goods List, along with its appendices and index. Volume 1 contains the other parts.

The Supplement holds the two documents the crew rely on in an emergency:

  • EmS Guide (Emergency Response Procedures for Ships Carrying Dangerous Goods). It has fire schedules F-A to F-J and spillage schedules S-A to S-Z.
  • MFAG (Medical First Aid Guide for Use in Accidents Involving Dangerous Goods).

The nine classes in brief:

  1. Explosives
  2. Gases: 2.1 flammable, 2.2 non-flammable non-toxic, 2.3 toxic
  3. Flammable liquids
  4. Flammable solids and related substances: 4.1 flammable solids, 4.2 liable to spontaneous combustion, 4.3 dangerous when wet
  5. Oxidizers and peroxides: 5.1 oxidizing substances, 5.2 organic peroxides
  6. Toxic and infectious: 6.1 toxic, 6.2 infectious
  7. Radioactive material
  8. Corrosives
  9. Miscellaneous dangerous substances and articles

Packing groups show the degree of danger for many substances: I is great, II is medium and III is minor. Not every class uses packing groups.

What it means in daily work on board

How much you deal with the Code depends on the ship type and your rank. Container, ro-ro, ro-pax and general cargo ships deal with it frequently.

Chief officer / cargo officer - Checks the dangerous goods (DG) manifest against the stowage plan before and during loading. - Verifies that stowage and segregation comply with Part 7. This includes the special rules for deck and under-deck stowage and for certain classes kept away from living quarters or heat sources. - Confirms that the ship's Document of Compliance for DG permits that class in that cargo space. - Identifies the EmS schedule for each DG entry and briefs the crew on high-risk units.

OOW and deck ratings - Inspect DG units during cargo watch for leaks, damage and missing or wrong placards. Report anything doubtful before it is loaded. - Know where DG units are stowed. In a fire, the Master needs to know at once what is near the seat of the fire. - Watch for fumigated cargo transport units. These carry a specific warning mark and must not be entered until cleared.

Engine department - Must understand the restrictions on DG cargo spaces. Depending on the ship's DOC, these can include ventilation, bilge pumping and certified-safe electrical equipment. - Supports fire and spill response, including fire pumps, fixed systems and isolation.

Training. Shore-side personnel training is set out in the Code itself. Seafarers' competence in dangerous cargo is covered by STCW, within the cargo handling and stowage functions for officers. Many companies and charterers also require a separate IMDG course. These requirements vary by company and trade, so check your SMS.

Documents and records the Code creates on board

Expect these documents on any ship carrying packaged DG:

  • Document of Compliance for the carriage of dangerous goods. The flag issues this under SOLAS II-2/19, or under earlier provisions for older ships. It lists which classes may go in which cargo spaces.
  • Special list, manifest or detailed stowage plan. Required by SOLAS VII. It shows every DG item and where it is stowed. A copy must also be available ashore to the designated authority before departure.
  • Dangerous goods transport document. This is the shipper's declaration. It includes the UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group where applicable, quantity and marine pollutant status.
  • Container/vehicle packing certificate. Signed by the person responsible for packing the unit.
  • Emergency response information for each consignment, usually via EmS and MFAG references.
  • Cargo Securing Manual. Required by SOLAS VI/5.6 for ships carrying cargo units; SOLAS VII/5 requires DG units to be stowed and secured in accordance with it.
  • Current IMDG Code, Supplement (EmS/MFAG) on board, in paper or an accepted electronic format, as the flag allows.

Reporting. SOLAS Chapter VII requires the Master to report incidents involving the loss, or likely loss, of DG overboard. MARPOL also has reporting duties for harmful substances. The exact reporting route depends on the coastal state and your SMS.

Worked scenario: leaking drum found during a container check

Situation: During the 0400–0800 watch, an AB finds liquid seeping from a 20-foot container on deck. It carries Class 8 placards.

  1. Stand clear and raise the alarm. Approach from upwind and inform the OOW. The OOW informs the Master and chief officer.
  2. Identify the cargo. Find the container number on the DG manifest. Get the UN number, proper shipping name and the EmS spillage schedule from the Dangerous Goods List.
  3. Open the EmS schedule. Follow its guidance on PPE, whether to use water, and whether the substance may react with water or other cargo.
  4. Check MFAG in case anyone has been exposed.
  5. Check the neighbours. Use the stowage plan to see what is stowed adjacent. Incompatible goods nearby change the risk.
  6. Contain and protect. Follow the SMS. Consider altering course to put the leak downwind of the accommodation and close ventilation inlets where needed.
  7. Record and report. Make log entries. Notify the company, and the next port or coastal state as the SMS and local rules require.

The EmS Guide is guidance for the person in charge. It does not replace judgement or the ship's own contingency plans.

What port state control typically checks

PSC officers usually work through the paperwork first and then walk the deck. Common areas:

  • DOC for DG is valid, and the cargo actually carried matches the permitted classes and spaces.
  • DG manifest or stowage plan is accurate and matches what is on board.
  • Segregation is correct on the bay plan. Inspectors may check a sample of units.
  • Placards and marks are present, legible and correct for the declared cargo.
  • Current amendment of the IMDG Code and Supplement is on board.
  • Crew familiarity. Officers are often asked to find the EmS schedule for a given UN number, or to explain the response to a fire in a DG space.
  • Equipment linked to the DOC, such as water supply, ventilation, protective clothing and portable instruments, is in order.
  • Cargo Securing Manual is on board and approved.

Detention practice and focus areas vary between PSC regimes and over time. Expect deficiencies to be raised under the ISM Code when they point to weak procedures rather than a one-off error.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners like

Q: Under which convention is the IMDG Code mandatory? SOLAS Chapter VII, Part A. It also gives effect to MARPOL Annex III for marine pollutants.

Q: You have a UN number. Where do you start? The Dangerous Goods List in Part 3 (Volume 2). It gives the class, packing group, special provisions, EmS numbers, stowage category and segregation codes.

Q: What do EmS 'F' and 'S' schedules mean? F schedules (F-A to F-J) cover fire. S schedules (S-A to S-Z) cover spillage.

Q: Does the Code apply to paint in the paint locker? The Code generally excludes ship's stores and equipment. However, stores are still subject to SOLAS fire safety rules and your SMS.

Q: Name the segregation terms. From least to most strict:

  • 'Away from'
  • 'Separated from'
  • 'Separated by a complete compartment or hold from'
  • 'Separated longitudinally by an intervening complete compartment or hold from'

Q: Who certifies that a container was packed correctly? The person responsible for packing it, by signing the container/vehicle packing certificate.

Key points
  • The IMDG Code covers dangerous goods in packaged form only; bulk cargoes come under the IMSBC, IBC and IGC Codes.
  • It has been mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII since 1 January 2004 and also implements MARPOL Annex III.
  • It is amended every two years, so confirm which amendment your flag and company treat as current.
  • Part 3's Dangerous Goods List is the starting point for any UN number on board.
  • Key documents: DOC for DG, DG manifest or stowage plan, transport documents, packing certificates and the Cargo Securing Manual.
  • The EmS Guide and MFAG in the Supplement are your first references in a DG fire, spill or exposure.
  • PSC focuses on the DOC, manifest accuracy, segregation, placarding, the current edition and crew familiarity with EmS.

Frequently asked questions

Is an IMDG Code course mandatory for ship officers?

STCW covers seafarers' competence in dangerous cargo within officers' cargo handling and stowage functions, so a standalone IMDG certificate is not a general STCW requirement. Many companies, charterers and some administrations require a separate IMDG course. Check your company SMS and flag requirements.

What is the difference between the IMDG Code and the IMSBC Code?

The IMDG Code deals with dangerous goods in packages, IBCs, portable tanks, containers and vehicles. The IMSBC Code deals with solid cargoes carried in bulk, such as coal, ores and concentrates (grain is excluded and falls under the International Grain Code). A substance can appear in both, depending on how it is shipped.

What is the Document of Compliance for dangerous goods?

The flag administration issues it under SOLAS Chapter II-2. It states which classes of dangerous goods the ship may carry in which cargo spaces, based on the ship's construction and equipment. Older ships may be certified under earlier SOLAS provisions.

How often does the IMDG Code change?

IMO amends it on a two-year cycle, and each amendment is identified by a number and year. There is usually a period when the new amendment may be applied voluntarily before it becomes mandatory. Always confirm the edition currently in force with your company.

Where do I find what to do if a DG container catches fire?

Find the EmS fire schedule (F-A to F-J) for that UN number in the Dangerous Goods List, then follow it in the EmS Guide in the IMDG Supplement. Use MFAG for any casualties. Apply both together with your ship's own fire contingency plans.

Test yourself

Under which instrument did the IMDG Code become mandatory?

SOLAS Chapter VII, Part A made the IMDG Code mandatory from 1 January 2004. MARPOL Annex III links to it for marine pollutants.

Which cargo is covered by the IMDG Code?

The IMDG Code applies to dangerous goods in packaged form. Bulk solids, bulk liquefied gases and bulk liquid chemicals fall under the IMSBC, IGC and IBC Codes.

In the EmS Guide, what does schedule 'S-D' refer to?

In the EmS Guide (IMDG Supplement), 'S' schedules (S-A to S-Z) cover spillage and 'F' schedules (F-A to F-J) cover fire.

Where would you first look up the class, EmS and segregation details for a given UN number?

The Dangerous Goods List in Part 3 (Volume 2) gives class, packing group, EmS numbers, stowage and segregation information for each UN number.

What does packing group I indicate?

Under the IMDG Code classification provisions (Part 2), packing group I means great danger, II medium danger and III minor danger. Marine pollutant status is a separate classification.
