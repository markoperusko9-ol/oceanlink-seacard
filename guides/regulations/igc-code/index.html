IGC Code: A Practical Guide for Gas Carrier Crews

Quick answer

The IGC Code (International Code for the Construction and Equipment of Ships Carrying Liquefied Gases in Bulk) is made mandatory through SOLAS Chapter VII and applies to LNG, LPG, ammonia and other gas carriers regardless of size. The revised Code, adopted by resolution MSC.370(93), entered into force on 1 January 2016 and applies to ships constructed on or after 1 July 2016. Every compliant ship carries an International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk, and it is one of the first documents port state control will ask to see.

What the IGC Code is and how it becomes mandatory

The IGC Code is the IMO's rulebook for designing, building and equipping ships that carry liquefied gases in bulk. It covers LNG, LPG, ammonia, ethylene, vinyl chloride and the other products listed in its summary table.

The Code does not stand alone. SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C makes it mandatory for gas carriers, so a breach of the IGC Code is a breach of SOLAS. That is why port state control can detain a ship over it.

For the Code's purposes, a liquefied gas is a liquid with a vapour pressure above 0.28 MPa absolute at 37.8°C. Some products below that threshold are also listed in Chapter 19 and must be carried under the Code.

The basic aim is simple. The Code minimises risk to the ship, her crew and the environment, and it scales the precautions to how hazardous each product is.

Which ships it applies to, and the key dates

The IGC Code applies to gas carriers regardless of size, including ships under 500 GT. Which edition applies to your ship depends on when she was built:

  • Original IGC Code (resolution MSC.5(48)): mandatory for gas carriers constructed on or after 1 July 1986. It was amended several times over the following decades.
  • Revised IGC Code (resolution MSC.370(93), adopted 2014): entered into force on 1 January 2016. It applies to gas carriers constructed (keel laid or similar stage) on or after 1 July 2016.
  • Older tonnage: ships built before the IGC Code era were built to earlier IMO gas carrier codes.

On joining, check your Certificate of Fitness. It states which edition of the rules the ship was built to.

The Code continues to be amended. Always work from the consolidated edition the company and flag have supplied. Do not rely on an old copy found in a cabin.

Do not confuse the IGC Code with the IGF Code. The IGF Code covers ships other than gas carriers that burn gas or low-flashpoint fuels. A gas carrier burning its own boil-off follows the cargo-as-fuel chapter of the IGC Code.

Structure of the Code, chapter by chapter

The revised Code has 19 chapters plus appendices, including the model Certificate of Fitness.

Ship types. The Code grades ships by the hazard of the cargoes they can carry:

  • 1G: the highest level of protection.
  • 2G: an intermediate level.
  • 2PG: a type 2G ship of 150 m in length or less with independent type C tanks designed for a MARVS of at least 0.7 MPa gauge and a design temperature of -55°C or above.
  • 3G: the lowest.

Chapter 19 tells you which ship type each product needs.

Tank types. The main types include integral, membrane and semi-membrane tanks, and independent tanks of types A, B and C. Secondary barrier requirements depend on the cargo temperature at atmospheric pressure:

  • At -10°C and above: no secondary barrier is required for any tank type.
  • Below -10°C: membrane, semi-membrane and type A tanks need a complete secondary barrier. For type A tanks between -10°C and -55°C, the hull may serve as the secondary barrier. Type B tanks need a partial secondary barrier. Type C pressure vessels need none.

Integral tanks are not normally allowed for cargoes below -10°C.

What it means in daily work on board

Most of the Code is aimed at shipyards and class. A good part of it still lands on the watchkeeper's desk.

  • Product check before loading. Every cargo nominated must appear on the Certificate of Fitness. Chapter 19 and Chapter 17 then tell you what extra precautions apply, such as toxic vapour detection, inhibitor requirements or special materials.
  • Filling limits. The basic filling limit is 98% of tank volume at the reference temperature. The actual loading limit is worked out from the cargo densities. Use the approved loading limit tables for your ship. Never use a rule of thumb.
  • Relief valve settings. Some ships have relief valves with more than one set pressure, for example a higher harbour setting. Changing the setting is a controlled operation under the ship's procedures, and it must be recorded.
  • ESD system. The cargo emergency shutdown system must stop cargo flow and pumps when triggered. Typical triggers are ESD pushbuttons, fusible plugs, high-high level or loss of control power. It is tested before cargo operations as required by the ship's procedures and the terminal.
  • Gas detection. Fixed gas detection covers cargo machinery spaces, hold and interbarrier spaces, airlocks and other required locations. Portable instruments must be calibrated and fit for the products carried.
  • Personnel protection. Protective clothing, breathing apparatus, eye wash and decontamination showers must be ready for use. For certain toxic cargoes, Chapter 14 requires emergency escape respiratory and eye protection for every person on board.
  • Fire protection. The water spray system protects tank domes, manifolds, cargo control areas and accommodation fronts. The dry chemical powder system covers the cargo area. Both are tested and maintained under the PMS.
  • Training. Gas tanker officers and ratings with cargo duties need STCW training under Regulation V/1-2: basic, plus advanced for those with immediate responsibility for cargo.

Worked scenario: an unfamiliar nomination

The charterer nominates a product the ship has not carried before. As Chief Officer, work through these steps:

  1. Confirm the product is listed on the Certificate of Fitness.
  2. Check the Chapter 19 entry: required ship type, tank type, vapour detection, gauging and any Chapter 17 special requirements.
  3. Confirm compatibility with tank and pipeline materials, and with the previous cargo. Gas-freeing or purging may be needed.
  4. Recalculate the loading limits for the new density and reference temperature.
  5. Check that personnel protection and detection equipment suit the hazard.
  6. Refer to the cargo data sheet and the company procedures, then report to the Master.

If the product is not on the certificate, the ship cannot load it. Updating the certificate is a matter for the Administration or the recognised organisation, not the ship.

Certificates and records the Code creates on board

International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk

This is the key document. It is issued after an initial survey and follows the harmonised survey cycle of annual, intermediate and renewal surveys, with validity not exceeding five years. It records:

  • the ship type;
  • the cargo containment system and design conditions;
  • the products the ship may carry, with any conditions attached.

Older ships built to earlier codes hold an equivalent certificate under those rules.

Other documents and records you should expect:

  • Approved loading limit (filling) information for the cargo tanks.
  • Cargo data or information for every product carried, so the crew know its properties, hazards and emergency procedures.
  • Cargo operations and system manuals covering containment, reliquefaction, inerting and so on, as required by the Code and the SMS.
  • Records of ESD tests, gas detector calibrations, relief valve settings and changes, and water spray and dry powder system tests. Exact formats depend on the company SMS and flag requirements.
  • STCW endorsements for liquefied gas tanker training for the officers and ratings concerned.

Survey intervals, documentation formats and some equipment details can vary by flag state and classification society. Check your flag's requirements and the class notation.

What port state control typically checks

A PSC officer boarding a gas carrier will usually start with paperwork and then look at the hardware the Code requires.

  • Certificate of Fitness: valid, endorsed for annual or intermediate surveys, and the cargo on board is listed.
  • Crew STCW certificates and liquefied gas tanker endorsements, matched against duties.
  • Fixed gas detection: working, with alarms tested and calibration records available. Portable detectors are calibrated.
  • ESD system: tests recorded, and the crew able to demonstrate how it works.
  • Water spray and dry powder systems: condition, and crew familiarity.
  • Personnel protection: breathing apparatus, protective clothing, decontamination showers and eye wash working. Escape sets are on board where required for the cargo.
  • Relief valves and vent masts: condition, and settings matching the documentation.
  • Hazardous area electrical equipment: no damaged fittings or unapproved portable electrical gear.
  • Airlocks and gas-tight doors where fitted: alarms and self-closing arrangements working.

PSC also checks drills and crew competence. Expect to be asked how to start the water spray, where the ESD pushbuttons are, or how to respond to a gas alarm in the compressor room.

Oral exam: what examiners ask about the IGC Code

Q: How is the IGC Code made mandatory? Through SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C.

Q: When did the revised Code enter into force, and which ships does it apply to? It entered into force on 1 January 2016. It applies to ships constructed on or after 1 July 2016.

Q: Name the tank types and their secondary barrier requirements. The answer depends on the cargo temperature at atmospheric pressure. At -10°C and above, no secondary barrier is required for any tank type. Below -10°C:

  • Membrane and semi-membrane: complete secondary barrier.
  • Type A: complete secondary barrier. Between -10°C and -55°C, the hull may serve as the secondary barrier.
  • Type B: partial secondary barrier.
  • Type C: none.
  • Integral tanks are not normally allowed below -10°C.

Q: Where do you find the requirements for a specific product? In the Chapter 19 summary table, read with any Chapter 17 special requirements it refers to.

Q: What certificate does the Code require? The International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk.

Q: What is the difference between IGC and IGF? The IGC Code covers gas carriers, including burning cargo as fuel. The IGF Code covers other ships using gas or low-flashpoint fuels.

Q: What is MARVS? The maximum allowable relief valve setting of a cargo tank.

Examiner's tip: candidates lose marks by quoting figures they are unsure of. If you do not know a number, explain where on board you would find it. That shows exactly the judgement the examiner is looking for.

Key points
  • The IGC Code is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C, for gas carriers of any size.
  • The revised Code (MSC.370(93)) entered into force on 1 January 2016 and applies to ships constructed on or after 1 July 2016.
  • Only cargoes listed on the International Certificate of Fitness may be carried.
  • Chapter 19 lists each product's ship type and requirements; Chapter 17 adds product-specific rules.
  • Below -10°C, membrane and type A tanks need a complete secondary barrier, type B a partial one and type C none; at -10°C and above, none is required.
  • Crew with cargo duties need STCW liquefied gas tanker training under Regulation V/1-2.
  • PSC focuses on the certificate, gas detection, ESD, water spray, personnel protection and crew familiarity.

Frequently asked questions

What is the full name of the IGC Code?

It is the International Code for the Construction and Equipment of Ships Carrying Liquefied Gases in Bulk. It is made mandatory through SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C, and applies to LNG, LPG, ammonia and other gas carriers.

Does the IGC Code apply to small gas carriers under 500 GT?

Yes. The Code applies to gas carriers regardless of size. Some flag states add their own requirements for small or domestic gas carriers, so check with your Administration.

What is the difference between the IGC Code and the IGF Code?

The IGC Code governs ships that carry liquefied gas as cargo, including using the cargo as fuel. The IGF Code applies to other ships that use gas or other low-flashpoint fuels for propulsion or power.

Can a gas carrier load a product not listed on its Certificate of Fitness?

No. The certificate lists the products the ship is approved to carry. Adding a product requires the Administration or the recognised organisation acting for it to assess the ship and amend the certificate before loading.

What training do I need to work on a gas carrier under the IGC Code?

The STCW Convention, not the IGC Code itself, sets the training: basic training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations under Regulation V/1-2, and advanced training for officers with immediate responsibility for cargo. Sea service and course requirements for endorsement vary by Administration.

Test yourself

Which certificate is issued to a ship complying with the IGC Code?

The IGC Code requires the International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk; the NLS certificate belongs to MARPOL Annex II. Reference: IGC Code Chapter 1.

The revised IGC Code (MSC.370(93)) applies to gas carriers constructed on or after which date?

It entered into force on 1 January 2016 but applies to ships constructed on or after 1 July 2016; 1 July 1986 is the original IGC Code date. Reference: SOLAS Chapter VII Part C; resolution MSC.370(93).

What secondary barrier does an independent type C cargo tank require?

Type C tanks are pressure vessels designed so that no secondary barrier is required at any cargo temperature; other tank types may need one when the cargo temperature is below -10°C. Reference: IGC Code Chapter 4.

Where in the IGC Code do you find the required ship type and special requirements for a specific product?

Chapter 19's table gives ship type, tank type and references to special requirements in Chapter 17 for each product. Reference: IGC Code Chapters 17 and 19.

Which STCW regulation covers mandatory training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations?

Regulation V/1-2 covers liquefied gas tankers; V/1-1 covers oil and chemical tankers and V/2 passenger ships. Reference: STCW Convention Regulation V/1-2 and STCW Code Section A-V/1-2.
