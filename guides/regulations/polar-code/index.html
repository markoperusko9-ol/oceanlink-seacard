The Polar Code: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

The Polar Code is IMO's mandatory set of safety and pollution-prevention requirements for ships operating in Arctic waters and the Antarctic area. It entered into force on 1 January 2017 through SOLAS chapter XIV and amendments to MARPOL. On board, it requires a Polar Ship Certificate, a Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM) and, depending on ship type and ice conditions, officers with STCW polar training. Port state control commonly checks these.

What the Polar Code is and when it came into force

The International Code for Ships Operating in Polar Waters, known as the Polar Code, adds polar-specific requirements on top of SOLAS and MARPOL. It does not replace them. The idea is simple. Ice, extreme cold, long periods of darkness or daylight, poor charts, limited communications and very long rescue times create hazards that the normal conventions do not fully cover.

The Code has two halves, adopted separately:

  • Safety part: adopted by the Maritime Safety Committee (resolution MSC.385(94)). It is made mandatory through a new SOLAS chapter XIV.
  • Environmental part: adopted by the Marine Environment Protection Committee (resolution MEPC.264(68)). It is made mandatory through amendments to MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V.

Both entered into force on 1 January 2017.

  • Ships built on or after that date had to comply from delivery.
  • Under the safety part, ships built before that date had to comply by their first intermediate or renewal survey after 1 January 2018.

The related STCW training requirements came in separately, on 1 July 2018.

Where and to whom it applies

Polar waters means Arctic waters and the Antarctic area.

  • Antarctic area: the sea south of latitude 60°S.
  • Arctic waters: defined by a line of coordinates set out in SOLAS chapter XIV and MARPOL. It is not simply "north of 60°N". The boundary bulges south near Greenland and dips north in the Norwegian Sea, so check the definition against your passage plan rather than assuming.

The safety part (SOLAS chapter XIV) applies to ships holding SOLAS certificates, broadly:

  • passenger ships; and
  • cargo ships of 500 GT and above on international voyages.

Fishing vessels, smaller cargo ships and pleasure yachts are outside the mandatory safety part. Some flag and coastal states apply their own rules to these vessels, so check with the administration.

The environmental part applies to ships covered by the relevant MARPOL annexes, which is a wider group of vessels.

Structure of the Code and ship categories

The Code is split into mandatory parts and guidance parts:

Part Content Status
Introduction Goals, definitions, hazard sources Mandatory
Part I-A Safety measures Mandatory
Part I-B Guidance on Part I-A Recommendatory
Part II-A Pollution prevention (oil, noxious liquids, sewage, garbage) Mandatory
Part II-B Guidance on Part II-A Recommendatory

Part I-A has chapters on:

  • the PWOM
  • structure
  • stability
  • watertight integrity
  • machinery
  • fire safety
  • life-saving appliances
  • navigation
  • communications
  • voyage planning
  • manning and training

Many chapters are written as goals and functional requirements rather than fixed specifications. This means the details for your ship often sit in her approved documents, not in the Code itself.

Ship categories drive much of what applies:

  • Category A: designed for at least medium first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions.
  • Category B: not Category A, but designed for at least thin first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions.
  • Category C: designed for open water, or ice lighter than Categories A and B.

The Part I-B guidance links these categories to IACS Polar Classes:

  • PC1 to PC5 broadly correspond to Category A.
  • PC6 and PC7 broadly correspond to Category B.

A Category C ship may have a lower ice class or no ice class at all.

You will also meet the Polar Service Temperature (PST). Ships intended to operate in low air temperature have a PST set at least 10°C below the lowest Mean Daily Low Temperature for the intended area and season. Equipment and materials must work at that temperature.

"Low air temperature" means areas where the lowest Mean Daily Low Temperature is below −10°C.

Certificates and documents it creates on board

Polar Ship Certificate

This is issued by or on behalf of the flag state after survey. It is supplemented by a Record of Equipment. Expect it to state:

  • the ship category;
  • the ice class, if any;
  • the PST, where applicable;
  • the operational limitations, such as ice types, temperatures and high-latitude limits;
  • the method used to assess operational capability in ice.

Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM)

This is ship-specific and carried on board. It covers:

  • the ship's capabilities and limitations;
  • procedures for normal operations;
  • risk mitigation, including icing and operating in ice or with icebreaker escort;
  • emergency procedures and how to get emergency response support.

The PWOM is a working document for the bridge and engine room, not a shelf ornament.

Operational limitation assessment

POLARIS (Polar Operational Limit Assessment Risk Indexing System), described in MSC.1/Circ.1519, is the IMO-recognised methodology and is widely used. It combines the ship's ice class with the ice regime to give a risk index. Depending on ice class, the index indicates normal operation, elevated operational risk, or operation subject to special consideration / not permitted. Other methods may be accepted. Use whatever your certificate and PWOM state.

Other records

You will also need:

  • a voyage plan meeting the polar voyage-planning chapter;
  • officers' STCW polar training certificates;
  • the MARPOL records you already keep, such as the Oil Record Book and Garbage Record Book, reflecting the stricter polar discharge rules.

What it means for your daily work

Deck

  • Voyage planning: the plan must take into account:
    • current ice information and statistical ice data;
    • limited or unreliable charts and hydrographic data;
    • areas of marine mammals and protected areas;
    • places of refuge and SAR availability;
    • routeing measures and ship reporting.
  • Navigation equipment: the Code requires a means of receiving ice and weather information and of keeping bridge windows clear of ice. It also requires suitable searchlights and other measures, some depending on category and build date. The Record of Equipment shows what your ship has.
  • Icing: ice accretion raises the centre of gravity and blocks vents, fire equipment and LSA. Follow the de-icing procedures in the PWOM and keep stability under watch.

Life-saving appliances

The Code assumes a maximum expected time of rescue of at least 5 days. This drives:

  • personal survival equipment;
  • group survival equipment;
  • emergency rations and thermal protection.

Check these kits during familiarisation and make sure you know where your immersion suit is.

Engine

Machinery must work at the PST. Key concerns include:

  • protecting sea suctions from ice blockage;
  • heating where needed;
  • preventing freezing of fire mains, emergency fire pumps and fuel lines.

Pollution

Any discharge of oil or oily mixtures into polar waters is prohibited, as is any discharge of noxious liquid substances. Sewage and garbage discharges are further restricted by distance from ice shelves and fast ice. Food waste must never be put onto the ice.

New Category A and B ships also have fuel tanks separated from the outer shell.

Separately, MARPOL Annex I bans the use and carriage of heavy fuel oil in the Antarctic area. Its Arctic HFO restrictions applied from 1 July 2024, with limited waivers available to Arctic coastal states until 1 July 2029. These are MARPOL measures rather than Polar Code text, but you will deal with them on the same voyage.

Training and manning requirements

Chapter 12 of the Code sets out who needs polar training. The training itself is defined in STCW Regulation V/4 and Section A-V/4. There are two levels:

  • Basic training for ships operating in polar waters: for masters, chief mates and officers in charge of a navigational watch.
  • Advanced training for ships operating in polar waters: for masters and chief mates. It requires the basic certificate, at least two months of approved seagoing service in the deck department (at management level or while keeping watch at operational level) within polar waters, or equivalent approved service, and completion of approved advanced training.

Which level is required depends on three things:

  • the ship type: tanker, passenger ship or other;
  • the ice conditions;
  • whether the waters are ice-free, open water (ice concentration below 1/10) or other waters.

In ice-free waters no polar training is mandated. In more severe ice, masters and chief mates need advanced training and OOWs need basic training. Tankers and passenger ships face requirements earlier than other ships. Check the table in chapter 12 against your actual voyage.

Engineers and ratings are not covered by the STCW polar certificates. However, the Code requires all crew to be familiar with the procedures and equipment in the PWOM relevant to their duties. Many companies add their own polar familiarisation through the SMS.

Where ice-navigation experience is required, some companies and coastal states also require an ice navigator or ice pilot on board. These rules vary, so check with the company and the coastal state.

Worked scenario and PSC focus

Scenario: You join as Chief Officer on a PC6 bulk carrier (Category B) bound for a Canadian Arctic port in summer. Ice reports show thin first-year ice at concentrations of 1/10 or more on part of the route.

  1. Check that the Polar Ship Certificate is valid and that the planned area and season fall within its operational limitations and PST.
  2. Confirm that you and the Master hold advanced polar certificates and the OOWs hold basic, as the chapter 12 table requires for these ice conditions.
  3. Read the PWOM, especially the sections on icing, operating in ice and emergency contacts.
  4. Build the voyage plan with:
    • current ice charts;
    • POLARIS assessments for the expected ice regimes;
    • mammal and protected areas;
    • any coastal state reporting requirements.
  5. Inventory the survival kits, check that heaters and de-icing gear work, and confirm that the emergency fire pump and hydrants are protected from freezing.
  6. Brief the crew. Record the POLARIS outcome before entering ice, and reassess whenever conditions change.

What PSC typically looks at

Inspections vary by MoU and coastal state, but inspectors commonly check:

  • the Polar Ship Certificate and Record of Equipment, and whether the voyage is within its limits;
  • the PWOM, and whether officers actually know its content;
  • the officers' polar training certificates against the chapter 12 table;
  • the voyage plan, including ice information and the operational assessment;
  • survival equipment, thermal protection and the condition of LSA in cold conditions;
  • discharge records against polar restrictions.

Missing polar certificates or a PWOM that doesn't match the ship are classic deficiencies.

Oral exam style questions

  • What is the PWOM and who uses it? A ship-specific manual giving capabilities, limitations and procedures. The whole bridge and engine team uses it.
  • What is the PST? A design temperature at least 10°C below the lowest Mean Daily Low Temperature for the intended area and season.
  • What does POLARIS do? It combines the ice class with the ice regime to produce a risk index guiding whether to proceed.
  • What expected time of rescue does the Code assume for survival equipment? At least 5 days.
Key points
  • The Polar Code became mandatory on 1 January 2017 via SOLAS chapter XIV and amendments to MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V.
  • Ships are categorised A, B or C by the ice conditions they are designed for.
  • Every ship operating in polar waters under SOLAS chapter XIV needs a Polar Ship Certificate and a ship-specific PWOM.
  • STCW Regulation V/4 sets basic and advanced polar training; who needs which depends on ship type and ice conditions.
  • Any discharge of oil or noxious liquid substances into polar waters is prohibited.
  • Survival equipment is sized for an expected time of rescue of at least 5 days.
  • PSC focuses on the certificate, the PWOM, officer training and the voyage plan.

Frequently asked questions

When did the Polar Code come into force?

It entered into force on 1 January 2017 for both the safety and environmental parts. Under the safety part, ships built before that date had until their first intermediate or renewal survey after 1 January 2018 to comply. The STCW polar training requirements entered into force on 1 July 2018.

Does the Polar Code apply north of 60°N?

Not exactly. In the south, the Antarctic area is everything south of 60°S. Arctic waters are defined by a line of coordinates that departs from 60°N in several places, so check the legal boundary in SOLAS chapter XIV or MARPOL against your route.

Who needs Polar Code basic or advanced training?

Masters, chief mates and officers in charge of a navigational watch, depending on ship type and ice conditions as set out in chapter 12 of the Code. In more severe ice, masters and chief mates need advanced training and OOWs need basic training. No polar training is mandated in ice-free waters.

What is a Polar Water Operational Manual?

The PWOM is a ship-specific manual carried on board. It describes the ship's polar capabilities and limitations and gives procedures for normal operations, operating in ice, icing and emergencies. Officers are expected to know it, and PSC may test that.

Does the Polar Code apply to fishing vessels and yachts?

The mandatory safety part does not, because it applies through SOLAS chapter XIV to SOLAS-certificated ships. The MARPOL-based environmental part can still apply, and some flag and coastal states impose their own requirements, so always check with the administration.

Test yourself

Through which instrument is the safety part of the Polar Code made mandatory?

SOLAS chapter XIV makes Part I-A of the Polar Code mandatory. STCW V/4 covers training only (SOLAS chapter XIV).

A ship designed for at least medium first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions, is which Polar Code category?

Category A covers at least medium first-year ice. Category B covers thin first-year ice, and PC7 broadly aligns with Category B (Polar Code, Introduction).

What is the Polar Code rule on discharging oil or oily mixtures in polar waters?

Part II-A prohibits any discharge of oil or oily mixtures into polar waters. The 15 ppm standard does not apply there (Polar Code Part II-A; MARPOL Annex I).

What minimum expected time of rescue does the Polar Code assume when specifying survival equipment?

The LSA chapter requires the maximum expected time of rescue to be taken as never less than 5 days (Polar Code Part I-A, chapter 8).

Which document sets out the ship's polar capabilities, limitations and operating procedures?

The PWOM is the ship-specific operational manual. The Record of Equipment only lists fitted equipment (Polar Code Part I-A, chapter 2).
