Working aloft and overside: procedure, checklists and lessons

Quick answer

Working aloft and overside is high-risk work. It is normally controlled by a permit to work, a risk assessment, isolation of radar, whistle, propeller and discharges, a full body harness with an independent lifeline, and a competent person standing by. Whether a height threshold applies, permit formats and who may sign them are set by your flag state and company SMS, so always check the ship's own procedures first.

What counts as aloft and overside, and why it kills people

Working aloft means work at height where a fall could cause injury. Typical jobs are on masts, radar platforms, crane jibs, funnel tops, hold ladders and staging inside cargo holds or tanks. Working overside means work outside the ship's rails or hull, where a person could fall into the water or onto a quay or barge. Typical jobs are hull painting from stages, draught mark work, freeing gear, and work from a bosun's chair.

There is no single worldwide height figure. The current UK Code of Safe Working Practices, for example, sets no minimum height: work at height is any place where a fall could cause injury. Many company SMSs use 2 metres as a practical benchmark, but your SMS defines it for your ship. If in doubt, treat the job as work at height.

Falls from height and falls overboard remain among the leading causes of death in the industry. Investigation reports keep finding the same causes:

  • No lifeline, or a lifeline attached to the same rope or staging that failed.
  • Rotten or chafed gantlines and stage ropes that nobody inspected.
  • A radar or whistle that was not isolated, startling or injuring the worker.
  • No standby person, or a standby person who left to fetch something.
  • Work started in weather or sea conditions that should have stopped it.
  • Complacency on a "quick five-minute job".

The regulatory framework in plain terms

No IMO convention gives you a ready-made procedure for working aloft. The obligation comes through several layers.

  • ISM Code (via SOLAS Chapter IX): the company must establish procedures, plans and instructions for key shipboard operations. Work at height and overside is almost always one of them. Your SMS procedure and permit form come from here.
  • MLC 2006, Regulation 4.3: the flag state must ensure ships have occupational safety and health policies and programmes. This includes risk evaluation, training and protective equipment.
  • Flag-state guidance and national law: many administrations publish codes of safe working practice. Some make them mandatory for their ships.
  • ILO code of practice on accident prevention on board ship: widely used guidance on staging, chairs and fall protection.

In practice, the company SMS and the permit to work prevail on board. If the SMS is stricter than what you learned elsewhere, follow the SMS. If you think the SMS is weaker than safe practice, raise it with the Master or safety officer. Do not quietly do your own thing.

Step-by-step procedure (general industry practice)

This is a typical sequence. Your SMS may order it differently or add steps.

1. Plan and risk assess - Define the job, location, duration and people involved. - Review or carry out the risk assessment. Check weather, sea state, ship's motion, light, other operations nearby, and the rescue arrangements. - Confirm everyone involved is fit, rested and familiar with the equipment.

2. Permit to work - Raise the permit for work aloft or overside as required by the SMS. - Get it authorised by the responsible officer, usually the Chief Officer for deck work and the Chief Engineer or 2nd Engineer for engine-room work. The Master must be informed or must approve, depending on the SMS. - Note the permit validity. Do not extend a permit beyond the period it allows without re-authorisation.

3. Isolations and communications - Bridge: informed. Radar scanners and transmitting aerials near the work are switched off and tagged. The whistle or typhon is isolated if work is near it. - Engine room: informed. There must be no overboard discharges, boiler blowdowns or funnel soot blowing near the work. For overside work, propeller and thrusters are immobilised as required. - Cargo office or terminal: informed if work is in port and near cargo or crane operations. - Agree the radio channel and a backup signal. Test the radios before starting.

4. Equipment check - Inspect the full body harness, lanyards, energy absorber, rope grab, karabiners and hooks for cuts, corrosion, stitching damage and expiry. - Inspect gantlines, stage boards, bosun's chair and their ropes. Reject anything doubtful. Do not use ropes known to have been used for other cargo work or exposed to chemicals. - Overside: wear a working lifejacket or buoyancy aid as the SMS requires. Have a lifebuoy with line ready at the work site. - Secure tools with lanyards or in bags. Barricade the area below.

5. Rig and attach - Rig the lifeline to a separate strong point, independent of the stage or chair suspension. - Rig the chair or stage under the supervision of a competent person, usually the Bosun. - Stay attached at all times when exposed to a fall. When you move between anchor points, use the double-lanyard technique, so that one lanyard is always connected.

6. Do the job with a standby person - A competent person stays at the work site throughout, in sight or voice contact. Their only job is to watch and to raise the alarm. - Stop the work if the weather, ship's motion or any condition changes from what was assessed.

7. Complete and close out - Remove all equipment and personnel. Clear the area. - Restore the isolations and inform the bridge and engine room. - Close the permit. Report any defects or near misses.

A rescue plan is not optional. A worker hanging in a harness after an arrested fall can develop suspension trauma. Everyone must know beforehand how they will get that person down or up quickly: rescue kit, recovery line, or lowering to deck. "Call for help" is not a plan.

Worked scenarios and real lessons

Scenario 1: Radar platform at anchorage. The 3rd Officer is asked to clean the X-band scanner. The OOW switches the radar to standby but does not isolate the supply or hang a tag. Another officer, unaware of the job, switches it to transmit to check traffic.

Lesson: standby is not isolation. Use the ship's lock-out tag-out arrangement for radar and aerial work, and keep the bridge team briefed.

Scenario 2: Painting draught marks from a stage in port. The stage ropes are made fast to the rail. The lifelines are tied to the same stanchion. The stanchion base is corroded and fails. Both the stage and the lifelines go with it.

Lesson: lifelines must have an independent strong point, and you must check the strength of that point. Do not assume it.

Scenario 3: Freeing a fouled flag halyard underway. An AB climbs the mast "just for a minute" with no permit. The ship rolls in a beam swell.

Lesson: short jobs are not exempt. Many SMSs prohibit or strictly restrict aloft work underway in a seaway. Alter course or speed if needed, or wait.

Scenario 4: Overside work during bunkering. The crew rig a stage near the bunker manifold while the barge is alongside.

Lesson: simultaneous operations need coordination. The permit authority must deconflict jobs, and the overside work may have to wait.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners at all levels like this topic because it tests whether you can manage risk, not just recite rules. Practise answering these aloud.

  • "You are OOW. The Bosun wants two ABs to work on the foremast. What do you do?" Check the permit and risk assessment. Isolate the radar and whistle as relevant. Confirm the PPE, the standby person and communications. Inform the Master per the SMS. Log it, and monitor the weather and traffic.
  • "Why must the lifeline be independent?" So that one failure, of a rope, the chair or an anchor point, does not take away both support and fall protection.
  • "What do you isolate for overside work aft?" The propeller and thrusters as required, and overboard discharges near the area. Agree the arrangement with the engine room.
  • "When would you stop the job?" Deteriorating weather or motion, loss of communications, the standby person leaving, a change to conditions on the permit, or anyone feeling unwell or unsure.
  • "What is suspension trauma and how do you plan for it?" It is a dangerous physiological effect of hanging motionless in a harness. Plan the rescue before the work starts, with the equipment ready at the site.
  • (Senior level) "How do you ensure the procedure works, not just exists?" Through drills, equipment inspection records, supervision, near-miss reporting and review of the procedure under the ISM Code.
Key points
  • Company SMS and the permit to work prevail; height thresholds vary by flag and company.
  • Always use a full body harness with a lifeline on an independent strong point.
  • Isolate and tag radar, aerials and whistle aloft, and propeller, thrusters and discharges overside.
  • A dedicated standby person with tested communications must stay at the site.
  • Plan the rescue of a suspended worker before anyone leaves the deck.
  • Stop the job if weather, motion or any permit condition changes.
  • No job is too short for a permit and proper fall protection.

Frequently asked questions

At what height does work become 'working aloft'?

There is no single international figure. Current UK guidance (COSWP and the Work at Height Regulations) sets no minimum height and covers any place where a fall could cause injury. Many companies use 2 metres as a practical benchmark. Your company SMS defines the threshold for your ship. If a fall could hurt you, treat the job as work at height.

Can we work overside while the ship is underway?

Generally it is avoided. Many SMSs prohibit it or allow it only with the Master's specific approval and extra precautions. Speed, motion and the difficulty of recovering a person from the water make it very hazardous. Check your SMS; if it is silent, ask the Master before assuming it is allowed.

Who signs the permit for working aloft?

It depends on the company SMS. Typically the Chief Officer authorises deck work and the Chief Engineer or 2nd Engineer authorises engine-room work. The Master is informed or approves as the procedure requires. The person doing the work and the standby person should also understand and acknowledge the permit conditions.

Is a lifejacket required when working overside?

Common practice and most SMSs require a lifejacket or working buoyancy aid, plus a lifebuoy with line ready at the site. Some companies specify the type, for example an inflatable working lifejacket that is compatible with a harness. Follow the PPE stated on your permit.

Why must the radar be isolated, not just put on standby?

On standby, someone can switch the radar back to transmit within seconds. Exposure to a rotating scanner or to radiated energy close to the antenna can injure the worker. Isolating the supply and tagging it under the lock-out tag-out procedure makes accidental start-up much less likely.

Test yourself

Where should the lifeline of a person in a bosun's chair be secured?

An independent anchor ensures a single failure does not remove both support and fall arrest. This is established safe working practice (e.g. ILO code of practice, flag-state codes) and is normally written into company SMS procedures.

Before an AB cleans the radar scanner, what is the correct action on the bridge?

Standby can be reversed instantly; isolation and tagging control accidental start-up. This is typically specified in company SMS procedures developed under ISM Code section 7.

What decides the height above which the ship's work-at-height procedure applies?

No IMO convention sets one global figure; flags and companies define it. Comes under the ISM Code, section 7 and MLC 2006, Regulation 4.3.

What is the main duty of the standby person during work aloft?

The standby person must not be diverted to other tasks. Typical SMS requirement under the ISM Code, section 7.

For overside work near the stern in port, which isolation is essential?

Propeller wash, thruster suction and discharges directly endanger overside workers. Typical SMS isolation requirement under the ISM Code, section 7.
