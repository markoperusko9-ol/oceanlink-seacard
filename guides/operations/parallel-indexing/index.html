Parallel Indexing: Radar Track Monitoring for Watchkeepers

Quick answer

Parallel indexing is a radar technique where you draw a line parallel to the planned track, offset by the charted perpendicular distance (cross-index range) from a fixed, radar-conspicuous target. If the ship is on track, the echo slides along that line, and any drift off it shows cross-track error over the ground in real time, independent of GNSS. It is planned during passage planning, set up before each leg and cross-checked against other fixing methods, and your company SMS and the Master's standing orders define how it is used on your ship.

What parallel indexing is and why it still matters

Parallel indexing (PI) turns the radar into a continuous cross-track monitor. You do not need to plot fixes every few minutes. You watch a fixed echo run along a pre-set line, and the moment it drifts off that line you know the ship is being set off track and roughly by how much.

The principle is simple. In relative terms, a fixed object moves past your ship along a path parallel and reciprocal to your track over the ground. Say the object lies 2 miles off the planned track. If the ship stays on track, the echo will pass along a line 2 miles off own ship, parallel to that track. Draw that line on the radar and you have a live track monitor.

Why it still earns its place on a bridge full of ECDIS and GNSS:

  • It is independent of satellite positioning. It uses radar range and gyro heading against a real, physical target. GNSS spoofing, jamming or a datum mismatch will not fool it.
  • It shows ground track, not water track. Set, drift and leeway are all included automatically, because you are watching your movement relative to the land.
  • It is continuous. You see the trend developing between fixes, which matters in pilotage waters where a few cables decide the outcome.
  • It frees your eyes. A glance at the echo against the line confirms you are on track, leaving more time for look-out and traffic.

It is a monitoring tool, not a substitute for fixing. Good practice is to run PI alongside regular position fixes by other means and the ECDIS display, so that any one method that goes wrong is caught by the others.

Planning PI on the chart: choosing targets and measuring the cross-index range

PI is prepared during passage planning, not invented on the bridge at the last minute. For each critical leg, the planning officer (usually the 2nd Officer) marks the PI targets and their cross-index ranges on the chart or in the ECDIS route notes. The OOW checks them before the leg.

Choosing a good target:

  • Fixed and charted. Use a lighthouse on a headland, a steep-to point, a breakwater head, a small rocky islet or a racon on a fixed structure.
  • Radar-conspicuous and identifiable. It should give a clear echo that you can positively match to the chart.
  • Steep-to. A cliff gives an echo close to the charted coastline. A low sandy shore or mangrove may paint well inland of, or short of, the charted line, and the effect can change with the state of tide.
  • Within sensible range and not shadowed. It should be visible for the whole leg, or you need a planned changeover to the next target.
  • Avoid floating aids where possible. Buoys can drag, be off station or be missed in clutter. If one must be used, treat it with suspicion and cross-check.

Measuring the cross-index range (CIR):

  1. Draw the planned track for the leg.
  2. From the chosen target, drop a perpendicular to the track line.
  3. Measure that perpendicular distance. That is the CIR.
  4. Note which side of the track the target lies on (port or starboard).
  5. Write it on the chart in a standard form, for example PI: Lt Ho, 1.8' stbd, track 312°T.

Also plan margin-of-safety lines. These are extra index lines offset to show the edge of the safe water. If the echo crosses one, you are outside your cross-track limit and must act. Lines for the wheel-over and for the next leg are covered below.

Setting up parallel index lines on the radar, step by step

Modern type-approved radars have dedicated PI line functions. Menus differ by manufacturer, so learn your own set during familiarisation. The method is the same everywhere:

  1. Check the radar first. Make sure it is properly tuned with gain and clutter set for the conditions. Check heading marker alignment, and check that range accuracy and gyro input are correct. Compare the gyro with the latest gyro error check.
  2. Use a stabilised, north-up or course-up display. PI lines are laid on true bearings. On an unstabilised head-up display, yaw swings the picture and the line is meaningless.
  3. Select an appropriate range scale. The target and the line should both be clearly visible with room to see trend.
  4. Set the line direction to the planned track direction in degrees true. A line on 312° is the same line as one on 132°, so the direction of the line itself does not matter. Getting the side and distance right is what counts.
  5. Set the offset equal to the CIR, on the same side as the target. If the target is to starboard of track, the line goes to starboard of own ship.
  6. Confirm the target echo is positively identified and lies on, or will come onto, the line.
  7. Add margin-of-safety lines either side if planned.
  8. Label or note the lines so the relieving OOW knows which line belongs to which target and leg.

On most current radars the PI lines are tied to own ship and automatically follow range scale changes. They work in both relative and true motion. Older sets or some modes may behave differently, so check how your equipment handles off-centring and range changes before relying on it.

Worked scenario: monitoring a coastal leg and the wheel-over

Situation: You are on a planned track of 045°T passing a headland with a lighthouse. The chart shows the lighthouse 2.4 miles to port of the track. The next leg is 090°T, with the wheel-over point (WOP) marked on the chart abeam a breakwater.

Set-up: On a north-up stabilised display, set a PI line on 045°/225° offset 2.4 miles to port of own ship. As you approach, the lighthouse echo should come down from ahead and run along this line.

Reading the echo:

What you see What it means Typical action
Echo running on the line On track over the ground Continue monitoring
Echo between the line and own ship (e.g. 2.0' off) Set towards the headland by about 0.4' Alter away (to starboard here) and apply a course correction
Echo outside the line (e.g. 2.8' off) Set away from the headland Alter towards track, checking the other side's safety margin
Echo crossing a margin line Outside the planned cross-track limit Immediate correction, inform Master per standing orders

Common trap: do not confuse echo inside the line with ship inside the track. If the echo is closer to you than the line, you are closer to the target than planned.

Wheel-over with PI: From the chart, measure the range of the lighthouse (or a better-placed target) at the moment own ship reaches the WOP. Mark that position on the index line, either with a VRM set to that range or using the line's truncation or marker feature if the set has one. When the echo reaches the mark, start the turn. Prepare a second PI line in advance for the 090° leg, using the CIR of a suitable target for that leg. That way you can confirm the ship settles on the new track as the turn completes.

Remember that the WOP itself was planned using the ship's turning characteristics, speed and conditions. PI tells you where you are. It does not decide when the ship needs to begin turning.

Errors, hazards and lessons from the bridge

PI is only as good as its inputs. The classic failures are:

  • Wrong target. A neighbouring headland, a ship at anchor or an unlit structure is mistaken for the planned target. The echo runs beautifully down the line while the ship heads into danger. Always identify the target by more than one feature, such as shape of coast, a racon or a visual check.
  • Line on the wrong side. Setting a starboard target's line to port puts you on a track that is double the CIR away from where you intended. Have the line checked during the pre-leg brief.
  • Gyro error not accounted for. The line is oriented by gyro, so uncorrected gyro error tilts it relative to the echo's real movement. The resulting cross-track error grows with distance along the leg. Keep the gyro error log current.
  • Range errors. A badly tuned radar, an incorrect range setting or a poor echo edge gives a false offset. Using the near edge of a broad echo is not the same as using a charted point.
  • Poor coastline response. Low, shelving coasts and areas affected by tide may not match the charted line. This is why steep-to targets are preferred.
  • Unstabilised display or loss of gyro input. The lines lose meaning. Recognise the alarm and switch methods.
  • Stale lines. PI lines from the previous leg are left on the screen and misread by the next watchkeeper. Clear them at the changeover and brief the relief.
  • Over-reliance. If ECDIS, GNSS and PI disagree, stop trusting any single source. Fix by independent means and call the Master if in doubt.

The lesson from many groundings and near misses is the same. The equipment did its job, but nobody cross-checked it against a second method or questioned an echo that looked right. Good bridge resource management means the pilot, Master and OOW all know which PI lines are in use and what they should look like.

Quick checklist for the OOW

Before the leg

  • PI targets and CIRs marked on the chart or in ECDIS notes for every critical leg
  • Targets steep-to, charted, identifiable and not floating where avoidable
  • Margin-of-safety lines and WOP markers planned
  • Gyro error known and applied, radar performance checked

Setting up

  • Display stabilised (north-up or course-up)
  • Line direction equals the track direction in degrees true
  • Offset equals the CIR, on the same side as the target
  • Target positively identified before relying on the line

During the leg

  • Echo on the line, with trend watched rather than single glances
  • Cross-check with fixes by other means and the ECDIS at the intervals required by the SMS and standing orders
  • Next-leg line ready before the WOP

At handover

  • Brief the relief on which lines are active and which target each refers to
  • Clear lines for completed legs

Oral exam style questions and model answers

Examiners like PI because it shows whether a candidate understands relative motion, not just buttons.

  • Q: Why does a fixed echo move parallel to your ground track? Its relative motion is equal and opposite to own ship's motion over the ground. A stationary object therefore traces a path on the display parallel and reciprocal to your ground track.
  • Q: Your echo is closer to own ship than the PI line. What has happened? The ship has been set towards the target. I would alter away and apply a correction to regain track. I would also check the other side's safety margin and confirm by an independent fix.
  • Q: Does PI account for tidal stream? Yes. It shows the actual movement relative to a fixed object, so set and leeway are included. That is its main advantage over dead reckoning.
  • Q: What effect does uncorrected gyro error have? The line is misaligned relative to the true track. The cross-track error grows the further you are from the point where the echo meets the line.
  • Q: Why not use a buoy? It can drag or be off station, and its echo may be weak or lost in clutter. If no fixed target exists, use the buoy only with extra cross-checks.
  • Q: How would you use PI for a turn? Mark on the index line where the target will be when own ship reaches the WOP, using a VRM or marker. Have the next leg's line ready, then confirm the ship settles on the new track.
  • Q: Can you use PI in true motion? On modern radars the lines are referenced to own ship, so yes. I would confirm the behaviour of the particular set during familiarisation.
Key points
  • Parallel indexing shows cross-track error over the ground continuously, using radar range and gyro bearing against a fixed target.
  • The cross-index range is the perpendicular distance from the target to the planned track, measured on the chart during passage planning.
  • Set the PI line parallel to the track, offset by the cross-index range on the same side as the target, on a stabilised display.
  • An echo closer to own ship than the line means you are set towards the target.
  • Choose steep-to, charted, positively identified fixed targets, and avoid low coastlines and floating aids where possible.
  • Gyro error, wrong-side lines and misidentified targets are the classic failures, so cross-check with independent fixes.
  • Prepare wheel-over markers and the next leg's line before the turn, and brief the relief on active lines.

Frequently asked questions

What is parallel indexing in navigation?

It is a radar technique where you draw a line parallel to the planned track at the charted perpendicular distance from a fixed target. If the ship is on track, the target's echo moves along the line. Any deviation shows cross-track error over the ground immediately.

How do I calculate the cross-index range?

On the chart, drop a perpendicular from the chosen fixed target to the planned track line and measure its length in nautical miles. Note which side of the track the target is on. That distance and side are what you enter on the radar PI line.

Does parallel indexing still matter if the ship has ECDIS?

Yes. ECDIS normally depends on GNSS, while PI uses radar and gyro against a physical target, so it gives an independent check. Many company SMSs and Masters' standing orders require PI or equivalent radar monitoring in coastal and pilotage waters. Check your own ship's procedures.

Can I use parallel indexing on a head-up radar display?

Not on an unstabilised head-up display. Yaw and course changes swing the picture, so a fixed line no longer represents the track. Use a gyro-stabilised north-up or course-up display.

What should I do if the PI, ECDIS and GPS positions disagree?

Do not trust any single source. Fix the position by independent means, such as visual bearings or radar ranges on identified targets, and check gyro error and radar set-up. Inform the Master in line with standing orders, and reduce speed or move to safer water if the doubt cannot be resolved quickly.

Test yourself

A target lies 1.5 miles to starboard of the planned track. Where should the PI line be set?

The line is offset by the cross-index range on the same side as the target. This is general radar navigation practice taught under STCW Code Section A-II/1 radar competence.

The target echo is running between own ship and the PI line. What does this indicate?

If the echo is closer than the cross-index range, own ship is closer to the target than planned, so it has been set towards it. Alter away and confirm by an independent fix (radar navigation competence, STCW Code Section A-II/1).

Why does parallel indexing automatically allow for tidal stream and leeway?

A stationary object's relative motion is the reciprocal of own ship's ground track, so all set and leeway are included without any external input (radar navigation competence, STCW Code Section A-II/1).

Which is the best choice of PI target?

A good target is fixed, charted, steep-to and identifiable, while buoys can drag, low coasts paint inaccurately and anchored ships move. Target selection forms part of passage planning under IMO Resolution A.893(21).

What is the effect of uncorrected gyro error on a PI line?

PI lines are laid on true bearings referenced to gyro heading, so a gyro error tilts the line relative to the echo's real track. Compass error should be determined regularly as part of watchkeeping (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2).
