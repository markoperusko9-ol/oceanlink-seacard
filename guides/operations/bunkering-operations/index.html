Bunkering Operations: A Practical Guide for Ship's Officers

Quick answer

Bunkering is the planned, supervised transfer of fuel or lube oil to the ship. It is controlled by the company SMS and a written bunker plan, and governed mainly by MARPOL Annex I (spill prevention, Oil Record Book) and Annex VI (fuel quality, sulphur, Bunker Delivery Note, samples). Most spills and disputes come from poor planning, wrong valve settings, topping off too fast and careless quantity checks.

Why bunkering deserves your full attention

Bunkering is one of the highest-risk routine operations on any ship. You are moving large volumes of oil, often at night, often from a barge alongside, into tanks with limited ullage. When it goes wrong, the result is a deck spill, oil over the side, a port detention, a fine, or a quantity or quality dispute that the company may end up paying for.

Three separate things are at stake:

  • Pollution and fire safety. An overflow through a tank vent or sounding pipe goes straight onto deck and, if the scuppers are open, overboard.
  • Compliance. Under MARPOL Annex VI the fuel must meet the sulphur limit for where you will burn it. The Bunker Delivery Note (BDN) and the MARPOL sample are your evidence.
  • Commercial. Short delivery, air-entrained ("cappuccino") fuel and off-spec fuel cost money, and only well-documented ships win the claims.

Who does what varies by company. Usually the Chief Engineer is responsible for the operation and signs the paperwork. The 2nd or 3rd Engineer handles soundings and valves, and the deck watch looks after moorings, draught, trim and the barge interface. Your SMS decides. Follow it.

Planning: before the barge arrives

Good bunkering is mostly done before the hose is connected. A typical planning sequence:

  1. Confirm the order. Check grade (for example ISO 8217 grade, sulphur content), quantity, delivery method and supplier contact. Check the grade against the voyage. If you are heading into an Emission Control Area, make sure you have enough compliant fuel.
  2. Sound all fuel tanks and record the remaining-on-board (ROB) figures. Settling and service tanks count too.
  3. Plan the segregation. New fuel should go into empty tanks wherever possible. Mixing fuels from different deliveries risks incompatibility, sludge and blocked filters. If commingling cannot be avoided, the CE decides after considering compatibility.
  4. Allocate tanks and set limits. Calculate the volume each tank will take up to the company's maximum fill level. This is commonly in the region of 90%, but the SMS sets the figure. Allow for the temperature of the incoming fuel and thermal expansion.
  5. Check stability, trim, list and draught. Check the effect of the loading sequence, under-keel clearance at the berth, and any stress limits on larger ships.
  6. Write the bunker plan. It should cover tank sequence, valve line-up, maximum and topping-off rates, manning, communication method, the agreed emergency stop signal and spill response. Everyone involved signs it.
  7. Hold a pre-bunker meeting. Walk through the plan, the valve line-up and each person's station. Make sure everyone knows how to stop the transfer.

Pre-transfer checks and the checklist

Most companies use a ship–barge (or ship–terminal) bunker checklist. It is completed jointly with the supplier and signed by both sides before pumping starts. Typical items:

Area Check
Containment Deck scuppers plugged; save-alls and drip trays at manifold and vents empty and drained; spill kit (SOPEP locker) at the manifold
Tanks and lines Valves set as per plan and double-checked; unused manifold blanked and fully bolted; sounding pipe caps closed; overflow tank empty; high-level alarms tested
Hose and connection Hose condition and test certification checked; adequate length for ranging and tide; properly supported; all flange bolts and gasket in place
Communications Primary and back-up method tested with the barge; language agreed; stop signal agreed
Fire and safety No smoking and no hot work; fire-fighting equipment ready; signals displayed as required (commonly flag "B" by day, red all-round light by night, subject to port rules)
Moorings and weather Barge securely moored and fendered; limits agreed for suspending operations
Barge figures Opening gauges/soundings of barge tanks witnessed, temperature taken, flow meter reading noted

Do not start until every item is satisfied. A missing blank or an open sounding cap is how most textbook spills begin.

During the transfer: start slow, top off slower

Starting. Begin at a low rate and confirm fuel is going into the intended tank. Sound or check gauges within the first few minutes. Check the manifold and hose for leaks. Only then agree an increase to the planned rate.

Monitoring. Take regular soundings and compare them with the expected rise. A tank that is not rising, or a tank that should be closed but is rising, means a valve problem. Stop and investigate. Keep watching the manifold pressure, moorings, hose and the water around the hull.

Changing tanks. Open the next tank valve before closing the full one. Never close against the pump. Many spills and burst hoses come from a rushed changeover.

Topping off. This is where the overflows happen. As each tank nears its limit:

  • give the barge clear advance notice;
  • reduce the rate for the final part of the tank;
  • put an experienced person on soundings, not the most junior.

Give the final stop early enough to allow for the time it takes the barge to react and for draining the hose.

Sampling. The MARPOL delivered sample is taken at the receiving ship's inlet manifold. It is drawn continuously throughout the delivery, normally by a drip sampler. The sample is sealed, and the seal is signed by the supplier's representative and the Master or the officer in charge of the bunker operation (often the Chief Engineer). Additional commercial samples are usually drawn at the same time, and their distribution is set by the charter or supply contract.

Stop immediately if: a leak or spill occurs, communication is lost, the weather or moorings deteriorate, a fire alarm sounds nearby, or anything does not match the plan.

Completion, documentation and disputes

After completion:

  1. Drain or blow the hose back to the barge (or into a ship's tank, as agreed in the plan) before disconnecting. Catch any residual drips in the drip tray, then blank the manifold.
  2. Allow tanks to settle, then take final soundings and temperatures. Calculate the quantity received: correct the observed volume to the standard 15 °C using the volume correction tables, then convert to mass using the density at 15 °C.
  3. Witness the closing barge gauges and meter reading.
  4. Check the BDN against your figures. Under MARPOL Annex VI it must show, among other things, the quantity in tonnes, the density at 15 °C and the sulphur content, plus the supplier's declaration that the fuel meets the relevant requirements. Keep the BDN on board for three years.
  5. Keep the MARPOL sample under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for at least 12 months.
  6. Make the Oil Record Book Part I entry for bunkering (code H). Make any other log entries your flag or SMS requires.

If there is a shortfall or discrepancy, do not sign the BDN "clean" without comment. Typical causes are air entrainment (foaming fuel, figures dropping as the fuel settles), barge gauge errors or temperature games. Follow the SMS: inform the company, record the figures, and issue a Letter of Protest or Note of Protest as instructed. Some major bunkering ports now require mass flow meters on barges. These reduce, but do not remove, quantity disputes.

Real lessons: how bunker spills actually happen

These scenarios are typical of what incident reports describe again and again.

  • Wrong tank filling. A valve on a tank thought to be closed was passing or left open. The tank overflowed through its vent while everyone watched the "active" tank. Lesson: sound all tanks, not just the one being filled, especially after a changeover.
  • Fast topping off. The rate was not reduced because the barge was in a hurry. The tank went from nearly full to overflowing in minutes. Lesson: the ship controls the rate. Do not let commercial pressure set it.
  • Sounding pipe cap off. The cap was left off after manual sounding, so fuel came straight up the pipe onto deck. Lesson: cap back on every time, and check caps on the checklist.
  • Open scupper. A small deck spill became pollution because one scupper plug was missing. Lesson: walk the deck and check every plug yourself.
  • Loss of communication. A radio battery died during topping off and nobody had agreed a back-up method. Lesson: test the back-up method, and treat any loss of communication as an automatic stop.

If a spill occurs, stop the transfer, raise the alarm and follow the SOPEP and port reporting requirements. Contain oil on deck first, and never wash it overboard.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like bunkering because it ties together engineering, MARPOL and management. Be ready for questions like these.

  • "Talk me through your bunker plan." Cover ROB, segregation, tank allocation with maximum fill, stability, rates, manning, communication, emergency stop, the checklist and the pre-bunker meeting.
  • "Where and how is the MARPOL sample taken?" At the receiving ship's inlet manifold, drawn continuously throughout delivery. It is sealed and signed by both parties and kept on board for at least 12 months, or until the fuel is substantially consumed if that is longer.
  • "What documents do you get, and how long do you keep them?" The BDN, kept for three years, together with the sample. Then make the ORB Part I entry.
  • "You suspect cappuccino bunkers. What do you do?" Look for foaming in soundings and a falling level as it settles. Take readings again after settling, inform the company, and issue a protest per the SMS. Do not sign without remarks.
  • "Oil appears on deck during bunkering. Your actions?" Stop the transfer, raise the alarm, contain the oil (scuppers already plugged), start the SOPEP response, report as required and investigate the cause before restarting.
  • "Why avoid commingling?" Fuels can be incompatible. Mixing can cause sludging, blocked filters and purifier problems, and can lead to engine trouble at the worst moment.
Key points
  • Plan first: ROB, segregation, tank limits per SMS, stability, rates and a signed bunker plan.
  • Plug scuppers, blank unused manifolds, close sounding caps and test communications before starting.
  • Start slow, confirm flow into the correct tank, and reduce the rate well before topping off.
  • The MARPOL sample is drawn continuously at the receiving ship's manifold and kept at least 12 months.
  • Keep the BDN for three years and make the Oil Record Book Part I code H entry.
  • Never sign for a disputed quantity without remarks; follow the SMS protest procedure.
  • Any spill, leak or loss of communication means stop the transfer immediately.

Frequently asked questions

Who is in charge of bunkering on a merchant ship?

Usually the Chief Engineer is responsible and signs the BDN and checklist, with the 2nd or 3rd Engineer handling soundings and valves. The deck department normally looks after moorings, draught and trim. Exact responsibilities are set by the company SMS and may differ between companies and ship types.

How long must the Bunker Delivery Note be kept on board?

MARPOL Annex VI requires the BDN to be kept on board and available for inspection for three years after delivery. It must be accompanied by the sealed MARPOL fuel sample, which has a separate retention rule.

How long do we keep the MARPOL bunker sample?

It is kept under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for not less than 12 months from delivery. Store it safely, away from heat, with the seal and label intact.

What is the maximum percentage we can fill a fuel tank to?

There is no single international figure. The company SMS sets the limit, commonly somewhere around 90%. You must also allow for thermal expansion when the fuel is heated. Always use your own company's figure in the bunker plan.

What should I do if the barge figures do not match ours?

Re-check your soundings after the fuel has settled, verify temperatures and corrections, and witness the barge's closing gauges. Inform the company. Then issue a Letter or Note of Protest as your SMS directs, and do not sign the BDN without noting the discrepancy.

Test yourself

For how long must the Bunker Delivery Note be retained on board under MARPOL Annex VI?

The BDN must be kept on board for three years after delivery. 12 months is the minimum for the sample, not the BDN (MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18).

How long must the MARPOL delivered fuel sample be retained?

The sample stays under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed and for at least 12 months (MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18).

Where is the MARPOL delivered sample normally drawn?

IMO sampling guidelines require a continuous drip sample at the receiving ship's inlet manifold for the whole delivery (Resolution MEPC.182(59)).

Outside Emission Control Areas, what is the current global limit on sulphur content of fuel oil used on board?

The global limit has been 0.50% m/m since 1 January 2020. 0.10% applies inside ECAs, and 3.50% was the previous global limit (MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 14).

What is the correct practice when a tank approaches its final level?

Topping off at a reduced, pre-agreed rate with early notice prevents overflows. Closing against the pump risks hose or line damage (good industry practice; company SMS procedures required under ISM Code Section 7).
