Lock-out Tag-out (LOTO) on Board: Isolation Done Properly

Quick answer

Lock-out tag-out is the procedure that isolates every energy source feeding a machine or system, secures each isolation with a lock and a warning tag, and proves it is dead before anyone puts hands or tools inside. On ships it usually sits within the permit to work system under the company SMS. The SMS procedure always takes precedence over any general guidance, including this page.

Why LOTO matters at sea

A ship is a compact power station sitting on top of pressurised pipework, hydraulic machinery and stored mechanical energy. Most of it is controlled remotely: from the ECR, from the bridge, by the power management system or by automatic standby changeover. The person who starts a pump often cannot see the person working on it.

LOTO closes that gap. It ensures that:

  • every source of energy is identified, not just the obvious electrical one
  • each isolation point is physically locked so it cannot be operated by accident
  • a tag tells anyone who arrives who isolated it, why and when
  • stored energy is released and the isolation is proven before work starts

There is no stand-alone LOTO convention for merchant ships. In practice the requirement comes through two routes:

  • The ISM Code. It is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter IX for passenger ships and for cargo ships of 500 GT and above. It obliges the company to establish procedures for key shipboard operations and maintenance.
  • MLC 2006. It requires flag states to have occupational safety and accident-prevention arrangements on board.

Detail such as lock types, who holds keys and how locks are removed varies between companies and flag states. Read your SMS on joining, not after the first job.

Energy sources you must think about

Experienced crew still get caught by the energy source they did not think of. Before any isolation, go through this list:

Energy type Typical shipboard examples
Electrical Motor starters, MSB and ESB breakers, HV switchgear, control and heater circuits fed separately, capacitors
Pneumatic Starting air, control air, air-operated valves
Hydraulic Steering gear, hatch covers, cranes, mooring winches, accumulators holding pressure
Thermal / pressure Steam, thermal oil, hot fuel, cooling water, boiler and economiser circuits
Mechanical / stored Springs, flywheels, a propeller turned by tide or current, coasting purifier bowls
Gravity Raised hatch covers, crane jibs, lifeboats in davits, suspended loads
Chemical Cargo lines, CO2 and other fixed fire-fighting systems, ammonia or refrigerant lines

Also watch for multiple feeds. Examples include:

  • back-feed through an interconnector
  • shore supply
  • an emergency switchboard feed
  • a separately supplied space heater
  • a pump that can be started locally, remotely and by auto-standby

Step-by-step procedure (general industry practice)

Your SMS will have its own forms and sequence. The general logic is:

  1. Plan and authorise. Raise the permit to work or isolation certificate. Use drawings, the one-line diagram and P&IDs to list every isolation point. The Chief Engineer or Chief Officer (as the SMS specifies) approves.
  2. Notify. Inform the OOW, the ECR and everyone affected. If the job affects propulsion, steering, fire-fighting or lifesaving equipment, the Master must be informed. In port, the port authorities may also need notice.
  3. Shut down normally. Stop the equipment using its normal controls. Do not stop it by pulling the breaker under load.
  4. Isolate. Open the breakers, and rack them out where they are designed for it. Remove fuses if the procedure says so. Close valves on both the suction and discharge sides. Put auto-start or standby selection to manual/off.
  5. Lock and tag. Apply a lock to every isolation point. Each person working fits their own personal lock, either directly or on a group lock box holding the isolation keys. The tag shows name, date, job and permit number.
  6. Release stored energy. Drain and vent lines, bleed hydraulic accumulators, and block or lower raised loads. Engage turning gear or brakes, let hot systems cool, and discharge or earth as appropriate.
  7. Verify. This is the most skipped step.
    • Try to start from every control position, then return the controls to off.
    • Test for dead with an approved tester, proved on a known live source before and after the test (live-dead-live).
    • Check that pressure gauges read zero and that the vents are clear.
  8. Do the work. If the scope changes, stop and re-assess the isolation.
  9. Restore.
    • Remove tools and rags, refit guards and count heads.
    • Each person then removes only their own lock.
    • Notify everyone, remove isolations in a controlled sequence and test-run under supervision.
    • Close the permit.

Removing someone else's lock is only done under the SMS procedure. Typically the Chief Engineer or Master authorises it after confirming the person is not at the equipment and has been contacted or accounted for. Never cut a lock because it is inconvenient.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Opening a fuel oil purifier bowl. The obvious isolation is the motor breaker at the group starter panel. You also need to:

  • set the purifier to local/manual so the PMS or remote panel cannot start it
  • close the feed and discharge valves
  • isolate the heater steam or thermal oil supply
  • close the operating water supply
  • wait for the bowl to stop completely. A purifier coasts for a long time, so confirm visually that the brake (if fitted) is applied and the bowl has stopped
  • lock and tag every isolation point, then try-start locally (and from any remote position) to verify the isolation, and return the controls to off

Scenario 2: Changing a seal on a main sea water pump with a standby unit running. The running pump is live, and its auto-changeover logic may try to start the pump you are working on.

  • Isolate the breaker.
  • Take the pump out of the standby sequence.
  • Close the suction and discharge valves, and check that they actually hold. A seawater valve that passes will flood the space when you open the casing.
  • Open the casing drain first and watch it. If water keeps running, stop.

Scenario 3: Main engine crankcase entry. Typical practice is to:

  • engage the turning gear and lock it
  • close and lock the starting air main valve, and vent the starting air line
  • open the indicator cocks
  • allow the engine to cool according to the maker's guidance before opening the doors

Then ventilate the crankcase and treat it as an enclosed space under the SMS. Inform the bridge that the main engine is immobilised. Confirm the turning gear is engaged and the shaft secured as the maker specifies, because a current running past the hull can turn the propeller and shaft.

Checklist before you sign the permit

  • Have all energy sources been identified, including control, heating and back-feeds?
  • Have auto-start, standby and remote control been disabled, not just the main feed?
  • Is every isolation point locked, with a personal lock from each worker?
  • Do the tags show name, date, job and permit reference?
  • Has stored energy been released (pressure zero, accumulators bled, loads lowered or blocked, rotation stopped)?
  • Has the isolation been verified by try-start and test-for-dead?
  • Have the bridge, ECR and other departments been told?
  • Does the job also need an enclosed space, hot work or working aloft permit?
  • Has handover been arranged if the job runs past a watch change?

Typical failures and lessons

Investigations into shipboard machinery injuries keep finding the same patterns:

  • Tag without lock. A tag is a warning, not a barrier. Someone fixing an alarm, or someone new to the ship, may not read it.
  • Isolating the wrong breaker. Starter labels and drawings do not always match after modifications. Verification by try-start catches this.
  • Only electrical isolation. Hydraulic steering gear or hatch covers can move under stored pressure with the pump stopped.
  • Shift change. The day worker who isolated the equipment goes off duty. The job then continues on someone else's isolation, or the equipment is restored with work still in progress. Handover must cover isolations explicitly.
  • Single valve against pressure. For high-pressure, hot or hazardous systems, many SMS require double block and bleed rather than trusting one valve.
  • Pressure to get the plant back. Equipment is restored early, before all personnel are clear, because the ship needs to sail. Restoring early is a Master and Chief Engineer decision under the SMS. It is never a shortcut for the person on the job to take.

High-voltage systems add their own rules: earthing switches, safety documents and authorised persons. These come under HV training and the company HV procedures, not just general LOTO.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners for watchkeeping and management certificates often test LOTO through practical scenarios. Expect questions like:

  • "You are going to overhaul a lube oil pump. Take me through your isolation." Cover the permit, all energy sources, auto-standby, valves on both sides, lock and tag, draining, verification and restoration.
  • "What is the difference between a lock and a tag?" A lock physically prevents operation. A tag tells people who isolated it, why and when. You need both.
  • "How do you prove a circuit is dead?" Use an approved tester proved on a known live source before and after testing. Do not rely on lamps or indications going out.
  • "A crew member has gone ashore leaving his lock on. The job is finished. What do you do?" Follow the SMS procedure for lock removal. The Chief Engineer or Master authorises it after confirming the person and equipment are safe, and the removal is recorded.
  • "What stored energy might remain after you stop a hydraulic hatch cover system?" Accumulator pressure, trapped pressure in cylinders, and gravity on raised panels.

Answer by describing what you would actually do, and say that you would follow the company SMS. Examiners want to see that you can apply the procedure, not just recite it.

Key points
  • LOTO isolates every energy source, locks it, tags it and proves it dead before work starts.
  • The company SMS procedure prevails, and detail varies between companies and flag states.
  • Think beyond electrical: hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, gravity, rotational and chemical energy.
  • Disable auto-start, standby and remote control, not just the main supply.
  • Lock and tag first, then verify by try-start and live-dead-live testing, the step most often skipped.
  • Each worker fits and removes only their own lock. Removing another person's lock needs formal authorisation.
  • Isolations must be covered explicitly at every watch handover.

Frequently asked questions

Is lock-out tag-out mandatory on merchant ships?

There is no single IMO convention dedicated to LOTO. In practice it is required through the company SMS under the ISM Code, which must cover shipboard operations and maintenance. The ISM Code is mandatory under SOLAS Chapter IX for passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and above. MLC 2006 health and safety requirements also apply, and some flag states publish specific safe working guidance, so check your flag's requirements and your SMS.

Who can remove a lock on board?

Normally only the person who fitted it. If that person is unavailable, the SMS will set out a removal procedure. This is usually authorised by the Chief Engineer or Master after confirming nobody is at risk, and the removal is recorded.

Is a tag alone enough for a quick job?

No. A tag only warns, and it does not stop anyone from closing a breaker or opening a valve. Where the equipment can be locked, lock it, however short the job.

Does LOTO replace the permit to work?

Usually it is part of the permit to work system rather than a substitute. The permit authorises the job and lists the isolations, and LOTO is how those isolations are applied and secured. How the two are linked depends on your company SMS.

Do ratings need to know LOTO?

Yes. Motormen, fitters, ABs and electricians often apply isolations and work behind them. Anyone working on equipment should fit their own lock and understand how the isolation was verified.

Test yourself

After locking and tagging a pump motor breaker, what should be done before starting work?

Verification proves the correct point was isolated and no energy remains. It is a core step in LOTO procedures, which companies set out in their SMS under the ISM Code.

What is the main purpose of the lock in LOTO?

The lock is the physical barrier and the tag is the information. Company LOTO procedures normally require both.

With the pump stopped and its breaker locked, which energy may still be present in a hydraulic hatch cover system?

Hydraulic systems store pressure and raised panels store gravitational energy, so both must be released or blocked before work starts.

A colleague's lock remains on completed equipment and he has gone ashore. What is the correct action?

Lock removal by anyone other than its owner must follow the formal SMS procedure, which includes authorisation and a record.

When testing for dead on an electrical circuit, what is the accepted method?

Live-dead-live testing confirms the tester works, so a dead reading is genuine. It is standard electrical safety practice.
