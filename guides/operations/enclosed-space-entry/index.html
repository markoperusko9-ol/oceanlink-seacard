Enclosed Space Entry: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

Treat every enclosed space as dangerous until it has been isolated, ventilated, tested and authorised under a permit, with a standby person and a rescue team ready before anyone goes in. The IMO recommendations and SOLAS set the framework. Your company SMS sets the exact procedure and limits you must follow.

Why enclosed spaces keep killing seafarers

Enclosed space deaths are among the most repeated accidents in shipping. The pattern rarely changes. Someone goes into a tank, hold, chain locker or access trunk "just for a minute" and collapses. Then one or two colleagues rush in to help and collapse as well.

The danger is that a lethal atmosphere usually looks, smells and feels normal. Oxygen deficiency gives no warning. A few breaths of very low-oxygen air can cause collapse with no time to react. Hydrogen sulphide deadens your sense of smell at dangerous concentrations, so you cannot rely on your nose either.

In general terms, IMO describes an enclosed space as one that has:

  • limited openings for entry and exit
  • inadequate ventilation
  • no design for continuous worker occupancy

Typical examples on board:

  • cargo holds, especially with oxygen-depleting or toxic cargoes
  • double bottoms, ballast and fuel tanks
  • cofferdams, void spaces and duct keels
  • cargo tanks and pump rooms on tankers
  • chain lockers
  • sewage tanks
  • boilers, engine crankcases and scavenge spaces
  • CO2 rooms after release
  • stairways and access trunks next to cargo holds
  • inter-barrier spaces on gas carriers

If you are in doubt, treat the space as enclosed.

The hazards: what is actually in there

Oxygen deficiency

This is the most common killer. Oxygen is consumed or displaced by:

  • rusting steel in closed ballast tanks and voids
  • cargoes that absorb oxygen, such as coal, logs and wood products, scrap metal, some grains and seed cakes, and fishmeal
  • inert gas, nitrogen or CO2 leaking or purged into the space
  • paint and coatings that are curing

An access trunk sharing a bulkhead or vent with a hold of oxygen-depleting cargo can become deadly. Many fatal accidents have happened in these trunks, not in the hold itself.

Toxic gases

  • Hydrogen sulphide (H2S): from sour crude, sewage, rotting organic matter and some cargoes.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO): from coal cargoes or exhaust.
  • Cargo vapours: for example benzene and other hydrocarbons.
  • Fumigants: in fumigated holds.

Flammable atmosphere

Hydrocarbon vapour from fuel or cargo, or gases released from cargo, can create a fire or explosion risk when hot work, non-certified lights or static are introduced.

Physical hazards

  • falls from ladders
  • poor lighting
  • slippery surfaces
  • heat stress
  • engulfment in bulk cargo
  • unexpected movement of machinery or pipework that is not isolated

Step-by-step entry procedure (general industry practice)

The sequence below reflects the IMO recommendations and common SMS practice. Your company's procedure, permit form and gas limits always take precedence.

  1. Assess the need. Can the job be done without entering? If not, a competent person carries out a risk assessment.
  2. Appoint people. Name the competent person who will authorise entry, the entrants and the attendant (standby person). Brief them all on the job, the hazards and the emergency plan.
  3. Isolate the space. Use lock-out tag-out on pumps, valves and machinery. Blank or isolate lines and inert gas or steam connections. Make sure no fixed fire-fighting release (for example CO2) can flood the space.
  4. Secure and ventilate. Open up and ventilate thoroughly by natural or mechanical means. Never ventilate with pure oxygen.
  5. Test the atmosphere. Do this before entry, from outside, with a calibrated, bump-tested instrument. Test at several levels and in remote corners, because gases stratify. Use a sampling line rather than leaning into the opening.
  6. Prepare rescue and communications. Have the following at the entrance:
    • breathing apparatus (SCBA)
    • resuscitator
    • rescue harness and lifeline
    • tripod or other lifting gear
    • stretcher
    • safe lighting
    • an agreed communication method and check interval
    • an agreed alarm signal
  7. Issue the permit. The responsible officer checks every item and signs. The permit is valid only for the stated job, space and time period. Inform the bridge or the engine control room.
  8. Enter and monitor. Entrants carry personal gas monitors. Keep ventilation running, and re-test at the intervals set by your SMS.
  9. Close out. Count everyone out and check the space for tools and rags. Close the permit and remove the isolations in a controlled way.

IMO-recommended atmosphere for entry without breathing apparatus

Parameter Recommended
Oxygen steady reading of 21% by volume
Flammable gas not more than 1% of LFL
Toxic gases less than 50% of the occupational exposure limit (OEL)

Check your SMS for the exact acceptance figures. Many companies set their own, often stricter, limits. If readings drift during the job, everyone leaves immediately.

The attendant and the rescue: where most second deaths happen

The attendant's job is simple but critical:

  • stay at the entrance
  • keep the agreed communication with the entrants
  • watch for changes
  • raise the alarm

The attendant must never enter to attempt a rescue alone.

In an emergency:

  1. Raise the alarm using the agreed signal and report to the bridge or ECR.
  2. Order any other entrants out.
  3. The rescue team musters with SCBA and rescue gear.
  4. Rescuers enter wearing breathing apparatus, never on a held breath or with a filter mask.
  5. Get the casualty breathing air: supply via a spare BA set or escape set if fitted, then lift out with harness and tripod.
  6. Give first aid and oxygen resuscitation, and seek radio medical advice.

An emergency escape breathing device (EEBD) is designed for escape only. It is not suitable for fire-fighting, rescue or planned entry into an oxygen-deficient space.

SOLAS requires drills so that this is rehearsed. Crew with enclosed space entry or rescue duties must take part in a drill at least once every two months. The drill should be realistic: real casualty weight, the actual tripod, and an awkward space.

Worked scenario: the forward store access trunk

Situation

A bulk carrier is loaded with logs. The bosun goes down the access trunk to the forward store to check a leaking hydraulic line. The trunk shares ventilation with No.1 hold. He does not report back. An AB finds him collapsed at the bottom of the ladder.

What goes wrong in real cases

The AB climbs down to help and also collapses. The chief officer arrives, and the same nearly happens again.

What should happen

  • Before: the space should have been identified as enclosed in the ship's list. It should also have been flagged as at risk because of the cargo. That means a permit, atmosphere testing and an attendant, even for a five-minute job.
  • On discovery: the AB stays at the top, raises the alarm and calls the bridge. The ship then follows its rescue plan.
  • Rescue: the team arrives with SCBA and lifting gear. Ventilation is started, and the atmosphere is treated as lethal until proven otherwise. A rescuer in SCBA goes down, gives the casualty air and rigs him for lifting.
  • After: the master reports in line with the SMS and flag requirements. The ship reviews why the space was not controlled.

Lesson: the hazard was not the job. It was the cargo next door. Cargo information and the ship's enclosed space list must cover adjacent spaces as well.

Equipment and paperwork you will be asked about

Gas detection

SOLAS requires every ship to carry suitable portable atmosphere testing instruments. At minimum they must measure oxygen, flammable gases or vapours, H2S and CO before entry. Tankers and gas carriers will also have instruments specific to their cargo.

You should know how to:

  • calibrate or bump test the instrument
  • check the expiry of calibration gas
  • use the sampling pump and hose

Catalytic LEL sensors need oxygen to read correctly. In an inerted tank, use the appropriate instrument, such as an infrared or tankscope-type meter for hydrocarbon in inert gas.

Permit and records

  • The entry permit, often linked to the ship's permit to work system.
  • Gas readings with time, level and instrument used.
  • Isolation records.
  • Drill records.
  • The ship's list of enclosed spaces.

Port state control inspectors commonly ask to see:

  • drill records
  • instrument calibration
  • that crew can actually operate the gas meter

Rescue kit

  • SCBA with spare cylinders
  • harness and lifelines
  • tripod or davit
  • rescue stretcher suitable for vertical lifts
  • resuscitator
  • intrinsically safe torches
  • portable radios

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: Define an enclosed space and give five examples from your last ship. Give the IMO characteristics: limited openings, inadequate ventilation, not designed for continuous occupancy. Then name real spaces from your ship, including access trunks next to holds.

Q: Why is a ballast tank that was dry and closed for months dangerous? Corrosion of steel consumes oxygen, so the atmosphere may be deficient.

Q: Why must you test at different levels? Gases stratify. H2S and CO2 are heavier than air, and some vapours are lighter. A reading at the hatch tells you little about the bottom.

Q: Your LEL reading is zero in a freshly inerted tank. Is it gas-free? Not necessarily. A catalytic sensor cannot read correctly without enough oxygen. Use the correct instrument and check oxygen first.

Q: What do you do if a man collapses inside? Raise the alarm, do not enter alone, rescue team in SCBA, give the casualty air, lift out, start resuscitation, get medical advice.

Q: How often are enclosed space rescue drills required, and by whom? At least every two months, under SOLAS chapter III, for crew with entry or rescue duties.

Q: Who can authorise entry? The master or a responsible person nominated under the company SMS. State your company's actual arrangement.

Key points
  • Treat every enclosed space, and any space connected to it, as dangerous until tested and authorised.
  • Isolate, ventilate, test at all levels, then issue the permit, in that order, following your company SMS.
  • IMO recommends a steady 21% oxygen, not more than 1% LFL and toxic gases below 50% of their occupational exposure limit before entry without BA.
  • The attendant never enters alone; rescuers always wear SCBA.
  • An EEBD is for escape only, not for entry or rescue.
  • SOLAS requires enclosed space entry and rescue drills at least every two months.
  • Oxygen-depleting cargoes can make adjacent access trunks and stores lethal.

Frequently asked questions

What oxygen level is safe for enclosed space entry on a ship?

The IMO recommendations call for a steady reading of 21% oxygen by volume. They also require not more than 1% of the lower flammable limit and toxic gases less than 50% of their occupational exposure limit (OEL). Your company SMS may set its own acceptance figures, so always use the limits on your permit form.

How often must enclosed space rescue drills be done?

SOLAS chapter III requires crew members with enclosed space entry or rescue responsibilities to take part in a drill at least once every two months. Drills should be realistic and recorded. Port state control inspectors often check the records.

Can I use an EEBD to go into a tank to rescue someone?

No. An EEBD gives a short supply of air for escaping from a dangerous atmosphere. It is not designed for rescue, fire-fighting or planned entry. Rescuers must use SCBA and follow the ship's rescue plan.

Who signs the enclosed space entry permit?

Normally the master or a responsible officer nominated in the company SMS authorises entry. Commonly this is the chief officer for deck spaces and the chief engineer or second engineer for engine spaces. The exact arrangement depends on your company procedure, so check your SMS and the permit form.

Is a cargo hold an enclosed space?

Yes, a cargo hold can be an enclosed space, especially when loaded with cargo that depletes oxygen or gives off toxic gas, or after fumigation. Access trunks and stores connected to the hold can also be affected. Check the IMSBC Code cargo schedule and the shipper's information, and test before entry.

Test yourself

A crew member collapses at the bottom of a ballast tank. What should the attendant at the manhole do first?

The attendant must never enter alone; rescue is carried out by a team wearing SCBA. Ref: IMO Revised Recommendations for Entering Enclosed Spaces Aboard Ships.

Why must the atmosphere in a tank be tested at several levels before entry?

Heavier and lighter gases settle at different heights, so all levels and remote areas must be tested. Ref: IMO Revised Recommendations for Entering Enclosed Spaces Aboard Ships.

Under SOLAS, how often must crew with enclosed space entry or rescue duties take part in a drill?

SOLAS requires enclosed space entry and rescue drills at least every two months for crew with such responsibilities. Ref: SOLAS regulation III/19.

A freshly inerted cargo tank shows 0% LEL on a catalytic-sensor meter. What is the correct conclusion?

Catalytic LEL sensors cannot read correctly in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, so a suitable instrument for inert atmospheres must be used. Ref: MSC.1/Circ.1477.

Which of the following is a common cause of oxygen deficiency in a ballast tank that has been closed for a long time?

Rusting steel absorbs oxygen, leaving a deficient atmosphere in closed tanks and voids. Ref: IMO Revised Recommendations for Entering Enclosed Spaces Aboard Ships.
