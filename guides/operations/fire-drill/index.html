Fire Drill on Board: Requirements, Procedure and Lessons Learned

Quick answer

On cargo ships, SOLAS requires every crew member to take part in at least one fire drill a month. A drill must be held within 24 hours of leaving port if more than a quarter of the crew have not taken part in drills on that ship in the previous month. Each drill must cover mustering, running a fire pump with at least two jets, checking firefighter's outfits and communications, testing fire doors, dampers and ventilation closures in the drill area, and preparing for abandoning ship. Your company SMS sets the detail, so always follow it.

Why the fire drill matters

A fire at sea is fought by the people on board, with the equipment on board. No fire brigade is coming. The first ten minutes usually decide the outcome, and in those minutes nobody has time to read a manual.

The drill is how a crew turns the muster list into muscle memory. Every crew member should already know:

  • where to go
  • what to carry
  • who reports to whom
  • what the first actions are

SOLAS expects drills to be run, as far as practicable, as if there were a real emergency. Port State Control inspectors and auditors may ask to witness a drill at short notice. A crew that has only ever done drills "on paper" is quickly exposed.

Under the ISM Code, the company must have programmes for drills and exercises to prepare for emergencies. The drill matrix, scenarios and records in your SMS are where that requirement is put into practice.

What SOLAS requires

The core requirements sit in SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19 (and Regulation 30 for passenger ships). SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 15 says fire drills are to be conducted and recorded in line with Chapter III.

Item Requirement (paraphrased)
Frequency, cargo ships Every crew member takes part in at least one fire drill and one abandon ship drill each month
Crew change Drills within 24 hours of leaving port if more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on that ship in the previous month
Passenger ships An abandon ship drill and a fire drill held weekly; the entire crew need not take part in every drill, but each crew member still drills at least monthly (SOLAS III/30)
Scenario planning Plan drills to practise the emergencies likely for that ship type and cargo
Equipment afterwards Return equipment used in drills to full operational condition immediately; remedy faults as soon as possible
Records Log the date, details of the drill and of any training. If a drill is not held when due, log the circumstances and the extent of what was done

The flag Administration may accept other arrangements for the crew-change rule on some ship types. Always check flag requirements and your SMS.

Each fire drill must include:

  1. Reporting to stations and preparing for the duties on the muster list.
  2. Starting a fire pump and using at least the two required jets of water to show the system works.
  3. Checking firefighter's outfits and other personal rescue equipment.
  4. Checking the relevant communication equipment.
  5. Checking watertight doors, fire doors, fire dampers and the main ventilation inlets and outlets in the drill area.
  6. Checking the arrangements needed if the ship must then be abandoned.

SOLAS also requires a way to recharge breathing apparatus cylinders used in drills, or enough spare cylinders to replace them.

How a typical drill runs, step by step

Team names and structure vary by company. A common layout is:

  • an emergency command team on the bridge
  • an attack (entry) team
  • a back-up team
  • a support or first-aid team
  • an engine room team

Your muster list is the authority.

Before the drill (planning)

  • Pick a realistic scenario from the drill matrix. Rotate locations: engine room, galley, accommodation, cargo spaces, paint store, and the manifold on tankers.
  • Brief the bridge and engine room so the drill does not compromise safe navigation or machinery.
  • Inform port or VTS if alongside or in congested waters, as the SMS requires.
  • Set the training objectives, for example BA entry control, boundary cooling or casualty recovery.

Running the drill

  1. Raise the alarm. The fire alarm signal is the one on your muster list. Signals differ between ships, so learn yours on joining. Follow with a PA announcement giving the location.
  2. Muster and headcount. Teams report to their stations. The headcount is reported to the bridge. Missing persons are treated as possibly trapped.
  3. Isolate and contain. Close fire doors. Stop ventilation and close dampers in the affected zone. Consider fuel quick-closing valves and electrical isolation for the space.
  4. Charge the fire main. Start a fire pump and get at least two jets working. Check pressure at the hydrants used.
  5. Dress the attack team. Put on the firefighter's outfit and BA. Buddy-check the face-mask seal and cylinder pressure. Record names, entry time and pressure at the entry control point.
  6. Communicate. Test the portable radios used by the fire party and confirm the reporting chain to the bridge.
  7. Attack and boundary cool. Simulate entry with hose discipline. Cool the boundaries on all six sides of the space.
  8. Casualty handling. The support team practises stretcher recovery and first aid.
  9. Escalate. Discuss when to withdraw and use the fixed system. Run a simulated abandon-ship transition.

After the drill

  • Restow hoses drained and dry. Recharge or replace BA cylinders. Reset doors and dampers.
  • Debrief: what went well, what failed, what changes.
  • Make the logbook entry and complete the SMS drill report. Raise defects for any equipment that did not work.

Worked scenario: purifier room fire

Situation: At 1030 at sea, the duty engineer reports smoke and flames from a fuel oil purifier. The engine room is manned.

Expected sequence in a good drill:

  1. The engineer raises the alarm and informs the bridge. If safe, the purifier is stopped and its fuel supply shut. The local fixed system, if fitted, is considered under the SMS.
  2. The bridge sounds the fire alarm and announces the location. The master takes command, considers course and speed to reduce wind effect on the fire, and notes the position.
  3. Musters complete. The engine room is evacuated as far as the scenario demands. The headcount is confirmed.
  4. The emergency fire pump is started, which is good practice if access to the main pump is threatened. Two jets are brought to bear at the engine room entrance.
  5. The BA team enters under entry control. The back-up team, also dressed in BA, stands by at the entrance.
  6. Boundary cooling is set up on adjacent spaces, for example the casing and any adjoining store or accommodation bulkhead.
  7. The fire is declared beyond control. The team withdraws. The master orders preparation for fixed CO2 release.
  8. Before simulated release:
    • all persons are accounted for
    • ventilation is stopped and dampers closed
    • openings are shut
    • quick-closing valves are operated
    • the release procedure is read through

CO2 is never actually released in a drill. 9. The drill closes with a discussion of re-entry: waiting time, cooling, gas testing and BA.

What examiners look for: correct order, a headcount before the fixed system, and the reasoning behind each step.

Common failings and real lessons

These problems turn up again and again in investigation reports and PSC findings:

  • Same scenario every month. The crew learn one route and one answer. Rotate scenarios and include night and unannounced drills where the SMS allows.
  • BA donning too slow or wrong. Watch for masks worn over beards or glasses, and for cylinders not fully opened. If a crew member is slow, practise until it is quick.
  • No entry control. If nobody records the entry time and cylinder pressure, nobody knows when the team must come out.
  • Ventilation forgotten. Fans left running feed the fire. Dampers seized open from lack of testing are found only on the day they are needed.
  • Fire doors wedged open. This is common in accommodation and galleys, and it defeats the structural fire protection.
  • Hoses and nozzles neglected. Look out for perished couplings, missing gaskets and nozzles stuck between spray and jet. A drill that charges hoses finds these faults.
  • Inaccurate headcount. Headcounts are often done by memory rather than by name. In a real fire, a wrong count can send a team into a space for nobody, or leave someone behind.
  • Communication breakdown. Radios on the wrong channel, flat batteries, or several people transmitting at once.
  • Equipment not restored. Wet hoses stowed or BA cylinders left empty leave the ship weaker after the drill than before it.

A drill that finds faults is a good drill. The failure is not reporting and fixing them.

Checklist for the officer running the drill

Adapt this to your SMS forms.

  • Scenario chosen and objectives set; bridge and engine room briefed
  • Alarm sounded and location announced
  • Musters complete and named headcount reported to the bridge
  • Fire pump started; at least two jets confirmed
  • Firefighter's outfits complete and inspected: suit, boots, helmet, lamp, axe, lifeline
  • BA donned and checked, with spare cylinders available
  • Fire-party radios tested and channel confirmed
  • Fire doors, dampers and ventilation closures in the drill area operated
  • Boundary cooling allocated
  • Casualty handling practised
  • Abandon-ship arrangements checked
  • Equipment restored, defects reported
  • Debrief held; logbook and drill record completed

Oral exam: questions you should be ready for

Examiners at officer level often ask some of these:

  • How often must fire drills be held on a cargo ship, and what is the 25% rule?
    • Each crew member does at least one fire drill a month.
    • A drill is held within 24 hours of sailing if more than 25% of the crew have not drilled on that ship in the previous month.
  • List what a fire drill must include under SOLAS.
    • Mustering, fire pump with two jets, firefighter's outfits, communications, doors, dampers and ventilation in the drill area, and abandon-ship arrangements.
  • What do you do before releasing CO2 into the engine room?
    • Account for all persons, stop ventilation, close all openings, operate quick-closing valves, stop fuel pumps, and follow the posted release procedure.
  • What is boundary cooling and why is it needed?
    • Cooling the bulkheads and decks around a fire to stop it spreading by conduction to the adjacent spaces.
  • What are the contents of a firefighter's outfit?
    • Protective clothing, boots, rigid helmet, approved safety lamp, axe, breathing apparatus and a fireproof lifeline.
  • How do you record a drill that could not be held?
    • Make a logbook entry stating the circumstances and the extent of the drill actually carried out.
  • What happens to equipment after a drill?
    • It is returned to full operational condition immediately, and faults are fixed as soon as possible.

Answer with the reason as well as the rule. Examiners want to hear that you understand why each step exists.

Key points
  • On cargo ships every crew member must take part in at least one fire drill a month; passenger ships also hold fire and abandon-ship drills weekly.
  • Hold a drill within 24 hours of sailing if more than 25% of the crew have not drilled on that ship in the previous month.
  • Every drill includes mustering, a fire pump with at least two jets, firefighter's outfits, communications, fire doors, dampers and ventilation, and abandon-ship arrangements.
  • Account for every person before any fixed-system release, and never release CO2 in a drill.
  • Restore all equipment used immediately and recharge or replace BA cylinders.
  • Rotate realistic scenarios and debrief honestly; drills exist to find weaknesses.
  • Your company SMS and the muster list set the detail, including the alarm signal and team structure.

Frequently asked questions

How often is a fire drill required on a cargo ship?

SOLAS requires every crew member to take part in at least one fire drill each month. An extra drill must be held within 24 hours of leaving port if more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on that ship in the previous month. Your SMS may require more frequent or additional drills.

What is the fire alarm signal on a ship?

It is the signal shown on your ship's muster list, and it is not the same on every ship. Learn it during familiarisation when you join. Do not confuse it with the general emergency alarm, which is seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast.

Is CO2 actually released during an engine room fire drill?

No. The drill simulates the release sequence: headcount, closing openings, stopping ventilation and fuel, and walking through the release procedure. The fixed system itself is never discharged as part of a drill.

Who should record the fire drill and where?

SOLAS requires drills to be recorded in the log-book designated by the Administration. Most companies also require a separate drill report under the SMS. The responsible officer named in the SMS, often the chief officer, normally completes it and the master signs it.

Do BA cylinders have to be refilled after a drill?

Yes. Equipment used in a drill must be restored to full operational condition immediately. SOLAS requires the ship to have either a way to recharge breathing air cylinders used in drills or enough spare cylinders to replace them.

Test yourself

On a cargo ship, how often must each crew member take part in a fire drill?

Every crew member must take part in at least one fire drill and one abandon ship drill each month (SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19). Weekly drills on board apply to passenger ships (SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 30).

More than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on board this ship in the previous month. When must a drill be held?

SOLAS requires drills within 24 hours of the ship leaving port in this situation. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

Which of these is a mandatory element of every SOLAS fire drill?

Each fire drill must include starting a fire pump and using at least two jets to show the system works. Fixed systems are never discharged in drills. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

What does SOLAS require for equipment used during a fire drill?

Equipment used during drills must be returned to full operational condition immediately, and any faults found must be remedied as soon as possible. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

In an engine room fire scenario, what must be confirmed before the fixed CO2 system is released?

Everyone must be out and accounted for, with openings closed and ventilation stopped, before release. Running fans would extract the gas and feed the fire. Ref: FSS Code, Chapter 5; SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 10.
