Oil Spill Response on Board: Working With Your SOPEP

Quick answer

The Shipboard Oil Pollution Emergency Plan (SOPEP) is required by MARPOL Annex I for oil tankers of 150 GT and above and all other ships of 400 GT and above. It sets out who to report to, what to do immediately to stop or limit an oil discharge, and how the ship coordinates with coastal authorities. Your company SMS and the ship's approved SOPEP always take precedence over any general guide, including this one.

What the SOPEP is and why it matters

An oil spill can quickly turn an ordinary bunkering or cargo watch into a detention, a fine and a criminal investigation, and sometimes the crew are held ashore. The SOPEP is how the ship shows it was ready and acted correctly.

Who must carry one: MARPOL Annex I requires a SOPEP approved by the flag Administration on:

  • every oil tanker of 150 GT and above, and
  • every other ship of 400 GT and above.

Ships that carry noxious liquid substances in bulk need a Shipboard Marine Pollution Emergency Plan (SMPEP) under Annex II. Many chemical tankers carry a combined SMPEP that covers both oil and NLS.

What MARPOL requires the plan to contain, in summary:

  1. The procedure the Master or other persons in charge follow to report an oil pollution incident.
  2. The list of authorities or persons to contact if an incident happens.
  3. A detailed description of the immediate actions to reduce or control the discharge.
  4. The procedures and the shipboard point of contact for coordinating with national and local authorities.

The plan must be in the working language of the Master and officers. Oil tankers of 5,000 tonnes deadweight and above must also have prompt access to computerised shore-based damage stability and residual structural strength programs. The ship's SOPEP will tell you how that service is reached.

Coastal states can add their own rules on top. A well-known example is the vessel response plan required for ships trading in US waters. Check what applies on your trade.

Know your plan and your SOPEP locker before you need them

The middle of a spill is the wrong time to open the SOPEP for the first time. After joining, make sure you know:

  • Where the SOPEP is kept and who holds controlled copies. The bridge, ship's office and cargo control room are typical locations.
  • The appendices. These hold the coastal state contact list, port contacts and ship interested parties (owners, managers, P&I, charterers). The contact lists go out of date fast, so check the revision date.
  • Your own duty on the muster list and in the SOPEP organisation.
  • The SOPEP locker: where it is, what is in it, and whether the inventory matches the checklist.

MARPOL does not set the contents of the SOPEP locker. The flag, the company, the charterer and some port requirements decide it, so follow your ship's inventory. Typical items are:

Item Use
Absorbent pads, rolls and booms Soak up and contain oil on deck
Granular absorbent or sawdust Spread on spills, then sweep up
Shovels, scoops, squeegees, buckets Collect the oil
Empty drums or bags with lids Hold oily waste
Scupper plugs (spare) Seal deck drains
Portable pump (often air-driven) Transfer collected oil
PPE (oil-resistant gloves, boots, coveralls, goggles) Protect the responders

Dispersants may be carried, but only use them on water if the coastal state has approved it. Many states restrict or ban their use, and unauthorised use can make the offence worse.

Step-by-step: responding to an operational spill

Most spills from merchant ships happen during routine work. Typical causes are a tank overflow during bunkering, a burst hose, or a leaking flange or pipeline. The sequence below shows the usual industry practice. Your SMS and SOPEP checklists prevail.

  1. Stop the source. Stop the transfer at once: emergency stop, close the manifold and tank valves, and inform the barge or terminal by the agreed signal or radio.
  2. Raise the alarm. Inform the OOW or duty officer, the Chief Officer or Chief Engineer, and the Master. The SOPEP team musters as per the plan.
  3. Assess the hazard. Volatile products such as crude oil, naphtha or gasoline cargoes create a fire and toxic-gas risk, and heated or H2S-containing fuels can also give off dangerous vapour. Remove ignition sources and stop hot work. Close accommodation ventilation intakes and doors as needed, and have the fire party ready.
  4. Contain on deck. Check that the scuppers are still plugged. Use pads, booms and absorbent to keep oil away from the ship's side.
  5. Relieve the pressure or level. For an overflow, open the filling valve to an empty or slack tank, or drain the line back. For a pipeline leak, drain the line into a suitable tank.
  6. Clean up. Collect the oil and contaminated absorbent into drums. Do not wash it overboard with water. Handle the waste as oily waste under the ship's procedures.
  7. Report. If any oil has gone, or may go, overboard, report without delay to the nearest coastal state using the SOPEP contact list. Also inform the port or terminal authorities and the company.
  8. Record and preserve. Note times, the quantity estimate and the actions taken. Take photographs and retain oil samples if the procedures call for it. Make the correct Oil Record Book entry for the accidental or exceptional discharge.

Do not restart the operation until the cause is understood and the Master or responsible officer, along with the terminal where relevant, agrees.

Casualty-related spills: grounding, collision, hull leakage

With a casualty, the ship's safety and the crew's safety come first. Pollution control comes next, but it still begins straight away. The SOPEP gives checklists for grounding, fire or explosion, collision, hull failure and excessive list. They usually cover:

  • Safety of life and ship. Sound the alarm, account for personnel and check for fire or gas.
  • Damage assessment. Sound all tanks and void spaces, and check stability and stress. On larger tankers, contact the shore-based damage stability service.
  • Limiting outflow. Internal transfer from damaged tanks to intact ones, if stability and stress allow it.
  • Hydrostatic balance. Reduce the oil level in a holed tank so the pressure inside does not exceed sea pressure outside. On some tankers, water may be introduced under the oil. Do this only on the Master's decision and with shore advice.
  • Ballasting, deballasting or lightering to reduce draught or list. Every one of these needs stability and strength checks.

Worked scenario. On a bulk carrier at anchor, the 2nd Engineer sees a sheen alongside and finds that the level in a side fuel tank is falling slowly with no transfer under way. This points to hull leakage. The steps are:

  1. Inform the Master and Chief Engineer.
  2. After checking stability and stress, transfer fuel from that tank to intact tanks with suitable free capacity, aiming to bring the oil level low enough to achieve hydrostatic balance with the sea.
  3. Monitor the sheen.
  4. Report to the coastal state and the company as per the SOPEP.
  5. Arrange a diver or underwater survey through the company.

A falling tank level combined with a sheen is a leak until proven otherwise.

Reporting: who, when and how

Reporting duties come from Protocol I of MARPOL. Report without delay any discharge, or probable discharge, of oil above what MARPOL permits, or any discharge resulting from damage. The report format follows IMO's general principles for ship reporting systems, and the SOPEP gives it in ready-to-fill form.

An initial report usually includes:

  • ship's name, call sign, IMO number and flag
  • position and time (UTC)
  • type and estimated quantity of oil, and whether the discharge is continuing
  • the cause and the actions taken
  • weather and sea conditions
  • whether assistance is needed

Follow up with updates as the situation changes.

Who:

  • the coastal state, either the nearest one or the one whose waters are affected
  • the port authority and terminal if you are in port
  • the company, through the contacts in the SOPEP, which normally include the DPA

The company informs P&I, the flag and charterers as its procedures require.

In port, report first and fast, even for a small quantity. Hiding a spill or delaying the report causes most of the serious legal trouble seafarers face over pollution.

Hazards and real lessons from the deck

Typical hazards

  • Fire and explosion from volatile oil on deck or on the water around the ship.
  • Toxic vapour, including H2S from some crudes and fuels. Use gas detectors and stay upwind.
  • Slips on oily decks, and skin contact with the product.
  • Enclosed space risks in drip trays, cofferdams or tanks during clean-up. Follow the entry procedures.

Recurring lessons from incident reports

  • Scupper plugs pulled to drain rainwater and not refitted, or not watertight. Check them before and during every transfer.
  • Tank overflow while a single person was watching several tanks during topping-off. Reduce the rate early and use extra people.
  • Valve line-up errors, such as an unexpected tank filling or a crossover left open. Two people should check the line-up against a plan.
  • Sounding pipe caps and air vents left open on deck.
  • Poor communication with the barge, where the stop signal was not agreed or not understood.
  • Stale contact lists, which cost time making the first report.

A well-run bunker or cargo plan, with agreed rates, ullage limits, manned save-alls and a tested emergency stop, prevents most of these.

Drills and record keeping

The ISM Code requires the company to plan for and drill shipboard emergencies. The company SMS sets how often SOPEP drills are held and what they cover, and charterers or terminals may expect extra drills. A useful drill includes:

  • mustering the SOPEP team
  • deploying real equipment, not just talking through it
  • practising the stop signal with a 'barge' or 'terminal'
  • filling in an initial report using the actual contact list

Also:

  • Record drills in the log book or the SMS drill record.
  • Keep the SOPEP locker inventory up to date.
  • Amend contact lists as soon as revisions arrive.

Port State Control and vetting inspectors routinely ask to see the approved SOPEP. They check that the contact list is current, inspect the locker and question crew on their duties.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to test whether you can act, not just recite. Expect questions like these:

  • Which ships must carry a SOPEP, and under which regulation? Oil tankers of 150 GT and above and other ships of 400 GT and above, under MARPOL Annex I. The plan is approved by the Administration.
  • What are the mandatory contents? Reporting procedure, list of contacts, immediate actions to reduce or control discharge, and the procedure and point of contact for coordinating with authorities.
  • You see oil on the water during bunkering. Your first actions? Stop the transfer, raise the alarm, inform the Master, contain, assess fire risk, then report.
  • What is hydrostatic balance and when would you use it? Lowering the oil level in a damaged tank so the internal pressure does not exceed sea pressure, which limits outflow. It is used after hull damage, with stability and strength checked.
  • Can you use dispersant alongside? Only with coastal state approval.
  • Where are the coastal state contacts? In the SOPEP appendices. Explain how you would check they are current.
  • What record do you make? An Oil Record Book entry, the log book, and the company incident report.
Key points
  • MARPOL Annex I requires an approved SOPEP on oil tankers of 150 GT+ and all other ships of 400 GT+.
  • First action in any operational spill: stop the source and raise the alarm.
  • Report any actual or probable overboard discharge to the coastal state without delay using the SOPEP contact list.
  • Fire and toxic vapour are the main hazards; remove ignition sources and close ventilation intakes.
  • Dispersants on water need coastal state approval.
  • SOPEP locker contents and drill frequency are set by the company, flag or port, not by MARPOL.
  • Record the incident in the Oil Record Book and preserve evidence.

Frequently asked questions

What does SOPEP stand for?

Shipboard Oil Pollution Emergency Plan. It is a ship-specific plan required by MARPOL Annex I and approved by the flag Administration. It covers reporting, contacts, immediate actions and coordination with authorities in an oil pollution incident.

What is the difference between SOPEP and SMPEP?

SOPEP covers oil under MARPOL Annex I. SMPEP covers noxious liquid substances under Annex II and is required on ships carrying NLS in bulk. A combined SMPEP covering both oil and NLS is common on chemical tankers.

What items must be in the SOPEP locker?

MARPOL does not list the contents. They are set by the company, the flag, and sometimes port or charterer requirements. Typical items are absorbents, booms, scupper plugs, shovels, scoops, drums, a portable pump and PPE. Follow your ship's own inventory checklist.

How often should SOPEP drills be held?

The frequency is set by the company SMS under the ISM Code requirement for emergency drills. Charterers, terminals or the flag may expect more. Check your ship's drill schedule.

Do I have to report a small spill in port?

Yes. Report any oil reaching the water, or likely to, to the port or coastal authority and the company as the SOPEP requires. Delaying or hiding even a small spill usually leads to far heavier consequences than the spill itself.

Test yourself

Which ships must carry a SOPEP under MARPOL Annex I?

MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 37 applies to oil tankers of 150 GT and above and every other ship of 400 GT and above. 500 GT is not a SOPEP threshold.

Oil is drifting from the ship's side in port. When may you spray dispersant on it?

Dispersant use on water is controlled by the coastal state, as reflected in the IMO SOPEP guidelines. Carrying it on board does not authorise its use.

During bunkering you see oil coming out of a tank vent onto deck. What is your first action?

Stopping the source comes first in every SOPEP operational spill checklist. Containment and reporting follow at once.

A fuel tank has hull damage below the waterline. What is the aim of 'hydrostatic balance'?

The IMO SOPEP guidelines describe reducing the head of oil in a damaged tank to limit outflow, subject to stability and strength checks.

Under which MARPOL Annex is a Shipboard Marine Pollution Emergency Plan for noxious liquid substances required?

MARPOL Annex II, Regulation 17 requires the SMPEP for NLS. Annex III covers packaged harmful substances, and Annex VI covers air pollution.
