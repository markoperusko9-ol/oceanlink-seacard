Piracy and Armed Robbery Precautions (BMP): A Practical Guide for Ship's Officers and Crew

Quick answer

Best Management Practices (BMP) are industry guidance on avoiding, deterring and delaying piracy and armed robbery. They work through a voyage-specific threat assessment, registration and reporting to the regional centre, physical ship protection measures, and a drilled plan for what to do if attacked or boarded. Your Ship Security Plan and company SMS always take precedence. They set the detail for your vessel.

What BMP is and why it still matters

BMP is not a convention. It is guidance written and endorsed by the main shipping industry bodies, with input from naval forces. It is built on hard lessons from the Somali piracy years and from attacks in the Gulf of Guinea and South-East Asia.

The best-known earlier editions are:

  • BMP5, which covers the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea
  • BMP West Africa
  • broader global counter-piracy guidance

In 2025 these were consolidated into a single global BMP Maritime Security publication. Older editions such as BMP5 and BMP West Africa may still be referenced in some SSPs. Check which edition your company SMS and Ship Security Plan (SSP) refer to before you sail.

The core idea fits in one line: avoid, deter, delay. Pirates rarely board a ship that sees them early, manoeuvres well, has good physical barriers and a crew who know where to go. Most successful boardings have involved a combination of these failures:

  • poor lookout
  • low freeboard
  • slow speed
  • an unprepared crew

The threat picture changes. Somali piracy fell sharply, and the industry-designated High Risk Area in the Indian Ocean was withdrawn at the start of 2023. Since then there have been fresh incidents off Somalia.

Since late 2023 the southern Red Sea and Gulf of Aden have also seen state-linked attacks on merchant ships using missiles, drones and uncrewed craft, plus a helicopter-borne hijack. That threat is different from classic piracy. Always work from the current advisories, not last year's memory.

Piracy vs armed robbery: the legal frame

Examiners like this distinction because it decides who has jurisdiction.

Term Where Basis
Piracy High seas, or outside any state's jurisdiction UNCLOS 1982, Article 101
Armed robbery against ships Internal waters, archipelagic waters, territorial sea IMO Resolution A.1025(26)

In practice, most incidents at anchor or in port are legally armed robbery. That covers places like Singapore Strait anchorages, Callao or West African anchorages. They are reported to the coastal state and the relevant reporting centre.

The regulatory backbone on board is SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and the ISPS Code:

  • The SSP must address security threats. IMO guidance (MSC.1/Circ.1334) and most flags and companies expect it to include piracy and armed robbery measures.
  • The Ship Security Alert System (SSAS) sends a covert alert ashore without sounding on board.
  • STCW Section A-VI/6 sets the security training you hold: awareness, designated duties or SSO.
  • IMO's MSC.1/Circ.1334 gives guidance to owners, masters and crews on preventing and suppressing piracy and armed robbery.

Armed guards (PCASP). Whether armed guards may be carried is a flag state decision. Coastal states may also prohibit foreign armed guards in their waters. Many Gulf of Guinea states do, and local security escort arrangements are used instead. Never assume. Check flag and coastal state rules through the company.

Before entering a risk area: step-by-step preparation

1. Threat and risk assessment. The company and master assess the specific voyage. Consider:

  • current advisories from UKMTO, JMIC, MDAT-GoG, ReCAAP ISC and the IMB Piracy Reporting Centre
  • the ship's freeboard and speed
  • cargo and crew nationality
  • planned anchorage or drift periods

The outcome sets the measures. It can also lead to a route change or, where flag and coastal state rules allow, the embarkation of armed security.

2. Registration and reporting. Follow the regional scheme in force. Typical arrangements:

  • Indian Ocean region: register with MSCHOA and report to UKMTO on entering the Voluntary Reporting Area. Make initial, daily and final reports, plus any change of route or ETA.
  • Gulf of Guinea: report to MDAT-GoG under its voluntary reporting scheme.
  • Asia: contact points through ReCAAP ISC and the coastal state.

3. SSP and passage plan. Brief the crew on the SSP's piracy section. Mark these on the passage plan:

  • reporting points
  • recommended transit corridors where they exist
  • areas to avoid drifting or anchoring

Put emergency contact numbers on the bridge, in the citadel and in the ECR.

4. Ship protection measures (checklist).

  • Physical barriers: razor wire on likely boarding points (lowest freeboard, aft quarters). Take care to protect the crew rigging it.
  • Water spray and fire hoses rigged and remotely operable. Foam monitors where fitted.
  • Accommodation, engine room and steering gear entry points secured against entry from outside, while remaining openable from inside for escape.
  • Ladders and portable tools removed or secured so they can't be used against the crew.
  • Bridge protection, such as sandbags or plating, if the assessment calls for it.
  • Additional lookouts, night-vision equipment, and searchlights ready.
  • Citadel prepared and supplied:
    • water and food
    • sanitation
    • first aid
    • independent communications
    • means to stop the engine and control the ship's systems where possible.

5. Drills. Run the piracy alarm and citadel drill before entry. The ISPS Code guidance calls for security drills at least once every three months. A pre-transit drill is good practice whatever the date of the last one. Everyone must know the distinct alarm and their muster point.

During the transit or at anchor

Watchkeeping

  • Enhanced lookout all round, with special attention astern and in the radar blind sectors.
  • Use radar on short ranges to pick up small craft. Small skiffs show poorly on radar in a sea.
  • Brief OOWs on warning signs:
    • craft closing at high speed
    • ladders or weapons seen
    • mother ships towing skiffs
    • fishing craft that change behaviour

Lights

BMP has advised dimming or switching off deck lighting at sea in some areas. Navigation lights must remain as required by COLREG. At anchor, good lighting of the hull and deck is usually the better deterrent. Your SSP decides.

AIS

IMO's AIS guidelines allow the master to switch off AIS where its operation compromises the safety or security of the ship. If you do, record it in the log and switch back on when the threat passes. Industry advice on AIS has varied by region and over time, so follow current guidance.

Speed and readiness

  • Maintain the best practicable speed through the risk area.
  • Keep the engine and steering on immediate notice.
  • Avoid drifting or waiting off a hotspot. Many West African and Asian incidents happen at anchor or while drifting.

At anchor

  • Hold regular anti-boarding rounds in pairs, with radio checks.
  • Keep the anchor chain hawse covered where practicable. Thieves climb the cable.
  • Keep the gangway up when not in use.

If attacked, boarded, or rescued by the military

Approach detected, not yet boarded:

  1. Sound the piracy alarm and make a PA announcement. The crew muster per the SSP.
  2. Increase to full speed. Use manoeuvres to create wash and stop skiffs coming alongside. Mind your own ship's safety and other traffic.
  3. Report immediately to the regional centre (UKMTO or MDAT-GoG, for example) and the company. Send a Mayday or distress alert if appropriate.
  4. Activate the SSAS.
  5. Start water spray and hoses.
  6. Show the ship is alert: searchlights, and the whistle if your SSP calls for it.

Boarding imminent:

  1. All crew go to the citadel or safe muster point. Every person must be accounted for.
  2. Stop the engine and secure the ship as the SSP directs, if you can do so safely.
  3. Establish communications from the citadel.
  4. Naval forces will normally only consider an opposed boarding to free a ship if the whole crew is confirmed inside a viable citadel with communications. One person left outside can change the whole decision. Military response is never guaranteed.

If captured before reaching the citadel:

  • Stay calm. Don't resist.
  • Keep your hands visible and follow instructions.
  • Don't make sudden movements.
  • Don't try to take photos or film.

If military forces board:

  • Keep low, preferably face down, with hands empty and visible.
  • Don't make sudden moves or photograph them.
  • Expect to be restrained until you are identified.
  • Brief the forces on the number and location of attackers when asked.

After the incident and real lessons

Immediately after:

  • Account for all crew.
  • Give first aid and seek medical advice.
  • Check the ship's systems before getting underway.

Reporting and records:

  • Report to the regional centre, the company, the flag and the coastal state as required.
  • Write log entries with times and positions.
  • Preserve the VDR data.

Evidence:

  • Don't clean up areas the attackers used.
  • Don't handle items they left behind.
  • Note descriptions of people, craft, weapons and language spoken.

Crew welfare matters. Shock and stress can show up days later, and many companies arrange counselling after an incident.

Lessons from incident reports:

  • Low and slow ships get boarded. Many successful boardings involved low freeboard and slow speed.
  • Partial citadels fail. If any crew member remains outside, naval forces are unlikely to attempt an opposed boarding, so the citadel loses much of its value.
  • Anchorage and slow-transit complacency. Many Asian robberies happen at night at anchor or during slow transits. The theft of stores is often discovered only afterwards.
  • Escape routes matter. Barriers that lock pirates out must not trap the crew in a fire.
  • Report even minor thefts. Reporting builds the threat picture that protects the next ship.

Oral exam: questions you should be able to answer

Q: Define piracy and armed robbery against ships. A: Piracy is defined in UNCLOS Article 101 and occurs on the high seas or outside any state's jurisdiction. Armed robbery against ships takes place within a state's internal waters, archipelagic waters or territorial sea, and is covered by IMO Resolution A.1025(26).

Q: What is the SSAS and who receives the alert? A: It is a covert alert system required by SOLAS XI-2. It does not sound on board. The alert goes to the competent authority designated by the flag state, which normally includes the company.

Q: Which instrument decides if you can carry armed guards? A: The flag state's law and policy, together with the coastal state's rules for its waters. BMP does not authorise it.

Q: What must be true for a citadel to be effective? A: All crew must be inside and accounted for. There must be independent two-way communications, adequate supplies, and protection against forced entry.

Q: You are OOW and see two skiffs closing fast from astern at night. What do you do? A:

  1. Call the master and raise the alarm per the SSP.
  2. Go to full speed and begin evasive manoeuvring while keeping a safe navigational situation.
  3. Report to the regional centre.
  4. Activate the protection measures.
  5. Muster the crew in the citadel as ordered.

Q: Can you switch off navigation lights to avoid detection? A: No. COLREG requirements stand. Deck lighting is a separate SSP matter.

Key points
  • BMP is industry guidance built on 'avoid, deter, delay'; your SSP and company SMS set the actual measures.
  • Piracy (UNCLOS Art. 101) is outside state jurisdiction; armed robbery (Res. A.1025(26)) is within a state's waters.
  • Register and report to the regional centre in force, such as UKMTO/MSCHOA or MDAT-GoG.
  • Ship protection measures include enhanced lookout, physical barriers, water spray, secured access and a prepared citadel.
  • A citadel only works if every crew member is inside with independent communications.
  • Armed guards depend on flag and coastal state rules, never on BMP alone.
  • If boarded, don't resist; if the military board, stay low with hands visible.

Frequently asked questions

Is BMP mandatory?

BMP itself is industry guidance, not a convention. However, the ISPS Code requires your Ship Security Plan to address security threats, and most companies write BMP measures into the SSP and SMS. Some flag states and charterers also expect BMP compliance, which makes it effectively required on your ship.

Who do I report to when transiting the Gulf of Aden or Indian Ocean?

Normally UKMTO is the primary point of contact for ships in the Voluntary Reporting Area, with registration through MSCHOA. Send initial, daily and final reports, plus any change of plan. Always check the latest advisories, as arrangements and areas change.

What is the difference between a citadel and a safe muster point?

A citadel is a pre-prepared space built to resist forced entry for a long period. It has supplies, independent communications and ideally the ability to control the ship. A safe muster point is a protected area for short-term mustering, often away from the ship's side, used when a full citadel isn't appropriate or not available. Your SSP defines which applies.

Can the master switch off AIS in a piracy area?

IMO's AIS guidelines allow the master to switch off AIS where its operation could compromise the safety or security of the ship. The action and reason must be logged, and AIS restored when the threat has passed. Follow current regional and company guidance, as advice on AIS has varied.

Does every seafarer need security training for piracy areas?

STCW Section A-VI/6 requires security-related training for seafarers employed on ships covered by the ISPS Code. Everyone needs at least security awareness, those with security duties need designated duties training, and the SSO needs the SSO certificate. Pre-transit briefings and drills on the specific threat are also expected under the SSP.

Test yourself

A tanker at anchor inside a coastal state's territorial sea is boarded and stores are stolen at night. How is this classified?

Acts within a state's internal waters, archipelagic waters or territorial sea are armed robbery against ships (IMO Res. A.1025(26)); piracy under UNCLOS Art. 101 is outside state jurisdiction.

Who decides whether privately contracted armed security personnel may be carried on board?

Carriage of armed guards is a matter of flag state law and policy, and coastal states may restrict it in their waters; BMP does not authorise it (see MSC.1/Circ.1405/1406 and flag state requirements).

What happens on board when the Ship Security Alert System is activated?

Under SOLAS Chapter XI-2, the SSAS sends a covert alert to the authority designated by the flag state and must not raise an alarm on board or alert nearby ships.

Why must every crew member be inside the citadel?

BMP guidance stresses that naval forces' decision to intervene depends on all crew being safely inside a citadel with communications; anyone outside makes intervention far less likely.

During a night transit through a piracy risk area, what is correct regarding lights?

COLREG Part C requirements for navigation lights still apply; deck lighting is a security measure decided under the SSP and BMP guidance.
