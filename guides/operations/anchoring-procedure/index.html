Anchoring Procedure: Planning, Letting Go, Anchor Watch and Weighing

Quick answer

Safe anchoring follows one sequence: plan the anchorage, prepare the windlass and forward team, and approach heading into the dominant wind or current with speed over ground near zero. You then lay the cable out as the ship drops back, confirm she is brought up, and keep a proper anchor watch until you weigh. Scope, depth limits for letting go and engine readiness are set by your company SMS and the master, so treat this guide as the general industry pattern only.

Why anchoring deserves respect

Anchoring looks routine, so it gets rushed. Many groundings, collisions in crowded anchorages, lost anchors and forecastle injuries start with an anchoring done in a hurry.

Keep one principle in mind. Ship's anchoring equipment is designed for temporary holding in reasonably sheltered water. It is not designed to hold a ship against heavy weather in an exposed roadstead. When conditions build, the anchor is not your safety. Early heaving up and going to sea often is.

Your company SMS will usually contain an anchoring procedure, a checklist and limits for:

  • the maximum depth at which the anchor may be let go on the brake
  • engine readiness at anchor
  • weather thresholds for heaving up

Those always prevail over anything general, including this guide.

Planning the anchorage

Anchoring is part of the passage plan, not an afterthought. Before arrival the master and navigating officer should agree on the following:

  • Anchor position: the designated anchorage or a position allocated by port control or VTS.
  • Charted depth and seabed: holding quality, and any cables, pipelines, wrecks or other obstructions.
  • Swinging room: a conservative swinging circle radius is roughly the cable you intend to pay out plus the ship's length, with a margin added. Check it clears shoals, other anchored ships' circles and fairways.
  • Approach track: plan it to finish heading into the stronger of wind and current, with a clear abort route.
  • Wheel-over and speed reduction points: mark distances to go to the drop position. Clearing bearings and ranges help, and parallel indexing on radar is useful.
  • Which anchor: decide which side, and how much cable.
  • Weather and tide: forecast, tidal range and stream changes during the stay.

Scope rules of thumb differ between companies and training schools. Most are expressed as a multiple of depth, increased for poor weather, poor holding or a long stay. Use the figure in your SMS or the one the master orders. Remember that more cable increases holding but also enlarges the swinging circle.

Also check the windlass manufacturer's limits. Windlasses are designed to lift the anchor and a limited length of cable from a limited depth. Deep-water anchorages need special care for both letting go and heaving up.

Preparing the windlass and forward team

Give the engine room notice in good time. They will need the main engine on standby, power to the windlass (hydraulic plant running and warmed up, or electrical supply available) and steering gear checks completed per the arrival checklist.

The usual forward team is an officer (often the Chief Officer), the bosun and at least one rating. The SMS may specify differently.

Typical forward checks:

  • Communications tested with the bridge (UHF radio, plus a backup).
  • Windlass run in both directions, out of gear and in gear.
  • Brake band, linings and brake screw condition checked.
  • Clutch engages and disengages freely.
  • Anchor ball and anchor lights ready.
  • Hawse pipe and spurling pipe covers removed. Anchor washing ready if fitted.
  • Lashings removed, leaving the brake on and the bow stopper (guillotine or claw) holding the cable.
  • Area clear of loose gear. Crew in helmet, goggles, gloves, safety shoes and hearing protection.
  • Lighting rigged for night operation.

The anchor is then normally walked out clear of the hawse pipe under power:

  1. Engage the clutch.
  2. Release the brake and heave slightly to take the weight off the bow stopper.
  3. Lift the stopper.
  4. Walk the anchor out under power until it hangs clear, or to the depth agreed with the master.
  5. Apply the brake, then take the windlass out of gear if the anchor is to be let go on the brake.

This confirms the anchor is free before the critical moment.

Never stand in the line of the cable or near the spurling pipe when the cable is running. Rust, mud and links can fly.

Step-by-step: approach and letting go

This is a general sequence. Adjust it to your ship, conditions and SMS.

  1. Approach slowly on the planned heading. The usual aim is to stem the dominant wind or current. Reduce speed progressively, using the planned distances to go.
  2. Bring speed over ground to near zero at the drop position, ideally with slight sternway. Use GNSS speed over ground (SOG), a Doppler log if fitted, and visual or radar transits to confirm.
  3. Confirm the depth under the forefoot with the echo sounder. Check it is within the limit for letting go.
  4. Let go on the master's order. In moderate depths the anchor is released on the brake with the clutch out. In deeper water, beyond the SMS limit, walk it back under power the whole way, or to near the seabed before using the brake.
  5. Control the cable with the brake as the ship drops back. Lay the cable out along the bottom rather than piling it on top of the anchor. Avoid letting it run away. A runaway cable can burn out brake linings or part the cable.
  6. Report regularly to the bridge: shackles in the water (or at the hawse pipe), direction the cable is leading (clock face or bearing relative to the bow), and the weight on it.
  7. When the required length is out, apply the brake firmly and let the ship lie back on the cable.
  8. Confirm brought up. The cable rises and tautens as the ship drops back. As the anchor holds, the ship moves forward again and the cable slackens. Report "brought up" only when you are satisfied.
  9. Secure the cable: brake hard on, bow stopper in place, clutch out, windlass power off if the SMS allows.
  10. On the bridge: fix and log the anchor position and the time. Show anchor lights or the shape. Inform port control or VTS. Set up anchor watch tools. Agree engine readiness with the master.

One shackle (shot) of cable is normally 15 fathoms (about 27.5 m). Joining shackles are usually marked with paint and wire seizings so the team can count them at night.

Anchor watch and detecting dragging

STCW requires the OOW at anchor to do the following:

  • Fix the position and keep checking it.
  • Keep a proper lookout.
  • Watch weather, tide and sea state.
  • Make rounds of the ship.
  • Ensure the correct lights, shapes and sound signals.
  • Call the master and take action if the ship drags.

Practical tools:

  • Anchor watch circle on ECDIS, plotted around the anchor position, not the GNSS antenna. Set an alarm with a sensible margin.
  • Visual and radar ranges and bearings of fixed objects, checked at regular intervals. Do not rely on GNSS alone.
  • Radar guard zones and AIS monitoring of ships anchoring or manoeuvring nearby.
  • Forecasts and barometer trend.

Signs of dragging:

  • The position steadily moves outside the swing pattern.
  • Bearings change in one direction.
  • The ship lies beam-on rather than yawing about the anchor.
  • The cable jerks or vibrates.

If dragging, call the master and the engine room at once. Pay out more cable, or use the engine to take the weight, as ordered. Be ready to heave up and re-anchor or proceed to sea.

Signals under COLREG:

Item Requirement
Lights All-round white forward and a lower all-round white at or near the stern. A ship under 50 m may show one all-round white
Working lights A ship of 100 m or more must also illuminate her decks
Day shape One ball forward
Restricted visibility Bell rung rapidly for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than one minute. A ship of 100 m or more also sounds a gong aft. An anchored ship may add one short, one prolonged, one short blast to warn an approaching ship

Weighing anchor

  1. Give notice to the engine room and forward team. Run the windlass up and test communications.
  2. Prepare to heave: engage the clutch, release the brake, heave slightly to take the weight off the bow stopper, lift the stopper and start heaving. Rig anchor washing where fitted.
  3. Report to the bridge: shackles remaining, cable lead and weight. Use the engine and helm to ease strain, steering towards the lead of the cable. Avoid making the windlass drag the ship up to her anchor.
  4. Report "up and down" when the cable is vertical, then "anchor aweigh" when it leaves the bottom. The ship is now underway. Lower the ball, switch off the anchor lights and log the time.
  5. Report "anchor sighted, clear" or "foul". A fouled anchor (its own cable, another ship's cable or a seabed obstruction) needs the master's decision before heaving it home.
  6. House the anchor: heave it home carefully, put the stopper on, apply the brake, take the clutch out, and fit lashings and spurling pipe covers before sea passage.

Typical hazards and real lessons

Recurring themes in incident reports:

  • Letting go with too much way on. The cable runs out uncontrolled, the brake overheats and the cable or windlass is damaged.
  • Letting go on the brake in deep water. The cable gains such speed it cannot be stopped, and anchor and cable are lost overboard. Respect the depth limit and walk the anchor back.
  • Heaving against wind and sea with no engine assistance. This overloads the windlass and has caused motor and gear failures.
  • Staying at anchor too long as weather deteriorates. Ships have dragged onto shoals or into other ships because the engine was not ready and the decision to leave came late.
  • Anchoring too close to other ships, or inside their swinging circles.
  • Injuries forward: crew struck by flying debris, or caught near the gypsy or brake while the cable runs.
  • Forgetting the bitter end. Make sure you know how the bitter end is secured in the chain locker. On most ships it can be released from an accessible position outside the locker, in case the cable must be slipped in an emergency.

Oral exam style questions

Q: How do you choose an anchorage? Give depth, holding ground, shelter, swinging room, obstructions, traffic, port instructions and an escape route.

Q: Why approach into wind or current? It gives control at low speed. The ship drops back along the line the cable will lie, so the cable is laid out cleanly.

Q: How do you know the ship is brought up? The cable rises and tautens as the ship drops back, then slackens as the ship moves forward on the anchor. Position checks confirm she is no longer moving away.

Q: What is your anchor watch routine? In line with STCW and the master's standing orders: regular position checks by more than one method, an ECDIS or radar alarm, lookout, weather monitoring, rounds, signals, and calling the master early.

Q: What do you do if you are dragging? Call the master and engine room. Pay out more cable or use the engine as ordered. Warn ships nearby and VTS. Prepare to heave up and re-anchor or go to sea.

Q: When does a ship at anchor become underway again? When she is no longer at anchor. In practice this is when the anchor is aweigh, so lights and shapes change at that point.

Key points
  • Company SMS and the master's orders set scope, depth limits and engine readiness. Follow them over any rule of thumb.
  • Plan the anchorage as part of the passage plan: depth, holding ground, swinging room and an escape route.
  • Approach into the dominant wind or current and let go with speed over ground near zero or slight sternway.
  • In deep water, walk the anchor back under power instead of letting it run on the brake.
  • Report shackles, cable lead and weight continuously, and confirm 'brought up' before securing.
  • Anchor watch uses several position methods, an ECDIS or radar alarm, weather monitoring and early calls to the master.
  • Anchoring gear is for sheltered water. In deteriorating weather, heaving up early is often the safest option.

Frequently asked questions

How many shackles of cable should I pay out when anchoring?

There is no single international figure. Rules of thumb are usually a multiple of depth, increased for bad weather, poor holding or a long stay. Your SMS or the master's order decides the length. Always check the swinging circle still clears hazards with the scope chosen.

How long is one shackle of anchor cable?

One shackle (also called a shot) is normally 15 fathoms, about 27.5 m. Joining shackles are marked with paint and wire seizings so the forward team can count the cable paid out, including at night.

How do I know if my ship is dragging anchor?

Watch for the position moving steadily out of the normal swing pattern. Other signs are bearings of fixed objects changing in one direction, the ship lying beam-on to the wind, and the cable jerking or vibrating. Confirm with radar ranges and visual bearings as well as GNSS, then call the master immediately.

Does the main engine have to be on standby while at anchor?

STCW requires the OOW to ensure the state of readiness of the main engines and other machinery is in accordance with the master's instructions. The actual notice period depends on the master's orders, the SMS, port requirements and the weather. It is commonly shortened when weather deteriorates or the anchorage is congested.

What signals does a ship at anchor show and sound?

Under COLREG Rule 30 a ship at anchor shows all-round white lights forward and aft (one is permitted under 50 m) and a ball forward by day. Ships of 100 m or more must also light their decks. In restricted visibility Rule 35 requires the bell, plus a gong aft for ships of 100 m or more, and permits an optional warning of one short, one prolonged, one short blast.

Test yourself

In restricted visibility, how should a ship at anchor ring her bell?

An anchored ship rings the bell rapidly for about 5 seconds at intervals of not more than one minute. Two-minute intervals apply to ships underway, and the three distinct strokes apply to a ship aground. Ref: COLREG Rule 35(g).

What anchor lights must a 180 m ship show?

Ships of 50 m or more show two all-round whites, with the forward light higher. Ships of 100 m or more must also illuminate their decks. Ref: COLREG Rule 30(a) and (c).

The anchorage depth exceeds your SMS limit for letting go on the brake. What is the correct practice?

In deep water a free-running cable can gain enough speed to defeat the brake, risking loss of anchor and cable. It is walked back under power per the SMS and the manufacturer's limits. Ref: company SMS and windlass manufacturer's instructions.

Which observation most reliably indicates the ship is dragging?

Normal yawing and small GNSS scatter are expected. A steady one-way drift outside the swing pattern, confirmed by more than one method, indicates dragging. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1 (watchkeeping at anchor).

What does 'brought up' mean when anchoring?

'Brought up' means the anchor has taken hold and the ship is riding to her cable. 'Up and down' and 'home' describe stages of heaving, not holding. Ref: standard seamanship practice and company SMS anchoring procedure.
