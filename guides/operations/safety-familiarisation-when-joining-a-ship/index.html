Safety familiarisation when joining a ship

Quick answer

Before you take up any shipboard duties you must receive basic safety familiarisation, and you must know your emergency duties before the voyage begins. Ship-specific training on the life-saving and fire-fighting appliances must follow as soon as possible, and no later than two weeks after joining. Your company SMS sets the exact checklist, who delivers it and the deadline, and it may be stricter than the international minimum.

Why it matters and what the rules require

Your STCW Basic Safety Training certificate shows that you understand fire, survival and first aid in general. It does not show that you know where the CO2 release cabinet is on this ship, which lifeboat is yours, or what the general alarm sounds like here. Familiarisation fills that gap, and several instruments require it:

Instrument What it requires (paraphrased)
STCW Reg. VI/1, Code Section A-VI/1 para 1 Everyone employed or engaged on board, other than passengers, receives safety familiarisation before being assigned shipboard duties
STCW Reg. I/14, Section A-I/14 The company ensures that seafarers on joining are familiarised with their specific duties and with the ship's arrangements, equipment, procedures and characteristics relevant to routine and emergency duties
STCW Section A-VI/6 Security-related familiarisation before being assigned shipboard duties
SOLAS Ch. III, Reg. 19 Crew with emergency duties must know them before the voyage begins. On-board training in LSA and fire-extinguishing appliances is given as soon as possible, and no later than two weeks after joining
ISM Code, section 6 Procedures ensure that new and transferred personnel get proper familiarisation. Instructions essential before sailing are identified, documented and given

The STCW minimum content covers the following:

  • Communicating on elementary safety matters.
  • Understanding safety signs, symbols and alarm signals.
  • Knowing what to do for a person overboard, for fire or smoke, and when the fire or abandon ship alarm sounds.
  • Finding muster and embarkation stations and escape routes.
  • Finding and donning a lifejacket.
  • Raising the alarm and basic use of portable extinguishers.
  • Taking immediate action at an accident or medical emergency.
  • Operating the fire, weathertight and watertight doors fitted in that ship (other than hull-opening doors).

Timeline: what must happen, and when

Think of familiarisation in three layers:

  1. Before you are assigned any duty. This is the basic STCW A-VI/1 and A-VI/6 familiarisation: alarms, muster station, lifejacket, escape routes and security basics. It normally happens within your first hours on board.
  2. Before the ship sails. You must know your emergency duties from the muster list, and anything else the SMS identifies as essential before sailing.
  3. As soon as possible, and within two weeks. This is hands-on training with the ship's LSA and fire-fighting appliances. It is often combined with drills, plus the detailed departmental familiarisation for your rank.

Many companies set tighter internal deadlines than these and use separate forms for general, departmental and rank-specific familiarisation. Follow your SMS. If it says 24 hours, then the deadline is 24 hours.

The drill rules also catch new joiners. If more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in abandon ship and fire drills on board that particular ship in the previous month, those drills must be held within 24 hours of the ship leaving port. Crew changes at a single port can easily trigger this.

Step-by-step: your first watch on board

This sequence is a typical industry pattern. Your company procedure always takes precedence.

  1. Report to the officer of the watch or the duty officer. Hand over your documents as instructed and get your cabin allocated.
  2. Read your muster card. It is usually in the cabin or on the back of the cabin door. Check your muster station, your lifeboat or liferaft, and your duty for fire, abandon ship and man overboard. Check the same against the muster list on the bridge, in the engine room or in the accommodation.
  3. Learn the alarms. Learn the general emergency alarm (seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast on the whistle and alarm bells). Also learn the fire alarm and the fixed-system release alarm for CO2 or other gas. Ask for a demonstration or recording if the ship has one. Ask whether there are any ship-specific signals.
  4. Do the walk-round with the designated officer or a crew member. Walk escape routes from your cabin and from your work area. Practise both routes, because one may be blocked by the fire.
  5. Locate and don your lifejacket and immersion suit. Do it for real. Sizes and models differ.
  6. Find the nearest fire-fighting equipment. Locate the extinguishers, hydrants and fire alarm call points near your cabin and workplace. Note the types of extinguisher.
  7. Open and close the doors. Operate the fire doors, weathertight doors and any watertight doors you will pass through, including their local controls.
  8. Get the security briefing. Cover access control, restricted areas and the security alert or piracy procedures for the trade.
  9. Cover the departmental items. See the next section.
  10. Sign the checklist only for what you have actually done. If something was skipped, say so.

Departmental checklists

Deck officers and ratings

  • Bridge layout: steering changeover, emergency stop, VHF/DSC and GMDSS, BNWAS, and the alarms panel.
  • Navigation equipment status and known defects. Read the standing orders and night order book.
  • Mooring stations, snap-back zones, the pilot ladder rig and the gangway.
  • Lifeboat launching controls, liferaft positions and hydrostatic release units.
  • Enclosed spaces list, gas detectors and rescue equipment.
  • On tankers or gas carriers: cargo hazards, emergency shutdown and the cargo-specific PPE.

Engine officers and ratings

  • Emergency stops for fuel pumps and ventilation, quick-closing valve controls, and the fixed fire-extinguishing system release station.
  • Emergency generator start, emergency fire pump, and emergency steering arrangement.
  • The engine room escape trunk and its exits. Locate the EEBDs.
  • Lock-out tag-out and permit-to-work arrangements. Learn the high-voltage restrictions if fitted.
  • Engineer's alarm and dead-man alarm, if fitted.

Catering

  • Galley fire risks: the fire blanket, deep-fat fryer protection and fixed galley hood systems where fitted.
  • Gas or electric isolation points.
  • Provision room alarms. Know how to get out of a cold room.

ETO

  • Main and emergency switchboards and isolation procedures.
  • HV equipment and the electrical permit system.
  • Battery rooms.

Typical hazards for new joiners and lessons from the industry

Accident investigation reports repeatedly identify unfamiliarity with the ship, its equipment or a new role as a contributing factor in serious incidents.

  • Assuming every ship is the same. Lifeboat release gear, CO2 release sequences and watertight door controls vary by maker. Experience on a sister ship can mislead you.
  • Fatigue on joining. Long flights and time-zone changes mean you may start work tired. Your hours of rest still apply. If you are not fit to keep a watch, tell the master.
  • Paper familiarisation. If you sign a form without the walk-round, nobody discovers the gap until an emergency or an inspection. Port State Control officers commonly test crew by asking them to demonstrate their emergency duties or don equipment. This often exposes poor familiarisation.
  • Enclosed spaces. New crew may not know which spaces are classed as enclosed on this ship. Never enter one, or attempt a rescue, without following the permit system.
  • Mooring and snap-back. Every mooring deck layout is different. Ask where the danger zones are before your first stand-by.
  • Language and communication. If you cannot understand the safety briefing language, say so. Elementary safety communication is part of the requirement.

The lesson is simple. The person who joined yesterday is the person most likely to be in the wrong place when the alarm sounds.

Worked scenario: joining at anchor, sailing tonight

You join as third engineer by launch at 1800. The ship is due to heave up at 2200, and your predecessor leaves on the same launch.

What a good joiner does:

  1. Before your predecessor leaves, get a focused handover covering defects, running machinery and anything unusual.
  2. Ask the duty officer for the essential pre-sailing familiarisation: your muster duty, alarms, lifejacket, escape routes, and engine room emergency stops and escape.
  3. Clarify your role at stand-by. Will you be in the ECR with the second engineer, or alone?
  4. Agree with the chief engineer when the full departmental familiarisation and LSA/FFA training will be done. Make sure it is within the SMS deadline and the two-week SOLAS limit.
  5. If essential items cannot be covered before 2200, tell the chief engineer or master clearly. The ISM Code expects essential instructions to be given before sailing.

What goes wrong: you sign the full form at 1830 "to save time". A fire alarm sounds at 0300 on your first watch, and you do not know which route avoids the purifier room.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners like this topic because it tests both regulations and common sense. Practise answering these out loud.

  • Q: Under which instruments is shipboard familiarisation required? STCW Regulation I/14 with Section A-I/14, Regulation VI/1 with Section A-VI/1, Section A-VI/6 for security, SOLAS Chapter III Regulation 19, and ISM Code section 6.
  • Q: Your BST is valid. Why do you still need familiarisation? BST is generic. Familiarisation is ship-specific: this ship's alarms, layout, equipment and your muster duties.
  • Q: When must LSA and fire-fighting appliance training be given to a new crew member? As soon as possible, and not later than two weeks after joining.
  • Q: As chief officer, how do you check that a new joiner is genuinely familiarised? Ask them to demonstrate their duties, don a lifejacket and walk their escape route. Do not rely on the signature alone.
  • Q: Where can crew find information on the ship's LSA? In the training manual required by SOLAS Chapter III. Fire-fighting information is in the fire training manual required by SOLAS Chapter II-2. Both are kept where crew can access them.
  • Q: Six of your twenty crew changed last week. What drill implication may follow? If more than 25% have not drilled on this ship in the past month, abandon ship and fire drills must be held within 24 hours of leaving port.
Key points
  • Basic safety familiarisation comes before you are assigned any shipboard duty.
  • You must know your emergency duties from the muster list before the voyage begins.
  • On-board LSA and fire-fighting training must be given as soon as possible, within two weeks of joining.
  • The ISM Code requires instructions essential before sailing to be identified, documented and given.
  • Your company SMS sets the checklist, the responsible person and often a tighter deadline.
  • Walk both escape routes and actually don your lifejacket and immersion suit.
  • Never sign for familiarisation you have not received. Raise gaps with the master.

Frequently asked questions

How soon after joining must I complete safety familiarisation?

Basic safety familiarisation must happen before you are assigned shipboard duties, and you must know your emergency duties before the ship sails. SOLAS allows up to two weeks for on-board training in the LSA and fire-fighting appliances. Many companies set shorter deadlines in their SMS, and those apply to you.

Does my STCW Basic Safety Training certificate exempt me from familiarisation?

No. BST covers general competence in survival, fire-fighting, first aid and personal safety. Familiarisation covers the specific ship: its alarms, layout, equipment and your personal emergency duties. Every ship you join requires it.

Who conducts the familiarisation and signs the checklist?

This depends on the company SMS. Typically a designated officer handles the general items and the head of department handles departmental items. The master keeps overall responsibility. You will usually sign alongside the person who delivered it.

Do I need familiarisation again if I rejoin the same ship?

Follow your company SMS. Most require at least a refresher because muster duties, equipment and procedures may have changed since your last contract. Even where the form is shorter, check your new muster card, as your role may differ.

What should I do if the ship sails before I am familiarised?

Tell your head of department or the master straight away and ask for the essential items before departure. These are your emergency duties, the alarms, your lifejacket, escape routes and key emergency controls. The ISM Code expects essential instructions to be given before sailing, so a responsible master will make time for them.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS, by when must a new crew member receive on-board training in the ship's life-saving and fire-extinguishing appliances?

The limit is two weeks after joining. Your company SMS may set a shorter deadline. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

Which part of the STCW Code sets out the safety familiarisation required before a person is assigned shipboard duties?

Section A-VI/1 covers safety familiarisation and basic training for all seafarers. The other sections deal with officer competence or watchkeeping. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

Abandon ship and fire drills must be held within 24 hours of leaving port if more than what proportion of the crew have not taken part in them on that ship in the previous month?

The trigger is more than 25% of the crew, which large crew changes can easily reach. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

What does the ISM Code expect regarding instructions that are essential before sailing for new personnel?

The company's procedures must identify these instructions, document them and give them before sailing. Reference: ISM Code, section 6.

Where should a new joiner first look to confirm their personal duties in an emergency?

The muster list, backed by individual muster cards, sets out each person's emergency duties and stations. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulations 8 and 37.
