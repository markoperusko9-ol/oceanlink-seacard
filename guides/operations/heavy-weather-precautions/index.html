Heavy Weather Precautions: A Practical Guide for Deck and Engine Officers

Quick answer

Heavy weather precautions are the planned steps a ship takes to secure the hull, cargo, machinery and people before bad weather arrives. They include keeping clear of the worst weather, and handling the ship so that dangerous rolling, slamming and green water are kept to a minimum. Your company SMS heavy weather checklist always takes priority. This guide explains the reasons behind each step, gives worked scenarios and covers the questions examiners like to ask.

Why heavy weather preparation matters

Most heavy weather casualties were not caused by one huge wave. They were caused by a chain of small failures.

  • A ventilator was left open.
  • A hatch cleat was not set.
  • A ballast tank was left slack.
  • Cargo lashings were not checked.
  • The ship kept going too fast for too long.

The sea then exploits every weakness at the same time, often at night and when people are tired.

Some well-known lessons:

  • Derbyshire (1980): a large bulk carrier was lost in a typhoon off Japan with all hands. The investigations focused on water getting in through damaged fittings and hatch covers on the forward deck.
  • APL China (1998): a post-Panamax container ship lost and damaged a large part of her deck cargo in the North Pacific. This casualty made parametric rolling widely known in the industry.
  • El Faro (2015): a US-flag ro-ro/container ship was lost with all hands in Hurricane Joaquin. The investigation pointed to how the voyage was planned and how the weather information was used, as well as to the condition of the ship.

The message is the same in every case. Preparation, a sound weather picture and early decisions save ships. Courage in the storm does not.

The regulations support this:

  • Under the ISM Code, the company must have procedures for key shipboard operations and for emergencies. Heavy weather is normally covered by a specific checklist.
  • SOLAS Chapter V requires proper voyage planning and makes the master responsible for reporting certain dangers to other ships.

Before the weather arrives: planning and the weather picture

Heavy weather precautions start at the passage planning stage, not when the barometer drops.

Planning stage

  • Check the seasonal routeing, pilot charts and Sailing Directions for areas and months where tropical revolving storms (TRS) or seasonal gales are likely.
  • Identify places of refuge, sheltered anchorages and alternative routes where they are realistic.
  • If the company uses a weather routeing service, understand its limits. It gives advice. The master keeps the responsibility.

On passage

  • Collect forecasts from NAVTEX, Inmarsat EGC SafetyNET, weather fax and any routeing service.
  • Compare forecasts with your own observations: barometer trend, wind direction and force, swell direction and period, cloud and visibility.
  • Correct barometer readings for the normal daily (diurnal) variation in the tropics. A fall below the seasonal normal is significant.
  • A long, heavy swell from an unusual direction often arrives before a TRS. Record it and act on it.

Decide early. The best heavy weather precaution is not being there. A change of route or speed made 48 hours ahead costs a little time. A change made at the edge of a storm may not be possible at all.

Deck and cargo checklist

The exact list comes from your SMS. The items below are typical on most ships.

Watertight and weathertight integrity

  • Close and secure all weathertight doors, portholes, deadlights and skylights.
  • Check hatch covers: cleats set, compression seals in good order, drain channels clear and non-return valves free.
  • Close ventilators and air pipe closing devices where the design allows. Secure sounding pipe caps.
  • Secure the anchors:
    • chain stoppers or bow stoppers on;
    • brakes hard on;
    • extra lashings where fitted;
    • spurling pipes and hawse pipes covered or sealed as your ship's practice requires.

Cargo and loose gear

  • Check and tighten lashings in line with the ship's Cargo Securing Manual. This applies to containers, vehicles, project cargo, timber deck cargo and steel.
  • Secure cranes, derricks, hooks and gangways.
  • Secure stores, spare gear, drums and anything movable on deck, in the steering gear room, paint store and workshops.
  • Secure the galley, mess rooms, hospital and cabins. Lock pantry drawers. Remove loose items from tables.

People

  • Rig lifelines on exposed decks where required.
  • Restrict access to the open deck. Many SMS require the master's permission, and sometimes a permit, for any deck work in heavy weather.
  • Brief the crew: who goes on deck, how they stay in contact, and on which side of the ship they move.
  • Check that lifeboats, liferafts and their lashings are secure but still ready for launching.

Stability

  • Avoid slack tanks wherever possible, because free surface effect reduces effective GM.
  • Review the stability condition against the expected rolling. Too little GM gives a slow, sluggish roll and large heel angles, and a zero or negative GM produces an angle of loll. Too much GM causes violent, short-period rolling that damages cargo and lashings.
  • Do not start ballast water exchange in heavy weather. The Ballast Water Management Convention allows the master to decide against an exchange that would threaten the safety of the ship.

Engine room and steering readiness

Heavy weather is a test for the engine room as much as the bridge. Typical preparations include the following.

Power and redundancy

  • Start a second generator so the plant can carry steering, thrusters and any pump that trips without causing a blackout. The decision follows the SMS and the chief engineer's standing orders.

Steering

  • Run both steering gear pumps where permitted.
  • Change to hand steering when the autopilot cannot hold the course safely.
  • Confirm that the emergency steering arrangements and communications are ready.

Main engine racing

  • When the propeller comes out of the water, the engine can race and overspeed.
  • Reduce revolutions as needed.
  • Make sure the governor and overspeed protection are working correctly.
  • Watch exhaust temperatures, turbocharger behaviour and load.

Tanks and suctions

  • Keep service, settling, lube oil sump and other tank levels high enough that pumps do not lose suction when the ship rolls and pitches.
  • Drain water from settling and service tanks before the weather arrives. Sediment will be stirred up.

Securing the engine room

  • Secure spare parts, tools, drums and chain blocks.
  • Close skylights and engine room ventilators exposed to the sea.

Watchkeeping

  • Consider manned operation of a UMS engine room, more frequent rounds and closer bilge monitoring, as the chief engineer directs.

The bridge must tell the engine room before any major change of speed or heading. The engine room must tell the bridge about any limit on available power.

Ship handling in heavy seas

IMO guidance to masters on avoiding dangerous situations in adverse weather (MSC.1/Circ.1228) describes several dangerous situations to watch for.

Phenomenon Typical conditions Usual remedy
Synchronous rolling Natural roll period close to the wave encounter period, often in beam seas Alter course and/or speed to change the encounter period
Parametric rolling Head or following seas; encounter period about half the natural roll period; ships with fine ends and flared bows are especially vulnerable Alter course and/or speed; follow any ship-specific guidance
Surf-riding and broaching Following or quartering seas with high speed relative to wave speed Reduce speed and/or alter course away from the dangerous zone
Loss of stability on a wave crest Following seas, wave length similar to ship length Avoid that combination of speed and heading
Slamming and green water Head seas at excessive speed Reduce speed; alter course a few points off the sea

Practical points

  • Reduce speed early. Slamming and green water damage rises very quickly with speed.
  • Heaving to with the sea fine on the bow, at the lowest speed that keeps steerage, is the classic option for many ships. Your ship's handling characteristics and the master's judgement decide.
  • Running before the sea reduces slamming but brings risks of pooping, broaching and stability loss on a wave crest.
  • Large alterations of course in heavy seas need care. Choose a relatively calm interval and warn everyone on board before turning.
  • Record all alterations, speed reductions and the reasons for them in the logbook.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: TRS in the Northern Hemisphere

Your ship is in the western North Pacific in typhoon season. A long swell is running from the south-east, the barometer has dropped below normal after correcting for diurnal variation, and the wind is steadily veering.

  1. Use Buys Ballot's law. In the Northern Hemisphere, with your back to the wind, the low lies on your left hand. Turn 8 to 12 points to the right of the direction the wind is coming from to get an approximate bearing of the centre.
  2. A veering wind in the Northern Hemisphere means you are in the dangerous semicircle. This is the right-hand side when facing the direction the storm is moving.
  3. The traditional action is to bring the wind fine on the starboard bow, about 1 to 4 points, and proceed at the best practicable speed. Alter course to starboard as the wind veers.
  4. If the wind is backing, you are in the navigable semicircle. Bring the wind on the starboard quarter and proceed at best speed.
  5. If the wind direction is steady while the barometer falls, you are probably in the path. Bring the wind on the starboard quarter and make for the navigable semicircle.

In the Southern Hemisphere, read port for starboard and reverse the rules for veering and backing.

Modern forecasts and track predictions should come first. These rules of thumb are your check and your fallback.

Scenario 2: sudden heavy rolling on a container ship

The ship is in a moderate head sea at reduced speed and starts rolling heavily, with roll angles building over successive cycles. This pattern suggests parametric rolling.

  1. Call the master.
  2. Change speed and/or heading to move away from the critical encounter period.
  3. Afterwards, check lashings and container stacks, but only when it is safe to send people on deck.

Reporting and record keeping

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31 requires the master to send a danger message to ships nearby and to the competent authorities when he meets any of the following:

  • a tropical storm;
  • winds of force 10 or above for which no storm warning has been received;
  • sub-freezing air temperatures with gale force winds that cause severe ice accretion;
  • dangerous ice or a dangerous derelict;
  • any other direct danger to navigation.

Regulation 32 lists what the message should contain, such as position, pressure, wind and sea conditions. Many flags and companies also expect voluntary weather observations as part of the Voluntary Observing Ships scheme.

On board, record the following:

  • the time precautions were completed and who checked them;
  • deck and engine log entries for weather, speed reductions, course alterations and machinery limits;
  • damage found, which is reported in line with the SMS and may be needed for class, flag or insurance.

After the weather has passed, do a full damage round. Check sounding pipes, ballast and void spaces for water, lashings, hatch covers and anchors before resuming normal operations.

Oral exam: questions you should expect

Examiners usually move from the checklist to the reasoning behind it. Be ready to answer the following.

  • How do you know a TRS is approaching? Discuss swell, barometer trend corrected for diurnal range, changes in wind direction, cloud and forecasts.
  • How do you find which semicircle you are in, and what do you do in each hemisphere? Explain veering, backing and a steady wind with a falling barometer.
  • What is the difference between synchronous and parametric rolling, and how do you stop each?
  • Why do slack tanks matter in heavy weather? Explain free surface effect, reduced effective GM and larger roll angles.
  • What would you check on hatch covers and forward fittings before bad weather?
  • Which SOLAS regulation covers danger messages, and when must you send one?
  • For engineers: what precautions do you take for engine racing and loss of suction, and why start a second generator?

Always tie your answer back to the company SMS and the master's overriding authority. Examiners want to see that you know the theory and also understand how decisions are made on board.

Key points
  • The best heavy weather precaution is early avoidance through sound passage planning and weather monitoring.
  • Weathertight integrity comes first: doors, vents, air pipes, hatch covers and anchors must all be secured and checked.
  • Avoid slack tanks, because free surface effect reduces effective GM and makes rolling worse.
  • Reduce speed early in head seas, and change course and/or speed to break synchronous or parametric rolling.
  • The engine room needs redundancy: a second generator, both steering pumps, and protection against racing and loss of suction.
  • SOLAS V/31 requires danger messages for tropical storms and for force 10 or above winds with no warning received.
  • Your company SMS heavy weather checklist always takes priority over general guidance.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main heavy weather precautions on a ship?

The main precautions are: - secure weathertight integrity (doors, vents, hatch covers, anchors); - check cargo lashings against the Cargo Securing Manual; - avoid slack tanks; - rig lifelines and restrict deck access; - prepare the engine room and steering for redundancy; - adjust course and speed to reduce motion and slamming. The detailed list is in your company SMS checklist.

How do I know if I am in the dangerous semicircle of a cyclone?

In the Northern Hemisphere, a steadily veering wind means you are in the dangerous semicircle and a backing wind means the navigable semicircle. In the Southern Hemisphere, a backing wind means the dangerous semicircle and a veering wind means the navigable semicircle. A steady wind direction with a falling barometer suggests you are in or near the storm's path. Always confirm with current forecasts and storm track information.

What is parametric rolling and which ships are affected?

Parametric rolling is a sudden build-up of large roll angles in head or following seas. It happens when the wave encounter period is about half the ship's natural roll period and the ship's stability changes as waves pass along the hull. Container ships and other hull forms with fine ends and large bow flare are particularly vulnerable. The remedy is to change speed and/or heading.

When must the master send a danger message for bad weather?

Under SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 31, a danger message is required in these cases: - when a tropical storm is encountered; - when winds of force 10 or above occur and no storm warning has been received; - when sub-freezing temperatures with gale force winds cause severe ice accretion. The message goes to ships in the vicinity and to the competent authorities.

Should ballast water exchange be done in heavy weather?

Generally, no. Ballast exchange at sea creates free surface and stress risks, and these risks are much higher in bad weather. The Ballast Water Management Convention allows the master not to carry out an exchange if he reasonably decides it would threaten the safety of the ship. That decision should be recorded.

A ship is rolling heavily in beam seas because her roll period matches the wave encounter period. What is the most effective immediate action?

Synchronous rolling is broken by changing the encounter period through course or speed, as MSC.1/Circ.1228 advises. Creating a slack tank adds free surface and drifting beam-on keeps the ship in danger.

Why should slack ballast and fuel tanks be avoided before heavy weather?

Liquid moving in a slack tank creates a virtual rise in G, which reduces effective GM and makes rolling worse. This is basic intact stability covered in STCW deck competence.
