Hot Work on Board: Permit, Procedure, Hazards and Exam Questions

Quick answer

Hot work is any job that produces heat, flame or sparks, such as welding, burning, cutting or grinding. Outside a designated workshop it is controlled by a permit to work, a risk assessment, gas testing, isolation of the area and a dedicated fire watch. The exact sign-off levels and limits come from your company SMS and flag requirements, so always follow those.

What counts as hot work and why it is controlled

Hot work means any work that could ignite flammable material or gas. It includes:

  • Electric arc welding and gas welding
  • Oxy-acetylene or other gas cutting and burning
  • Grinding, and cutting with abrasive discs
  • Brazing, soldering with a flame, heat-shrinking with a torch
  • Some SMS also include non-intrinsically safe power tools, chipping hammers or needle guns in hazardous areas

Fire at sea is one of the worst situations a crew can face. Hot work brings an ignition source into places that were never designed for one. A spark falling through a grating, heat passing through a bulkhead into a fuel tank, or a smouldering lagging that flares an hour later have all caused serious fires.

The SOLAS fire protection chapter is built around reducing the chance of ignition. The ISM Code requires the company to have documented procedures for key shipboard operations. Hot work is almost always one of them. That is why every SMS has a hot work procedure and a permit form. Where this guide and your SMS differ, the SMS prevails.

Designated hot work area versus everywhere else

Most ships have a designated hot work area. This is usually the engine room workshop, with a welding bench, extraction and fire extinguishing equipment close by. Routine jobs in that area are normally covered by standing workshop rules rather than a fresh permit each time. Check what your SMS says about this.

Anywhere outside that area normally needs a hot work permit. Common features are:

  • A risk assessment before the permit is issued
  • Master's approval, with the Chief Engineer or Chief Officer usually responsible for the job, depending on the location
  • Company or shore approval for higher-risk locations, such as cargo areas on tankers, fuel tanks, or near dangerous goods. The exact triggers depend on the company and the ship type.
  • A time limit on the permit, with re-validation if the work runs over or conditions change

On tankers and gas carriers, hot work in the cargo area is treated far more strictly. Many operators only allow it when there is no realistic alternative. Some ports and terminals do not allow it at all, or require their own permission. Always check local regulations before planning hot work in port.

Step-by-step: a typical hot work procedure

This is the general industry sequence. Your SMS may order or name the steps differently.

  1. Question the need. Can the part be removed and worked on in the workshop? Can a cold method be used, such as a mechanical joint or a temporary clamp?
  2. Plan and risk assess. Identify the location, the adjacent spaces (above, below and on all sides), what pipes, tanks and cables pass through, and what could burn.
  3. Isolate. Drain, flush and blank or disconnect pipelines. Lock out and tag out pumps, valves and electrical supplies. Treat tanks and pipes that held oil, fuel or cargo as suspect until proven otherwise.
  4. Prepare the area.
    • Remove combustibles, or cover them with fire blankets.
    • Clean oil residues out of bilges and tank tops.
    • Cover openings and gratings so sparks cannot fall through.
  5. Check the other side. Inspect adjacent compartments. Move combustibles away from the bulkhead or deck being heated, and post a watch there if needed.
  6. Gas test. Test the work area and adjacent spaces with a calibrated, bump-tested meter. Check oxygen, flammable gas and any toxic gases relevant to the space. If it is also an enclosed space, the enclosed space entry procedure applies as well.
  7. Set up fire precautions.
    • Provide suitable extinguishers.
    • Rig a fire hose, run out and charged where the SMS requires.
    • Brief a dedicated fire watch.
    • Establish communications with the bridge or ECR.
  8. Check equipment and PPE. See the checklist section below.
  9. Toolbox talk and permit sign-off. Everyone involved knows the job, the limits, the stop-work triggers and the emergency actions. The permit is signed and posted at the job site.
  10. Carry out the work.
    • Repeat gas tests at the intervals set by the permit and after any break.
    • Stop at once if conditions change, for example a gas alarm, a change in cargo or bunker operations, or rising wind on deck.
  11. Finish and monitor.
    • The fire watch stays on after the work ends, for the period your SMS sets. Many companies set 30 to 60 minutes.
    • Check the area, including the far side, for heat and smouldering material.
  12. Close out.
    • Restore isolations.
    • Reinstate any fire detection that was isolated, and tell the bridge.
    • Close the permit and record it.

A note on fire detection: some SMS allow local detector heads to be isolated during hot work to prevent false alarms. Others prohibit it. Where it is allowed, it must be logged, kept to the smallest area possible, and restored immediately afterwards. Forgotten isolations have left spaces unprotected for days.

Equipment and site checklist

Electric arc welding

  • The welding machine is in good condition, with insulated cables and no damaged sections or bare joints.
  • The return (earth) lead is clamped as close to the work as possible. Never use handrails, pipework or distant structure as the return path. Stray current can arc elsewhere and damage equipment.
  • Electrode holders are insulated. Dry gloves and dry clothing are worn.
  • Never change electrodes with wet hands, or while standing in water.

Gas welding and cutting (e.g. oxy-acetylene)

  • Cylinders are secured upright and kept outside the confined work space where practicable. The cylinder valves stay accessible.
  • Flashback arrestors are fitted, and non-return valves are fitted per the SMS and manufacturer.
  • Hoses are in good condition, correctly colour-coded and leak-tested with soapy water. Never test for leaks with a flame.
  • No oil or grease on oxygen fittings. Oxygen under pressure can make oil ignite violently.
  • Cylinder valves are closed at breaks. Hoses and torches are removed from enclosed spaces when the job stops, so gas cannot leak into the space unattended.

Site

  • The permit is posted. The area is barricaded and signed if needed.
  • Ventilation is adequate for fumes.
  • Fire watch, extinguisher, hose, fire blanket and communications are in place.
  • PPE includes welding helmet or goggles with the correct shade, leather gloves, flame-retardant overalls and safety boots. Respiratory protection is used where fumes demand it.

Typical hazards and lessons the industry keeps relearning

These are generic, recurring lessons from casualty investigations and near-miss reports:

  • Heat through the bulkhead. Welding on one side ignites cargo, insulation, paint, lagging or fuel vapour on the other. Lesson: the far side gets the same preparation and a watch.
  • "Empty" is not gas-free. Tanks and pipes that once held oil or fuel give off vapour from sludge and scale when heated. Lesson: clean, ventilate, test and keep re-testing.
  • Sparks travel. Grinding sparks can travel well beyond the immediate work area and drop through gratings onto oily tank tops. Lesson: cover openings and clean below.
  • The fire nobody was watching. Smouldering material flares up after the team has left. Lesson: keep the post-work fire watch and do a final inspection.
  • Cargo holds with cargo. Hot work near combustible cargoes such as cotton, coal, wood products or packaged goods has caused major hold fires. Lesson: many SMS prohibit it or restrict it heavily. Check the cargo documents and the DG manifest.
  • Simultaneous operations. Bunkering, cargo transfer, tank venting or gas freeing nearby can bring vapour to the job. Lesson: hot work must be coordinated with every other operation, and stopped if one starts.
  • Fumes and enclosed spaces. Welding consumes oxygen and produces toxic fumes. Leaking gas hoses can enrich or deplete the atmosphere. Lesson: ventilate, monitor and follow enclosed space rules.

Worked scenario: repairing a pipe bracket in the purifier room

The 2nd Engineer wants to weld a cracked bracket on a fuel oil line support in the purifier room. Here is how a well-run ship handles it:

  • Can it go to the workshop? No, the bracket is welded to structure. Could a bolted clamp do the job? The Chief Engineer decides a weld is the proper repair.
  • Risk assessment. It identifies:
    • fuel oil lines and purifiers close by
    • oily residues in the bilge below the gratings
    • a fuel settling tank on the other side of the bulkhead
  • Controls.
    • The purifier is stopped, isolated and tagged.
    • The nearby fuel line section is drained.
    • Oil-soaked rags are removed and the bilge is cleaned.
    • Fire blankets cover the purifier and the gratings.
    • If the weld is on or near the settling tank boundary, the tank is emptied, cleaned, ventilated and gas-tested gas-free, or the job is redesigned to avoid heating the boundary. Company approval is obtained where the SMS requires it for hot work on fuel tank boundaries.
  • Gas test. It is carried out in the purifier room and at the bilge. The readings are recorded on the permit.
  • Fire watch. An oiler has an extinguisher and a charged hose. Another crew member checks the tank side.
  • Approval. The permit is signed by the Chief Engineer, approved by the Master, and posted. The bridge is informed.
  • Close-out. After the weld, the fire watch stays for the SMS period. The area is felt for heat, the isolations are removed and the permit is closed.

If an examiner asks what the most likely failure would be, a good answer is: "Missing the settling tank behind the bulkhead, or leaving before the steel cooled."

Oral exam style questions and model answers

Q: Define hot work. Work that produces heat, flame or sparks capable of igniting flammable material or gas, such as welding, cutting, burning and grinding.

Q: Who authorises hot work outside the designated area? The Master, after the responsible officer has completed the risk assessment and permit. The company must also approve where the SMS requires it, for example in tanker cargo areas.

Q: What gas tests do you carry out and when? - Oxygen, flammable gas, and toxic gases relevant to the space. - Done before work starts, at the intervals stated on the permit, after breaks, and whenever conditions change. - Use a calibrated instrument that has been function-checked.

Q: Where do you connect the welding return lead and why? As close to the work as possible. This gives a short, defined current path and prevents stray currents arcing through pipework, bearings or other structure.

Q: What are the duties of a fire watch? - Stay at the site, and on the far side if required, and do nothing else. - Have extinguishing equipment ready and know how to raise the alarm. - Stop the work if unsafe conditions arise. - Remain for the specified period after completion and check for smouldering.

Q: What would make you stop hot work immediately? - A gas detector alarm or rising readings. - Smoke or fire. - A change in nearby operations, such as bunkering or cargo work. - Loss of the fire watch or communications. - Any condition that differs from the permit.

Key points
  • Hot work is any work producing heat, flame or sparks: welding, cutting, burning, grinding.
  • Outside the designated workshop it needs a risk assessment, a permit and Master's approval, plus company approval where the SMS requires.
  • Isolate and clean the area first, and treat 'empty' tanks and pipes as suspect until tested.
  • Always check and protect the other side of bulkheads and decks being heated.
  • Gas test before starting, at set intervals, after breaks and whenever conditions change.
  • A dedicated fire watch stays on after the work ends for the period set by the SMS.
  • Close out properly: restore isolations and fire detection, inspect the site and close the permit.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a hot work permit to weld in the engine room workshop?

Usually not, if the workshop is the designated hot work area and your SMS covers it with standing rules. Fire precautions and PPE still apply. Check your SMS, as some companies have extra conditions even in the workshop.

Who signs the hot work permit on a ship?

Normally the officer responsible for the job prepares it: the Chief Engineer for machinery spaces, the Chief Officer for deck and cargo areas. The Master approves it. For higher-risk locations, especially tanker cargo areas, many companies also require shore approval before work starts.

What gas readings are needed before hot work?

The space must have a normal oxygen level and flammable gas well below the limit set by your SMS, with toxic gases also checked where relevant. Tanker industry guidance commonly sets a very low flammable limit, often not more than 1% of the lower flammable limit, for cargo areas. Always use the figures in your company procedure.

How long should the fire watch stay after hot work?

For the period your SMS specifies, which is commonly 30 to 60 minutes after the work stops. The watch should also confirm the area and the far side have cooled and that nothing is smouldering before the permit is closed.

Can hot work be done while the ship is in port?

Sometimes, but many ports and terminals restrict or prohibit it, or require their own permission. You also need company approval where the SMS requires it. Simultaneous operations such as bunkering or cargo handling make it riskier, so coordinate with the terminal and stop hot work if those operations start.

Test yourself

Who normally gives final shipboard approval for hot work outside the designated hot work area?

The Master approves hot work permits on board, and company approval is added for higher-risk locations under the SMS (company SMS developed under ISM Code Section 7).

Where should the return (earth) lead of an arc welding set be connected?

A return lead close to the work gives a short, controlled current path and prevents stray currents arcing through pipework or machinery (company SMS welding procedure).

Which statement about gas testing for hot work is correct?

Atmospheres can change during work, so testing is repeated as the permit and SMS require (ISM Code, Section 7; IMO Resolution A.1203(33) where enclosed spaces are involved).

When may the fire watch stand down?

Smouldering material can ignite well after hot work ends, so the watch continues for the period specified in the company SMS (company SMS developed under ISM Code Section 7).

You are welding on a steel bulkhead. What about the compartment on the other side?

Heat conducts through steel and can ignite material or vapour on the far side, so adjacent spaces must be prepared and watched (industry hot work guidance; company SMS).
