Passage Planning: Appraisal, Planning, Execution and Monitoring

Quick answer

Ships covered by SOLAS Chapter V must have a voyage plan from berth to berth before sailing. SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 34 requires it, and IMO Resolution A.893(21) sets out the four stages: appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring. Your company SMS decides the format, the approval process and the figures such as under-keel clearance and fix intervals. Your job is to build a plan that is complete and checked, and that the bridge team actually follows.

Why passage planning matters and where the requirement comes from

Investigations into groundings, near-misses and collisions keep pointing to the same causes:

  • A plan that was never properly made.
  • A plan made but not checked.
  • A plan that was checked but then ignored.

Passage planning is how the bridge team agrees, before departure, where the ship will go, where she must not go, and what happens when things change.

The main instruments are:

  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34 requires the master to make sure the intended voyage has been planned using appropriate charts and publications, taking into account the IMO guidelines. The plan must:
    • identify a route that accounts for routeing systems;
    • allow sufficient sea room;
    • consider anticipated navigational hazards and weather;
    • consider marine environmental protection measures.
  • IMO Resolution A.893(21), Guidelines for Voyage Planning sets out the four stages: appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring. It makes clear that the plan runs from berth to berth, pilotage waters included.
  • STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2 (watchkeeping) repeats that the voyage must be planned in advance and that the plan is checked and followed.
  • STCW Code, Section A-II/1 lists 'Plan and conduct a passage and determine position' as a required competence for officers in charge of a navigational watch.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 27 requires charts and nautical publications to be adequate and up to date.

On most ships the 2nd Officer is the navigating officer who prepares the plan. The master reviews and approves it, and every OOW must read it and understand it. The format varies: company template, ECDIS route plus checklist, or both. The principles do not change.

Stage 1 – Appraisal: gather everything before you draw a line

Appraisal means collecting and studying all the information relevant to the voyage. Most errors start here, with a missing correction or an unread warning. A practical checklist:

Charts and publications

  • ENC coverage and permits, or paper charts, corrected to the latest notices.
  • Sailing directions, list of lights, list of radio signals.
  • Tide tables and tidal stream atlases.
  • Routeing guides and the ship's routeing publications.
  • Mariner's handbook, distance tables.

Current information

  • Navigational warnings (NAVTEX, EGC/SafetyNET).
  • Temporary and preliminary notices.
  • Weather routeing and forecasts.
  • Ice information where relevant.
  • Security and piracy advisories.

Regulatory areas

  • Traffic separation schemes, ship reporting systems and vessel traffic services (VTS).
  • Pilotage requirements.
  • Load line zones.
  • Emission control areas and fuel change-over requirements.
  • Ballast water and other environmental restrictions.
  • Particularly sensitive sea areas and areas to be avoided.

Own ship

  • Departure and arrival draughts, trim and air draught.
  • Squat and under-keel clearance (UKC) per company policy.
  • Manoeuvring data and turning circles.
  • Defects affecting navigation, propulsion or steering.
  • Bunkers and stores.
  • Cargo restrictions, such as dangerous goods or a heavy-weather-sensitive deck cargo.

People

  • Watch arrangements, rest hours and pilot requirements.
  • Whether extra bridge manning is needed in confined waters or restricted visibility.

The appraisal often ends with the master deciding the broad route, for example coastal or offshore, or which side of an island group to take. Only then does detailed planning start.

Stage 2 – Planning: building the plan berth to berth

Now you turn the appraisal into a plan on the charts or ECDIS, supported by notes or a company form. A well-built plan usually contains:

  • Tracks and courses with distances, plus planned speed and ETAs for key points.
  • No-go areas, marked clearly. On ECDIS, set the safety contour and safety depth for the ship's actual draught, squat and the company UKC margin. Do not leave the default settings.
  • Margins of safety and cross-track limits for each leg, reflecting the width of safe water.
  • Wheel-over positions and turn radius, worked out from the ship's manoeuvring data, not guessed.
  • Parallel index lines, clearing bearings and clearing ranges on conspicuous radar or visual marks.
  • Abort points and points of no return in channels and harbour approaches, with contingency anchorages or holding areas.
  • Tidal windows, worked out where UKC or air draught depends on the height of tide.
  • Reporting points, VTS channels, pilot boarding positions and the information to be given.
  • Position-fixing methods and intervals for each phase (ocean, coastal, pilotage). The intervals are set by company policy and the master's judgement.
  • Changes in engine or bridge status: standby, full away, manoeuvring, ECA fuel change-over points, and steering changes from auto to hand.
  • Extra manning and equipment notes, for example an additional officer on the bridge in a TSS, or anchors cleared away before entering pilotage waters.

Check before approval

  • On ECDIS, run the route check function and resolve every alarm or indication. Do not just acknowledge them.
  • Visually scan each leg at a suitable scale, because automated checks can miss things.
  • Have another officer cross-check the plan where the SMS requires it.
  • Get the master's approval.
  • Brief the bridge team so every OOW knows the critical points.

Stage 3 – Execution: putting the plan into action

Execution is carrying out the plan in the conditions actually met. Before and during the voyage, the master and OOW consider:

  • ETA reliability at critical points, so that tidal windows and daylight transits can still be met.
  • Weather and sea state, and whether these affect squat, leeway, speed or the plan itself.
  • Traffic density in TSSs and at focal points.
  • Equipment status: tests before arrival and departure, and steering gear checks per SOLAS and SMS.
  • Manning and fatigue, especially before a long pilotage or night arrival.

Pilotage waters

The plan still applies when a pilot boards. Exchange information with the pilot: the pilot's intended route and speeds, ship's particulars and any defects. Agree the plan, record any changes, and keep monitoring. The pilot's presence does not relieve the master or OOW of their responsibility for the ship's safety.

When the plan has to change

If the plan changes, for example a new weather route, a port change or a closed channel, revise it formally rather than improvising. The changes must be checked, approved and briefed in the same way as the original.

Stage 4 – Monitoring: proving the ship is where the plan says

Monitoring means continuously checking the ship's progress against the plan.

Fixing the position

  • Fix at the planned intervals, using more than one independent method where possible: GNSS, radar ranges and bearings, visual bearings.
  • Do not rely on a single sensor. Cross-check GNSS against radar overlay and visual marks.
  • Watch for gyro errors and for log or echo sounder faults.

Using the planned aids

  • Parallel indexing gives continuous cross-track monitoring without plotting a fix.
  • Echo sounder readings compared with charted depths are a simple check that is often forgotten.

ECDIS alarms

  • Keep the anti-grounding and cross-track alarms meaningful.
  • An alarm silenced or permanently acknowledged is no alarm at all. Investigations into ECDIS-assisted groundings have repeatedly found audible alarms disabled or settings inappropriate.

Acting on deviations

If the ship is off track, ahead or behind schedule, or a planned mark cannot be identified, act early. Correct the error or call the master in line with standing orders.

Monitoring also covers the plan's continuing validity. New navigational warnings received during the voyage must be checked against the route.

Worked scenario: coastal leg with a tidal restriction

A loaded vessel is to transit a buoyed channel to an anchorage. The channel's charted depth is less than the ship's draught plus the company UKC margin and the estimated squat at planned speed. The transit therefore needs height of tide.

  1. Appraisal: Check the corrected ENC and the latest notices for depth changes. Read the sailing directions for local currents. Obtain tidal predictions for the channel and note any VTS reporting requirements.
  2. Planning:
    • Calculate the minimum height of tide needed. Allow for squat at the planned speed and any company allowance for weather or survey uncertainty.
    • Identify the window when the tide exceeds that minimum.
    • Set the ECDIS safety contour for the actual draught and margin.
    • Mark an abort point outside the channel entrance and a contingency anchorage.
    • Plot parallel index lines on a well-defined radar target.
  3. Execution: The ship is running late because of head seas, and the revised ETA puts her at the channel entrance after the window closes. The master decides to slow down and wait at the planned contingency anchorage for the next window, rather than push through with reduced UKC.
  4. Monitoring: On the next tide the OOW monitors the parallel index and confirms depths against the echo sounder. Each VTS report is logged as planned.

Lesson: The abort point and contingency anchorage made the safe decision easy. Without them, commercial pressure would have favoured pressing on.

Oral exam: questions examiners commonly ask

Expect the examiner to push past textbook answers. Typical lines of questioning:

  • "Where is the requirement for a passage plan?" SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34, with the IMO Guidelines for Voyage Planning (A.893(21)) giving the four stages. Mention STCW A-VIII/2 as well.
  • "From where to where?" Berth to berth, including pilotage waters.
  • "The pilot has his own plan. What do you do?" Exchange information, compare the two plans, agree changes, and keep monitoring. The master's responsibility remains.
  • "How do you set the safety contour?" From the ship's draught, squat and company UKC margin. Explain that ECDIS will use the next deeper contour available in the ENC if your value is not a charted contour.
  • "What is an abort point?" The last position at which the manoeuvre can be safely abandoned. Beyond it, the ship is committed.
  • "Your GNSS position and radar fix disagree. What next?" Treat it as a navigational emergency in confined waters. Fix by independent means, check gyro and radar settings, reduce speed if necessary, and call the master.
  • "A navigational warning affects your route mid-passage. What do you do?" Assess it, amend the plan, get it approved and brief the team, as the SMS requires.

Answer with the regulation, then the practical action. Examiners want to see that you would actually do it.

Key points
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34 requires a voyage plan before sailing; IMO Resolution A.893(21) gives the four stages.
  • The plan runs berth to berth, pilotage waters included, and a pilot on board does not remove the master's responsibility.
  • Appraisal means corrected charts, current warnings, regulatory areas, own-ship limitations and manning, all gathered before drawing tracks.
  • A good plan shows no-go areas, wheel-over positions, parallel indexes, abort points, contingency anchorages and tidal windows.
  • On ECDIS, set the safety contour and depth for the actual draught and UKC margin, then resolve every route-check alarm.
  • Monitoring means fixing by independent methods, cross-checking GNSS, and acting early on any deviation.
  • UKC margins, fix intervals and approval steps come from your company SMS and the master's orders.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for making the passage plan on a merchant ship?

The master is responsible under SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34 for ensuring the voyage is planned. In practice the navigating officer, usually the 2nd Officer, prepares it and the master reviews and approves it. Every OOW must read and understand the plan before keeping a watch on it.

Does the passage plan have to cover pilotage waters?

Yes. The IMO Guidelines for Voyage Planning call for a plan from berth to berth, including areas where a pilot will be on board. The pilot's plan should be compared with yours and changes agreed. The bridge team continues to monitor throughout.

What under-keel clearance should I use in the passage plan?

There is no single international figure; the minimum UKC is set by your company SMS and sometimes by port or channel authorities. Calculate it from draught, squat at the planned speed, and allowances for sea state and survey accuracy as your company requires. Use that same margin when setting ECDIS safety values.

Is an ECDIS route enough as a passage plan?

The ECDIS route is a major part of the plan on ships using ECDIS as the primary means of navigation. It usually needs supporting information such as tidal calculations, reporting requirements, contingency plans and checklists, often on a company form. Check your SMS for what must be documented and how approval is recorded.

What should I do if the plan needs changing during the voyage?

Treat it as a revision, not an improvisation. Appraise the new information, amend the route, run the checks again and get the master's approval. Then brief the other watchkeepers, all in line with your SMS.

Test yourself

Which SOLAS regulation requires the master to ensure the voyage is planned before departure?

Safe navigation and voyage planning are covered by SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34. Regulation 19 of Chapter V deals with carriage of navigational equipment, not planning.

What is the correct order of the four stages of passage planning?

IMO Resolution A.893(21) sets out appraisal first, then planning, execution and monitoring. Information must be gathered before tracks are drawn.

A pilot boards for river transit. What happens to the master's responsibility for the passage?

The pilot's presence does not relieve the master or OOW of their duties for the ship's safety; plans are exchanged and monitoring continues (IMO Resolution A.893(21); STCW Code A-VIII/2).

What is the purpose of the ECDIS safety contour?

The safety contour, set from the ship's draught, squat and UKC margin, separates safe from unsafe water and drives anti-grounding alarms (IMO ECDIS Performance Standards, Res. MSC.232(82); IHO S-52).

What is parallel indexing mainly used for during the monitoring stage?

Parallel indexing lets the OOW see at a glance whether the ship is on track relative to a fixed radar target, without plotting fixes. This supports the monitoring stage of IMO Resolution A.893(21).
