Ship Stability Basics: GM and Free Surface Effect

Quick answer

GM (metacentric height) is the vertical distance between the centre of gravity G and the transverse metacentre M, and it measures a ship's initial stability. Positive GM means she returns upright; negative GM means she cannot float upright and will either settle at an angle of loll or, if there is no positive range of stability, capsize. Every slack tank causes a virtual rise of G called free surface effect, which reduces GM. That loss depends on the length and especially the breadth (cubed) of the free surface, the liquid's density and the ship's displacement, not on how much liquid is in the tank.

Why GM and free surface matter on every watch

Stability is not just the chief officer's spreadsheet. Ballasting, bunkering, firefighting water, ice on deck, a crane plumbing a heavy lift over the side: each of these moves G, and most stability casualties happen when several small moves add up unnoticed.

A few well-known lessons:

  • Herald of Free Enterprise (1987). Water entering an undivided vehicle deck produced a massive free surface effect, and the ship capsized within minutes.
  • Cougar Ace (2006). This car carrier took a heavy list and was nearly lost during a ballast water exchange at sea. Ballast sequence matters.
  • Firefighting. Water pumped into upper decks and holds both raises G and creates free surface. Ships have capsized alongside from firefighting water, not from the fire itself.

STCW requires officers in charge of a navigational watch to know basic stability and to use stability tables and diagrams. Chief mates and masters must go much deeper. Regardless of rank, everyone who touches a ballast valve should understand what follows.

The key points and how GM is found

Point Meaning Where you get it
K Keel, the datum for vertical heights Fixed
B Centre of buoyancy, the centroid of the underwater volume Hydrostatic tables (KB)
M Transverse metacentre, valid for small angles Hydrostatic tables (KM)
G Centre of gravity of the whole ship Calculated by you or the loading computer (KG)

GM = KM − KG. KM comes from the hydrostatics at the current draught (KM = KB + BM, where BM = I/V). KG comes from summing moments of all weights.

At small angles of heel (roughly up to 7–10°), the righting lever is GZ ≈ GM × sin θ, and the righting moment = displacement × GZ. At larger angles the ship's real behaviour is shown by the GZ curve from the cross curves (KN tables), not by GM alone.

  • Large GM (stiff ship): a short, jerky roll period. This brings risk of cargo shift, lashing failure, and damage to people and structure.
  • Small GM (tender ship): a long, lazy roll. It is comfortable, but there is little reserve against wind heel, free surface or a shift of cargo.
  • Negative GM: the ship will not float upright. She lies at an angle of loll if GZ becomes positive at a larger angle; otherwise she capsizes.

The roll period gives a rough cross-check. It is inversely proportional to the square root of GM, so a ship that suddenly rolls much more slowly than usual has lost GM. Find out why before doing anything else.

Free surface effect: what it is and how to calculate it

When a partly filled tank heels, the liquid flows to the low side and its centre of gravity moves outboard. The effect is the same as if G had risen. This is the virtual rise of G, also called the free surface correction (FSC).

  • FSM (free surface moment), in tonne-metres = i × ρ, where i is the second moment of the liquid surface about its own centreline and ρ is the density of the liquid in the tank.
  • For a rectangular tank, i = l × b³ / 12 (l = length, b = breadth of the free surface).
  • FSC = Σ FSM / displacement.
  • GM fluid (corrected) = GM solid − FSC.

Things examiners love to test:

  • Breadth is cubed. Wide tanks are the problem.
  • The effect does not depend on the quantity of liquid or the tank's height in the ship. A tank 10% full and one 90% full of the same shape give the same FSM, as long as the surface is not pocketed against the tank top or bottom.
  • Dividing a tank with a centreline longitudinal bulkhead reduces the effect to one quarter. Dividing it into n equal longitudinal compartments reduces it to 1/n².
  • Full (pressed) and empty tanks have no free surface.
  • Real tanks are not rectangles. Use the FSM values in the ship's tank tables at the actual sounding. The stability booklet states whether actual or maximum FSM must be applied for a given condition, so follow it.

Worked example: one slack tank

A ship has displacement 10,000 t, KM 8.00 m and KG 7.20 m.

Step 1 – Solid GM: 8.00 − 7.20 = 0.80 m.

Step 2 – FSM of a slack double-bottom tank: The tank is rectangular, 12 m long and 10 m wide, and holds sea water (1.025 t/m³). - i = 12 × 10³ / 12 = 1,000 m⁴ - FSM = 1,000 × 1.025 = 1,025 t-m

Step 3 – FSC: 1,025 / 10,000 = 0.10 m (rounded).

Step 4 – Fluid GM: 0.80 − 0.10 = 0.70 m.

Same tank with a centreline bulkhead: Each half is 12 × 5 m. - i per half = 12 × 125 / 12 = 125 m⁴, so 250 m⁴ for both halves. - FSM = 256 t-m, and FSC ≈ 0.026 m. - Fluid GM ≈ 0.77 m. That is a quarter of the original loss.

Adding a weight: Load 500 t at Kg 12.0 m. - New KG = (10,000 × 7.20 + 500 × 12.0) / 10,500 = 78,000 / 10,500 ≈ 7.43 m. - Take the new KM from the hydrostatics at the new draught. Do not reuse the old KM.

Shifting a weight on board: GG₁ = w × d / Δ. G moves parallel to the shift. A transverse shift produces a list, and tan list = GG₁ / GM (fluid).

Practical procedure: checking stability before and during operations

This is a general industry outline. Your company SMS, the approved stability booklet and the loading instrument procedures always take precedence.

  1. Gather the inputs. You need actual soundings and ullages for every tank, with densities and temperatures. You also need the cargo weights and stowage positions, and the constant and deadweight items (stores, crew, deductible water).
  2. Calculate departure, intermediate and arrival conditions. Arrival is often the critical one, after bunkers and fresh water are consumed and tanks become slack.
  3. Apply free surface for every slack tank. This includes the fuel, lube, fresh water, sludge and bilge tanks that are easy to forget.
  4. Compare against the criteria in the booklet. For most ships these include the IS Code's general criteria: minimum areas under the GZ curve, minimum GZ at 30° or more, the angle of maximum GZ, a minimum initial GM, and the weather criterion. Grain, timber deck cargo, tankers and gas carriers have additional or different requirements.
  5. Plan the ballast sequence, not only the end state. Check stability at each stage, especially during ballast exchange or simultaneous cargo and ballast operations. Keep the number of slack tanks at any moment to a minimum.
  6. Verify against reality. Compare observed draughts and list with the calculated values. A large unexplained difference means a wrong input, so find it.
  7. Monitor. Re-check whenever conditions change: icing, firefighting water, heavy lifts, cargo shifts, a damaged or flooded space.

Quick checklist before ballasting - Are the tank soundings verified, not copied from the last noon report? - How many tanks will be slack at the same time? - Is the sequence agreed between the bridge or cargo control room and the engine room? - Has the loading computer been tested as required, with results recorded? - Is the master informed if any stage approaches a limit?

List or loll? Diagnosing and correcting

A ship that is not upright either has a list, caused by an off-centre G with positive GM, or is at an angle of loll, caused by negative GM with G on the centreline. Never confuse the two. The corrective action for one can be dangerous for the other.

Signs of loll - She lies to one side, then flops to the other side in a seaway or when a small weight moves. - She has a long, sluggish roll period around the inclined position. - Tanks are slack, she has burned fuel from low tanks, deck cargo has gained weight (rain or ice), or water is on deck.

Correcting a loll (general principle) 1. Confirm there is no off-centre weight that explains the inclination. 2. Lower G. Press up or fill low tanks, remove high weights where possible, and eliminate slack tanks. 3. If ballasting into empty double-bottom tanks, start with a small, subdivided tank on the low side. Every newly slack tank first adds free surface. Filling the high side first can make her flop violently to the other side. 4. Never try to correct a loll by pumping liquid from the low side to the high side as if it were a list. 5. Re-check the condition. Report to the master and follow the SMS emergency procedures.

Correcting a list: Move weight or liquid transversely to bring G back to the centreline, having first confirmed that GM is positive.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners actually ask

  • Define GM and explain why it is only an initial measure of stability. M is valid only at small angles. Beyond that you need the GZ curve.
  • Why doesn't the quantity of liquid in a slack tank change its free surface effect? FSM depends on the shape of the free surface (l × b³) and on density, not on volume.
  • Effect of a centreline bulkhead? It reduces free surface effect to one quarter. Two longitudinal bulkheads making three equal tanks reduce it to one ninth.
  • How do you tell list from loll, and how do you correct each? See the section above. Be ready to explain why the low-side small tank goes first.
  • What happens to stability when the ship is drydocked or touches bottom? The upthrust at the keel acts like removing weight at the keel, which causes a virtual rise of G and a loss of GM. Calculate the critical period and the critical draught.
  • Effects of icing? Weight is added high up, so KG rises and GM is lost. Windage also increases.
  • What does the stability booklet contain, and who approves it? It is approved by the flag administration or by a recognised organisation on its behalf. It contains hydrostatics, cross curves, tank tables, loading conditions and criteria.

Use correct terms (GM fluid, FSM, virtual rise) and always state where a value comes from. Examiners pass candidates who reason clearly, not candidates who recite formulae.

Key points
  • GM = KM − KG. KM comes from the hydrostatics at the current draught, and KG comes from weight moments.
  • Free surface causes a virtual rise of G: FSC = Σ FSM ÷ displacement.
  • For a rectangular tank, FSM = l × b³ / 12 × density. Breadth is cubed, and the quantity of liquid does not matter.
  • A centreline bulkhead cuts free surface effect to one quarter, and n equal divisions cut it to 1/n².
  • Always check intermediate stages of ballasting and cargo work, not only the final condition.
  • A list (off-centre G) and a loll (negative GM) need opposite thinking. Confirm which one you have before acting.
  • The approved stability booklet and your company SMS govern the criteria and procedures for your ship.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good GM for a ship?

No single figure suits every ship. The IS Code sets a minimum initial GM, and other criteria apply to the whole GZ curve, but the right working GM depends on ship type, cargo and voyage. Too little leaves no reserve, and too much makes her violently stiff. Use the loading conditions and limits in your approved stability booklet.

Does a half-full tank have more free surface effect than a nearly full one?

Not for a regular-shaped tank. The FSM depends on the length and breadth of the free surface and on the liquid's density, not on the volume in the tank. The exception is when the surface is pocketed near the tank top or bottom, or the tank shape changes with height, so use the tank tables at the actual sounding.

What is the difference between GM solid and GM fluid?

GM solid is calculated as if all liquids were solid, so no free surface is considered. GM fluid is GM solid minus the free surface correction for all slack tanks. GM fluid is the value that shows the ship's real initial stability and is the one compared with the criteria.

How do I know if my ship has an angle of loll and not a list?

A lolling ship has negative upright GM. She tends to lie to one side or the other and may flop across, and she rolls sluggishly about the inclined position. A list is caused by off-centre weight with positive GM, and she stays steadily on one side. Check the calculated GM fluid and the tank and cargo distribution before taking any corrective action.

Can I rely on the loading computer for stability?

The loading instrument is a tool. It is only as good as the inputs and only valid if it is approved and tested as your flag and SMS require. Always verify soundings, densities and weights, compare the results with observed draughts and list, and be able to do a basic GM and free surface check by hand.

Test yourself

A ship has KM 7.80 m, KG 7.10 m, displacement 8,000 t and total FSM 800 t-m. What is her GM fluid?

GM solid = 7.80 − 7.10 = 0.70 m, and FSC = 800 ÷ 8,000 = 0.10 m, so GM fluid = 0.60 m. Free surface always reduces GM and never adds to it (standard stability theory; IS Code 2008, Part A).

A rectangular slack tank is fitted with a centreline longitudinal bulkhead. Its free surface effect becomes:

FSM varies with breadth cubed. Two tanks of half breadth give 2 × (b/2)³ = b³/4, which is one quarter of the original effect.

Which change will NOT alter the free surface moment of a regular rectangular slack tank?

FSM = i × density, where i depends only on the length and breadth of the free surface. The volume of liquid does not enter the formula, provided the surface is not pocketed.

A ship is found lolling to port with several empty double-bottom tanks. The best first ballasting action is:

Loll is cured by lowering G while adding as little free surface as possible. Starting low-side and small avoids a violent flop to the other side. Transferring liquid to the high side treats the loll as a list and is dangerous.

Over what range is GZ ≈ GM × sin θ considered reliable?

The metacentre is effectively fixed only at small angles. Beyond that, GZ must be taken from the cross curves (KN) and the GZ curve, which is what the IS Code criteria assess.
