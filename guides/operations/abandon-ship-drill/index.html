Abandon Ship Drill: Requirements, Procedure and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

Under SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19, every crew member on a cargo ship must take part in at least one abandon ship drill each month. A drill must also be held within 24 hours of leaving port if more than a quarter of the crew have not drilled on that ship in the previous month. A proper drill is more than a muster and a headcount: it covers alarms, dress, lifejackets, preparing and lowering a lifeboat, running its engine, liferaft davits and radio life-saving appliances, all run as if the emergency were real and carried out under your company SMS.

Why the abandon ship drill matters

Abandoning ship is the last resort, and you rarely get a second attempt at it. It may happen at night, with a list, in bad weather, with smoke in the alleyways and half the crew still half asleep. A drill is your only chance to find out beforehand whether people know their muster station, whether the davit winch works, and whether the boat engine starts.

There is a second reason the drill matters. Lifeboat drills themselves have a long record of injuries and deaths. Most were caused by on-load release hooks opening prematurely, falls or fittings failing, or winch brakes not holding. A good drill therefore tests two things: the crew's readiness for a real emergency, and the ship's ability to exercise its equipment without hurting anyone.

Port State Control inspectors know all this. They regularly ask for an abandon ship drill, or watch one, and a poorly run drill is a common reason for deficiencies and detentions.

What SOLAS requires: frequency, content and records

The core rules are in SOLAS Chapter III. The muster list (Regulations 8 and 37) must be prepared before the ship sails. It must be posted in conspicuous places, including the bridge, the engine room and crew accommodation. It shows the general alarm signal, how the abandon order will be given, each person's duties, and substitutes for key persons.

Frequency (Regulation 19):

  • Every crew member takes part in at least one abandon ship drill and one fire drill every month.
  • If more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on board that particular ship in the previous month, the drills must be held within 24 hours of leaving port.
  • Passenger ships have additional, more frequent requirements, including weekly drills and passenger musters. Check the regulation and your flag's interpretation.

Elements of each abandon ship drill, in paraphrase:

  • Summon crew (and passengers) with the general emergency alarm, followed by a drill announcement on the PA, and make sure they know how the abandon order will be given.
  • Report to stations and prepare for the duties on the muster list.
  • Check that people are suitably dressed and that lifejackets are correctly donned.
  • Lower at least one lifeboat after any necessary preparation for launching.
  • Start and operate the lifeboat engine.
  • Operate the davits used for launching liferafts.
  • Where passengers are carried, conduct a mock search and rescue of passengers trapped in their cabins.
  • Give instruction in the use of radio life-saving appliances.

Launching intervals:

Craft Requirement (paraphrased)
Lifeboats (davit-launched) Different boats used at successive drills as far as practicable; each boat launched with its assigned operating crew aboard and manoeuvred in the water at least once every 3 months
Free-fall lifeboats Crew board, secure themselves in their seats and go through the launch procedure up to, but not including, actual release at least every 3 months. The boat must also be free-fall launched with its operating crew, or lowered by the secondary means of launching, and manoeuvred in the water at set intervals; simulated launching is permitted in some circumstances. Check SOLAS III/19.3.3 and your flag administration's requirements for the exact intervals
Rescue boats Launched with crew and manoeuvred each month where reasonable and practicable, and in any case at least every 3 months

Emergency lighting for mustering and abandonment must be tested at each abandon ship drill.

The date, the details of the drill and any training must be recorded in the log book. If a full drill was not held at the required time, the log entry must say what was done and why. If you write 'drill carried out' and nothing else, an inspector will ask questions.

Step-by-step: a typical drill on a cargo ship

The sequence below is general industry practice. Your company SMS, the muster list and the manufacturer's instructions always take precedence.

Before the drill (planning)

  1. The Master and Chief Officer agree the scenario, time, location and which boat will be lowered. Weather, sea state, traffic and port rules all count. Many ports require permission before a boat is put in the water.
  2. Hold a risk assessment and toolbox talk for the launching party. Confirm hook status, fall condition, winch brake and fall preventer devices, if the SMS requires them.
  3. The engine room is briefed so it can prepare lighting, power to the davits and any isolation needed.

During the drill

  1. The general emergency alarm sounds: seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast, on the whistle and the alarm bells. This is followed by a PA announcement making clear it is a drill.
  2. Crew go to their muster stations with lifejacket, immersion suit (where assigned), warm clothing and hard hat as the SMS requires.
  3. The muster station in charge takes a headcount and reports it to the bridge. Missing persons trigger a search party.
  4. Officers check dress and lifejacket donning. They should actually tighten straps and check lights and whistles, not just look from a distance.
  5. The boat crew prepare the boat: plug, painter, gripes and harbour pins, hook status, engine pre-start checks.
  6. The bridge simulates the abandon order verbally, as the muster list sets out.
  7. The boat is lowered under the officer in charge's control. The engine is started and run, and the boat is manoeuvred if it is waterborne.
  8. The liferaft davit is operated, and the hand-held VHF, EPIRB and SART are explained. The SART and EPIRB are not activated except in properly controlled self-test mode.
  9. The boat is recovered. Hooks are reset and the reset is visually confirmed by a competent person. The boat is then stowed and secured.

After the drill

  • Hold a short debrief: what went wrong, and what was slow?
  • Raise defects and record them in the PMS.
  • Make the log book entry and complete the drill record per the SMS. Record any non-attendees so they can make up the drill.

Hazards and real lessons from lifeboat drills

Drill accidents usually come from the same few causes:

  • On-load release hooks that were not fully reset, were wrongly adjusted or were badly maintained. They opened under load and dropped the boat. SOLAS was amended so that existing hook systems had to be evaluated against improved LSA Code standards and replaced where they did not comply. Even so, a compliant hook is only safe if it has been reset correctly.
  • Fall preventer devices fitted wrongly, or removed at the wrong moment.
  • Winch brake or fall failure. SOLAS Regulation 20 and the IMO requirements for maintenance, thorough examination and operational testing of LSA require weekly and monthly inspections, regular servicing, and periodic examination by trained or authorised persons. The falls must be turned end for end and renewed at set intervals.
  • Trapped hands and fingers at gripes, harbour pins and the painter.
  • Hurrying. People rush to finish before arrival, or try to drill in a swell they would never choose to launch in.

IMO guidance on safety during abandon ship drills (MSC.1/Circ.1578) encourages companies to plan drills so that nobody is exposed to unnecessary risk. Under that approach, many SMS procedures keep people out of the boat during lowering and recovery unless the hook system has been checked and the procedure allows it. They still meet the SOLAS requirement that, at the stated intervals, the boat is manoeuvred in the water with its operating crew aboard. Follow exactly what your SMS says. If you are unsure about the hook, stop the drill.

Lesson from experience: a boat that is never really launched will not work on the day you need it. Seized gripes, flat batteries and painted-over shackles all turn up during real drills. Skipping the launch to save time only moves the problem to a worse moment.

Your personal checklist when joining and at every drill

SOLAS requires every crew member with emergency duties to be familiar with them before the voyage begins. On-board training in the ship's life-saving and fire-fighting appliances must be given as soon as possible, and no later than two weeks after joining. STCW also requires safety familiarisation before you are assigned shipboard duties.

On joining, check these:

  • Your muster station, your lifeboat or liferaft, and your duty on the muster list (it is also shown on your cabin card).
  • Both escape routes from your cabin and from your workplace to the muster station.
  • Where your lifejacket and immersion suit are, and whether they fit you. Try the immersion suit on.
  • The general alarm signal and how the abandon order will be given.
  • Who your substitute is, and whose substitute you are.

At each drill, check these:

  • You are properly dressed, with long sleeves, safety shoes and a hard hat as required.
  • Your lifejacket is donned correctly, and its light and whistle work.
  • You know your boat duty, whether that is plug, painter, engine, gripes or the winch brake. You should be able to do it in the dark.

Worked scenario: crew change in port

Your ship sails from a crew change port at 1800. Eight of the twenty crew are new joiners who have not drilled on this ship.

Analysis: 8 out of 20 is 40%, which is above the 25% threshold. An abandon ship drill and a fire drill must therefore be held within 24 hours of departure. Before the voyage begins, the new joiners must also be familiarised with their emergency duties, and their on-board LSA training must be completed within two weeks of joining.

Good practice:

  • Brief the joiners on their muster list duties before sailing.
  • Plan the drill for the next morning in daylight, once the ship is clear of traffic.
  • Lower a boat that was not used at the last drill.
  • Use the debrief to confirm the newcomers can carry out their duties in the boat.
  • Record the 24-hour drill in the log book with its details.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners for OOW and Chief Mate certificates like this topic. Typical questions, with model lines of answer:

  • How often must the crew take part in an abandon ship drill? At least one each month per crew member, and within 24 hours of leaving port if more than 25% of the crew have not taken part on that ship in the previous month (SOLAS III/19).
  • List the elements of an abandon ship drill. Alarm and announcement, reporting to stations, dress and lifejacket checks, lowering at least one lifeboat, running its engine, operating the liferaft davits, instruction on radio LSA, and a mock search on passenger ships. Emergency lighting is tested at each drill.
  • How often must each lifeboat be launched and manoeuvred? Each davit-launched lifeboat must be launched with its assigned operating crew aboard and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months. Free-fall lifeboats follow their own intervals for boarding and for actual or simulated launches.
  • What is on the muster list and where is it displayed? The alarm signals, how the abandon order is given, each person's duties and substitutes. It is displayed on the bridge, in the engine room and in crew accommodation.
  • What do you check before lowering a lifeboat with people in it? Hook reset and indicator, falls, brake, fall preventer devices per the SMS, a clear area below, painter rigged, plug in, communications, and a risk assessment.
  • What if weather prevents the launch? Do not launch. Carry out the parts that are safe, record the reason in the log book, and complete the launch at the first safe opportunity within the required interval.

Examiners want to hear safety first. They also want to see that you know the intervals and that the SMS governs the detail.

Key points
  • Every crew member must take part in at least one abandon ship drill per month (SOLAS III/19).
  • If more than 25% of the crew have not drilled on that ship in the previous month, drill within 24 hours of sailing.
  • Each davit-launched lifeboat must be launched and manoeuvred in the water with its assigned operating crew at least every three months; free-fall lifeboats follow their own intervals.
  • The general emergency alarm is seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast; the abandon order itself is given as stated on the muster list.
  • Most drill accidents involve on-load release hooks, falls or winch brakes, so check hook reset and follow the SMS without shortcuts.
  • Log every drill with details, and record the reason if any element was not carried out.
  • Company SMS, the muster list and the manufacturer's instructions govern the detailed procedure.

Frequently asked questions

How often is an abandon ship drill required on a cargo ship?

SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19 requires every crew member to take part in at least one abandon ship drill each month. An extra drill is needed within 24 hours of leaving port if more than 25% of the crew have not taken part in drills on that ship in the previous month. Your company SMS may require more frequent drills.

Does a lifeboat have to be lowered into the water at every drill?

At each abandon ship drill at least one lifeboat must be lowered after any necessary preparation. It does not have to reach the water every time. However, each davit-launched lifeboat must be launched with its assigned operating crew aboard and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months. Free-fall lifeboats have their own intervals for boarding and for actual or simulated launches.

What is the abandon ship signal?

SOLAS defines the general emergency alarm as seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast on the ship's whistle and alarm bells. The order to abandon ship is usually given verbally by the Master, as described on the muster list. Some companies add their own signals, so always check the muster list on your ship.

What happens if the weather is too bad to launch the lifeboat on drill day?

Do not launch in conditions that put people at risk. Carry out the parts of the drill that can be done safely, and enter the reason for the incomplete drill in the log book. Then launch at the first safe opportunity so the three-month interval is still met.

Can new joiners wait until the monthly drill to learn their emergency duties?

No. SOLAS requires crew with emergency duties to be familiar with them before the voyage begins. On-board training in life-saving and fire-fighting appliances must be given no later than two weeks after joining. STCW also requires safety familiarisation before you take up shipboard duties.

Test yourself

Within what period after leaving port must an abandon ship drill be held if more than 25% of the crew have not drilled on board that ship in the previous month?

The drill must take place within 24 hours of leaving port in that case. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

How often must each davit-launched lifeboat be launched with its assigned operating crew aboard and manoeuvred in the water?

Each davit-launched lifeboat must be launched with its operating crew and manoeuvred in the water at least every three months. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

Which is the general emergency alarm signal defined by SOLAS?

The general emergency alarm is seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast on the whistle and alarm bells. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 6, and LSA Code Chapter VII.

Which of the following must be tested at every abandon ship drill?

Emergency lighting for mustering and abandonment is tested at each abandon ship drill. Live EPIRB activation and actual liferaft or free-fall launches are not part of every drill. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

What must be done if a full abandon ship drill could not be held at the required time?

If a full drill is not held at the appointed time, the log book must record the circumstances and extent of what was done. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.
