Man Overboard Procedure on a Merchant Ship

Quick answer

When someone goes over the side, the first few seconds decide the outcome: raise the alarm, release a lifebuoy with light and smoke, mark the position, keep the person in sight and put the helm toward the side they fell from. Then choose the return manoeuvre that suits the situation, prepare the rescue boat and recovery gear, inform the MRCC, and follow your ship-specific recovery plan. Your company SMS and the ship's SOLAS recovery plan always take precedence over any general guide.

Why man overboard is different from every other emergency

Most shipboard emergencies give you minutes to organise. A man overboard (MOB) gives you seconds. A head in the water disappears in the wake almost immediately, especially at night, in swell or in poor visibility. At sea speed the ship covers a long distance in the time it takes to call the bridge.

Three things kill people in the water:

  • Losing sight of them. Once lost, the operation becomes a search rather than a recovery.
  • Cold water. Cold shock in the first moments, then loss of strength in the hands and arms, then hypothermia. In cold water a person may become unable to help themselves well before hypothermia sets in.
  • The recovery itself. Propeller, ship's side, a rolling freeboard and poorly handled lifting have all turned a successful search into a fatality.

SOLAS (regulation III/17-1) requires ships to which it applies to have ship-specific plans and procedures for recovering persons from the water. Know where yours is kept and what it says for your ship's freeboard, equipment and crew numbers.

Immediate actions: the first minute

Whoever sees it (any rank):

  1. Shout "Man overboard, port side!" or "starboard side". Say the side every time.
  2. Throw the nearest lifebuoy, or anything that floats, toward the person.
  3. Keep watching and pointing. Do not leave your position to report it. Get someone else to call the bridge, or use a radio if you carry one.

Officer of the watch:

  1. Helm hard over to the side the person fell from. This swings the stern and propeller away from them.
  2. Release the bridge-wing lifebuoy with self-activating light and smoke signal on that side.
  3. Press the MOB button on GNSS/ECDIS to mark the position and time. If there is no MOB function, note the position, time, course and speed.
  4. Sound the MOB alarm as per your muster list, call the master and put the engine room on standby.
  5. Post lookouts as high as practical with binoculars, and assign one person to do nothing but point at the casualty.
  6. Hoist flag O (International Code of Signals: man overboard) by day, and keep a sharp lookout for traffic.
  7. Begin the return manoeuvre (see next section).

Master on arrival: take the con, confirm the manoeuvre, initiate radio communications (DSC distress alerts include a "man overboard" category) and inform the MRCC and nearby vessels as appropriate. Then organise the rescue boat crew, the recovery team and the hospital party according to the muster list.

Do not forget a headcount if nobody saw the fall, for example after a shout or a found lifejacket. The longer you take to confirm who is missing, the larger the search area.

Choosing the turn: Williamson, Anderson, Scharnow

The IAMSAR Manual Volume III describes three standard return manoeuvres. The figures below are the usual textbook values. Actual angles depend on your ship's handling characteristics, so check the manoeuvring data and your SMS.

Turn How it is done Best used when Drawbacks
Anderson (single turn) Rudder hard over toward the casualty. After about 250° of turn, rudder midships and prepare to stop engines when approaching. Immediate action, person in sight, handy ship Hard to run in a straight line on the person; difficult for large, slow-turning ships
Williamson Rudder hard over toward the casualty. After deviating 60° from the original course, rudder hard over the other way. When heading about 20° short of the reciprocal, rudder midships and steady on the reciprocal. Immediate or delayed action; night and restricted visibility Takes the ship further from the scene; slower
Scharnow Rudder hard over. After deviating about 240°, rudder hard over the other way. When about 20° short of the reciprocal, midships. Delayed action, when you want to return to your own wake quickly Not effective for immediate action; needs some distance already run; cannot be carried out effectively unless the time since the fall is known

Rule of thumb for the exam:

  • Person in sight, immediate action: Anderson or Williamson.
  • Person lost from sight or missed at night: Williamson.
  • Person missing (delayed action): Williamson, or Scharnow if the time since the fall can be reasonably estimated, then a planned search.

Whichever turn you use, reduce speed in time so you are not overrunning the casualty. Plan to stop the engine before the person is close to the stern.

Approach and recovery

Bringing the ship back is only half the job. Getting the person out of the water alive is where many attempts fail.

Ship approach:

  • Generally, approach so the casualty ends up on the lee side, with the ship providing shelter. A ship usually drifts faster than a person in the water, so plan for the ship to drift down toward them, not away.
  • Stop the propeller well before the person comes near the stern. A controllable pitch propeller at zero pitch is still turning, so treat it as a hazard.
  • Have heaving lines, a lifebuoy with line, and the recovery equipment named in your recovery plan ready on the correct side. This may be a scramble net, a rescue sling, a recovery basket or a cradle.

Rescue boat:

  • Launch only when the master judges it safe. Launching and recovering a rescue boat in a seaway is itself a high-risk operation.
  • The crew must wear immersion suits or anti-exposure suits and lifejackets, and carry a portable VHF.
  • Make the lee before launching and recovering the boat.

Lifting the casualty:

  • Where possible, recover a person who has been in cold water in a horizontal or near-horizontal position. A vertical lift after prolonged immersion can cause circulatory collapse.
  • Handle them gently, remove wet clothing in shelter, insulate them and give medical care per the ship's medical guide. Get radio medical advice early.

When the person is not found: searching

If the casualty is not sighted on return, the operation becomes a search. Use the search planning in IAMSAR Volume III and coordinate with the MRCC, who may assign search areas and other vessels.

  • Datum: start from the marked MOB position, corrected for elapsed time and drift (current plus leeway). The lifebuoy and smoke float are useful drift indicators.
  • Pattern: when the datum is reasonably well known and the area is small, use a sector search or expanding square. Your speed, track spacing and visibility determine the legs.
  • Lookouts: rotate them often, because a tired lookout misses a head in the water. Assign sectors and use searchlights at night.
  • Records: log times, positions, patterns and communications. Bridge and engine logs, the VDR and ECDIS data will all be examined later. The master may need to save VDR data.

The decision to end a search is taken with the MRCC, not by the ship alone.

Prevention, drills and real lessons

Most MOB cases start with someone working near an unprotected edge or alone on deck. Typical causes are:

  • Working overside or aloft without a harness secured to a proper point, or without a permit
  • Being on deck in heavy weather with no one aware of it
  • Pilot ladder and gangway transfers
  • Mooring stations and lashing work on container ships
  • Crew alone on deck at night, including smoking or personal calls by the rail

Lessons that recur in investigation reports:

  • The alarm was delayed because the witness ran to the bridge instead of shouting and pointing.
  • The person was not missed until the next watch or the next meal, which made the search area huge.
  • Recovery gear was stowed, untested, or unsuitable for the ship's freeboard.
  • The rescue boat crew had never launched in anything but flat calm.

Drills: the ISM Code requires the company to prepare for and drill emergency situations. The frequency of MOB drills is set by your SMS and flag. Make them realistic. Throw a dummy, time the response, use the recovery equipment named in the plan, and debrief honestly.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

"You are OOW at night, full sea speed. The AB on the starboard wing shouts that someone has fallen from the starboard side. What do you do?" Helm hard to starboard. Release the starboard bridge-wing lifebuoy with light and smoke. Press MOB on GNSS/ECDIS. Sound the alarm and call the master. Engine room on standby. Keep the AB pointing. Commence a Williamson turn because it is night, and reduce speed in time. Prepare the rescue boat and recovery gear. Master to alert the MRCC.

"Why helm toward the side the person fell?" To swing the stern and propeller away from the casualty.

"Which turn is unsuitable for immediate action and why?" Scharnow. It needs distance already run to bring the ship back into its own wake.

"The cook reports a crew member missed breakfast and cannot be found. Action?" Raise the alarm and do a full headcount and search of the ship. Inform the master. Estimate the time window from when the person was last seen. Turn back on the reciprocal of the track with a Williamson turn, or a Scharnow turn only if the time since the fall can be reasonably estimated. Alert the MRCC, plan a search from the track over that time window, and post lookouts.

"How do you recover a hypothermic casualty?" Horizontally if possible, handled gently, then sheltered, dried, insulated and treated with radio medical advice.

"Where is the requirement for recovery plans?" SOLAS Chapter III, regulation 17-1, with IMO guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1447.

Key points
  • Shout, throw a lifebuoy and keep pointing; never take your eyes off the casualty to report it.
  • OOW puts the helm hard over toward the side the person fell to swing the propeller clear.
  • Mark the position immediately with the GNSS/ECDIS MOB function and log the time.
  • Williamson suits night and delayed action, Anderson is fastest when the person is in sight, and Scharnow is for delayed action only.
  • Stop the propeller well before the casualty nears the stern and make a lee for recovery.
  • Recover cold-water casualties horizontally where possible and handle them gently.
  • SOLAS III/17-1 requires a ship-specific recovery plan; your SMS and that plan always prevail.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first thing to do if someone falls overboard?

Shout "man overboard" with the side, throw a lifebuoy or anything that floats, and keep pointing at the person. Get someone else to raise the alarm with the bridge. Losing sight of the casualty is the biggest single cause of failed recoveries.

Which man overboard turn is best at night?

The Williamson turn is generally preferred at night and in restricted visibility, because it brings the ship back onto the reciprocal of its original track. It is slower than the Anderson turn, so its advantage is accuracy rather than speed. Your SMS and the ship's manoeuvring data may specify otherwise.

What does flag O mean in the International Code of Signals?

Flag O means "man overboard". It is hoisted to warn nearby vessels so they keep clear and can assist with lookout.

Is a man overboard drill mandatory?

The ISM Code requires companies to prepare for and drill emergency situations, and SOLAS requires ship-specific plans for recovering persons from the water. The exact MOB drill frequency is set by your company SMS and flag state requirements, so check your ship's drill schedule.

Why should a casualty be lifted horizontally from cold water?

After prolonged immersion in cold water, a vertical lift can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure and circulatory collapse. Recovering the person horizontally or near-horizontally, and handling them gently, reduces that risk.

Test yourself

A crew member falls from the port side while you are OOW. What is your immediate helm order?

Putting the helm toward the side the person fell swings the stern and propeller away from them. This is the standard immediate action described in IAMSAR Manual Vol III.

Which manoeuvre is NOT effective when action is taken immediately after the person falls?

The Scharnow turn needs distance already run to bring the ship back into its own wake, so it suits delayed action only. Reference: IAMSAR Manual Vol III.

In the textbook Williamson turn, when is the rudder put hard over to the opposite side?

The rudder is reversed after a 60° deviation from the original course and put midships about 20° short of the reciprocal, though values vary by ship. Reference: IAMSAR Manual Vol III.

Which SOLAS regulation requires ship-specific plans for recovery of persons from the water?

SOLAS III/17-1 requires recovery plans, supported by MSC.1/Circ.1447. III/19 covers drills, and V/33 covers distress situations and the obligation to assist.

How should a casualty who has been in cold water for some time ideally be lifted aboard?

Horizontal recovery reduces the risk of circulatory collapse after prolonged cold-water immersion. Reference: IMO guidance on recovery of persons from the water (MSC.1/Circ.1447).
