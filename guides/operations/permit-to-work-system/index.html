Permit to Work System on Ships: How It Works and How to Use It Properly

Quick answer

A permit to work (PTW) is a written control system, set up under your company's SMS, that authorises a specific hazardous job, at a specific place, for a limited time, and only after hazards are identified, isolations made and conditions checked. It covers jobs such as enclosed space entry, hot work, work aloft or overside, and work on live or pressurised systems. The exact forms, signatories and limits come from your company SMS and flag requirements, and these always take precedence over general guidance.

What a permit to work is, and what it is not

A permit to work is a formal, written agreement between the person authorising a hazardous job and the people doing it. It records what the job is, where it is, what the hazards are, what precautions are in place and how long the authorisation lasts.

The most important point, and the one examiners push hardest: the permit does not make the job safe. The precautions make the job safe. The permit is only the record that someone competent has checked those precautions before the work starts.

A PTW normally sits inside a wider set of controls:

  • Risk assessment for the task, which identifies the hazards and controls
  • Isolation / lock-out tag-out of energy sources such as electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, steam and pipeline pressure
  • Atmosphere testing where gas, vapour or oxygen deficiency is possible
  • Toolbox talk so everyone on the job understands the plan and the stop-work triggers
  • Emergency arrangements, including rescue equipment and communications

If any of these are missing, a signed permit is just a piece of paper.

Where the requirement comes from

No convention prints a single international permit form. The obligation comes from several instruments working together:

  • ISM Code – the company must assess identified risks and set safeguards (Section 1.2). It must also set up procedures and instructions for key shipboard operations (Section 7). In practice, the PTW system is one of those procedures, written into the SMS.
  • SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 – certain ships must carry a suitable portable atmosphere testing instrument for enclosed spaces.
  • SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19 – requires enclosed space entry and rescue drills at set intervals.
  • IMO Revised Recommendations for Entering Enclosed Spaces Aboard Ships (an Assembly resolution) – sets out the expected entry process. This includes an authorised person, testing, ventilation, an attendant and a permit or checklist.
  • MLC 2006, Regulation 4.3 – covers occupational health and safety protection and accident prevention on board.

Many flag administrations publish their own safe working codes. Terminals, charterers and oil majors may add their own rules on top. Where these differ, the stricter requirement usually applies in practice, but your SMS tells you how to handle conflicts.

Typical permits and who signs them

Permit types vary by company. Common ones include:

Permit Typical hazards controlled
Enclosed space entry Oxygen deficiency or enrichment, toxic and flammable atmosphere, access and rescue
Hot work (outside a designated workshop) Fire, explosion, fumes, heat transfer through bulkheads
Working aloft / overside Falls, dropped objects, ship movement, weather
Electrical work / isolation Shock, arc flash, unexpected energisation
Work on pressurised or hazardous systems Stored energy, release of fluid or gas
Cold work in hazardous areas (some fleets) Sparks, release of cargo vapour

The roles usually include:

  • Authorising / issuing officer – often the Master, Chief Officer or Chief Engineer, depending on the permit and the space. Many SMSs require the Master's approval for hot work outside the workshop, and some also require office notification or approval.
  • Responsible person / person in charge – the officer who supervises the job at the site.
  • Work party – the people doing the job, who must understand the permit.
  • Attendant / standby person – for entry and some overside work. This person stays outside, keeps communications and raises the alarm. They never enter to rescue alone.
  • Officer of the watch (deck and engine) – must be informed, because ship operations can affect the job.

Check your SMS for the exact signatories. Do not assume another ship in the fleet does it the same way.

Step-by-step: from planning to close-out

This is the general industry sequence. Your SMS forms will set the exact order and wording.

  1. Plan the job. Define the scope precisely, for example 'renew gasket on No. 2 FO purifier inlet valve', not 'purifier work'. Agree timing with other departments, and avoid clashes with bunkering, cargo, ballast or manoeuvring.
  2. Risk assessment. Review or create one. Identify hazards, controls and emergency measures.
  3. Isolate and prove. Make the isolations, then apply locks and tags. Prove dead or depressurised before work starts. List every isolation on the permit or the linked isolation certificate.
  4. Prepare the space or site. Ventilate, drain, clean and remove combustibles as needed. For hot work, check the other side of the bulkhead or deck too.
  5. Test the atmosphere where required, using a calibrated and bump-tested instrument. Test at different levels, because gases stratify. Record the readings and times. The IMO entry recommendations look for oxygen at about 21% by volume, flammable vapour not above 1% of LFL, and toxic gases not more than 50% of their occupational exposure limit (OEL). Your SMS may set its own criteria.
  6. Rig emergency equipment. This means rescue gear, breathing apparatus, extinguishers or charged hoses, a fire watch, and communications that have been tested.
  7. Issue the permit. The authorising officer visits the site before signing. The permit must state the location, the work, the precautions, the validity period and any re-testing intervals.
  8. Brief the team. Hold a toolbox talk covering the job, the hazards, stop-work conditions and emergency actions. The work party signs to confirm they understand.
  9. Display the permit. Post a copy at the worksite and keep a copy at the control point, such as the ECR, CCR or bridge, per your SMS.
  10. Monitor. Re-test the atmosphere as specified. Keep the attendant or fire watch in place.
  11. Suspend when conditions change. Examples are an alarm, a change in weather, a change of shift or personnel, a nearby operation starting, or a gas reading drifting. Re-validate before restarting.
  12. Complete and cancel. Account for all people, tools and materials. Inspect the site. Remove isolations only under control. Keep a fire watch after hot work for the period your SMS specifies. Sign off, then file the permit for the record period your SMS sets.

The validity period is set by the SMS. It is usually limited to a shift or a stated number of hours, and never 'until the job is done'.

Worked scenario: hot work on a tanker deck

Situation: A product tanker is at anchor with cargo tanks inerted and the deck area around the job confirmed gas-free. The Chief Officer wants a cracked bracket on a deck fire-main support welded.

How a competent team handles it:

  • The Master is consulted first. The SMS requires his approval for hot work outside the workshop, and the office is notified per company procedure.
  • The site is checked for hazards within the radius specified in the SMS. This includes tank vents, PV valves, cargo lines, Butterworth openings and the area below the deck.
  • Adjacent tanks and lines are confirmed as not containing hydrocarbons. Gas readings are taken at the site and in nearby openings, then recorded.
  • Tank openings in the area are closed. Vent risers are checked, and the IG system configuration is confirmed with the CCR.
  • A charged fire hose and an extinguisher are at the site, and a dedicated fire watch is posted with a radio.
  • The welding equipment is inspected: cables, earth return close to the work, and screens.
  • The bridge and ECR are informed. No cargo, ballast or tank operations may start without first suspending the permit.
  • After welding, the fire watch remains in place for the period your SMS specifies, and the area is checked for heat transfer.
  • The permit is cancelled only after the final inspection.

Where it goes wrong in real life: a ballast operation starts and vents vapour from an adjoining space. Or the fire watch is called away for mooring. The permit should have been suspended at that moment.

Typical failures and lessons from investigations

Casualty investigation reports from many flag states show the same patterns again and again:

  • Signing without seeing. The permit is issued from the office or ECR, without a site visit.
  • Isolations assumed, not proved. A valve is left 'closed' but passing, or a breaker is opened on the wrong feeder.
  • One-time gas test. The atmosphere is fine at 0800 but changes after cargo, ballast or sludge is disturbed, or after ventilation stops.
  • Testing only at the hatch. Heavier or lighter gases stratify, so a reading at one level misses them.
  • Rescuers become casualties. Crew enter a space to help a collapsed colleague without breathing apparatus. In many enclosed space fatalities, more than one person dies this way. The attendant's job is to raise the alarm, not to go in.
  • Shift change gaps. The new team does not know about the permit's conditions.
  • Conflicting work. Two permits are issued for adjacent jobs, such as hot work near an open tank, or electrical work on a system another team is testing.
  • Permits closed early, or never. Tools are left behind, or a system is re-energised while someone is still working.

The lesson for juniors: you have the right and the duty to stop work if the conditions on the permit no longer match what you see. No good SMS punishes a justified stop.

Oral exam: questions you should be able to answer

Q: Does a permit to work make a job safe? No. The precautions make it safe. The permit records that they have been checked and authorises work within defined limits.

Q: Who can issue a permit on your ship? State what your SMS says. A good answer names the roles for each permit type and explains when the Master's or office approval is needed.

Q: Your gas reading drifts during an entry. What do you do? Stop work and evacuate the space. Suspend the permit, investigate the cause, re-ventilate and re-test. Only restart after the permit is re-validated.

Q: What is the attendant's role if the entrant collapses? Raise the alarm, keep communications going and start the ship's rescue plan. Do not enter without proper breathing apparatus and an organised rescue team.

Q: Why is the OOW informed of a permit? Ship operations such as ballast, cargo, ventilation, steering tests and radar transmission can affect the worksite. The OOW also controls those operations.

Q: When is a permit suspended or cancelled? It is suspended on any change in conditions, an alarm or emergency, or a change of personnel. It is cancelled when the work is complete, the site is inspected and isolations are reinstated under control, or when the validity period expires.

Key points
  • A permit to work authorises a defined hazardous job, at a defined place, for a limited time. It does not make the job safe by itself.
  • The ISM Code requires risk assessment and written procedures. The PTW system is the company's way of meeting that for hazardous work.
  • Isolations must be proved, not assumed. Atmospheres must be tested at several levels and re-tested during the work.
  • The authorising officer should visit the site before signing, and the OOW must be informed.
  • Any change in conditions means stop and suspend the permit, then re-validate before restarting.
  • Attendants raise the alarm. They never make an unprotected rescue entry.
  • Your company SMS sets the forms, signatories, validity and gas criteria, and it takes precedence over general guidance.

Frequently asked questions

Which jobs on a ship need a permit to work?

Commonly: enclosed space entry, hot work outside the workshop, working aloft or overside, electrical isolation work, and work on pressurised or hazardous systems. The exact list is set by your company SMS and can include other tasks. If in doubt, ask before starting, not after.

Who signs the permit to work on board?

It depends on the SMS. Typically the Master, Chief Officer or Chief Engineer authorises, and a responsible officer supervises at the site. Many companies require the Master's approval, and sometimes office approval, for hot work outside the workshop. The work party usually signs to confirm they have been briefed.

How long is a permit to work valid?

Only for the period written on it. This is usually limited to a shift or a stated number of hours, as your SMS defines. A permit is also suspended immediately if conditions change, and it must be re-validated before work continues.

What gas readings are acceptable for enclosed space entry?

IMO's enclosed space entry recommendations look for oxygen at about 21% by volume, flammable vapour not above 1% of the lower flammable limit, and toxic gases not more than 50% of their occupational exposure limit (OEL). Your company SMS may set specific criteria or extra tests. Always use a calibrated, bump-tested instrument and test at different levels.

Is a permit to work the same as a risk assessment?

No. The risk assessment identifies the hazards and the controls needed. The permit confirms those controls are actually in place at the site and authorises the work for a limited time. Both are needed, and they reference each other.

Test yourself

What is the main purpose of a permit to work?

A PTW is a control and authorisation system for hazardous work. It supports, but does not replace, risk assessment (ISM Code, Sections 1.2 and 7).

During an enclosed space entry, the oxygen reading starts to fall. What is the correct immediate action?

Any change in atmosphere means stop and evacuate, then investigate and re-validate before re-entry (IMO Revised Recommendations for Entering Enclosed Spaces Aboard Ships).

An entrant collapses inside a tank. What should the attendant do first?

Unprotected rescue entry is a well-known cause of multiple fatalities. The attendant raises the alarm and the trained rescue team responds (IMO enclosed space entry recommendations; practised in the drills required by SOLAS Ch III Reg 19).

Which statement about isolations under a permit to work is correct?

Valves can pass and breakers can be misidentified, so isolations must be verified before work begins. This is part of the safeguards the ISM Code requires the company to establish (Section 1.2).

Hot work is in progress on deck when the OOW plans to start a ballast operation in an adjacent tank. What should happen?

Simultaneous operations can change conditions, for example by venting vapour, so the permit must be suspended and re-validated. This is why the OOW must be told about active permits (company SMS procedures developed under ISM Code, Section 7).
