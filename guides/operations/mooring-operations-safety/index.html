Mooring Operations Safety: A Practical Guide for Deck Crew and Officers

Quick answer

Mooring is routine work that regularly kills and maims seafarers, mainly through snap-back of parting lines and being caught in bights or on drums. Safe mooring depends on well-maintained lines and winches, a clear plan briefed before arrival, one person in charge at each station, and everyone staying out of the line of fire. The steps below are general industry practice; your company SMS and the ship's mooring plan always take precedence.

Why mooring safety matters

Seafarers do mooring so often that it starts to feel harmless. That is the problem. Accident investigation bodies keep reporting the same pattern: an experienced crew, a routine berthing, a line under load that parts or slips, and someone standing where they should not have been.

The main ways people get hurt are:

  • Snap-back. When a tensioned line parts, the stored energy throws both ends back towards and past their points of attachment, often around fairleads and pedestals in directions you would not predict.
  • Bights and coils. A line that suddenly runs out takes feet and legs with it.
  • Drums, fairleads and rollers. Hands and clothing get drawn in.
  • Tug lines. Sudden loads come on when the tug manoeuvres or the ship moves ahead or astern.
  • Slips, trips and falls on cluttered, wet or oily decks, often at night.

Under SOLAS Regulation II-1/3-8, as amended, mooring arrangements and equipment must meet design and selection requirements on newer ships, and all ships must inspect and maintain mooring equipment, including lines, in line with IMO guidelines. Many companies, especially in the tanker sector, also apply the OCIMF Mooring Equipment Guidelines (MEG4) as industry best practice. The ISM Code then requires your company to turn all of this into procedures, a risk assessment and training.

Know your equipment before you need it

You should know the following before the ship ever approaches a berth. Most of it is in the ship-specific mooring documentation, such as the mooring arrangement plan and the line management plan required under the IMO guidelines.

  • Ship Design MBL (MBLsd). This is the minimum breaking load the mooring arrangement was designed around. Lines, winches, fittings and stoppers are all rated with reference to it.
  • Line type. Wire, polyester, polypropylene/polyester mixes and HMPE behave very differently. HMPE stretches very little, so it gives almost no visible warning before it parts, and it still snaps back.
  • Winch brake holding capacity. The brake is designed to render (slip) before the line parts. Industry practice is to set and test brakes against a defined percentage of MBLsd. Follow the maker's instructions and your SMS for the setting and test interval.
  • Lines on the brake, not in gear. At berth, lines should be held on the brake with the clutch disengaged, unless the SMS says otherwise for a specific winch type. A winch left in gear cannot render, so the line or the fitting takes the shock instead.
  • Stoppers. Use a chain stopper on wire and a synthetic stopper on fibre ropes, and only on the type of line the stopper was designed for.
  • Condition. Check for chafe, crushed or glazed fibres, broken wires, worn fairleads, seized rollers and corroded bitts. Damaged rollers and sharp edges cut lines quickly under load.

Step-by-step: a typical berthing

This is the general sequence on most merchant ships. Your SMS and the master's orders override it.

1. Planning (master and chief officer) - Review the berth information, the expected mooring pattern, tugs, tide, current and wind. - Decide line numbers and leads for each station. - Brief the station officers so everyone knows the plan.

2. Preparation (well before arrival) - Test the winches and windlass, and check that brakes, clutches and controls work. - Flake out lines clear of walkways. Lay out heaving lines and messengers. - Prepare stoppers and rat guards. Make sure deck lighting works. - Test communications: radios on the agreed channel, spare batteries. - Clear unnecessary people from the stations.

3. Toolbox talk at each station - Who is in charge. - The order lines will go out. - Where everyone stands. - Hand signals in case the radio fails. - The stop command.

4. Passing tug lines - Stay clear while the tug line is heaved in or let go. - Never let go until the tug confirms it is ready. - Watch the eye on the bitt. Keep hands off once the tug starts taking weight.

5. Sending lines ashore - Throw heaving lines with an unweighted monkey's fist, after warning the linesmen. - Pay out under control. Stay out of bights and away from the drum.

6. Heaving up and making fast - Heave evenly, keeping lines in the same direction of service sharing the load. - Put lines on the brake, then disengage the clutch. - If you have to transfer a line from the drum end to bitts, apply the stopper correctly and confirm it is holding before easing the line.

7. Report and secure - Report "all fast" to the bridge with line numbers. - Fit rat guards. - Clear and tidy the deck.

Unmooring is the reverse. The risk is often higher because lines are under tension, dock workers are hurried, and tugs are already pulling.

Hazards and the habits that prevent them

Hazard Habit that prevents it
Snap-back of a parting line Treat the whole mooring deck as a potential snap-back zone. Painted zones are not reliable. Stand behind something solid, out of the line of fire.
Caught in a bight Never stand in or step over a bight or coil. Watch your feet whenever a line is running.
Hands drawn into a drum Keep hands well clear of the drum. Keep cuffs tight and no loose clothing. One person handles the line, the other operates the winch.
Line jumping off a warping end Use enough turns for the line type, keep steady back-tension, and never surge wildly.
Mixed lines in the same lead Do not mix wire and fibre, or lines of different stretch, in the same direction. The stiffer line ends up taking most of the load.
Overloaded single line Tend lines during cargo and tide changes so the load stays shared.
Communication failure Use one channel, short orders and agreed hand signals. Anyone may call stop.
Fatigue and short manning Raise it before arrival. The master can delay berthing. Fatigue is a real factor in accidents.

Lessons investigators keep repeating:

  • People were injured while standing outside painted snap-back zones, because lines led around fairleads change the snap-back path.
  • Lines parted at loads well below their rated strength, because of age, UV damage, chafe or a poor splice.
  • Winch brakes had not been tested, or were set wrongly, so they slipped too early or did not render at all.

Worked scenario: passing ship surge

You are the officer on the aft station during cargo operations. A large vessel passes the berth at speed. Your ship surges astern. The aft spring tightens hard and the stern line goes slack.

What a good officer does:

  1. Shouts a warning and moves everyone behind protection, away from the tight spring and its fairlead lead.
  2. Does not send anyone forward to "check the line" while it is under shock load.
  3. Lets the brake do its job. If the brake renders, that is the design working as intended.
  4. Once the surge passes, heaves the slack lines back in and checks for chafe or rendered brakes. Resets and re-tensions lines as needed.
  5. Reports to the bridge or the duty officer and the master. Considers asking the port for speed control of passing traffic, and adds extra lines if the berth is exposed.
  6. Records the event and any damage. If a line or fitting is suspect, removes it from service according to the line management plan.

The mistake to avoid is engaging the winch in gear and heaving hard on a shock-loaded line. That moves the failure point to the line, the fairlead or the person standing near it.

Oral exam: what examiners ask

Expect questions like these at OOW and Chief Mate orals. Answer in practical terms.

  • "What is snap-back and how do you protect your crew?" Explain the stored energy in a tensioned line, that the danger extends around fairleads, and the industry move away from relying on painted zones. Then cover positioning, briefing and keeping non-essential crew away.
  • "Why hold lines on the brake rather than in gear?" The brake is set to render before the line breaks. A winch left in gear will not render.
  • "Can you put a chain stopper on a synthetic line?" No. Match the stopper material to the line.
  • "What is MBLsd?" The ship design minimum breaking load. Lines, winch brakes and fittings are referenced to it.
  • "What does SOLAS require for mooring equipment?" Regulation II-1/3-8: appropriate design and equipment selection, plus inspection and maintenance in line with IMO guidelines, applied through the SMS.
  • "A line shows heavy chafe during cargo work. What do you do?" Relieve the load with another line in the same lead, replace or end-for-end the damaged line, protect the chafe point, and record it.
  • "Who is in charge at a mooring station?" The officer in charge, under the master's orders. The officer must not get personally involved in handling lines and lose sight of the whole station.

Quick pre-arrival checklist

Adapt this to your SMS checklist.

  • Mooring plan and berth details briefed
  • Winches and windlass tested, brakes and clutches confirmed
  • Lines inspected, flaked clear, with spares available
  • Stoppers of the correct type at each station
  • Heaving lines ready, monkey's fists unweighted
  • Radios tested, channel agreed, hand signals agreed
  • Deck lighting working, deck clear and non-slip
  • PPE: helmet with chinstrap, safety shoes, fitted gloves, coverall, high-visibility vest
  • Enough rested crew at each station
  • Toolbox talk held and positions assigned
Key points
  • Treat the whole mooring deck as a potential snap-back zone; do not rely on painted markings.
  • Hold lines on the brake with the clutch out, so the brake can render before the line parts.
  • Never stand in a bight, step over a line under tension, or put hands near a drum.
  • Keep the same type of line in each direction of service and tend lines so loads stay shared.
  • SOLAS II-1/3-8 requires inspection and maintenance of mooring equipment, including lines, on all ships.
  • One officer in charge per station, one radio channel, and anyone may call stop.
  • Your company SMS, mooring plan and the master's orders override general practice.

Frequently asked questions

What is snap-back in mooring operations?

Snap-back is the violent recoil of a mooring line when it parts under tension. The stored energy throws the broken ends back towards and beyond their attachment points. The path can be deflected by fairleads and rollers, so it is very hard to predict. Current industry guidance treats the whole mooring area as potentially dangerous.

Should mooring lines be left on the brake or in gear?

General practice is to hold lines on the brake with the clutch disengaged. The brake is set to render before the line reaches its breaking load. A winch left in gear cannot render, so the line or fitting takes the shock. Always follow the maker's instructions and your SMS for your winch type.

Are painted snap-back zones still used?

Many older ships still have them. However, OCIMF guidance has moved away from relying on painted zones because they can give a false sense of safety. Crew have been injured while standing outside the marked areas. Treat the whole mooring deck as a risk area and position yourself out of the line of fire.

How often should mooring lines and winch brakes be inspected?

Inspection and maintenance intervals come from the ship's line management plan, the equipment makers and the company SMS. They reflect the IMO guidelines referenced by SOLAS II-1/3-8. There is no single universal figure that fits every ship, so check your own vessel's plan.

What PPE is needed for mooring operations?

Typically you need a safety helmet with chinstrap, safety footwear, close-fitting gloves, a coverall with no loose ends, and high-visibility clothing, especially at night. Some companies also require a lifejacket near unguarded edges. The exact requirement is set by your SMS and the risk assessment.

Test yourself

Why is a mooring winch normally left on the brake rather than in gear at the berth?

The brake is set to slip at a defined load below the line's strength, protecting the line and fittings. This is per maker's guidance and SOLAS II-1/3-8 guidelines applied through the SMS.

Which stopper should be used on a polyester mooring line?

Stoppers must match the line material. A chain stopper damages fibre lines and may not hold. This is standard industry practice reflected in company SMS procedures.

What is the safest assumption about snap-back danger on a mooring deck?

Lines led around fairleads change the recoil path, and all line types store energy. Industry guidance therefore treats the whole deck as a risk area rather than relying on painted zones.

Two lines lead in the same direction: one wire, one high-stretch synthetic. What is the problem?

The stiffer line carries the load while the stretchy line barely tightens. Lines in the same direction of service should be of the same type, which is a basic principle of mooring arrangement guidance.

Under SOLAS II-1/3-8 as amended, which requirement applies to all ships regardless of build date?

The design and selection provisions target newer ships. The inspection and maintenance requirements, including lines, apply to all ships under SOLAS II-1/3-8.
