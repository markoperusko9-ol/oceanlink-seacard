Cargo Watch on Tankers: A Practical Guide for Officers and Ratings

Quick answer

A cargo watch on a tanker means continuously monitoring the transfer, the tanks, the deck, the moorings and the ship–shore interface so that any leak, overflow or abnormality is caught early and the transfer can be stopped at once. The duty officer works to the chief officer's cargo plan and standing orders, and the company SMS and terminal requirements always take priority over any general guide.

Why the cargo watch matters

Most serious tanker incidents in port follow a familiar pattern: a valve left open or shut in error, a tank nobody was watching, a hose or manifold leak noticed late, or a rate that was never reduced in time for topping off. The cargo watch exists to break that chain.

The STCW Code requires the master of a ship carrying hazardous cargo to arrange a safe watch in port, and the officer in charge must understand the cargo, the operation in progress and the emergency measures. On a tanker that principle becomes a set of concrete duties:

  • Prevent pollution by spotting leaks, overflows and wrong line-ups before oil or chemical reaches the deck or the sea.
  • Prevent fire and explosion by keeping the tank atmosphere and the deck under control.
  • Protect the ship's structure by following the loading sequence, so stress and stability stay within the approved limits.
  • Protect people from toxic vapours such as H2S and benzene, and from the hazards of a crowded deck.
  • Keep the ship–shore interface safe, including moorings, gangway, hoses and arms, and communications.

The chief officer normally plans and oversees cargo work. The duty officer, with the duty AB or pumpman, runs the watch minute to minute. Who may operate valves, who may change rates and when the chief officer must be called are set by the company SMS and the chief officer's standing orders. Read them, sign them and follow them.

Taking over the cargo watch

A sloppy handover is one of the commonest roots of cargo incidents. Do not accept the watch until you are satisfied on each point below. Walk the deck if you need to.

Handover checklist (general – adapt to your SMS):

  • Current operation, grade(s) and the stage of the cargo plan.
  • Tanks currently loading or discharging, their ullages or levels, and the time each is expected to reach topping-off.
  • Current rate, manifold pressure and any rate changes agreed with the terminal.
  • Valve line-up: which tank, line, crossover and manifold valves are open. Check against the line-up sheet or mimic, not from memory.
  • Ballast operations running at the same time and the planned sequence.
  • Inert gas status where fitted: oxygen content, deck main pressure, and the plant running or stopped.
  • Draught, trim, list, and the latest loading computer printout showing shear forces and bending moments within limits.
  • Ship/shore safety checklist items due for repetitive checks, and any items with conditions attached.
  • Moorings, gangway, fire wires (where required), and the weather forecast.
  • Communications: the working channel, the agreed emergency stop signal and the contact person ashore.
  • Anything abnormal: a weeping flange, a slow tank, a gauge out of order, visitors or contractors on board.

The outgoing officer stays responsible until the relief has formally accepted the watch. Never hand over in the middle of topping off or a critical valve change. Finish it first, or both stay until it is done.

Step by step: loading watch in general industry terms

The sequence below reflects common industry practice for oil and chemical tankers. Your company procedures, the chief officer's plan and the terminal's rules prevail.

  1. Before starting. Complete the pre-transfer conference and the ship/shore safety checklist. Confirm the line-up independently, with a second person if the SMS requires it. Close and check overboard and sea valves in the cargo system. Plug scuppers. Have SOPEP gear ready at the manifold. Confirm the gauges and the high-level and overfill alarms have been tested as your procedures require.
  2. Start slowly. Begin at the reduced initial rate agreed with the terminal. Check that cargo is going only into the intended tank, and that nothing is reaching tanks that should stay static. Look for leaks at the manifold, hoses or arms and the deck lines. For static-accumulating cargoes, keep the initial rate restricted until the tank inlet is covered, as your procedures specify.
  3. Increase to the agreed rate only after the chief officer is satisfied, and tell the terminal before any change.
  4. Routine monitoring. Each round, check ullages and levels against the plan, manifold pressure, IG pressure and oxygen (if inerted), moorings, the gangway, the sea around the ship and the deck generally. Record readings at the interval your SMS requires.
  5. Ballast/deballast in step with cargo, as the plan sets out. Watch the trim, the list and the stress readings.
  6. Topping off. Warn the terminal well in advance to reduce the rate. Top off one tank at a time where possible. Open the next tank before closing the full one, so you never close against the flow. Have a person at the tank and keep communications open.
  7. Completion. Agree the stop with the terminal. Drain and blow lines as the procedures set out. Close valves, take final ullages, temperatures and samples, and complete the records.

When discharging, the emphasis shifts to pump monitoring, back-pressure, stripping, and keeping IG pressure positive so that tanks do not draw air or go into vacuum.

Rounds checklist and records

A good round is systematic. Many officers walk the same route every time so that something out of place stands out.

Area What to check
Manifold Leaks, drip trays, hose or arm condition and support, blank flanges on unused connections
Deck and lines Weeping flanges, valve positions, sounding pipes and ullage ports closed, scuppers plugged
Tanks Ullage trend matches the plan; idle tanks not changing level
Venting / IG Vent system set correctly, IG pressure and O2 within limits, deck seal or non-return arrangement normal
Overside Sheen on the water, leaks from sea chests or overboard lines
Moorings and access Lines tended as the ship rises or falls, gangway safe and watched
Accommodation Doors and ports closed as required, air conditioning on recirculation if required

On an oil tanker, cargo and ballast operations are recorded in the Oil Record Book Part II under MARPOL Annex I. On a chemical tanker carrying noxious liquid substances, operations go in the Cargo Record Book under MARPOL Annex II. Each entry is signed by the officer in charge of the operation. Your cargo log and deck log entries must be consistent with these records.

Typical hazards and lessons from incident reports

These are recurring themes in published tanker incident investigations, not single events:

  • Passing or wrongly set valves. A tank that should be idle rises, or cargo goes to the wrong grade. Lesson: always compare all tank levels, not only the active ones.
  • Overflow during topping off. This usually happens because the rate was not reduced in time, or because two tanks were finishing together. Lesson: plan tank completions so they are staggered, and give the terminal early warning.
  • Pressure surge. Closing a valve quickly against full flow can burst a hose or damage lines. Lesson: open the next tank first, and do not close ship's valves against shore pumping to stop the flow. Use the agreed emergency stop with the terminal (linked ESD systems on gas carriers are designed for this).
  • Toxic exposure. Sour crudes and some products release H2S or benzene, especially at ullage ports and during sampling. Lesson: use closed gauging where fitted, wear a personal gas monitor, and stand upwind.
  • Electrical storms. If lightning is near, the usual industry practice on non-inerted ships handling volatile cargo is to stop the transfer and close tank openings. The exact criteria are in your SMS and terminal rules.
  • Mooring failures. Lines left slack as draught changes can let the ship range and stress the hoses or arms.
  • Fatigue. Long port stays with back-to-back watches are a known factor. Rest hour records must be truthful, and the chief officer must plan accordingly.

Gas carriers add cryogenic and pressure hazards: cold burns, brittle fracture of steel, and rollover or pressure build-up. Monitoring therefore covers tank pressures and temperatures, the emergency shutdown system and boil-off handling, in line with the IGC Code and specialised training.

Worked scenario: an idle tank starts rising

Situation: You are loading into 3P and 3S. On your round, the ullage in 4C, which should be idle, has dropped by a few centimetres. That means its level is rising.

Reasonable actions, in general terms:

  1. Treat it as a line-up error or a passing valve until you have proved otherwise.
  2. Inform the chief officer at once, as your standing orders require.
  3. If you cannot quickly identify and isolate the cause, or there is any risk of overflow, ask the terminal to stop. Use the agreed emergency stop if necessary.
  4. Check the line-up on the mimic and physically at the valves: the drop line, the crossovers and the branch valve to 4C.
  5. Once the cause is isolated, take fresh ullages of all tanks, recalculate the quantities, and update the plan and the loading computer.
  6. Record the event and report it through the SMS near-miss system.

The principle examiners look for is simple: stop first, investigate second. A stopped transfer costs a little time. An overflow costs far more.

Oral exam style questions

Typical questions for OOW and chief mate orals, with the points examiners expect you to cover:

  • "What do you check before accepting the cargo watch?" Cover the operation and plan, line-up, levels and rates, IG status, stress and stability, moorings, the checklist, communications and any abnormalities.
  • "How do you top off a tank safely?" Give early notice to reduce the rate, top off one tank at a time, open the next tank before closing, keep a person at the tank, and maintain communications.
  • "What would make you stop cargo?" Leaks, overflow risk, a loss of communications, an electrical storm (per your procedures), fire, mooring failure, IG failure or high oxygen, and any doubt about the line-up.
  • "Explain the importance of keeping IG pressure positive during discharge." It keeps air out of the tanks so the atmosphere stays non-flammable, and it protects the tank structure from vacuum.
  • "Where are cargo operations recorded?" ORB Part II for oil, the Cargo Record Book for NLS, plus the cargo and deck logs.

Answer from the procedure, then add a short example from your own sea time. Examiners value real experience.

Key points
  • The company SMS, the chief officer's plan and terminal rules always prevail over general guidance.
  • Do not accept the watch until you have verified the line-up, levels, rates, IG status, stress and moorings.
  • Start slowly, confirm cargo is going only to the intended tanks, then build up the rate with agreement.
  • Watch idle tanks as closely as active ones, because a changing idle tank means a line-up error or a passing valve.
  • At topping off, reduce the rate early and open the next tank before closing the full one.
  • When in doubt, stop the transfer first and investigate afterwards.
  • Record operations in ORB Part II (oil) or the Cargo Record Book (NLS), and keep the logs consistent.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for the cargo watch on a tanker?

The chief officer normally plans and supervises cargo operations, and the duty officer keeps the watch to that plan and the standing orders. Duty ratings such as the AB or pumpman assist with rounds and valves as the SMS assigns. The master retains overall responsibility.

How often should ullages be taken during loading?

There is no single international figure. The interval is set by company procedures and the chief officer's orders, and readings are taken more often as tanks approach topping-off. Follow your SMS and record readings at the required frequency.

What oxygen level is required in inerted cargo tanks?

Under the FSS Code, the inert gas system must be able to keep cargo tank oxygen at no more than 8% by volume, at a positive pressure. Many companies set tighter operational limits, so check your procedures.

Can the duty officer stop cargo without calling the chief officer first?

Yes. Any officer on watch should stop the transfer immediately if there is a leak, an overflow risk or another emergency, and then inform the chief officer. Standing orders normally state this clearly, and terminals expect the agreed emergency stop to be used without hesitation.

Is the cargo watch different on chemical and gas tankers?

The principles are the same, but the hazards and rules differ. Chemical tankers follow the IBC Code and MARPOL Annex II, with toxicity and cargo compatibility as major concerns. Gas carriers follow the IGC Code, with close monitoring of pressure, temperature and the emergency shutdown system. Each needs the appropriate STCW tanker training.

Test yourself

Under the FSS Code, what is the maximum oxygen content to be maintained in inerted cargo tanks?

The system must deliver gas at not more than 5% oxygen and maintain the tanks at not more than 8%. Candidates often confuse the two figures (FSS Code, Chapter 15).

During loading you notice oil leaking at the manifold flange. What is your first action?

Stop first, then contain and investigate. Throttling against shore pumps risks a pressure surge (industry guidance such as ISGOTT; agreed ship/shore emergency stop procedure; company SMS).

A tank is approaching its final ullage during loading. Which practice is correct?

Closing against full flow can cause a pressure surge. Opening the next tank first and topping off at a reduced rate is standard practice under the company SMS.

Which record must show cargo and ballast operations on an oil tanker under MARPOL?

Part I covers machinery space operations. The Cargo Record Book is for noxious liquid substances (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 36).

While discharging, the inert gas deck pressure starts falling towards zero. What is the main risk?

Positive IG pressure keeps air out and prevents vacuum. If it is lost, discharge must be stopped or reduced as procedures require (SOLAS Chapter II-2; FSS Code, Chapter 15).
