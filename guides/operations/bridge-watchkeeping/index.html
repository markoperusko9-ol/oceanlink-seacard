Bridge Watchkeeping: A Practical Guide for the Officer of the Watch

Quick answer

Bridge watchkeeping means keeping the ship safe on her passage plan by maintaining a proper lookout, monitoring position and traffic, keeping equipment checked, and calling the master early. The international minimum standards are set by STCW Regulation VIII/2 and Section A-VIII/2 of the STCW Code. Your company SMS and the master's standing orders add detail and always take precedence on board.

Why bridge watchkeeping matters

The officer of the watch (OOW) is the master's representative on the bridge. Until the master formally takes over, the OOW is responsible for the safe navigation of the ship. That responsibility stays with the OOW even when the master is on the bridge.

Most groundings and collisions are not caused by equipment failure. They come from basic lapses: a lookout who was not looking, a fix that was not plotted, a CPA that was not checked, or a master who was not called in time.

The framework you work within:

  • STCW Regulation VIII/2 and Code Section A-VIII/2 set out the principles for keeping a navigational watch: handover, lookout, watch arrangements, and when to call the master.
  • STCW Section A-VIII/1 and MLC 2006 Standard A2.3 cover fitness for duty and hours of rest.
  • COLREG, especially Rule 5 (look-out), Rule 7 (risk of collision) and Rule 19 (restricted visibility).
  • SOLAS Chapter V, including Regulation 19 (carriage of equipment such as BNWAS), Regulation 24 (use of heading/track control) and Regulation 34 (voyage planning).
  • Your company SMS and the master's standing and night orders. These are often stricter than the convention minimums. Where they differ, you follow them.

Fitness for duty and rest hours

You cannot keep a safe watch if you are exhausted. STCW and MLC set minimum rest requirements, and flag administrations write them into national law.

  • Minimum rest: at least 10 hours in any 24-hour period, and at least 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • Splitting rest: the rest may be split into no more than two periods. One period must be at least 6 hours long.
  • Gap between rest periods: the interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.
  • Records: rest hours must be recorded accurately. Port State Control inspectors check these records and may compare them against the logbooks, watch schedules and other records.
  • Alcohol: STCW requires flag administrations to set a limit of not greater than 0.05% blood alcohol, or 0.25 mg/l in breath, for watchkeepers. Most companies apply a stricter or zero-alcohol policy, and that policy is what you follow.
  • Exceptions: limited exceptions exist under flag state rules. The master may also suspend rest requirements in an emergency affecting the safety of the ship, people or cargo, or to help others in distress. Compensatory rest must then be given.

If you are too fatigued to keep a safe watch, tell the master before you take over. Do not wait until the watch has started.

Taking over the watch: step by step

Under STCW, the relieving officer must not take over until they are sure they can do the job. That includes having eyes fully adjusted to the light conditions.

The handover must be deferred if the officer being relieved is in the middle of a manoeuvre or avoiding action. The handover takes place once that action is complete.

A practical sequence:

  1. Arrive early. Arriving 10–15 minutes before the watch is common practice; check your SMS. At night, use this time to let your eyes adjust before you accept the watch.
  2. Read the orders. Read the master's standing orders and night orders, then sign them.
  3. Check the navigation:
    • Position, course, speed and draught.
    • Progress against the passage plan, with the next waypoint and the next course alteration.
    • Any no-go areas, safety contour settings and parallel index lines.
  4. Check the conditions:
    • Tide, current, weather, sea state and visibility, and how they are expected to change.
    • Possible effects of heel, trim, water density and squat on underkeel clearance.
  5. Check the equipment:
    • Operational status of navigation and safety equipment.
    • Gyro and magnetic compass errors.
    • Steering mode.
    • BNWAS status.
    • Machinery state and any manoeuvring restrictions.
  6. Check the traffic. Note all vessels in sight and on radar, their CPA/TCPA, and any you are concerned about. Confirm targets visually, not only on the screen.
  7. Check what else is happening on board. Ask about any work affecting navigation, such as people working aloft or on deck, or engine room maintenance.
  8. Formally accept the watch. Say it clearly, for example: 'I have the watch.' Then log the handover as your SMS requires.

If anything does not make sense to you, ask before you take over.

During the watch: routine checks

Lookout comes first. COLREG Rule 5 requires a proper lookout at all times. This means by sight and hearing, plus all available means such as radar, AIS and VHF.

The lookout must be able to give full attention to that job. Do not give them other tasks that would interfere with it.

Under STCW, the OOW may act as sole lookout only in daylight, and only when both of the following apply:

  • The situation has been carefully assessed, taking account of weather, visibility, traffic density and proximity of dangers.
  • Assistance can be summoned to the bridge immediately when any change in the situation requires it.

In addition, your company SMS and the master's orders may restrict or prohibit this; some companies do not permit a sole lookout at any time.

Typical routine checks (frequency set by your SMS and the passage plan):

Check Minimum guidance
Position fix At intervals suited to the area. Use more than one method, for example a GNSS position cross-checked by radar range and bearing.
Compass error Once a watch, and where possible after any major alteration of course (STCW A-VIII/2).
Manual steering Test at least once a watch when on autopilot (STCW A-VIII/2). Also test before entering areas needing special caution (SOLAS V/24).
Navigation lights Check they are lit and working, by the panel and visually.
Traffic Plot continuously. Take compass bearings of approaching vessels.
Logbook Record events, fixes, weather and alterations as required.

Using bridge equipment safely:

  • Use the radar whenever restricted visibility is encountered or expected. In congested waters, use it at all times.
  • Remember ARPA limitations: vectors lag behind real manoeuvres, and targets can swap.
  • Do not use VHF to agree collision avoidance with another ship. Misidentifying the other vessel has caused real accidents. Apply the COLREGs.

Using the engines and helm:

  • Do not hesitate to use helm, engines or sound signals when needed.
  • Where possible, give the engine room timely notice of intended speed changes.
  • Make sure you know exactly how to take manual control of both helm and engines.

When to call the master

STCW A-VIII/2 lists situations in which the OOW must notify the master immediately. The master's standing orders usually add more, such as a CPA limit or traffic in a TSS. In summary, call the master if:

  • Restricted visibility is encountered or expected.
  • Traffic conditions or the movements of other ships cause concern.
  • You have difficulty maintaining course.
  • You fail to sight land or a navigation mark, or to obtain soundings, by the expected time.
  • Land, a navigation mark or a change in soundings appears unexpectedly.
  • The engines, propulsion remote control, steering gear, or any essential navigation equipment, alarm or indicator breaks down.
  • Radio equipment malfunctions.
  • In heavy weather, you are in any doubt about possible weather damage.
  • The ship meets a hazard to navigation, such as ice or a derelict.
  • Any other emergency arises, or you are in any doubt.

Calling the master does not end your responsibility. Keep taking whatever immediate action safety requires while the master is on the way. Then give a clear, short report: situation, action taken, and what you need.

No competent master will criticise you for an early call. Late calls are what end careers.

Pilot on board and anchor watch

With a pilot on board:

The pilot's presence does not relieve the master or the OOW of their duties and obligations for the safety of the ship.

  • Exchange information with the pilot early, using a master–pilot information exchange.
  • Keep fixing the position and monitoring the pilot's orders against the passage plan.
  • Confirm that helm and engine orders are carried out correctly.
  • If you are in any doubt about the pilot's actions or intentions, ask the pilot for clarification. If the doubt remains, inform the master immediately and take any necessary action before the master arrives.

At anchor:

An anchor watch is still a navigational watch. STCW expects the OOW to:

  • Fix and plot the ship's position on arrival, then check frequently that the ship is not dragging. Use fixed marks or radar ranges.
  • Keep a proper lookout and make inspection rounds of the ship periodically.
  • Observe weather, tide and sea state.
  • Notify the master and take action if the ship drags or visibility deteriorates.
  • Keep the engines and machinery at the readiness the master has ordered.
  • Show the correct lights and shapes, and make sound signals where required.
  • Take measures to protect the environment from pollution by the ship.

Typical hazards and real lessons

Scenario 1: ECDIS over-reliance. A ship grounds on a charted shoal that sat inside the planned track's safety margin.

  • The safety contour had been set too shallow, and the audible alarm had been turned off.
  • Lesson: check the ECDIS safety settings at handover, keep alarms enabled, and cross-check the position by radar.

Scenario 2: Distraction at night. The OOW is doing paperwork in the chartroom with no lookout posted. A fishing vessel is not seen until it is very close.

  • Lesson: paperwork never outranks the lookout. Post a lookout at night as STCW and your SMS require.

Scenario 3: 'He'll pass clear.' A crossing vessel is tracked only on ARPA. Its CPA slowly closes from 1.5 nm to 0.3 nm, but no compass bearings were taken.

  • Lesson: under COLREG Rule 7, a steady bearing with decreasing range means risk of collision. Act early and substantially, as Rule 8 requires, and call the master as your orders require.

Scenario 4: Late call. Visibility drops gradually and the OOW waits 'to see if it clears'. By the time the master arrives, the ship is in dense fog in heavy traffic, at full sea speed, without fog signals.

  • Lesson: call the master when restricted visibility is expected. Do not wait until you are already in it.

Common threads: fatigue, an unclear handover, too much trust in a single sensor, and hesitation to call. Bridge Resource Management training exists to break these chains.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners test understanding, not recitation. Practise answering these out loud.

  • 'You are taking over and the 2nd Officer is altering for a crossing vessel. What do you do?'
    • Do not take over.
    • Wait until the avoiding action is complete and the situation is clear, then complete the handover.
  • 'What do you check before accepting the watch?'
    • Orders, position and progress, conditions, equipment and compass errors, and traffic.
    • Work being done on board, and whether your night vision has adjusted.
  • 'When may you be sole lookout?'
    • Daylight only, after careful assessment, with assistance immediately available, and only if your company and master permit.
  • 'The master is on the bridge. Who has the con?'
    • The OOW does, until the master clearly states that they are taking over. That transfer should be stated and logged.
  • 'List five situations when you would call the master.'
    • Answer from the STCW list, and add your own standing orders.
  • 'How do you know your gyro is right?'
    • By an azimuth or amplitude, a transit, or comparison against other references.
    • Check once a watch and after major course alterations where possible.
  • 'Your autopilot has been on for six hours. What should you have done?'
    • Tested manual steering at least once each watch.

Always finish your answer with: 'and I would follow the company SMS and the master's standing orders.'

Key points
  • The OOW stays responsible for safe navigation until the master clearly takes over, even when the master or a pilot is on the bridge.
  • Never take over the watch during a manoeuvre or avoiding action; defer the handover until it is complete.
  • Minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days, in no more than two periods with one of at least 6 hours.
  • Check compass error once a watch, and test manual steering at least once a watch when on autopilot.
  • The OOW may be sole lookout only in daylight, after careful assessment, with help immediately available, and only if the SMS allows it.
  • Call the master early, and always when in any doubt; calling does not stop you taking immediate action.
  • The company SMS and the master's orders prevail, and are often stricter than STCW minimums.

Frequently asked questions

What are the duties of the officer of the watch on the bridge?

The OOW keeps a proper lookout, navigates the ship safely along the passage plan, and monitors traffic and applies the COLREGs. The OOW also checks navigation equipment and compass errors, keeps the logbook, and calls the master when required. The detailed duties come from STCW Section A-VIII/2, your company SMS and the master's standing orders.

Can the OOW be the sole lookout at night?

Under STCW, the OOW may act as sole lookout only in daylight, and only after careful assessment of weather, visibility, traffic and nearby dangers, with assistance immediately available. At night, a separate lookout is expected. Your company SMS and the master's orders may restrict this further, and some companies do not permit a sole lookout at any time.

When should the OOW call the master?

Call the master when restricted visibility is encountered or expected, when traffic causes concern, or when equipment, steering or engines fail. Also call when expected land or soundings are not obtained, or unexpected ones appear. Heavy weather concerns, hazards such as ice, any emergency, and any doubt at all are further reasons to call. The master's standing orders usually add specific triggers such as minimum CPA.

How many hours of rest must a watchkeeper have?

STCW and MLC 2006 require at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. Rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, and the gap between rest periods must not exceed 14 hours. Flag states apply these through national law, and some permit limited exceptions.

Who is responsible for navigation when a pilot is on board?

The pilot advises and usually gives the con orders, but the presence of a pilot does not relieve the master or OOW of their duties for the safety of the ship. The OOW keeps fixing the position and monitors the pilot's orders. If in doubt, the OOW asks the pilot for clarification and calls the master.

Test yourself

Under STCW, what is the minimum rest a watchkeeper must have in any 24-hour period?

The minimum is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1, mirrored in MLC 2006 Standard A2.3.

You arrive to take over and the officer on watch is altering course to avoid a crossing vessel. What should happen?

The watch must not be handed over while the relieved officer is engaged in a manoeuvre or avoiding action; the handover is deferred until it is complete. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

How often should manual steering be tested when the ship is on autopilot?

Manual steering should be tested at least once a watch when the automatic pilot is in use. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2; see also SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 24.

With a pilot conning the ship, the OOW doubts one of the pilot's intended actions. What is the correct response?

The pilot's presence does not relieve the master or OOW of their duties; the OOW must seek clarification and, if still in doubt, notify the master and take any necessary action. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

Under STCW, in which conditions may the OOW act as sole lookout?

Sole lookout by the OOW is permitted only in daylight after careful assessment of conditions and with assistance immediately available; equipment does not replace a lookout. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2 and COLREG Rule 5.
