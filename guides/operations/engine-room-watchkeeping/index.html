Engine Room Watchkeeping: Taking Over, Keeping and Handing Over a Safe Watch

Quick answer

An engineering watch means keeping propulsion, power and auxiliary machinery running safely, answering the bridge promptly and spotting trouble before it becomes a breakdown, fire or pollution incident. The principles come from STCW Section A-VIII/2. Your Chief Engineer's standing orders and the company SMS set the detailed procedure on your ship.

What an engineering watch is and why it matters

The engineering watch is the engine room's version of the bridge watch. The officer in charge of the engineering watch (OICEW) is the Chief Engineer's representative while on duty. You are responsible for the safe and efficient operation and upkeep of machinery that affects the safety of the ship. On many ships the watch is kept with a rating forming part of an engineering watch, typically a motorman or oiler.

The framework comes from STCW:

  • Regulation VIII/2 and Section A-VIII/2 set out watchkeeping arrangements and the principles for keeping an engineering watch.
  • Section A-VIII/1 covers fitness for duty and minimum hours of rest.
  • Section A-III/1 lists the competences an OICEW must show, including keeping a safe engineering watch.

STCW gives principles, not a ship-specific checklist. Your Chief Engineer decides the watch composition, taking account of:

  • the type of ship and machinery
  • whether the machinery space is manned or periodically unattended (UMS)
  • the weather, the area and the operation in progress

The Chief Engineer's standing orders, night orders and the company SMS always prevail over anything general, including this guide.

Most serious engine room fires, blackouts and pollution incidents are caught, or missed, by the watch. A blackout in a traffic separation scheme, or a fuel spray onto a hot exhaust, is a watchkeeping matter long before it becomes an emergency.

Taking over the watch: step by step

Arrive early enough to do a proper round before you accept the watch. STCW expects the relieving officer to be satisfied that the watch is being properly kept and that they are fit to take it over. A typical takeover:

  1. Read the orders. Check the Chief Engineer's standing and night orders, and any special instructions about the vessel's operation.
  2. Get the verbal handover. Ask the outgoing engineer about:
    • the condition and operating mode of main and auxiliary machinery, including the power distribution set-up
    • maintenance or repair work in progress, and who is working where (including any shore or repair personnel)
    • isolations, lock-out tag-out points and active permits
  3. Check the control console. Look at the alarm list, any inhibited or blocked alarms, and equipment running on manual or local control.
  4. Do a physical round. Cover the main engine, generators, purifiers, boilers, compressors and steering gear. Look, listen, feel (carefully) and smell.
  5. Check the levels. These include:
    • bilges and bilge wells
    • sludge, bilge holding and slop tanks
    • fuel settling and service tanks, and their temperatures
    • fresh water and sewage tanks
    • ballast, if handled from the engine room
  6. Check fire safety. Confirm the fire detection panel status, fixed systems and portable extinguishers, and that no fire doors or quick-closing valves are left in an abnormal state.
  7. Confirm bunkering, transfers and sludge or bilge operations. Ask whether any are planned or in progress, and the Oil Record Book position.
  8. Accept formally. Do not take over if you are unsure, unfit or have unresolved doubts. Ask, then accept.

If an important operation is under way at changeover, the outgoing engineer normally completes it unless the Chief Engineer orders otherwise. Examples are a generator changeover, a fuel changeover before an emission control area, or a purifier start. Never take over halfway through a job you did not set up.

During the watch: rounds, records and checks

The frequency of rounds and the exact log format are set by the company and Chief Engineer. Typical content of a round:

Area What you are looking for
Main engine Temperatures and pressures within limits, leaks (fuel, lube, cooling water), abnormal noise or vibration, scavenge and crankcase indications
Generators Load sharing, exhaust temperatures, lube oil pressure, leaks, alarms
Fuel system Settling and service tank levels and temperatures, viscosity control, filter differential pressures, leak-off tanks
Purifiers Normal discharge, no overflow, correct temperatures
Boiler / economiser Water level, steam pressure, burner operation, soot-blowing per routine
Steering gear Oil level, no leaks, motors and pumps normal, communication available
Compressors / air Starting air receiver pressures, drains, auto start/stop working
Bilges and tanks Bilge levels and trends, sludge and bilge water tank levels
General Housekeeping, oily rags, hot surfaces lagged, guards fitted, fire hazards

Records:

  • Engine room log book. Record running parameters, machinery changes, alarms and significant events, plus bridge orders for speed or direction changes.
  • Oil Record Book Part I. Any operation listed under MARPOL Annex I goes here. It is recorded without delay as required by MARPOL Annex I Regulation 17, signed by the officer in charge of the operation, with each completed page signed by the Master. Typical examples are bilge water discharge through the OWS, sludge transfers and bunkering.
  • Trends matter. A temperature creeping up over three rounds is information. A single number inside limits is not proof that all is well.

You must not be given duties that stop you supervising the main propulsion and auxiliaries. Maintenance during a watch is acceptable only if you can still respond immediately.

Working with the bridge

The engine room and bridge must operate as one team. In practice:

  • Execute bridge orders promptly. Changes of speed or direction are carried out without delay and recorded.
  • Tell the bridge early. Report anything that may limit propulsion, power or steering, such as:
    • planned or unplanned slowdowns
    • a generator trip
    • a fuel changeover affecting manoeuvrability
    • steering gear problems
  • Agree before you act. Agree with the OOW before any planned action affecting speed or power, for example blowing tubes, changing over generators in congested waters, or stopping for repairs.
  • Check readiness. Make sure machinery is ready for manoeuvring or reduced speed when the bridge gives notice. Typical preparations, per company checklist, are standby generators, air compressors on, and the steering gear second pump.
  • Before arrival, pilotage and narrow waters: expect stand-by to be rung. Have enough generators on the board and keep the engine control room manned.

Worked scenario: at 0300 a fuel filter differential pressure alarm on the main engine comes up. The ship is in coastal traffic.

  1. Inform the bridge that you may need to reduce power briefly.
  2. Change over to the standby filter if fitted.
  3. Monitor fuel pressure and the engine.
  4. Log the event.
  5. Call the Chief if the cause is not obvious or the alarm repeats.

You do not surprise the bridge with a slowdown.

When to call the Chief Engineer

Standing orders list the specific triggers. STCW principles and good practice say call the Chief Engineer without hesitation:

  • when machinery damage or malfunction occurs that may endanger the safe operation of the ship
  • when any malfunction may lead to damage or breakdown of the propulsion plant, auxiliaries, or monitoring and governing systems
  • in any emergency, or whenever you are in doubt

Typical examples:

  • an oil mist detector alarm
  • a scavenge fire indication
  • a blackout
  • a significant fuel or lube leak
  • an unexplained rise in bilge level
  • a steering gear fault
  • any fire alarm

Call early. Do not wait while trying to solve it first. A good Chief Engineer would rather be woken for a genuine doubt than called too late.

While you wait for the Chief, take immediate action to protect the ship and the machinery. Raise the engineers' alarm or fire alarm if needed, and keep the bridge informed.

Periodically unattended machinery spaces (UMS)

Ships with suitable automation and alarm systems may operate the engine room unattended for periods. The SOLAS Chapter II-1 requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces apply, together with flag and classification society approval. Watchkeeping does not stop on a UMS ship. It changes form.

  • Duty engineer. One engineer is on duty, and alarms extend to their cabin and to public spaces as approved.
  • Daytime manning. The engine room is normally manned by day. Before it is left unattended, the duty engineer does a final round to a company checklist. Typical items:
    • tanks topped up
    • purifiers stable
    • no leaks
    • bilges dry
    • fire hazards removed
    • alarm system and its extension to the duty engineer tested or confirmed
    • bridge informed that the engine room is going unmanned
  • Answering alarms. You go down promptly and inform the bridge. Many companies use a dead-man or lone-worker alarm when an engineer enters the space alone at night.
  • Reverting to manned. The Chief Engineer, or company procedure, will require manned watchkeeping in certain conditions. Common examples are restricted visibility, heavy traffic, pilotage, manoeuvring, heavy weather, or defects in the alarm system.

The exact UMS routines, final round time and alarm group settings differ by company and ship. Learn your ship's version during familiarisation.

Typical hazards and lessons from real incidents

Recurring themes in casualty investigations:

  • Fuel or lube spray onto hot surfaces. This is one of the most common causes of engine room fires. Examples are a failed pipe fitting, an unlagged exhaust section or a loose filter cover. Report and fix missing lagging and leaking fittings at once.
  • Crankcase explosions. After an oil mist alarm, act as the maker's instructions and standing orders direct (typically reduce speed or stop, as agreed with the bridge). Keep people clear of crankcase doors and relief valves. Do not open the crankcase until the maker's cooling time has passed. Fresh air into a hot crankcase can trigger an explosion.
  • Blackout through poor power management. Common causes are starting a large consumer without enough generator capacity, or a changeover gone wrong. Know your preferential trips and the blackout recovery sequence.
  • Lone working injuries. Rotating machinery, hot surfaces and falls happen with nobody watching. Tell someone where you are going, and use the dead-man alarm where fitted.
  • Pollution through the wrong valve. Bilge or sludge going overboard illegally often starts with a misaligned line. Check line-ups, and record every operation honestly in the Oil Record Book. The consequences of falsification fall on individuals, not only the company.
  • Fatigue. Under STCW Section A-VIII/1 (which applies to watchkeepers), and consistent with the minimum rest hours option in MLC 2006 Standard A2.3, the minimum is 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. A tired engineer misses trends. Record hours truthfully.
  • Heat and noise. Wear hearing protection and drink water. Heat stress affects judgement before you notice it.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Practise answering these aloud, in a logical order: safety first, then inform, then act, then record.

  • What do you check before taking over an engineering watch? Orders, machinery status, work in progress and isolations, tank and bilge levels, fire safety status, alarms and inhibits, planned operations.
  • Can you refuse to take over the watch? Yes. You should not take over until you are satisfied with the state of the watch and the handover, and you are fit yourself. If there are unresolved doubts or problems, do not accept the watch; inform the Chief Engineer. Equally, the outgoing engineer must not hand over to a relief who appears unfit.
  • You get an oil mist detector high alarm at sea. Actions? Inform the bridge and slow down or stop per standing orders and maker's guidance. Call the Chief Engineer. Keep people away from the engine. Do not open the crankcase until the maker's cooling period has passed. Investigate the cause before restarting.
  • Blackout during your watch. Actions? Inform the bridge and call the Chief Engineer (engineers' alarm). Confirm the emergency generator has started and is supplying the emergency board. Identify the cause. Restore main power and essential services in the correct sequence. Restart the main engine when ready. Log everything.
  • What goes in the Oil Record Book Part I? The machinery space operations listed in MARPOL Annex I. Examples are bilge water discharge and transfers, sludge disposal, bunkering and fuel/oil residue handling, and failures of oil filtering equipment.
  • When does a UMS ship revert to manned watchkeeping? When the Chief or company procedures require it. Typical cases are manoeuvring, restricted visibility, heavy traffic, alarm system defects or adverse conditions.
Key points
  • STCW Section A-VIII/2 sets the principles. Chief Engineer's orders and the company SMS set the detail on your ship.
  • Never accept a watch until you have read the orders, done a round and resolved any doubts.
  • An important operation in progress at changeover is normally completed by the outgoing engineer unless the Chief Engineer orders otherwise.
  • Execute bridge orders promptly, log them, and warn the bridge early of anything that may limit power or steering.
  • Call the Chief Engineer early in any emergency, malfunction or doubt, and take immediate protective action meanwhile.
  • UMS still means a watch: final round to checklist, alarms confirmed, bridge informed, prompt response to alarms.
  • Fuel spray on hot surfaces, crankcase explosions, blackouts and wrong line-ups are the classic watchkeeping failures.

Frequently asked questions

Who decides the composition of the engine room watch?

The Chief Engineer, in consultation with the Master. They take account of the ship type, machinery, UMS status, weather, area and operations. The company SMS and any flag state safe manning requirements must also be met.

Can I do maintenance work during my engine room watch?

Yes, but only if it does not stop you supervising the propulsion plant and auxiliaries and responding immediately to alarms or bridge orders. Major jobs needing your full attention should be planned outside your watch or covered by another engineer.

What is the difference between a manned and UMS engine room?

A manned engine room has an engineer on watch in the space or control room at all times. A UMS ship, approved by flag and class, may leave the space unattended for periods, with alarms routed to a duty engineer who must respond. The duty engineer still carries the watch responsibility.

What rest hours apply to engine room watchkeepers?

Under STCW Section A-VIII/1 (which applies to watchkeepers), and consistent with the minimum rest hours option in MLC 2006 Standard A2.3, the minimum is 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The rest may be split into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours. Flag states may allow limited exceptions, so check your flag's rules and record hours truthfully.

Do I need to inform the bridge before changing over generators?

Good practice and most company procedures say yes, especially in congested or restricted waters. A failed changeover can cause a blackout. Agree the timing with the OOW and follow your ship's power management procedure.

Test yourself

At watch changeover, a generator changeover is halfway through. Who normally completes it?

STCW watchkeeping principles provide that an important operation under way at handover is concluded by the officer being relieved unless the Chief Engineer directs otherwise. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

The bridge orders a change of main engine speed during your watch. What is the correct response?

Bridge orders for speed or direction changes are to be executed promptly and recorded. Concerns are raised with the bridge and the Chief, but the order is not delayed. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.

What are the minimum rest hours for watchkeepers under STCW and MLC 2006?

The minimum is 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days, subject to limited flag state exceptions. STCW requires the rest-hour regime for watchkeepers; MLC 2006 also permits flag states to use maximum hours of work instead for other seafarers. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3.

The main engine oil mist detector gives a high alarm. Which action is correct?

Opening a hot crankcase admits air and can cause an explosion. Follow maker's instructions and standing orders, and inform the bridge and Chief. Ref: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2 (calling the Chief Engineer); maker's instructions.

Before a machinery space is left unattended on a UMS ship, what should the duty engineer do?

UMS operation depends on a working alarm system and a prepared plant. Company procedures require a final round, alarm checks and informing the bridge. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part E; company SMS.
