Squat and Shallow-Water Effect: What Every Watchkeeper Must Know

Quick answer

Squat is the bodily sinkage and change of trim a ship experiences when underway. It becomes significant in shallow or confined water and increases roughly with the square of speed, so slowing down is the most effective way to control it. Include squat when you plan under-keel clearance, follow your company UKC policy and port requirements, and expect sluggish handling whenever depth is close to your draught.

What squat actually is and why it matters

When a ship moves, the water ahead is pushed aside and has to flow back past the hull towards the stern. Between the hull and the seabed (and the banks, in a channel) that return flow speeds up. Faster flow means lower pressure, so the ship sinks bodily and usually trims as well. That combined sinkage and trim is squat.

In deep open water the effect is small and nobody notices. In shallow water, and especially in a dredged channel or canal, the return flow is squeezed. Squat can then reach the order of metres on a large, full-form ship at speed. If your passage plan assumed the static draught and ignored squat, the under-keel clearance (UKC) you planned does not exist.

Squat has been a recognised factor in groundings of deep-draught ships. One well-known case is the 1992 grounding of a large passenger liner off the US east coast. Investigators found that the ship's speed in shallow water and underestimated squat contributed to the hull reaching a shoal. The lesson examiners keep coming back to is simple: speed in shallow water costs depth.

The factors that drive squat

You need to be able to list these quickly in an oral exam and, more importantly, recognise them on the bridge:

  • Speed through the water – the dominant factor. Squat varies approximately with the square of speed. Halve your speed and squat drops to roughly a quarter.
  • Block coefficient (Cb) – full-form ships (tankers, bulk carriers) squat more than fine-form ships at the same speed.
  • Depth/draught ratio – the smaller the clearance relative to draught, the greater the effect.
  • Blockage factor – the ratio of the ship's immersed midship cross-section to the cross-section of the channel. A narrow, shallow channel gives a higher blockage factor and more squat than open shallow water.
  • Static trim – on a ship that is already trimmed, squat may add to the deeper end.
  • Passing or overtaking another ship – interaction in a channel can increase squat on both vessels while they are abeam.
  • Heel – not squat as such, but heel from wind or a turn increases the effective draught at the bilge and must be considered alongside it.

Which end goes down? Industry training uses a general guide from Barrass's work. From even keel, a ship with a high Cb (roughly above 0.7, e.g. tankers and bulk carriers) tends to squat by the head. A fine-form ship (roughly below 0.7, e.g. container ships) tends to squat by the stern. Static trim modifies this, so treat it as a tendency, not a guarantee.

Estimating squat: the working formulas and a worked example

The most commonly taught quick estimates are Barrass's approximations for maximum squat. Speed is in knots through the water and the result is in metres:

Condition Approximate maximum squat
Open shallow water (unrestricted width) Cb × V² ÷ 100
Confined channel or canal 2 × Cb × V² ÷ 100

These are empirical approximations, valid only within certain limits of depth/draught ratio and blockage. They are good for bridge planning and exams. Ports, pilots and some companies use more refined methods or dynamic UKC systems. Your company SMS and port guidance decide which method you actually use.

Worked scenario – laden bulk carrier, Cb 0.82, approaching a dredged channel

  1. Planned speed in channel 10 knots: confined squat ≈ 2 × 0.82 × 100 ÷ 100 = 1.64 m.
  2. Same ship at 7 knots: 2 × 0.82 × 49 ÷ 100 = 0.80 m.
  3. Reducing speed by 3 knots has roughly halved the squat and recovered over 0.8 m of UKC.

Worked scenario – container ship, Cb 0.60, channel at 16 knots

  • 2 × 0.60 × 256 ÷ 100 ≈ 3.07 m, mostly at the stern. A fine-form ship is not immune when speed is high.

Then build the UKC check:

  • Start with charted depth plus height of tide.
  • Subtract the static draught at the deepest point.
  • Subtract squat.
  • Subtract any allowances your company UKC policy requires, such as heel, wave response, density and chart/survey uncertainty.

Compare the result against the minimum UKC set by the company or port. If it fails, change speed, tide window or draught – not the assumptions.

Other shallow-water effects on handling

Squat is only one part of the picture. As depth reduces towards your draught, expect:

  • Sluggish steering and a larger turning circle – the ship needs more room and more rudder to turn.
  • Loss of speed and drop in rpm for the same power, with increased vibration as the propeller works harder.
  • Larger stern and bow waves – visible changes in the wave pattern and wash are an early cue that you are in shallow water.
  • Changed stopping performance – do not assume deep-water manoeuvring data applies.
  • Echo sounder readings falling as squat develops, even where charted depth is constant.
  • Bank effect in channels – the stern is drawn towards the nearer bank and the pressure cushion pushes the bow away. The result is a sheer away from the bank that can be violent.
  • Ship-to-ship interaction – when passing or overtaking closely, ships can be drawn together or sheer unexpectedly. This is worst when depth is limited.

The ship's manoeuvring information (wheelhouse poster, pilot card, manoeuvring booklet) usually shows turning and stopping data, and may include shallow-water data. Know where it is before the pilot boards.

Step-by-step: managing squat on passage

This is a general sequence. Your company SMS, master's standing orders and local port rules always take precedence.

At the planning stage 1. Identify every shallow area, channel, bar and canal on the route. 2. Obtain the latest charts and corrections, port UKC rules and any local squat guidance. 3. Calculate static draughts for arrival, including fresh-water allowance and any expected trim. 4. Estimate squat at the intended speed for each area using the company-approved method. 5. Apply tides and the company's UKC allowances, and identify the tidal window and maximum speed for each leg. 6. Mark no-go areas, abort points and speed-limit legs on the chart or ECDIS, and set the safety contour and safety depth appropriately.

Before entering - Brief the bridge team: maximum speed, minimum UKC, abort points, expected handling. - Confirm the echo sounder is working with its alarm set, engine and steering are on standby, and anchors are ready if required. - Exchange information with the pilot, including draught and squat figures.

In the area - Monitor speed through the water, not just over the ground. - Watch the echo sounder trend, rudder response and wake. - Reduce speed early before bends, passing or overtaking. - Avoid large rudder angles at speed, which induce heel. - If UKC falls below plan, slow down and inform the master immediately.

Checklist and common traps

Quick bridge checklist - Static draught fore, aft and at the deepest point known? - Squat estimated for channel and open shallow water? - Tide height and timing applied to the right reference? - Company or port minimum UKC met with margin? - Maximum speed for each leg marked and briefed? - Echo sounder alarm and ECDIS safety settings set? - Manoeuvring data reviewed?

Traps seen again and again - Using speed over ground with a following current, then forgetting the ship is moving faster through the water. - Calculating squat for open water when the ship will actually be in a confined channel (that factor of two). - Planning a passing manoeuvre in a narrow channel without allowing for interaction squat. - Assuming a container ship 'doesn't squat' because it is fine-lined. - Trusting chart datum in an area that silts or has old surveys.

Oral exam: questions you should be ready for

Examiners like squat because it tests whether you understand the physics and can apply it. Expect:

  • Define squat. Reduction in UKC caused by bodily sinkage and trim. These result from increased flow velocity and lower pressure around a moving hull in restricted water.
  • Main factor? Speed – squat varies roughly with speed squared.
  • Give the formulas. Cb × V² ÷ 100 in open water; double it in a confined channel.
  • Head or stern? From even keel, a full-form, high-Cb ship tends to squat by the head; a fine-form, low-Cb ship tends to squat by the stern.
  • Signs you are in shallow water? Sluggish steering, speed and rpm drop, vibration, changed wave pattern, falling echo sounder readings.
  • Explain bank effect and how you counter it. Reduce speed, keep away from the bank where possible, and apply rudder towards the bank to counter the sheer, as the pilot or conditions require.
  • What does COLREG say? Rule 6 lists the draught in relation to the available depth as a factor in safe speed. Rule 3(h) defines a vessel constrained by her draught, and Rule 28 covers the lights and shapes such a vessel may show.

If you cannot give the head-or-stern answer with the reasoning behind it, the examiner will probably keep you there until you can.

Key points
  • Squat is sinkage plus trim caused by faster return flow and lower pressure around a moving hull in shallow water.
  • Squat varies roughly with speed squared: slowing down is the most effective control.
  • Barrass approximation: Cb × V² ÷ 100 in open shallow water, doubled in a confined channel.
  • From even keel, high-Cb ships tend to squat by the head and low-Cb ships by the stern.
  • Shallow water also brings sluggish steering, bigger turning circles, speed loss, bank effect and interaction.
  • Always include squat in the UKC calculation and follow the company UKC policy and port rules.
  • Use speed through the water, not over the ground, when estimating squat.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate ship squat quickly?

The common bridge estimate is Barrass's approximation. Maximum squat in metres equals Cb × V² ÷ 100 in open shallow water, and twice that in a confined channel, with V in knots through the water. It is an empirical guide; your company or port may require a different or more detailed method.

Does a ship squat by the head or by the stern?

As a general tendency from even keel, full-form ships with a high block coefficient squat by the head. Fine-form ships with a low block coefficient squat by the stern. Static trim and hull shape change this, so check the deeper end in your own calculation.

At what depth does shallow-water effect start?

The effects increase progressively as the depth approaches the draught rather than switching on at one figure. Different references quote different depth/draught ratios. Rely on your company guidance and the ship's manoeuvring data, and watch for the physical signs on the bridge.

What is the minimum under-keel clearance a ship must keep?

There is no single international figure. Minimum UKC is set by the company's UKC policy in the SMS and by port, canal or pilotage authorities, and these vary widely. Your plan must meet whichever requirement is stricter.

Why does a ship sheer away from a bank in a channel?

Pressure builds up between the bow and the near bank, pushing the bow away. At the same time, the accelerated flow near the stern lowers pressure and draws the stern towards the bank. The result is a sheer away from the bank, worse at higher speed and in shallower water.

Test yourself

A ship reduces speed from 12 knots to 6 knots in shallow water. Approximately what happens to squat?

Squat varies approximately with speed squared, so halving speed reduces it to about a quarter. Basis: Barrass's empirical squat relationship (squat ∝ V²); knowledge of squat and shallow-water effects is required under STCW Code Table A-II/2 (and A-II/1).

Using Barrass's approximation, what is the maximum squat for a ship with Cb 0.8 at 10 knots in a confined channel?

Confined channel: 2 × 0.8 × 100 ÷ 100 = 1.6 m. The 0.8 m answer is the open-water figure, a common mistake.

Full-form ships with a high Cb tend to trim by the head when squatting; fine-form ships tend to trim by the stern.

Which speed should be used when estimating squat?

Squat depends on flow around the hull, so speed through the water is what matters; a following current can hide a high water speed. Squat estimates such as Barrass's approximation use speed through the water; include them in the voyage plan required by SOLAS V/34.

Which COLREG rule lists draught in relation to available depth as a factor in determining safe speed?

Rule 6 lists the draught in relation to the available depth among the factors for safe speed. Rule 28 covers lights and shapes for a vessel constrained by her draught.
