Working on a Passenger Ferry

Quick answer

Passenger ferries carry people, and usually vehicles, on fixed short or overnight routes, with frequent port calls, tight schedules and responsibility for hundreds or thousands of passengers. Beyond your normal CoC and basic STCW courses, you need the passenger ship training in STCW Regulation V/2, and ro-ro passenger ships add passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training. Operators favour candidates with ro-ro experience, good English and a calm, customer-facing manner.

What a passenger ferry does and where it trades

Under SOLAS, a passenger ship is any ship carrying more than twelve passengers. In everyday use, a "ferry" is a passenger ship running a fixed, scheduled route. Most ferries fall into one of these groups:

  • Ro-ro passenger ferries (ro-pax): the most common type in international trade. Cars, coaches and freight trailers drive on and off through bow, stern or side doors and ramps.
  • Passenger-only ferries: often fast craft on short harbour or island routes.
  • Overnight and cruise-ferries: longer crossings with cabins, restaurants and shops. Hotel staff can outnumber the marine crew.
  • Domestic and inter-island ferries: a large sector in the Philippines, Indonesia, Greece, Scandinavia and elsewhere. These are regulated mainly by the national administration rather than directly by the international conventions.

The trade is repetitive by design. You run the same route, the same berths and often the same pilotage waters, sometimes several round trips a day. Some ports let regular ferry masters and officers hold a pilotage exemption. Whether this is available, and on what conditions, is set locally by the port or pilotage authority.

Layout, operations and the key hazards

A typical ro-pax has these spaces from the bottom up:

  • Machinery spaces and tanks at the bottom.
  • One or more long, open vehicle decks, sometimes with hoistable car decks.
  • Accommodation and public spaces above the vehicle decks.
  • The bridge on top.

Because the vehicle deck runs most of the ship's length with few transverse bulkheads, a small amount of water on it can destroy stability through free surface effect.

Two disasters still shape the rules today:

  • Herald of Free Enterprise (1987): capsized after leaving Zeebrugge with her bow doors open. The inquiry fed directly into what became the ISM Code.
  • Estonia (1994): lost after her bow visor failed. This led to tougher ro-ro damage stability standards, including the regional Stockholm Agreement in north-west Europe, and to the passenger ship training now in STCW.

The main hazards on the job are:

  • Hull openings: bow, stern and shell doors, ramps and watertight doors. The bridge has indicators and monitoring. Never sail on an assumption that a door is closed.
  • Fire on the vehicle deck: fuel and electrical faults in vehicles, reefer units, and dangerous goods carried under the IMDG Code. Electric vehicles are a growing concern, and companies are adding procedures for them.
  • Cargo securing and shift in heavy weather, especially freight trailers.
  • Vehicle deck traffic: crew working among moving cars and trucks, with exhaust fumes and poor visibility.
  • Mooring: many berthings a day mean many chances for a snap-back injury.
  • Watertight doors: these power-operated doors can crush anyone caught in them.
  • Fatigue: short crossings break watches into fragments, and port time is not rest time.
  • Passenger behaviour: intoxication, medical emergencies, people in restricted areas, and panic during an incident.

Certificates and experience you will be expected to hold

Everyone needs the usual baseline:

  • STCW basic safety training.
  • Security training appropriate to your duties.
  • A valid medical certificate.
  • Your CoC or rating certificate with any flag endorsement.

On top of that, STCW Regulation V/2 and Section A-V/2 add passenger ship training:

Training Who needs it
Passenger ship emergency familiarisation All personnel, before being assigned shipboard duties
Safety training for personnel giving direct service to passengers Crew working in passenger spaces
Crowd management Those designated on the muster list to assist passengers in an emergency
Crisis management and human behaviour Masters, chief mates, chief engineers, second engineers and anyone with responsibility for passenger safety in emergencies
Passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity On ro-ro passenger ships: masters, chief mates, chief engineers, second engineers and those directly responsible for embarking passengers, cargo handling and securing, or closing hull openings

For the crowd management, crisis management and hull integrity courses, STCW requires refresher training, or evidence of maintaining the standard, at intervals of no more than five years. How your administration delivers and certifies these courses varies. Some issue separate certificates, and some accept company-delivered training for parts of the requirement. Check with your flag administration and a training institute approved by your certificate-issuing administration before you pay for anything.

Experience that helps:

  • Ro-ro or car carrier time.
  • Tidal and confined-water ship handling.
  • Any customer-facing work.

For domestic ferries, national rules may add or substitute requirements.

Crew composition and who does what

Larger ferries add a substantial hotel department alongside deck and engine, and it can be bigger than the marine crew.

  • Deck: master, chief officer, navigating officers, bosun, ABs and OSs. Deck officers often have cargo deck supervision as a core duty: loading plans, lashing, door closing and the stability check before departure. Some operators also employ a dedicated safety or staff officer.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second engineer and other engineers, an ETO on larger ships, and engine ratings. Expect heavy manoeuvring loads, thrusters and frequent mode changes. On larger modern ships there are also safe-return-to-port systems.
  • Hotel and catering: purser or hotel manager, reception, catering, cabin and shop staff. They are not "just hotel crew". Every one of them has a muster list duty, usually guiding and controlling passengers.

Crew sizes depend on the safe manning document, the route and the hotel services, so they vary widely. On many European routes, two complete crews alternate on and off the ship. Elsewhere, long contracts as on cargo ships are normal. Rotation patterns are company-specific.

Life on board

The rhythm is very different from deep-sea work.

What tends to suit people:

  • You are rarely far from land, and communications are usually good.
  • On rotation systems, time at home is predictable.

What can wear you down:

  • Repetition: the same berths every day. Complacency is the real enemy, and the Herald capsize is the textbook example.
  • Drills: frequent and taken seriously. Under SOLAS, abandon ship and fire drills on passenger ships are held weekly, with each crew member attending at least monthly. Port state inspectors and flag surveyors watch ferry drills closely.
  • Passengers: you will deal with complaints, seasickness, lost children and people who should not be near the car deck. Patience and clear English, plus the local language on some routes, matter.
  • Scrutiny: high-profile ships attract inspections, audits and public attention when anything goes wrong.
  • Tight schedules: pressure to sail on time is real. The master's authority to delay for safety is backed by the ISM Code, and you should use it.

Worked scenario: last-minute pressure at departure

Situation. The ship is five minutes behind schedule. The loading officer reports the bow door closed, but the bridge panel shows one locking pin not confirmed. The ramp crew say it is "just a sensor".

What a competent officer does:

  1. Does not sail. Departure waits until the hull opening is confirmed secure, either on the indicator or by a physical check under the company procedure.
  2. Sends a crew member to check the pin locally and report by radio.
  3. Calls the engineers or ETO to investigate the sensor if the physical check shows the pin is home.
  4. Logs the defect and follows the SMS for any temporary arrangement. Some companies require the master's or office approval and extra monitoring.
  5. Completes the passenger count and the departure stability check before letting go. SOLAS requires the number of persons on board to be known before departure.

The lesson: schedule pressure never overrides confirmation that the ship is watertight.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners like

  • "Why are ro-pax ships so sensitive to water on the vehicle deck?" A large, undivided deck allows a big free surface. Water moves freely and quickly erodes GM and righting levers, so the ship can capsize with surprisingly little water.
  • "What training does STCW require for passenger ships?" Name the five elements in Section A-V/2 and say who each applies to.
  • "How are passengers mustered on longer voyages?" Under SOLAS, when passengers are scheduled to be on board for more than 24 hours, newly embarked passengers are mustered before or immediately on departure. Shorter voyages rely on announcements and safety briefings.
  • "Your role on the muster list as a junior officer?" Typically control of an assembly station, a boat or MES, or a search party. Know your muster list, the evacuation routes and your passenger counting method.
  • "What would you do if a fire alarm activates on the vehicle deck?" Raise the alarm, confirm the fire, and close openings and ventilation as the procedure requires. Prepare the drencher system, stop cargo operations, account for persons on the car deck, and keep passengers out.

How to get your first ferry contract

  1. Complete passenger ship training early. At minimum, do crowd management. Officers should add crisis management and human behaviour, and the ro-ro hull integrity course where your administration certifies it separately.
  2. Build ro-ro experience. Car carrier or ro-ro cargo time shows you understand ramps, lashing and vehicle deck fire risk.
  3. Target the right recruiters. Many international operators hire through crewing agencies. Domestic ferry companies often recruit directly and may require national certification.
  4. Expect assessment. Ferry interviews often probe English, emergency procedures, teamwork under pressure and customer handling, not just technical knowledge.
  5. Consider junior entry. AB, OS, junior officer and cadet places on ferries can lead to permanent positions, especially with operators running rotation systems.
  6. Keep refreshers current. Expired passenger ship training can stop you being signed on, so check validity dates before applying.
Key points
  • A passenger ship carries more than 12 passengers. Most international ferries are ro-ro passenger ships with large open vehicle decks.
  • STCW Regulation V/2 adds passenger ship training on top of basic safety training: emergency familiarisation, safety training for personnel serving passengers, crowd management, crisis management and human behaviour, and, on ro-ro passenger ships, passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity.
  • Crowd management, crisis management and ro-ro hull integrity training must be refreshed, or the standard evidenced, at intervals not exceeding five years.
  • The critical hazards are hull openings, water on the vehicle deck, vehicle deck fires, cargo shift, mooring and fatigue.
  • Every crew member, including hotel staff, has an emergency duty, and drills on passenger ships are held weekly.
  • Ro-ro or car carrier experience and good English are the strongest advantages when applying.
  • Domestic ferries follow national rules, so check your administration's requirements.

Frequently asked questions

What certificates do I need to work on a passenger ferry?

You need your normal STCW basic safety training, security training, medical certificate and CoC or rating certificate. On top of these come the passenger ship courses in STCW Section A-V/2, appropriate to your role. On ro-ro passenger ships, senior officers and anyone handling cargo, passengers or hull openings also need passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training.

Is crowd management training mandatory for all ferry crew?

Crowd management is required for crew designated on the muster list to assist passengers in an emergency, which on ferries is usually a large share of the crew. All personnel need passenger ship emergency familiarisation before taking up duties. Exactly how this is certified depends on your flag administration.

How often must passenger ship training be refreshed?

STCW requires refresher training, or evidence of maintaining the required standard, at intervals not exceeding five years for crowd management, crisis management and the ro-ro hull integrity training. Some administrations and companies apply stricter internal intervals, so check your flag and company SMS.

Can I join a ferry without ro-ro experience?

Yes, particularly as a rating or junior officer, but ro-ro or car carrier experience gives you a clear edge. Without it, show strong safety and emergency knowledge, good English and complete passenger ship training. Some operators run familiarisation and mentoring for new joiners.

Is life on a ferry easier than on a deep-sea ship?

It is different rather than easier. You are close to land and, with some operators, on predictable rotations. However, the frequent port calls, weekly drills, passenger contact and schedule pressure make the days intense, and fatigue management is a constant issue.

Test yourself

Under STCW, who must complete passenger ship emergency familiarisation?

Emergency familiarisation applies to all personnel on passenger ships before they take up shipboard duties. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-V/2.

What is the maximum interval for refresher training or evidence of competence in crowd management and crisis management under STCW?

STCW requires refresher training, or evidence of maintaining the standard, at intervals not exceeding five years. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/2.

Under SOLAS, how often are abandon ship and fire drills held on passenger ships?

Passenger ships hold abandon ship and fire drills weekly, with each crew member attending at least once a month. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

When passengers are scheduled to be on board for more than 24 hours, when must newly embarked passengers be mustered?

Since the 2015 SOLAS amendments, the muster of newly embarked passengers must take place before or immediately upon departure, not within 24 hours. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

Why is a small amount of water on a ro-ro vehicle deck so dangerous?

The long open deck allows water to move freely, and the resulting free surface effect sharply reduces GM and righting levers. This is the basis of the enhanced ro-ro stability standards in SOLAS Chapter II-1 and the regional Stockholm Agreement.
