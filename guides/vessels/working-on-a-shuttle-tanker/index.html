Working on a Shuttle Tanker

Quick answer

A shuttle tanker is a crude oil tanker fitted with dynamic positioning and a bow loading system. It loads directly from offshore installations such as FPSOs and loading buoys, then discharges ashore. Operators usually want solid oil tanker experience first, a Dangerous Cargo Endorsement for oil tankers and, for deck officers, progress in a recognised DP training scheme.

What a shuttle tanker does and where it trades

A shuttle tanker is a pipeline at sea. Many offshore fields have no export pipeline. The crude is stored in an FPSO or FSO, or it is pumped to a loading buoy. A shuttle tanker then comes alongside in open water, loads, and runs the cargo to a terminal or transhipment port.

The main trades are harsh-weather and deepwater fields:

  • North Sea and Norwegian Sea, the traditional home of the trade
  • Barents Sea
  • Offshore Brazil, a large and growing deepwater fleet
  • Eastern Canada

Voyages are short and repetitive compared with conventional crude trading. The demanding part is the offshore loading, which can take place in weather that would stop most cargo work on a conventional tanker.

Think of the ship as two things at once: a crude carrier under MARPOL Annex I and SOLAS, and a DP vessel working close to a manned installation.

Layout and equipment that sets it apart

From aft she looks like any Aframax or Suezmax. The bow and the bridge tell you otherwise.

  • Bow Loading System (BLS): a bow manifold and coupler that receive the offloading hose. It also has a chain stopper for the mooring hawser where tandem moored loading is used. The BLS is interlocked with the cargo system and the emergency shutdown (ESD) logic.
  • Submerged Turret Loading (STL), on some ships: a buoy is pulled up into a compartment in the forward hull and the ship weathervanes around it.
  • Dynamic positioning: commonly built to DP class 2 notation. DP2 means redundancy, so that a single failure should not cause loss of position. Expect tunnel and azimuth thrusters forward and aft and DP reference systems. Look out for position reference systems specific to offshore loading, such as relative GNSS and short-range radio or laser systems. Some ships also use hydroacoustics.
  • Propulsion: often controllable pitch propellers, and on some ships twin screws or diesel-electric plant, to give fine control.
  • Telemetry link with the installation: ESD signals pass between the tanker and the FPSO or buoy, so either end can stop the transfer.
  • Cargo plant: inert gas and crude oil washing as on any crude carrier. Many ships also carry VOC recovery or reduction equipment, linked to the VOC management plan that crude oil tankers must carry.

Engine and electrical departments look after much more redundancy and power management than on a conventional tanker. High voltage switchboards are common on the larger DP ships.

Offshore loading, step by step

The details come from the field-specific operations manual and the company SMS. They differ between fields and operators. A typical tandem loading from an FPSO goes roughly like this:

  1. Pre-arrival: run the checklists, test the DP and thrusters, and check the cargo and inert gas plant. Confirm the weather limits in the field manual. Exchange information with the installation.
  2. Approach: enter the installation's 500 m safety zone only with permission. Take the approach sector and speed set by the field procedures. At a defined distance, change over from manual or joystick control to DP.
  3. Connection: a messenger line is passed. The hawser is picked up and secured in the chain stopper, then the hose is pulled in and coupled at the bow. The telemetry and ESD links are tested.
  4. Loading: the ship holds a set position and heading relative to the FPSO, inside defined operating zones. The DPO and the cargo officer watch position, hawser tension, thruster load, power, cargo rates and tank pressures.
  5. Disconnection: stop loading and flush the hose as required. Disconnect the hose, then release the hawser, and back off along the agreed sector.

Worked scenario: you are mid-loading and the DP raises a redundancy alarm because one thruster has tripped. You still hold position. You are no longer in your normal fully redundant DP2 mode. The usual response is to follow your activity-specific operating guidelines (or the field procedures where those apply). These normally call for an advisory or yellow status, informing the installation, and preparing to stop loading. If position cannot be held, initiate ESD and disconnect. Never improvise a lesser condition because loading is nearly finished.

Key hazards and how they are managed

Hazard Main controls
Collision with the FPSO or buoy Approach sectors, DP operating limits, alert status system, rehearsed emergency disconnect
Drive-off (thrusters push the ship away) or drift-off (loss of thrust) DP redundancy, FMEA-based procedures, trained DPOs, trials before each operation
Hawser or hose failure Tension monitoring, weather limits, inspection and retirement of hawsers and hoses
Oil spill at the bow ESD link, dry-break or quick-release couplings, SOPEP and field response arrangements
Hydrocarbon gas, VOC and H2S Inert gas, closed loading, gas detection, H2S procedures where the crude requires them
Heavy weather and cold Field weather criteria, ice and winterisation measures in northern fields
Fatigue Short, intense loading cycles; follow hours of rest rules and the company manning plan

Two terms you can expect to be asked about are drive-off and drift-off. Both can put a tanker into a manned installation in very little time. That is why the ESD and disconnect procedures are drilled hard.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Requirements vary by operator, flag and charterer. The well-established ones are:

  • All crew: STCW basic safety training, ship security awareness or designated security duties as applicable, and a valid medical certificate. Officers and ratings with specific duties related to cargo or cargo equipment also need basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations (STCW V/1-1). In practice many tanker operators require it of most crew.
  • Master, chief officer, chief engineer, 2nd engineer and others with immediate responsibility for cargo: advanced training for oil tanker cargo operations. This is the Dangerous Cargo Endorsement under STCW regulation V/1-1. Previous crude tanker sea service is normally expected.
  • Deck officers: a DP certificate under a recognised scheme. The Nautical Institute scheme is the most widely known; check whether your operator also accepts another. On shuttle tankers the DPOs are usually the watchkeeping officers. ECDIS generic and type-specific training, and BRM, also apply.
  • Engineers and ETOs: high voltage training where the plant requires it. Familiarity with DP power management and thruster systems is valued.
  • Offshore survival (BOSIET/HUET): required only where helicopter transfers are used or the operator or field requires it. It is not a general requirement for shuttle tanker crew.

National flag requirements apply on top of these. Norwegian, for example, may differ from Bahamas or Liberia. Charterers' vetting can also set minimum experience levels for officers, so check before you apply.

Crew and life on board

Crewing is broadly that of a crude tanker, with extra emphasis on DP:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, 2nd and 3rd officers, often all DP-qualified. Bosun, ABs, OS and cadets. On some ships the deck crew also handle the BLS forward.
  • Engine: chief, 2nd, 3rd and 4th engineers, an ETO, motormen or oilers, a fitter and a wiper. The ETO matters more here because of the DP electrics and the telemetry.
  • Catering: cook and steward.

Life on board is busy and cyclical. You load offshore, steam for a day or a few days, discharge, and go back. There is less ocean passage time and more manoeuvring, drills and checklists. Port calls are short. Weather in northern fields can be severe, especially in winter.

Rotations and crew nationality depend heavily on flag and operator. Some fleets work shorter rotations than deep-sea tankers. Confirm the terms in your contract and the CBA rather than relying on what you heard from others.

How to get your first shuttle tanker contract

Few people go straight onto shuttle tankers without tanker background. A realistic path:

  1. Build crude or product tanker sea time and get your Dangerous Cargo Endorsement for oil tankers.
  2. Deck officers: start the DP scheme early. Do the DP induction course. Logging DP time is easiest once you are on a DP vessel, so some officers first work on OSVs or PSVs. Check the current scheme rules for how shuttle tanker DP time is counted.
  3. Engineers: gain HV and DP-plant experience, for example on DP offshore vessels or diesel-electric ships. Get strong electrical and automation skills.
  4. Apply directly to managers known to operate shuttle tankers. Also ask your present tanker company whether it runs any in its fleet. Some have cadet or junior officer intakes.
  5. Keep a clean record. Vetting, appraisal reports and a clean safety history carry real weight, because charterers inspect closely.
  6. Use only licensed manning agents (RPSL in India, DMW-licensed in the Philippines). Never pay for a job offer.

Ratings with oil tanker experience and an endorsement are the best placed. Show familiarity with offshore mooring and hose handling, and good PPE habits.

Oral exam style questions

Questions a master, superintendent or examiner may put to you:

  • Explain the difference between drive-off and drift-off. Drive-off: the thrusters push the ship off position, often because of a reference or control fault. Drift-off: thrust is lost and the environment moves the ship.
  • What does DP class 2 mean in practice? Redundancy so that a single active component failure should not cause loss of position. Fire and flood of a compartment are not covered, unlike class 3.
  • What happens when you press ESD during loading? Cargo transfer stops at both ends through the telemetry link. Then follow the field procedure for disconnect if required.
  • Why does a crude tanker need a VOC management plan? To control the release of volatile organic compounds during loading and the voyage, under MARPOL Annex VI.
  • Who can stop the operation? Anyone with a safety concern, through the agreed communication and ESD arrangements. Stop work authority applies offshore just as it does in port.
Key points
  • Shuttle tankers load crude offshore from FPSOs, FSOs and buoys, usually through a bow loading system.
  • Most are built to DP class 2 notation, and deck officers normally work as DPOs.
  • Drive-off, drift-off, hawser failure and spills at the bow are the defining hazards.
  • An oil tanker Dangerous Cargo Endorsement and prior crude tanker experience are normally expected.
  • A recognised DP certificate is the key extra qualification for deck officers.
  • BOSIET/HUET is required only where helicopter transfers are used or the operator or field requires it.
  • Field manuals, company SMS and flag rules govern the details, so always check them.

Frequently asked questions

Is a shuttle tanker the same as an FPSO?

No. An FPSO produces, processes and stores oil at a fixed location. A shuttle tanker is a trading tanker that comes to the FPSO, loads, and carries the crude to shore.

Do I need a DP certificate to work on a shuttle tanker?

Deck officers who operate the DP usually need a certificate from a recognised scheme, or must be progressing through one. Ratings and engineers are not normally required to hold DPO certificates. Engineers do need to understand the DP power and thruster systems they maintain.

Can I join a shuttle tanker with only bulk carrier experience?

It is difficult. Operators and charterers normally expect oil tanker sea time and the matching endorsement first. The usual route is to move to conventional crude or product tankers before applying.

Do shuttle tanker crew need BOSIET?

Only if the operator or field uses helicopter crew transfers or requires it locally. Most shuttle tanker crew changes take place in port, but check your company's requirements.

Is life on a shuttle tanker harder than on a VLCC?

It is more intense. Loading cycles are frequent, there is lots of DP and manoeuvring work, and the weather is often harsh. In return there is less long ocean passage. Many officers value the ship-handling and DP experience it gives.

During tandem loading, a DP reference fault causes the thrusters to push the shuttle tanker away from its set position. This is called:

Drive-off is powered movement off position caused by the DP system itself. Drift-off is uncontrolled movement after loss of thrust (industry DP guidance such as IMCA, and the operator's DP procedures).

Which qualification is required under STCW for the officers with immediate responsibility for cargo on a shuttle tanker?

Shuttle tankers carry crude oil, so officers with immediate responsibility for cargo need the oil tanker advanced endorsement (STCW Code, Section A-V/1-1).

What does a DP class 2 notation primarily provide?

Class 2 covers single failures of active components. Fire and flood are addressed at class 3 (IMO MSC.1/Circ.1580).

Why must a crude oil tanker carry a VOC management plan?

VOC from crude oil cargo must be managed under a ship-specific plan (MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 15).

These are offshore industry courses, not STCW certificates. They are needed where helicopter travel or field rules require them (operator and field requirements).
