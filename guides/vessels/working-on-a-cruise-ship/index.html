Working on a Cruise Ship

Quick answer

A cruise ship is a passenger ship carrying thousands of people, so the job is mainly about safety management, crowd control and keeping a floating hotel running. On top of your normal CoC or rating certificate you need STCW Regulation V/2 passenger ship training and the operator's own induction. Most marine officers come in through company cadet schemes or by moving across with strong cargo-ship experience, usually through a licensed manning agency.

What a cruise ship does and how she is laid out

A cruise ship is a passenger ship under SOLAS. She carries more than 12 passengers, and in practice often several thousand plus the crew. Her 'cargo' is people, and her design follows from that.

Typical trades

  • Seasonal itineraries: Caribbean, Mediterranean, Northern Europe and Baltic, Alaska, Asia and Australia.
  • Repositioning voyages between seasons.
  • Expedition cruising in polar waters on smaller ships.

Most voyages are short port-to-port legs with a call almost every day. Turnaround day in the home port is the busiest day of the cycle.

Layout you need to know on day one

  • Main vertical zones (MVZs): fire boundaries running up through the ship. Their fire doors can be released remotely from the bridge or safety centre and closed locally. You will learn your zone and stairways before you learn your cabin.
  • Passenger decks: cabins, restaurants, theatres, pools and casino.
  • Crew decks: usually the lower decks. A long service corridor, often nicknamed the I-95 or main crew alley, runs fore and aft for stores, laundry and crew movement.
  • Lifeboats and tenders: along the promenade or a dedicated embarkation deck. Some of the boats double as tenders for anchor ports.
  • Machinery: many modern cruise ships are diesel-electric. They have high-voltage generators and podded or azimuthing propulsion, plus several bow and sometimes stern thrusters. A growing number burn LNG.
  • Safety centre: sits on or next to the bridge. Fire detection, watertight doors, public address and CCTV all report there.

Passenger ships constructed on or after 1 July 2010 with a length of 120 m or more, or with three or more main vertical zones, are designed to SOLAS Safe Return to Port requirements. After a casualty below a defined threshold, the ship must be able to reach port with essential systems still running. Engineers and ETOs will meet this in the system redundancy and in the drills.

Operations and key hazards

Cargo work is replaced by passenger operations. The main activities and their risks are:

  • Embarkation and disembarkation: thousands of people pass through the gangways in a few hours. Crowd flow, gangway safety and security screening under the ISPS Code are critical.
  • Passenger muster: under SOLAS Chapter III, passengers on a voyage of more than 24 hours must be mustered before or immediately on departure.
  • Tender operations: boat work at anchor with fare-paying passengers aboard, often in swell. Boat handling, embarkation platforms and weather limits matter.
  • Close-quarters manoeuvring: frequent berthing with pods and thrusters, often in tight harbours with no tugs.
  • Bunkering and storing: often done at the same time as passenger operations, so segregation and the permit-to-work system matter.

Key hazards

Hazard Why it matters on a cruise ship
Fire Huge accommodation volume, galleys, laundries and theatres, plus people who do not know the ship
Evacuation and crowd behaviour Panic, mobility-impaired guests and language barriers
Flooding and damage control Watertight doors in constant use by crew
Public health Gastro-intestinal outbreaks spread fast. Ships calling US ports face sanitation inspections.
Environmental compliance Large sewage, grey water and garbage streams under MARPOL Annexes IV and V, with passenger-ship rules in some special areas
Security Large numbers of people and visitors, so access control is constant

SOLAS requires an abandon ship drill and a fire drill weekly on passenger ships. Expect the drill programme to take up a real part of your week.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your normal qualification still comes first:

  • Officers need a CoC for the rank.
  • Deck and engine watch ratings need Rating Forming Part of a Watch or Able Seafarer.
  • STCW basic training. It is required for anyone with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties, and in practice cruise companies demand it of almost all crew.
  • Ship Security Awareness or Designated Security Duties.
  • A valid medical certificate.

The passenger-ship layer comes from STCW Regulation V/2 and Code Section A-V/2:

  • Passenger ship emergency familiarisation: for all personnel, before they are assigned shipboard duties.
  • Safety training for personnel providing direct service to passengers: for crew working in passenger spaces.
  • Crowd management training: for anyone designated on the muster list to assist passengers in an emergency.
  • Crisis management and human behaviour training: for the master, chief engineer, chief mate and second engineer, and anyone designated with responsibility for passenger safety in an emergency.
  • Passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training: applies only to ro-ro passenger ships, so usually not to pure cruise ships.

Anyone required to hold crowd management or crisis management training must show they have kept that standard up at intervals not exceeding five years. Check with your administration exactly what evidence it accepts.

Required where applicable:

  • ECDIS generic training for deck officers on ECDIS-fitted ships.
  • IGF Code training (STCW Regulation V/3) on LNG-fuelled ships.
  • Polar Code training (STCW Regulation V/4) for the master, chief mate and officers in charge of a navigational watch on ships operating in polar waters.
  • PSCRB for crew assigned to survival craft or rescue boats, and fast rescue boat proficiency where you crew one.

Often expected by the company, depending on rank:

  • High Voltage training for engineers and ETOs.
  • Type-specific ECDIS training.
  • BRM or ERM.

Flag states and companies differ on exactly which courses they accept and where they must be taken. Confirm with the operator before you book anything.

Crew composition

A large cruise ship is effectively two organisations under one master.

Marine and technical

  • Command: the Master, plus a Staff Captain as deputy, who is often responsible for safety, discipline and security.
  • Deck officers: chief officers, first and second officers, and junior officers.
  • Engineering: Chief Engineer, Staff Chief Engineer, and a large engineering department with several ETOs.
  • Specialist officers: many operators carry a dedicated Safety Officer, Security Officer and Environmental Officer.
  • Deck and engine ratings: bosun, ABs, OSs, motormen, fitters and plumbers.
  • Medical team: under MLC 2006, ships carrying 100 or more persons on international voyages of more than three days must carry a qualified doctor. Cruise ships carry doctors and nurses.

Hotel

  • A Hotel Director with food and beverage, housekeeping, galley, guest services, entertainment and shore excursions teams.
  • Concessionaires such as spa, shop and photo staff. They are often employed by third-party companies but still sail as crew.

Rank titles and reporting lines vary between companies, so learn your ship's organisation chart early. Under MLC 2006, every person employed in any capacity on board is a seafarer, including waiters, cabin stewards and entertainers. Every one of them has a muster station and emergency duties.

Life on board

Work

  • Bridge and engine room: watches are structured. Many operators use formal bridge team roles such as navigator and co-navigator, with strict cross-checking.
  • Day work: there is a heavy load of drills, inspections and training on top of watchkeeping.
  • Hotel departments: long working days are common, within MLC hours of work and rest limits.

Accommodation

  • Senior officers usually have single cabins.
  • Many junior officers, ratings and hotel crew share cabins.
  • Crew areas include a crew mess, crew bar, gym and welfare facilities.

Conduct

  • Uniform and grooming standards are strict.
  • Rules govern when crew may enter guest areas.
  • Alcohol policies are enforced, including testing.
  • Behaviour ashore reflects on the company.

Social life

  • Crews are highly multinational, often with dozens of nationalities. English is the working language, and your communication skills are assessed.
  • Social life can be good, but privacy is limited.

Contracts and shore leave

  • Contract lengths and leave rotations differ widely between companies, departments and collective agreements. Marine officers often work shorter rotations than hotel staff. Check yours in writing.
  • Port time can be short, and duty or drill rotas decide who gets ashore.

How to get a first contract

  1. Choose your route.
    • Cadets: apply to operators or manning companies running passenger-ship cadet programmes.
    • Experienced cargo-ship officers and ratings: apply through the cruise line's crewing office or its appointed agency. Strong BRM, ECDIS and boat-handling records help. For engineers, HV and diesel-electric experience help.
  2. Use only licensed agencies.
    • Indian seafarers: use an agency holding a DG Shipping RPSL.
    • Filipino seafarers: use a manning agency licensed by the Department of Migrant Workers (formerly POEA).
  3. Never pay a recruitment fee. MLC 2006 Regulation 1.4 prohibits charging seafarers for recruitment or placement. A request for 'processing' or 'placement' money is a red flag.
  4. Prepare documents.
    • CoC and flag endorsement.
    • Seaman's book (CDC) and passport.
    • V/2 certificates.
    • US C1/D crew visa if the ship calls the US.
    • Yellow fever certificate if the itinerary needs it.
    • Pre-employment medical to the company's standard.
  5. Expect a structured interview. You will usually face a technical or competency interview, often an English assessment, and sometimes a simulator check for deck officers.
  6. On joining: complete your familiarisation before taking on any duties. Learn your muster station, your MVZ escape routes and your emergency role in the first days.

Oral exam: passenger ship questions examiners like

Q: What does STCW Regulation V/2 cover? It sets the minimum training for masters, officers, ratings and other personnel on passenger ships. This covers emergency familiarisation, safety training for direct-service staff, crowd management, and crisis management and human behaviour.

Q: Who must hold crisis management and human behaviour training? The master, chief engineer, chief mate and second engineer, and anyone designated on the muster list with responsibility for passenger safety in emergencies.

Q: When must passengers be mustered? On a voyage of more than 24 hours, they must be mustered before or immediately on departure, under SOLAS Chapter III.

Q: How often are abandon ship and fire drills held on a passenger ship? Weekly.

Q: What is a main vertical zone? A section of the ship bounded by fire-resisting divisions running up through the decks, used to contain fire and smoke.

Q: What is the idea behind Safe Return to Port? After a casualty below a defined threshold, the ship should reach port under her own power with essential services maintained. Passengers then stay on board instead of abandoning ship.

Worked scenario: a fire alarm sounds in a cabin corridor at 0200. As the OOW:

  • Check the detector location on the fire detection panel and send a patrol to confirm.
  • Call the master and the staff captain.
  • Be ready to sound the crew alert signal and close the fire doors in that MVZ.
  • Hold PA announcements to passengers until the master decides.

The examiner wants to hear speed, containment and command, not heroics.

Key points
  • A cruise ship is a SOLAS passenger ship whose real cargo is people, so safety and crowd management dominate the work.
  • STCW Regulation V/2 passenger-ship training is needed on top of your CoC or rating certificate.
  • Crisis management training is mandatory for the master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and those responsible for passengers in emergencies.
  • Abandon ship and fire drills are weekly on passenger ships, and passengers are mustered before or immediately on departure.
  • Every crew member, including hotel staff, is a seafarer under MLC 2006 and has an emergency duty.
  • Contract lengths, rank titles and extra course requirements differ by company and flag, so confirm them before you sign.
  • Never pay a recruitment fee; MLC 2006 forbids charging seafarers for placement.

Frequently asked questions

Can a cargo-ship officer move to cruise ships?

Yes, many do. You will need STCW V/2 passenger-ship training and usually type-specific ECDIS. Engineers and ETOs will also need high-voltage training. Cruise operators value clean records, good English and strong BRM or ERM habits, and some start lateral hires a rank lower until they are familiar with passenger operations.

Which STCW courses do I need for a cruise ship?

You need your normal STCW basic training and security course, plus the relevant passenger-ship training under STCW Regulation V/2. That means emergency familiarisation, safety training for direct-service staff, crowd management if you have a muster-list role with passengers, and crisis management for senior officers. Some flags and companies require courses from specific approved centres, so check before booking.

Are hotel and entertainment staff counted as seafarers?

Yes. MLC 2006 defines a seafarer as anyone employed, engaged or working in any capacity on board, so they have the same protections on hours, accommodation and repatriation. They also have muster-list duties and must complete the required passenger-ship safety training.

Do I have to pay an agency to get a cruise ship job?

No. MLC 2006 Regulation 1.4 prohibits recruitment and placement fees being charged to seafarers. In India, use an agency holding a DG Shipping RPSL; in the Philippines, use one licensed by the Department of Migrant Workers. Report any agency asking for placement money.

Is a US C1/D visa needed for cruise ship work?

Only if the ship calls US ports or you need to transit the US to join, but many itineraries do. Companies running US-based itineraries usually expect a valid C1/D before or soon after you are selected. Confirm with your agency, which normally issues the support letters.

Test yourself

Under STCW Regulation V/2, who must hold crisis management and human behaviour training?

Crisis management training covers senior officers and anyone designated with responsibility for passenger safety in emergencies. STCW Code, Section A-V/2.

On a voyage of more than 24 hours, when must passengers be mustered?

Amendments to SOLAS removed the old 24-hour allowance, so passengers must be mustered before or immediately on departure. SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

An agency asks you for a 'placement fee' to secure a cruise ship contract. What is the correct position?

Recruitment and placement services must not charge seafarers for finding them employment. MLC 2006, Regulation 1.4 and Standard A1.4.

What is the status of a cabin steward under MLC 2006?

MLC defines a seafarer as any person employed, engaged or working in any capacity on board a ship to which the Convention applies. MLC 2006, Article II.

How often must abandon ship and fire drills be held on a passenger ship?

Monthly is the general crew participation interval, but passenger ships hold abandon ship and fire drills weekly. SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 30 (passenger ships) and Regulation 19 (general crew drill requirements).
