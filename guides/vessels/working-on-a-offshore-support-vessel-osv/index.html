Working on an Offshore Support Vessel (OSV)

Quick answer

Offshore support vessels supply, tow, moor and stand by offshore oil, gas and wind installations. Much of the work is done close to the installation under dynamic positioning or on an open aft deck. Most companies expect the STCW basics plus DP training for deck officers. Offshore survival training such as BOSIET/HUET and region-specific medicals depend on the operator and the sector. Life on board means short trips, two-watch systems, heavy motion and regular port calls at the supply base.

What an OSV is and what it does

"OSV" is an umbrella term rather than a single ship type. It covers any vessel built to support offshore installations such as drilling rigs, production platforms, FPSOs and wind farms. The main families are:

  • Platform Supply Vessel (PSV): carries deck cargo and bulk liquids and powders out to installations, then brings backload in.
  • Anchor Handling Tug Supply (AHTS): tows rigs, runs and recovers anchors and mooring systems, and also carries some supply cargo.
  • Multi-purpose and construction support vessels (MPSV, DSV, ROV support): carry cranes, ROV hangars or diving systems for subsea work.
  • Standby / emergency response and rescue vessels: stay on station near installations for rescue and recovery.
  • Crew transfer vessels and walk-to-work vessels: move technicians to installations. CTVs mainly serve offshore wind. Walk-to-work vessels serve both wind and oil and gas.

The trade is regional. Vessels work out of a supply base and run between the base and one or more fields. A trip can last hours or several days. Areas such as the North Sea, the Gulf of Mexico, West Africa, Brazil, the Middle East, India's western offshore and South-East Asia all have their own operator rules, so expect procedures to change when you move between regions.

Layout, cargo and typical operations

A typical PSV or AHTS has:

  • the accommodation and bridge well forward;
  • a long, low open cargo deck aft;
  • an aft-facing bridge console for DP and manual control while working alongside an installation.

Propulsion is often diesel-electric with azimuth thrusters, plus tunnel thrusters fore and aft.

Below-deck tanks commonly carry:

  • fuel and drill water;
  • liquid mud (drilling fluids);
  • brines;
  • base oil;
  • sometimes methanol or other chemicals.

Dry bulk tanks carry cement, barite and bentonite, which are blown to the rig with compressed air.

Deck cargo includes containers, baskets, drill pipe, casing and equipment. It is lifted on and off by the installation's crane.

AHTS vessels add large towing and anchor-handling winches, a stern roller, and wire-stoppers and guide pins at the stern (shark jaws and towing pins). They also have chain lockers for rig anchor chain.

A typical supply run looks like this:

  1. Load at the base.
  2. Steam to the field.
  3. Complete the installation's checklist before entering the 500 m safety zone.
  4. Set up on DP or manual control, usually on the lee side if weather allows.
  5. Discharge bulk through hoses passed down by the installation's crane.
  6. Work deck cargo and backload.
  7. Leave the zone and move to the next installation or back to base.

Key hazards on board

OSV work is high-risk because heavy loads, wires and position-keeping all come together near a fixed structure. The main hazards are:

  • Loss of position near an installation. A drive-off or drift-off on DP, or a mistake in manual control, can cause contact with the structure. This is why there are DP trials, checklists and agreed limits before and during work in the 500 m zone.
  • Deck cargo operations. Risks include:
    • swinging and landing loads;
    • cargo shifting when the vessel rolls;
    • pinch points between units.

Crew should use the deck safe havens and follow the company's rules on hooking up and tag lines. Never stand between a load and a fixed object. - Seas on deck. The low aft deck can ship green water in weather. Deck work may have to stop. - Anchor handling. Hazards include: - wires and chain under high tension; - snap-back zones; - work close to an open stern; - the heeling effect of a tow wire led off the side.

The vessel must stay within its approved stability condition throughout. - Bulk liquids. Brines can be corrosive and toxic. Methanol is flammable and toxic. Base oil and mud bring pollution risk during hose transfers. - Tank cleaning and entry. Mud and brine tanks can build up hydrogen sulphide and oxygen-deficient atmospheres. Every tank entry is an enclosed space entry under permit. - Personnel transfer. Basket, gangway and boat transfers each have their own risk assessment and weather limits. - Fatigue. Two-watch systems, night cargo work and constant motion wear people down. The STCW minimum rest of 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days still applies.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your CoC and the STCW basics are the foundation, as on any ship. On top of these, the following are widely expected. Exact requirements depend on the flag, the charterer (the oil company or wind developer) and the region.

Item Who usually needs it Notes
Dynamic Positioning training and certificate Deck officers who will act as DPO; masters Usually an industry certification scheme. STCW gives only non-mandatory guidance on DP training in Part B.
BOSIET / HUET or equivalent Anyone who may fly by helicopter to or from installations Often an operator or regional requirement rather than a flag one.
Offshore-specific medical Some sectors, in addition to your flag medical Check the region before joining.
Fast rescue boat proficiency Crew on standby and rescue vessels STCW proficiency.
High-voltage training Engineers and ETOs on diesel-electric vessels with HV systems HV competence is part of the STCW standards for management-level engineers (A-III/2) and ETOs (A-III/6). Additional HV courses for other engineers are usually company-driven.
ECDIS type-specific, GMDSS, BRM / ERM Officers As for any trading ship.

Charterers often look at offshore experience as closely as at certificates: DP hours, anchor-handling trips, the type of installation you have worked and the region. If you are coming from deep-sea, expect a supernumerary period or a junior role while you build those hours.

Crew composition and life on board

Crews are small compared with deep-sea ships. Numbers depend on the vessel's minimum safe manning document and the charter. A typical PSV or AHTS carries:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, one or two further officers (most of them DPOs or trainee DPOs), and ABs. Larger vessels also carry a bosun.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second engineer, often a third or junior engineer and a motorman or oiler. An ETO is common on diesel-electric DP vessels.
  • Catering: cook, sometimes with a steward or messman.
  • Extras on construction or AHTS work: additional ABs, a crane operator, or client representatives.

What day-to-day life is like:

  • Watches. A two-watch system, often six-on/six-off or twelve-on/twelve-off, is common because crews are small. Separately, DP operations close to an installation normally require two qualified DPOs on the bridge.
  • Trips. Trips are short and you are back at the base regularly. Port time is busy loading and backloading rather than rest time.
  • Motion. These are small, stiff vessels that move a lot in weather. Seasickness in your first weeks is normal.
  • Rotations. Equal time on and off is common in some regions. Other companies run longer contracts. Ask before you sign.
  • Culture. There is strong emphasis on stop-work authority, toolbox talks and charterer audits. You will meet client representatives and offshore personnel far more than on a deep-sea ship.

How to get your first OSV contract

Officers coming from deep-sea:

  1. Get your STCW and CoC documents current, including any endorsement the flag requires.
  2. Complete a DP induction or basic course with an accredited centre. This lets you start logging DP sea time as a trainee DPO.
  3. Get BOSIET/HUET or the regional equivalent if you are targeting sectors where helicopter transfer is used.
  4. Apply to offshore-focused managers and operators. Be willing to start as a junior officer on a PSV or other DP vessel to build DP sea time.
  5. Keep a clean, verifiable record of DP hours, the vessel's DP class and the operations you have done. Charterers check these.

Engineers:

  • Experience with diesel-electric plant, thrusters and power management systems counts heavily.
  • DP awareness and HV training help.

Ratings:

  • An AB moving from deep-sea is valued for mooring, cargo-securing and crane-signal experience.
  • Ask the company which offshore courses they want before applying, rather than collecting certificates at random.

Cadets:

  • Some offshore companies take cadets.
  • Check that the training record book tasks can be completed on that vessel type.
  • Check that the national administration accepts the sea service.

Oral exam and interview questions to prepare

Offshore interviews and CoC orals for officers with OSV service often include questions like these. Answer them from your own experience.

  • "Explain DP equipment class 1, 2 and 3."
    • Class 1 has no redundancy.
    • Class 2 should keep position after a single failure of an active component or system.
    • Class 3 also covers loss of a compartment through fire or flooding.
  • "What do you check before entering the 500 m zone?"
    • The installation and company checklist.
    • DP and manual control checks, and thruster and generator status.
    • Weather against the agreed limits.
    • Communications with the installation.
    • Escape route and set-up position, normally on the lee side.
  • "Your vessel starts to drift off on DP while discharging. What do you do?"
    • Follow the vessel's operating guidelines.
    • Take manual control and move away from the installation.
    • Stop the cargo operation.
    • Inform the installation.
    • Investigate once safe.
  • "Why is a mud tank an enclosed space even after cleaning?"
    • Residues can generate hydrogen sulphide.
    • Oxygen can be depleted.
    • So the tank needs a permit, ventilation, testing and a standby person.
  • "What affects stability during anchor handling?"
    • Wire tension and its lead.
    • Free surface in partially filled tanks.
    • Deck cargo and chain weight.
    • Water on deck.
Key points
  • "OSV" covers PSVs, AHTS, construction and rescue vessels and crew transfer vessels, all supporting offshore installations.
  • Most work near an installation is done on DP or manual control inside a 500 m safety zone under strict checklists.
  • The main hazards are loss of position, deck cargo handling, anchor handling under tension and toxic atmospheres in tanks.
  • DP certification for deck officers is usually an industry scheme. STCW gives only guidance on DP training.
  • BOSIET/HUET and offshore medicals depend on operator and region, so check before joining.
  • Expect small crews, two-watch systems, short trips and heavy motion.
  • Verified DP hours and offshore experience often matter as much as certificates.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a DP certificate to work on an OSV?

Not for every role, but deck officers who will keep a DP watch are generally expected to be DP-trained, and charterers often require a full DP certificate for senior DPOs. You can join as a trainee DPO after an induction course and build sea time. Engineers and ratings do not normally need DP certification.

Is BOSIET or HUET mandatory for OSV crew?

It is not an STCW requirement. Operators in many offshore regions require it for anyone who may travel by helicopter to or from an installation. Check the requirements of the sector and charterer you are joining.

Can a deep-sea officer move into offshore?

Yes, many do. The usual route is a DP induction course, offshore survival training where required, and a junior officer or trainee DPO position on a PSV or other DP vessel. Expect to start below your deep-sea rank until you have offshore experience.

What is the difference between a PSV and an AHTS?

A PSV mainly carries deck cargo and bulk liquids and powders to installations. An AHTS has powerful winches, a stern roller and towing gear to tow rigs and handle anchors, and can also carry some cargo. Anchor handling is generally regarded as the more hazardous operation.

What is the watch system on an OSV?

A two-watch system, often six-on/six-off or twelve-on/twelve-off, is common because crews are small. Separately, DP operations close to an installation normally require two qualified DPOs on the bridge. STCW and MLC hours of rest still apply, and the company must manage cargo work so they are met.

Test yourself

What does DP equipment class 2 mean?

Class 2 is redundancy against a single active failure. Class 3 adds fire and flooding of a compartment. See IMO MSC.1/Circ.1580.

Which cargo is typically carried in a PSV's below-deck tanks?

PSV tanks carry drilling-related liquids and fuel and water for installations. Dry bulk such as cement goes in separate bulk tanks. See IMO Res. A.863(20) and, for noxious liquids in bulk, the OSV Chemical Code (Res. A.1122(30)).

Before entering an installation's 500 m safety zone, what should normally be done?

Checklists, trials and agreed weather limits are completed before entering the zone. See the company SMS and MSC.1/Circ.1580.

What is the status of the commonly held offshore DP certificate under STCW?

STCW Code Section B-V/f gives guidance on DP training. The certificate itself is usually issued under an industry scheme.

A mud tank has been cleaned and looks dry. How should entry be treated?

Mud and brine residues can generate H2S and deplete oxygen. See SOLAS Reg. XI-1/7 and the IMO revised recommendations for entering enclosed spaces.
