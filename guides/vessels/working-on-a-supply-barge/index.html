Working on a Supply Barge

Quick answer

A supply barge delivers fuel, lubricants, fresh water, stores or deck cargo to ships at anchor, at berth or offshore. The work is short-trip and port-based, with frequent alongside operations day and night. Certificate needs depend on the cargo and on whether the barge is a convention ship or a locally licensed harbour craft, so the flag or port administration's rules decide what you need.

What a supply barge actually is

"Supply barge" is a loose term. In job adverts it can mean any of these:

  • Bunker barge or bunker tanker. This is the most common meaning. It delivers marine fuel oil, gas oil and sometimes lubricants to ships in port or at anchorage. Many are self-propelled small tankers. Some are dumb barges pushed or towed by a tug.
  • Water barge. It supplies fresh water to ships, often in ports with limited shore connections.
  • Stores or provisions barge. It carries spares, provisions and general supplies out to ships at anchor.
  • Offshore or deck cargo supply barge. It is usually towed or pushed. It carries pipe, containers and equipment to offshore construction sites, rigs or island projects.
  • LNG bunkering vessel. These are increasingly common in major bunkering hubs. They are gas carriers in their own right and are treated very differently for training.

Before applying, read the advert closely. A "barge" job can range from a self-propelled tanker with a full convention crew to a dumb barge with a barge master and two hands. The hazards, certificates and lifestyle differ a lot between them.

Typical trade and layout

Most supply barges work inside or close to one port or anchorage. Typical areas are major bunkering hubs, large anchorages and offshore fields near the coast.

A typical day runs like this:

  1. Load at a terminal or from a larger storage tanker.
  2. Shift to the anchorage.
  3. Moor alongside the receiving ship.
  4. Deliver.
  5. Cast off and repeat.

A self-propelled bunker barge usually has:

  • cargo tanks forward of or around a small aft accommodation block
  • a pump room or deck-mounted cargo pumps
  • a hose-handling crane or derrick
  • a cargo manifold
  • heavy fendering, often Yokohama-type pneumatic fenders, for coming alongside larger ships

A deck cargo barge is different. It is a flat-deck pontoon with:

  • towing brackets
  • emergency towing gear
  • sometimes a small deckhouse or no accommodation at all

The crew often lives on the attending tug.

Many of these craft operate under national harbour-craft or near-coastal rules rather than full convention requirements. This depends on their size, trading area and flag. That affects manning, certificates and survey regimes, so check what applies to the specific vessel.

Operations and key hazards

On a bunker barge, the core job is a safe transfer of oil between two moving hulls. The usual sequence is:

  • Approach and mooring alongside. The barge berths on a ship at anchor in whatever swell and current there is. Line handling, fender placement and parting lines are a serious risk to people.
  • Pre-transfer checks. Both sides agree on:
    • grades and quantities
    • pumping rate
    • VHF channel
    • emergency stop signal
    • who closes which valve
    • initial soundings and tank readings

Most companies use a ship–barge checklist for this. - Hose connection. Hose lifting, flange bolting and checking the hose condition and test date are deck tasks done close to a suspended load. Never stand under the hose while it is lifted. - Pumping. Start slowly, then increase to the agreed rate while watching: - pressures and flows - the receiving ship's ullage reports - weather - the moorings - Sampling and paperwork. The fuel sample is drawn during delivery, and the Bunker Delivery Note is issued under MARPOL Annex VI. Some ports also require metering systems or third-party surveys. - Disconnection. Drain and blank the hose, then cast off.

Key hazards:

  • Overflow and spills. The usual causes are miscommunication of the rate, an open wrong valve, or the receiving ship topping off too fast. Spill kits, scuppers plugged and SOPEP readiness are basic.
  • Hydrogen sulphide (H2S). Residual fuels can release H2S in tank vapours. Personal gas detectors are standard on many bunker operations.
  • Hose or flange failure under pressure.
  • Mooring line snap-back during ranging alongside.
  • Fire and static.
  • Enclosed spaces. These include cargo tanks, voids and pump rooms.
  • Personnel transfer. People move between the barge and a much larger ship by pilot ladder, gangway or crane basket.
  • Fatigue. Back-to-back deliveries at night are the hidden hazard of this trade.

On deck cargo barges the hazards shift to:

  • towing
  • lashing and securing
  • stability with high deck loads
  • boarding a barge at sea

Certificates and experience usually expected

The baseline for almost every seagoing role is:

  • STCW basic safety training
  • a valid seafarer medical certificate
  • Security Awareness or Designated Security Duties, as assigned

What comes on top depends on the cargo:

Barge type Extra training commonly required
Oil bunker barge / bunker tanker Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations for anyone with cargo duties; Advanced Oil Tanker training for masters, chief mates, chief engineers and second engineers, and any other person with immediate responsibility for cargo (such as a cargo officer)
LNG bunkering vessel Basic and Advanced Liquefied Gas Tanker training under STCW Regulation V/1-2, since it is a gas carrier
Water, stores or deck cargo barge Usually no cargo-specific STCW endorsement; national harbour-craft or near-coastal requirements may apply

The competency certificate you need depends on the barge's size, trading limits and the administration's rules. Officers on bigger self-propelled bunker tankers often hold standard STCW certificates. Smaller harbour craft may need a locally issued licence or a near-coastal certificate instead.

Check before you count on tanker sea time. Whether time on a harbour bunker barge counts as "seagoing service on oil tankers" for advanced tanker training or for upgrading is decided by your administration. Get it in writing from your training institute or administration first. Many candidates assume it counts and are disappointed.

The following are often asked for but are company requirements, not universal ones:

  • crane or hose-handling experience
  • previous ship-to-ship transfer experience
  • familiarity with the port's own bunkering rules

Crew composition and life on board

Crews are small. The number depends on the size, flag and the safe manning document. A self-propelled bunker tanker typically carries:

  • master
  • one or two mates
  • an engineer or two
  • a pumpman or cargo rating
  • a handful of ABs or deckhands
  • sometimes a cook

Smaller craft may run with just a skipper, an engineer and a couple of hands. Everyone handles lines, hoses and paperwork.

Life on board is different from deep-sea work:

  • Short passages, constant operations. There is little steaming time to rest. The work follows the demand of the ships waiting at anchor.
  • Irregular hours. Deliveries run around the clock. Hours of rest under STCW and MLC apply on convention ships, and equivalent national rules usually apply to harbour craft. They are a common inspection item (port state control on foreign-flagged ships, flag or port authority inspections on local craft). Record them honestly.
  • Close to shore, but not always ashore. You may see the city lights every night and still get little shore leave during busy periods.
  • Contract patterns vary. Some operators use short rotations with crew living locally. Others run normal sea contracts with crew changes at the base port.
  • Small-team culture. One careless person on a small crew affects everyone. Reliability matters more than rank.

Local licensing, nationality rules or cabotage restrictions in some ports limit who can be employed on harbour craft. Find out before you travel for an interview.

Worked scenario: rate change during a delivery

You are the cargo officer on a bunker barge alongside a bulk carrier at anchor. The agreed maximum rate has been reached and the delivery is about 70% complete. The receiving ship's chief engineer calls on VHF and asks you to reduce the rate because he is changing over tanks.

Correct actions:

  1. Acknowledge the request and reduce the rate at once. Do not argue over the radio first.
  2. Confirm the new rate back and note the time in the transfer log.
  3. Watch the manifold pressure for a rise. If the receiving ship closes a valve before opening the next one, pressure will build.
  4. If pressure climbs or communication is lost, stop pumping by the agreed emergency stop and inform both masters.
  5. Resume only when the receiving side confirms their line-up and you agree a new rate.

The key lesson: the receiving ship controls its own tanks, but the barge controls the pump. A stop is always acceptable. A spill is not.

Oral exam style questions

These are the kinds of questions examiners and interviewing superintendents ask barge candidates:

  • What do you agree with the receiving ship before starting a bunker transfer?
    • grades and quantities
    • rate
    • communications
    • emergency stop signal
    • sampling method
    • scupper plugs and spill readiness
    • mooring and fender arrangements
  • Why carry a personal gas meter during bunkering? H2S can be present in residual fuel vapours, and it can be dangerous at low concentrations.
  • What is the Bunker Delivery Note and who keeps it? It is the supplier's record of the fuel delivered under MARPOL Annex VI. The receiving ship retains it.
  • What do you do if a mooring line starts to fail while alongside? Warn people clear of the snap-back zone, reduce or stop pumping, inform both bridges, and re-secure or prepare to disconnect.
  • Who decides to stop a transfer? Either side can, at any time, for any safety reason.

How to get your first supply barge contract

  1. Choose the type first. Oil bunker work needs tanker training. Water, stores or deck cargo work mostly does not.
  2. Complete the right STCW courses. For oil bunker work, complete Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations before applying. Many operators will not consider a candidate without it.
  3. Target operators and manning agents in bunkering hubs and in offshore construction regions. Ask whether the vessel is convention-certified or a local harbour craft. That tells you whether your CoC will be recognised or whether you need a local licence or a flag state endorsement.
  4. Highlight transferable experience:
    • mooring work on deep-sea ships
    • bunkering from the receiving side
    • crane operation
    • tanker deck work
  5. Treat it as a deliberate career step. Some seafarers use barge work to gain tanker familiarity or to stay near home. Confirm with your administration how the sea time will count towards your next certificate.
Key points
  • "Supply barge" covers bunker, water, stores and deck cargo barges, so check which type the job is.
  • Oil bunker barges normally require tanker cargo training for anyone with cargo duties (subject to the administration's rules for harbour craft); LNG bunkering vessels need gas tanker training.
  • The main hazards are spills, H2S, hose failure, mooring snap-back alongside, and personnel transfers.
  • Fatigue from round-the-clock deliveries is a real risk; hours of rest rules (STCW/MLC or national equivalents) still apply.
  • Many barges are locally licensed harbour craft, so national rules decide manning and certificates.
  • Confirm with your administration whether barge time counts as tanker sea time before relying on it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need tanker training to work on a bunker barge?

If you will have duties related to oil cargo or cargo equipment, STCW requires Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations. Masters, chief mates, chief engineers, second engineers and anyone else with immediate responsibility for cargo need the advanced level. Exact application to small harbour craft can depend on the administration.

Does sea time on a bunker barge count towards my CoC?

It depends on your administration, the vessel's tonnage and whether its service is classed as seagoing. Harbour or port-limited service is sometimes treated differently. Get written confirmation from your administration before relying on it.

Is working on a supply barge good for a fresher or cadet?

It can give strong practical experience in mooring, hose handling and cargo transfers. However, it may not provide the approved onboard training record needed for a first CoC. Check with your training institute whether the vessel is acceptable for structured cadet training.

Can I work on a bunker barge in another country with my national CoC?

On convention ships, a foreign CoC normally needs a flag state endorsement from the flag administration. Some ports also require local licences for harbour craft crew or restrict employment. Confirm the requirements with the operator before travelling.

What is the most common cause of bunker spills from barges?

Communication failures are a frequent factor, for example on pumping rates, tank changeovers or valve line-ups. Overfilling at topping off is another. A thorough pre-transfer agreement and an agreed emergency stop procedure prevent most of them.

Test yourself

Under STCW, who needs Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations on a bunker tanker?

The advanced level is required for masters, chief engineer officers, chief mates, second engineer officers and any other person with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit or handling of cargo. STCW Regulation V/1-1 and Section A-V/1-1.

During a bunker transfer, the receiving ship asks you to reduce the pumping rate. What should you do first?

The receiving ship controls its tank line-up, so a rate reduction request is acted on at once and confirmed. This is standard ship–barge transfer practice under the company SMS (ISM Code).

Which document records the fuel supplied to a ship and is required under MARPOL Annex VI?

The supplier provides a Bunker Delivery Note, which the receiving ship retains. MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18.

Why do bunker barge crews commonly carry personal gas detectors during fuel oil transfers?

Residual fuels can release H2S, which is toxic at low concentrations, so personal monitoring is common company practice. Hazard identification under the ISM Code, Section 1.2.

An LNG bunkering vessel delivers liquefied natural gas to ships. Which STCW training applies to crew with cargo duties?

An LNG bunkering vessel is a gas carrier, so liquefied gas tanker training applies to crew with cargo duties. STCW Regulation V/1-2.
