Working on a Tugboat

Quick answer

Tugs assist ships in port, escort tankers and gas carriers, and tow barges, rigs and dead ships across oceans. The work is short, intense and close-quarters, with real hazards such as girting, towline snap-back and interaction under a ship's bow. Most jobs need a standard STCW CoC (often a near-coastal or under-500 GT one, depending on the administration) plus company training, and many harbour tug jobs go to local or national crews.

What a tug does and the main types

A tug is a small, very powerful vessel built to push or pull other craft. Its pulling power is quoted as bollard pull in tonnes, proven by a bollard pull test. Ports and charterers specify the bollard pull they need for each job.

The main trades:

  • Harbour towage: berthing and unberthing ships, and assisting through locks and narrow channels. Jobs are short, there are many per day, and the crew is usually on call.
  • Escort towage: tethered to the stern of a laden tanker or gas carrier in confined waters, ready to steer or stop the ship if it loses steering or propulsion.
  • Coastal and barge towing / pushing: moving barges, pontoons and dredging equipment.
  • Ocean towage and salvage: long-distance tows of rigs, hulks and dead ships, and emergency response. These overlap with salvage vessels and AHTS work.

By propulsion you will meet:

Type Propulsion Typical feature
Conventional Single or twin screw aft, rudder(s) Tows from a hook or winch near amidships, line leading aft; most prone to girting
ASD (azimuth stern drive) Two azimuth thrusters aft Very common harbour tug; works bow-to-bow or over the bow winch
Tractor Cycloidal (Voith) or azimuth units forward Tows over the stern; strong in escort work

The tug master's handling of the controls matters far more than on a ship, because thrust can go in any direction within seconds.

Layout and how a towing job runs

A harbour tug is mostly engine room and towing gear. Expect:

  • a compact wheelhouse with all-round visibility and control stations facing fore and aft
  • a towing winch forward and/or aft, and a towing hook on some designs
  • fendering all round
  • low freeboard on the working deck
  • limited accommodation

Many tugs also carry fire-fighting pumps and monitors for external fire-fighting.

A typical berthing assist, step by step:

  1. The tug receives the job and the pilot's VHF working channel. The master confirms the tug's position (e.g. centre lead forward, stern) and whose line will be used (tug's or ship's). This follows port and company practice.
  2. The tug approaches the ship while it is still making way. The ship's crew sends down a heaving line, and the tug's deckhand bends it on to the towline or messenger.
  3. The ship heaves the eye up and places it on a bitt. The tug crew pay out under control, then clear the deck before the line takes weight.
  4. The tug works on the pilot's orders ("push half", "pull dead slow", direction of pull). Whistle signals are the fallback if VHF fails.
  5. On release, the ship's crew must slack the line down under control. They must never throw the eye off to fall onto the tug's deck or into the water near the propellers.

If you are a ship's officer or AB on a mooring station, knowing what the tug crew needs from you makes this safer for everyone.

Key hazards on tugs

Tug accidents tend to be sudden and serious. Every tug crew must understand these:

  • Girting (girding / tripping): the towline leads out near the beam and the tow pulls sideways faster than the tug can respond. The tug is heeled over and can capsize in seconds. Conventional tugs towing from a hook near amidships are most at risk. The controls are:
    • quick-release gear on hook and winch
    • a gob rope to move the effective tow point aft
    • keeping the line within safe angles
    • keeping watertight doors closed
  • Bow interaction: working close under the bow of a ship making way exposes the tug to strong pressure and suction effects. These can pull it under the bow or across the ship's path. Ship speed when making fast is critical.
  • Snap-back: towlines under load store huge energy. Stay out of snap-back zones, and stay off the working deck once the line is fast unless you must be there.
  • Line handling: crushing at fairleads and bitts, being pulled in by a running line, and lines fouling the propellers or thrusters.
  • Man overboard and green water: low freeboard, wet decks and night work.
  • Fatigue: on-call harbour work at all hours can erode rest unless properly managed.

Emergency release of the towline must work under full load. Testing it is a routine you should see recorded on board.

Certificates and experience usually expected

There is no single international "tug certificate". What you need depends on the flag, the tug's size and its trading area:

  • CoC: deck officers need a CoC appropriate to the tonnage and area.
    • Many harbour tugs are under 500 GT and trade near-coastal, so a CoC issued under STCW Regulation II/3 or a national limited licence may apply.
    • Ocean-going tugs need certificates not restricted to near-coastal voyages. Tonnage limits may still apply depending on the tug's size.
    • Engineers need a CoC covering the propulsion power installed.
    • Check the flag's requirements, not a rumour from another port.
  • Towing-specific licences or endorsements: some administrations issue these, with documented practical assessment on towing vessels. Confirm with your administration.
  • STCW basic safety training and a medical fitness certificate are always required. Ratings need the appropriate watch rating or Able Seafarer certificate if their duties require it.
  • Company training: most operators run their own familiarisation and assessment for tug masters and mates. This often includes simulator ship-handling and a period understudying an experienced master before taking command.

What really gets you hired is relevant small-boat handling and deck work. For engineers, it is experience with medium/high-speed diesels and azimuth thrusters.

Crew and life on board

Crews are small. A harbour tug may run with only a master, an engineer and one or two deckhands. Larger or ocean tugs add a mate, more engineers, ABs and a cook. Exact manning is set by the flag's safe manning document.

What this means day to day:

  • Everyone multitasks. Deckhands handle lines, keep watch, clean, paint and help in the engine room. Engineers often stand on deck during jobs.
  • Harbour tugs often work rotations, with crew living on board or near the base and responding to job calls. Patterns vary widely between companies and countries.
  • Ocean towage means long, slow passages (towing speeds are low), heavy-weather towline management and long stretches away from port.
  • Motion is lively, the galley and cabins are small, and personal space is limited. Teamwork matters more than rank.
  • Rest hours still apply. On a seagoing tug, STCW and MLC rest-hour requirements are the same as on any other seagoing ship. Harbour tugs working only within port limits may instead fall under national working-time rules. Either way, the master must manage on-call work so that rest is respected.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners ask about towing

Candidates for deck CoCs are regularly quizzed on towing. Be ready for these:

  • What lights does a power-driven vessel towing astern show? Masthead lights in a vertical line (two if the tow is 200 m or less, three if longer), sidelights, sternlight, and a yellow towing light above the sternlight. A diamond shape is shown by day where the tow exceeds 200 m. (Rule 24)
  • When does a towing vessel also show RAM signals? When the tow severely restricts the ability of the towing vessel and tow to deviate from their course. (Rule 27)
  • What is the restricted visibility signal? One prolonged followed by two short, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. A manned towed vessel sounds one prolonged followed by three short. (Rule 35)
  • Explain girting and how to prevent it. Describe towline angle, tow point position, gob rope and quick release.
  • What should a ship's crew do when releasing a tug's line? Slack it down under control on the tug's signal, and never let it run free.
  • Why limit ship's speed when tugs make fast forward? Interaction and girting risk rise sharply with speed.

How to get your first tug contract

  • Know the market. Harbour towage is local, and many countries restrict tug jobs to national seafarers or holders of national licences. Ocean towage, salvage and offshore towing recruit more internationally.
  • Ratings: an AB with good seamanship, rope work and line-handling experience is the natural fit. Fitness and willingness to work both deck and engine are valued.
  • Deck officers: the usual route is joining as mate and progressing to master after the company's assessment. Ship-handling and pilotage-area experience helps.
  • Engineers: highlight high/medium-speed diesels, azimuth thrusters, hydraulics and fire-fighting pumps.
  • Paperwork first: make sure your CoC suits the tonnage and area. Get a flag state endorsement if the tug flies a foreign flag.
  • Use your contacts: tug crews meet pilots, agents and other operators daily, and local towage companies often hire by word of mouth.

Tug work suits people who like ship-handling, short intense jobs and small teams. It rewards skill quickly and punishes complacency just as quickly.

Key points
  • Tugs do harbour assists, escort, barge towing, ocean towage and salvage; bollard pull defines their power.
  • Girting can capsize a tug in seconds; tow point, line angle, gob rope and quick release are the controls.
  • Bow interaction and snap-back are major hazards; ship speed and deck discipline matter.
  • No universal tug certificate: CoC level depends on flag, tonnage and trading area, plus company assessment.
  • Crews are small and multi-skilled; manning is set by the safe manning document.
  • Rest-hour rules (STCW/MLC on seagoing tugs, national rules in port-only service) apply despite on-call working patterns.
  • Harbour towage jobs are often restricted to local or national crews; ocean towage recruits internationally.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a tugboat?

There is no single international tug certificate under STCW. You need a CoC suitable for the tug's tonnage, power and trading area as required by the flag, plus basic safety training and a medical certificate. Some administrations have towing-specific licences or endorsements, and most companies run their own assessment before you take command.

What is girting on a tug?

Girting (also called girding or tripping) is when a towline pulling near the beam heels the tug over so far that it can capsize. It happens quickly, especially on conventional tugs towing from a hook. Prevention relies on keeping the line within safe angles, moving the tow point aft with a gob rope, quick-release gear and watertight integrity.

How many crew work on a harbour tug?

Usually very few: often a master, an engineer and one or two deckhands, though it varies by size, flag and trade. The flag's safe manning document sets the minimum. Ocean-going tugs carry larger crews, including mates, more engineers and a cook.

Can a foreign seafarer get a job on harbour tugs?

It depends on the country. Many states restrict harbour towage to national crews or holders of national licences under cabotage rules. Ocean towage, salvage and offshore towing operators are generally more open to international crews.

Is tug experience useful for a career at sea?

Yes. Tug work builds strong ship-handling, line-handling and close-quarters judgement, which is valued in pilotage, offshore and salvage roles. Experience requirements for any specific post still depend on the employer and administration.

Test yourself

In which situation is a conventional tug most at risk of girting?

A towline leading out near the beam under load can heel and capsize the tug. This is the classic girting condition covered in towing practice and company procedures.

A power-driven vessel is towing astern with a tow length exceeding 200 m. How many masthead lights in a vertical line does it show?

Three masthead lights in a vertical line are shown when the length of tow exceeds 200 m, and two when it is 200 m or less. COLREG Rule 24(a).

What sound signal does a vessel engaged in towing make in restricted visibility?

A towing vessel sounds one prolonged followed by two short. One prolonged followed by three short is for a manned vessel being towed. COLREG Rule 35(c) and (e).

What is the main purpose of a gob rope on a conventional tug?

A gob rope pulls the towline down towards the stern. This moves the effective tow point aft and reduces the heeling lever that leads to girting (standard towing seamanship).

Under STCW A-VIII/1, what is the minimum rest a watchkeeper on a seagoing tug must have in any 24-hour period?

At least 10 hours' rest is required in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7 days, divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours. This is the same as on any other seagoing ship (STCW Code A-VIII/1; MLC Standard A2.3 rest-hours option).
