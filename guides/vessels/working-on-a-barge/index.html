Working on a Barge: A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

"Barge" covers everything from an unmanned deck barge on a towline to a self-propelled bunker barge or an offshore crane barge carrying a large mixed marine and project crew. Most manned barge work calls for your STCW basics plus cargo-specific or offshore training. Many jobs are local or regional and governed by national rules, so check your administration and the operator before you sign.

What a barge is and what it actually does

A barge is a flat-bottomed, usually box-shaped hull built to carry cargo or equipment rather than to make long passages under its own power. Many are non-self-propelled and move only when towed or pushed by a tug. Others have their own engines and steer like any small ship.

The word covers very different jobs:

Type Typical work Usually manned?
Deck barge Project cargo, steel, modules, containers, breakwater stone Often unmanned under tow
Hopper barge Dredged spoil, aggregates, bulk Varies; some self-propelled
Tank barge (oil/chemical) Coastal and inland liquid cargo, lightering Often manned or with a riding crew
Bunker barge Delivering fuel to ships at anchor or alongside Manned, often self-propelled
Crane / heavy-lift barge Offshore lifts, salvage, port construction Manned, large crew
Pipelay / construction barge Laying subsea pipe, offshore installation Manned, very large crew
Accommodation barge Housing project personnel Manned

As a seafarer, you only work on a barge if it carries crew. That is why most barge careers sit in one of three worlds: port and coastal liquid trades (bunker and tank barges), inland waterways, or offshore construction.

Layout and how the work runs

On most barges the hull is mostly void spaces and ballast or cargo tanks under a strong flat deck. Access to the voids is through manholes and small hatches. There may be a small machinery space forward or aft for generators, ballast pumps and mooring winches. Self-propelled units also have propulsion machinery.

How the operation runs depends on the type:

  • Bunker and tank barges: you come alongside the receiving ship and moor up. You connect hoses, agree the transfer plan and stop signals, then pump and monitor the cargo. There is a lot of mooring and unmooring, often several deliveries a day in busy anchorages.
  • Towed deck and hopper barges: the marine work is mostly done from the tug. If there is a riding crew, they check the towing connection, emergency towing gear, lights, lashings and void soundings.
  • Crane and pipelay barges: many are positioned on an anchor spread handled by anchor-handling tugs, though some newer units use DP. Marine crew handle the anchors and winches, ballast control, deck lifts and cargo transfer from supply vessels. The project crew handle the construction work.

One point catches people out. The marine crew and the project or client crew often answer to different superintendents. You need to know who holds authority over marine safety on that particular barge, which is normally the barge master, and how that is written in the SMS or bridging document.

Key hazards on barges

Barge work is unglamorous, and it is hazardous in specific ways.

  • Low freeboard and open decks. Green water reaches the deck easily, and many barges have minimal bulwarks. Man-overboard risk is real, especially during night mooring work. Wear your lifejacket or work vest where the SMS requires it, and stay inside the guard rails.
  • Void spaces. Barge voids are classic enclosed spaces. They can be closed for long periods, rust consumes oxygen, and tank barges can have hydrocarbon or H₂S ingress. No entry without a permit, ventilation and atmosphere testing.
  • Mooring and towlines. Snap-back from parting lines and wires, and winches running under load. Tugs can be girted if the tow sheers across.
  • Stability. High deck cargo raises the centre of gravity. Ballast mistakes, flooded voids from leaking manholes and free surface in slack tanks can all quickly reduce stability on a box-shaped hull.
  • Cargo transfer. On bunker and tank barges you face hose failures, overflows, static, toxic vapour and spill response.
  • Lifting operations. Heavy and complex lifts, tag lines, dropped objects and suspended loads over people on crane and construction barges.
  • Personnel transfer. Gangways, crew boats, personnel baskets or helicopters, depending on the job. Each has its own risks and procedures.
  • Fatigue. Long shifts and round-the-clock project work.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Requirements depend heavily on where the barge trades and which flag or national authority regulates it. Seagoing barges under a flag that applies STCW will expect the normal STCW portfolio. Inland and harbour craft are often regulated under national rules with their own licences. Check with your administration and the operator.

What employers commonly ask for:

  • STCW Basic Safety Training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR) and a valid seafarer medical certificate.
  • Security awareness or designated security duties training where the ISPS Code or company policy applies.
  • Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations, and advanced training for officers in charge of cargo, on tank and bunker barges where the administration treats them as tankers under STCW.
  • BOSIET/HUET (an industry standard, not STCW) for offshore construction barges reached by helicopter.
  • Rigging, slinging and crane operator qualifications for deck crew on lifting barges. Schemes differ by country and client, so ask which one the operator recognises.
  • A Certificate of Competency for officer roles. Some administrations issue near-coastal or harbour-craft licences that are only valid for that trade.

Experience matters as much as paper. Mooring and towing experience, anchor-handling exposure, tanker cargo watches or offshore deck work all make you far more employable.

Crew composition and life on board

Crew size ranges from a handful of people to several hundred.

  • Bunker or small tank barge: typically a master or skipper, an engineer or mechanic and a few deckhands. Some barges also have a cargo officer or pumpman.
  • Offshore crane or pipelay barge: the marine department typically includes the barge master, barge engineers, a ballast control operator, a bosun, riggers, ABs and crane operators. Catering, medic and the project crew (welders, surveyors, engineers, divers or ROV teams) sit on top of that. Titles and structures vary between contractors.

Life on board reflects the trade:

  • Port and coastal barges can mean frequent port time and sometimes daily crew changes. In some local trades you go home between shifts. Expect short-notice jobs, broken sleep and lots of mooring.
  • Offshore barges usually run 12-hour shifts on rotation, with long periods stationary at a worksite. Accommodation can be shared, and there is a strong permit-to-work culture. You live alongside a large, mixed, multinational workforce.
  • Motion: a box hull at anchor can roll sharply in a swell, which affects sleep, cooking and lifting limits.

Whether MLC 2006 applies depends on the vessel. The Convention does not apply to ships navigating exclusively in inland waters or in waters within, or closely adjacent to, sheltered waters or areas where port regulations apply (Article II), so check what protections your contract and flag actually give you.

Oral exam style: what an examiner might ask

Barges come up in orals mainly through COLREGs, towing, stability and enclosed space entry. Practise answering these out loud:

  1. "You see a tug with a barge astern by day; the tow is over 200 m. What shapes do you expect?"
    • The towing vessel shows a diamond, and so does the towed barge (Rule 24).
  2. "A tug is pushing a barge, rigidly connected. How is it lit?"
    • If it is a true composite unit, it is lit as a power-driven vessel under Rule 23.
    • If it is not rigidly connected, the pushing vessel shows two masthead lights in a vertical line, sidelights and a sternlight (Rule 24(c)), with no towing light. The pushed barge shows sidelights at its forward end (Rule 24(f)(i)).
  3. "Why are barge voids dangerous?"
    • Oxygen depletion from rusting, possible toxic or flammable ingress, and poor access and rescue routes.
    • Treat them as enclosed spaces, with a permit, ventilation, testing, standby and a rescue plan.
  4. "You load a heavy module high on a deck barge. What happens to stability?"
    • KG rises, so GM falls.
    • The deck module also adds windage and may need sea-fastening checks and a lashing plan.
  5. "What would you check before a barge tow departs?"
    • Towing arrangement and emergency towing gear.
    • Navigation lights and shapes, and watertight closures and manholes.
    • Void soundings, cargo sea-fastening, and the documented approval required by the operator, insurer or warranty surveyor.

Worked scenario: Your bunker barge is alongside a ship at anchor when the swell picks up and the moorings start surging. Follow these steps:

  1. Stop pumping using the agreed signal.
  2. Drain and disconnect the hose in line with the transfer procedure.
  3. Tend or double up the moorings.
  4. Inform the receiving ship and your office.
  5. Only resume once both masters agree it is safe. Log the stop and the reason.

How to get your first barge contract

  • Decide which barge world you want. Port bunkering, inland and coastal tank trades, and offshore construction recruit through different channels.
  • Target local operators directly. Bunker and harbour barge operators in major bunkering ports often hire locally or through specialised agencies.
  • Use offshore contractors and their manning agents for crane and pipelay barges. Have BOSIET/HUET and an offshore medical ready if the client requires them.
  • Come in through deck or rigging roles. AB, rigger or crane operator experience is the common entry point. Officers and engineers with towing, anchor-handling or tanker cargo experience transfer well.
  • Check that your licence fits the trade. Make sure your CoC, any flag endorsement or national licence, and your tanker endorsements match the barge's flag and operating area before you accept the job.
  • Ask the right questions at interview. Find out the rotation, who the marine authority is, how crew changes and transfers are done, and what training the company provides on joining.

Keep your seaman's book and sea service records accurate. Barge time is sometimes recorded differently from seagoing service. Confirm with your administration whether it counts towards your next certificate before relying on it.

Key points
  • "Barge" covers unmanned towed hulls, self-propelled bunker and tank barges, and large manned offshore crane and pipelay barges.
  • Certificate requirements depend on flag, trading area and cargo; inland and harbour barges often fall under national rules rather than STCW.
  • Barge voids are enclosed spaces: never enter without a permit, ventilation and atmosphere testing.
  • Low freeboard, mooring lines, lifting and personnel transfers are the main hazards.
  • Tank and bunker barges may require tanker training; offshore barges commonly demand BOSIET/HUET.
  • Rigging, mooring and towing experience is the most marketable background for barge work.
  • Confirm whether barge service counts as qualifying sea service before relying on it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need STCW certificates to work on a barge?

On seagoing barges flagged with an administration that applies STCW, yes: expect Basic Safety Training, a medical certificate and any cargo-specific endorsements. Inland and harbour barges are often regulated under national rules with their own licences. Check with the operator and your administration.

Does barge sea service count towards my CoC?

It depends on your administration, the vessel type and where it trades. Some service on non-propelled or inland craft may not count, or may count only partially. Get written confirmation from your administration before planning your career around it.

Do I need tanker training for a bunker barge?

Often yes. Many administrations and operators treat oil bunker barges as tankers for training purposes, so Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations is commonly expected, and advanced training for those in charge of cargo. Confirm with the flag and the company.

What is the difference between a barge master and a ship's master?

A barge master is in charge of marine operations on a barge, which on offshore construction barges means anchors, ballast, stability and marine safety while a project team runs the work. The licence required varies with the flag, the type of barge and whether it is self-propelled.

Are offshore barge jobs good for a first contract?

They can be, especially for ABs, riggers and crane operators, because crews are large and there is a lot of deck work to learn from. Expect long shifts and a strict permit-to-work culture. Offshore clients may require BOSIET/HUET and an offshore medical.

Test yourself

A tug is pushing a barge ahead, rigidly connected so that they form a composite unit. How should the unit be lit?

A rigidly connected pushing vessel and pushed vessel forming a composite unit are regarded as a power-driven vessel and lit under Rule 23. COLREG Rule 24(b).

By day, a barge is being towed and the length of tow exceeds 200 m. What shape should the towed barge show?

When the tow exceeds 200 m, the towed vessel or object shows a diamond shape, as does the towing vessel. COLREG Rule 24(a) and (e).

A void space on a barge has been closed for several months. What is the correct approach before entry?

Closed voids can be oxygen-deficient or contain toxic or flammable gas with no smell. Follow the enclosed space entry procedure in IMO Resolution A.1203(33) and the company SMS.

A heavy module is loaded high on the deck of a barge. What is the general effect on stability?

Adding weight above the existing centre of gravity raises KG, which generally reduces GM (the change in KM with draught aside). Basic ship stability principles.

Which statement about BOSIET/HUET is correct?

BOSIET/HUET is an offshore industry (OPITO) standard demanded by many offshore operators. It does not replace STCW requirements set under STCW Regulation VI/1.
