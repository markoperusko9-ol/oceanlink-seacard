Working on a Ro-Ro Ship

Quick answer

Ro-Ro ships carry wheeled and rolling cargo such as trucks, trailers, roll trailers and heavy plant, which is driven or towed on and off over ramps. The work involves fast port turnarounds, constant vehicle movement on deck, lashing, door and ramp operations, and a serious focus on vehicle-deck fire and stability. A cargo Ro-Ro needs no special STCW endorsement beyond your normal certificates, but operators prefer candidates with lashing, ferry, car carrier or short-sea experience.

What a Ro-Ro ship does and where it trades

A Ro-Ro (roll-on/roll-off) ship loads cargo on wheels instead of lifting it with cranes. Typical cargo includes:

  • Unaccompanied trailers and semi-trailers, often moved by the terminal's tug masters (tractor units)
  • Roll trailers (MAFIs) carrying steel, paper, project cargo or breakbulk
  • Accompanied freight, where the lorry driver travels with the vehicle
  • New and used cars, buses, construction and agricultural machinery
  • On con-ro designs, containers carried on an upper deck as well

This guide covers cargo Ro-Ro ships. Ships carrying more than 12 passengers are Ro-Pax and fall under passenger ship rules. Pure car carriers (PCC/PCTC) are a specialised branch with their own guide.

Trades:

  • Short-sea freight routes. Fixed liner schedules between nearby ports, with a port call often every day or two.
  • Deep-sea Ro-Ro. Long liner services carrying high-and-heavy cargo, machinery and project cargo between continents.

Layout: ramps, decks and doors

The ship is built around long, mostly open vehicle decks with few transverse bulkheads. Key features you will work with:

  • Stern ramp. Sometimes a quarter (angled) ramp so the ship can use berths without a dedicated linkspan. Some ships also have a bow door or bow visor with an inner bow ramp.
  • Internal ramps and lifts linking the decks. Some are fixed and some are hoistable.
  • Hoistable or liftable car decks that are raised or lowered to suit cargo height.
  • Lashing points (D-rings, star cups or cloverleaf sockets) set into the deck, plus portable gear such as chains, tensioners, trestles and webbing.
  • Powered ventilation for the vehicle spaces, needed to clear exhaust fumes during loading and discharge.
  • Fire protection for the cargo spaces. This is a fixed fire-extinguishing system. It may be a sectioned water-spraying (drencher) system, or on closed decks that can be sealed, often a fixed CO2 system. It works together with detection and CCTV.
  • Shell door indicators and alarms on the bridge showing whether doors and ramps are open, closed and secured.
  • Reefer sockets for refrigerated trailers.

What this means in practice:

  • Deck crew spend a lot of time maintaining hydraulics, ramp hinges, seals, cleats and locking pins.
  • Engineers carry heavy loads from hydraulic power packs, ventilation fans and reefer power.
  • The ETO looks after door sensors, CCTV and detection loops.

Key hazards and why they matter

Shell doors and watertight integrity

The Herald of Free Enterprise capsized in 1987 after leaving port with its bow doors open. The Estonia was lost in 1994 after its bow visor failed. Both were Ro-Pax, but the lesson applies to every Ro-Ro: water on a large open vehicle deck can destroy stability within minutes.

  • Treat door and ramp closing as a safety-critical checklist item.
  • Confirm closure locally and on the bridge indication before the ship sails.

Fire on vehicle decks

Cargo-space fires are a known risk on Ro-Ro ships. Common sources include:

  • Fuel and electrical faults on vehicles
  • Reefer units
  • Battery-electric vehicles, for which many operators now have specific procedures

Fires on these decks are fought mainly with the ship's fixed system, boundary cooling and controlled access. The fixed system is either a water-spraying drencher or, on closed decks that can be sealed, CO2. A CO2 release requires the space to be sealed and the ventilation stopped. Exactly how you handle the fire, including ventilation, depends on the ship's fire control plan and the company's SMS.

Free surface from firefighting water

Water from drenchers and hoses collects on decks with few bulkheads. That water creates a large free surface effect and reduces the effective GM.

  • Keep scuppers and drains clear at all times.
  • During any firefighting, watch the list and the drainage.

Moving vehicles

Tug masters reversing at speed, poor visibility and noise make the vehicle deck one of the most dangerous workplaces at sea.

  • Wear high-visibility clothing.
  • Use the marked walkways.
  • Make eye contact with drivers.
  • Never stand behind a reversing unit.

Lashing, cargo shift and slips

Cargo must be secured in line with the approved Cargo Securing Manual. Poorly secured trailers can shift in heavy weather. Lashing chains, deck sockets and oily decks also cause many trips, finger injuries and back strains.

Fumes and atmosphere

Exhaust gas builds up quickly if ventilation is not running during loading. Treat any deck with ventilation stopped as a potentially poor atmosphere.

Dangerous goods

Trailers often carry IMDG cargo. Stowage and segregation follow the IMDG Code and the ship's Document of Compliance for dangerous goods, where one is held.

Worked scenario: a typical short-sea turnaround

This is a typical sequence for an evening arrival and morning departure. The detail always follows the company SMS.

  1. Before arrival. Run the ramp hydraulics. Test the ventilation fans. Check the discharge and loading plans from the terminal, including the DG list, heavy units and reefer requirements.
  2. Alongside. The ramp is lowered under the officer's control and the ventilation is started. Lashing gangs, either stevedores or crew depending on the port, release lashings in the order of the discharge plan.
  3. Discharge. Officers and ABs supervise vehicle movements and check for damage or leaks. They keep pedestrians clear of the tug masters.
  4. Loading. The chief officer's stowage plan sets the positions for heavy units, DG trailers and reefers. Crew check that lashings meet the Cargo Securing Manual. Reefers are plugged in and their temperatures logged.
  5. Stability. Before departure, the chief officer confirms the actual loaded condition, axle and deck loads, and stability using the loading computer.
  6. Securing for sea. Close the ramp and shell doors and confirm them locally and on the bridge panel. Patrol the decks for loose gear and running engines. Report to the bridge.
  7. At sea. Carry out fire patrols of the vehicle decks as the SMS requires, with particular attention to reefers and DG units. In heavy weather, check that the lashings have not slackened.

This cycle can repeat every day. Managing hours of rest is one of the hardest parts of the job.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Mandatory training is the same as on any cargo ship:

  • STCW basic safety training
  • The proficiencies your rank needs, such as PSCRB, AFF and medical training
  • A security certificate appropriate to your duties
  • A valid CoC and flag state endorsement for officers

There is no separate STCW Ro-Ro endorsement for cargo Ro-Ro ships.

Passenger training only applies to passenger ships. The STCW passenger ship training, which includes crowd management and crisis management and human behaviour, applies to Ro-Pax and other passenger ships. It is not required on a pure cargo Ro-Ro.

What operators commonly look for:

  • Previous Ro-Ro, Ro-Pax, car carrier or container experience, with lashing familiarity
  • Comfort with frequent port calls and cargo supervision
  • Hydraulics experience, for engineers and fitters
  • Dangerous goods awareness, which some companies cover with their own familiarisation or IMDG course
  • Strong ECDIS and pilotage-water experience for officers on short-sea routes

Requirements vary between operators. Check exactly what each company asks for rather than assuming.

Crew composition and life on board

Crew size depends on the ship's size, trade, flag and safe manning document. A typical cargo Ro-Ro carries:

  • Deck: Master, Chief Officer, 2nd Officer, 3rd Officer, often a deck cadet, Bosun, ABs and OS
  • Engine: Chief Engineer, 2nd Engineer, 3rd Engineer, 4th Engineer, ETO, fitter, motormen or oilers, and sometimes an engine cadet
  • Catering: Cook and steward or messman

On some short-sea routes, lashing is done ashore. On others the crew do most of it.

Life on board

  • Short-sea service means many ports, short stays and constant cargo work. Shore leave can be limited by turnaround times.
  • Rotations vary by company. Some short-sea operators, particularly in European trades, use shorter fixed rotations than deep-sea ships.
  • Deep-sea Ro-Ro has longer sea passages, a more conventional routine and heavier project cargo.
  • Hours of rest. STCW requires at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period for watchkeepers and those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties. MLC 2006 uses the same rest limits where the flag state applies minimum hours of rest. Record your hours honestly.
  • Fatigue is a recognised risk in short-sea trades, so a firm stand on rest hours protects you as well as the ship.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners and company interviewers often ask questions like these.

  • Why is a Ro-Ro vehicle deck so sensitive to water ingress?
    • Water spreads freely across a long space with few bulkheads.
    • The large free surface reduces effective GM and can cause rapid capsize.
  • What do you check before reporting the ship secure for sea?
    • Ramps and shell doors are closed and locked, confirmed locally and on the bridge indicator.
    • Lashings are complete and loose gear is stowed.
    • Engines are off, reefers are connected and the drains are clear.
  • How are vehicle decks protected against fire?
    • Detection, CCTV and patrols.
    • A fixed fire-extinguishing system as required under SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 20. This is a water-spraying system or, for closed spaces that can be sealed, a fixed gas (CO2) system.
    • Boundary cooling and drainage management.
  • Where do you find the securing requirements for a trailer?
    • The ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual.
  • What precautions apply to a trailer carrying dangerous goods?
    • Check the DG documentation.
    • Stow and segregate according to the IMDG Code and the ship's DG compliance document.
    • Inspect for leaks and placards, and follow the emergency procedures.

How to get your first Ro-Ro contract

  1. Use licensed manning agencies. In India, use agencies licensed by the Directorate General of Shipping (RPSL). In the Philippines, use agencies licensed by the Department of Migrant Workers. Ask specifically about Ro-Ro or Ro-Pax fleets.
  2. Highlight transferable experience. Useful experience includes container lashing, car carrier, ferry or general cargo work, hydraulics, or frequent-port coastal trades.
  3. Keep documents complete and valid. This means your CoC, endorsements, medical certificate, STCW courses, seaman's book and passport. Hold visas too, if the operator's trade needs them.
  4. Consider a sister fleet. Companies running both Ro-Pax and Ro-Ro often move crew between the two. Holding the passenger ship courses can widen your options.
  5. Show you understand the risks. At interview, demonstrate awareness of door security, vehicle-deck fire and moving-vehicle safety. Companies value this more than generic answers.
Key points
  • Ro-Ro ships carry wheeled cargo loaded over ramps, from trailers and roll trailers to heavy plant.
  • Shell door and ramp closure is safety-critical; confirm it both locally and on the bridge before sailing.
  • Vehicle-deck fire and free surface from firefighting water are the defining hazards of the ship type.
  • Moving tug masters and lashing work cause most day-to-day injuries, so use walkways and high-vis.
  • Cargo Ro-Ro has no extra STCW endorsement; passenger ship courses apply only to Ro-Pax.
  • Short-sea Ro-Ro means frequent port calls, so manage hours of rest carefully.
  • Lashing, ferry, car carrier or container experience strengthens your application.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a Ro-Ro ship?

For a cargo Ro-Ro, no extra STCW endorsement is required beyond your normal certificates for your rank. If the ship carries more than 12 passengers, it is a Ro-Pax and the STCW passenger ship training applies. Some companies add their own requirements, such as a dangerous goods familiarisation course.

What is the difference between a Ro-Ro and a Ro-Pax?

A Ro-Pax carries passengers as well as rolling cargo and is treated as a passenger ship. That brings additional construction, manning and training requirements. A cargo Ro-Ro carries freight and at most 12 passengers, often lorry drivers travelling with their vehicles.

Is working on a Ro-Ro harder than on a bulk carrier or tanker?

It is different rather than harder. Short-sea Ro-Ro means frequent port calls and constant cargo supervision, so workload and fatigue can be high. In return, sea passages are short, there is less tank or hold cleaning, and on some trades rotations are shorter.

Who does the lashing on a Ro-Ro ship?

It depends on the port and the operator. Some terminals use shore lashing gangs, while on other routes the crew do the lashing. Either way, ship's officers are responsible for checking that cargo is secured in line with the ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual.

Can a deck cadet start on a Ro-Ro ship?

Yes, many Ro-Ro and Ro-Pax operators carry cadets. Cadets gain plenty of cargo, mooring and pilotage experience on these ships. Check that the company's training programme lets you complete your training record book and meets your administration's sea-service requirements.

Test yourself

What was the key safety lesson from the Herald of Free Enterprise capsize in 1987?

The ship sailed with its bow doors open and flooded through the vehicle deck. The lesson led to stronger door closure verification and bridge indication under SOLAS Chapter II-1 requirements for ro-ro passenger ships; on cargo Ro-Ros, door indication and securing arrangements follow class rules and flag requirements.

Why is firefighting water on a Ro-Ro vehicle deck a stability concern?

Water spreading freely across a long, undivided deck creates free surface effect, which reduces GM. That is why drainage and scuppers must be kept clear (SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 20).

What is the minimum rest a seafarer must have under STCW and MLC?

STCW (A-VIII/1) requires at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. MLC 2006 (Standard A2.3) uses the same rest limits where the flag state applies minimum hours of rest.

On which ships is STCW crowd management training required?

Crowd management and related passenger ship training apply to passenger ships, including Ro-Pax. They are not required on a cargo Ro-Ro (STCW Code Section A-V/2).

What should be running on a vehicle deck during loading and discharge?

Vehicle engines produce exhaust gases that build up quickly, so the powered ventilation of the space must run during cargo operations (SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 20).
