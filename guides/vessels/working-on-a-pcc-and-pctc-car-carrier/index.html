Working on a PCC and PCTC (Car Carrier)

Quick answer

A PCC (pure car carrier) or PCTC (pure car and truck carrier) is a closed ro-ro ship with many vehicle decks. Cargo drives on and off over ramps, so ports come quickly and port stays are short. The job centres on safe cargo handling, lashing, ventilation, fire safety and stability. No ship-type STCW endorsement is needed, but operators look for solid fire-fighting, stability and ro-ro awareness.

What a PCC/PCTC is and what it carries

Car carriers are the large box-shaped ships you see in vehicle export ports. They have very high freeboard, slab sides, no hatch covers and a stern quarter ramp. The hull is effectively a multi-storey car park with a bridge on the front.

  • PCC (pure car carrier): built mainly for cars. Deck heights suit passenger vehicles.
  • PCTC (pure car and truck carrier): some decks are strengthened and given extra height for trucks, buses, agricultural and construction machinery, and rolling cargo on MAFI trailers. Most of the modern fleet is PCTC.

Capacity is quoted in car equivalent units (CEU), sometimes RT43 units. Trade lanes run from manufacturing regions such as Japan, Korea, China, Europe and the Americas to markets worldwide. Many ships run liner-type rotations with several load and discharge ports per voyage.

Operators include Japanese, Korean and Scandinavian lines, and more recently Chinese owners building new tonnage. Crews are drawn from many countries, with large numbers from the Philippines and India.

Layout: decks, ramps and the equipment you will work with

Knowing the layout is half the familiarisation on a car carrier. Typical features:

  • Stern (quarter) ramp and side ramp(s): heavy hydraulic or wire-operated ramps. They double as watertight or weathertight closures, so securing and indicator checks before sailing matter.
  • Internal ramps: fixed and hinged ramps connect the decks. Some are also covers between decks, with their own securing and sealing.
  • Hoistable (liftable) decks: panels raised or lowered to change deck height for high-and-heavy cargo. They are lifted by a deck lifter or built-in system and pinned or locked in position.
  • Lashing points: D-rings or cloverleaf sockets set into each deck. Cars are secured with webbing belts or hooks, heavy units with chains and tensioners. Securing follows the ship's Cargo Securing Manual.
  • Ventilation: many large fans with dampers and fire-closing arrangements, essential for removing exhaust fumes during cargo work.
  • Ballast and anti-heeling systems: used constantly to keep the ship upright and trimmed as cargo moves between sides and decks.
  • Fixed fire-extinguishing system for the vehicle spaces: commonly CO2, with fire detection and a means of closing ventilation and openings.

Engineers spend a lot of time on ramp and deck-lift hydraulics, ventilation fans and dampers, ballast pumps and the fire systems, as well as the usual main and auxiliary machinery.

Cargo operations: how a port call actually runs

Port stays are often short, sometimes only hours. The chief officer runs cargo with a stowage plan from the operator's planners. Who does what (driving, lashing, checking) depends on port, operator and the company SMS.

A typical load call, step by step:

  1. Arrival preparations: ramp and access checks, ventilation tested, lighting on, stability condition pre-calculated from the planned cargo.
  2. Ramp lowered under the officer's control. Its landing and angle are checked against tide and draught limits.
  3. Toolbox talk with the stevedore foreman covering traffic routes, speed limits, walkways, which decks are open and the emergency signal.
  4. Driving gangs load vehicles. Crew or shore lashers secure them as each lane fills. Officers patrol for damage, correct stowage, fuel leaks, open doors or windows and lashing quality.
  5. Ballast and heel are adjusted continuously. Ramp angle and ship heel must stay within limits.
  6. Completion: final lashing check, hoistable decks and internal ramps secured, ramps closed and indicated, ventilation set for sea and stability confirmed against actual figures.

Discharge is the reverse. Watch for vehicles that won't start, flat batteries and units moved by tow or jump-start equipment.

Key hazards and how good ships manage them

Moving vehicles. Being struck by a vehicle on a dim, noisy deck is the classic car-carrier injury. Wear hi-vis, use marked walkways, never stand behind a reversing unit and keep eye contact with drivers.

Exhaust fumes. Running engines produce carbon monoxide and other gases. Ventilation must run during cargo work. Treat poorly ventilated holds as a real atmosphere hazard and carry a personal gas detector if the SMS requires it.

Fire. Each vehicle carries fuel, a battery and combustible materials, and the large open decks have little subdivision to stop a fire spreading. Several major car-carrier fires have led to total losses. The fire risk of electric vehicles is under active industry and IMO attention. Vehicles and battery-powered equipment are listed in the IMDG Code (e.g. UN 3166, UN 3171), with special provisions setting the conditions for carrying vehicles in vehicle and ro-ro spaces. Good practice:

  • regular fire rounds and detection checks
  • early alarm and boundary cooling
  • knowing exactly how to stop ventilation, close the space and release the fixed system
  • strict control of smoking and hot work in cargo spaces

Stability. High-sided ships with many decks are sensitive to cargo weight and centre of gravity, ballast and free surface, and wind heeling. Two well-known PCTC casualties, the Hoegh Osaka (2015) and the Golden Ray (2019), were found by investigators to have had insufficient stability when they departed. Base your calculations on real weights and sounded ballast, not assumptions.

Falls and trips. Lashing points, deck edges near open liftable decks, ramp hinges and wet or oily decks all cause falls.

Windage. The huge side area makes berthing, anchoring and narrow-channel transits sensitive to wind. Expect more tugs and tighter weather limits in port.

Certificates and experience operators usually expect

Unlike tankers and gas carriers, the STCW Code has no ship-type endorsement for car carriers. You need the standard certificates for your rank:

  • STCW basic training, PSCRB and security training appropriate to your duties
  • Advanced Fire Fighting and Medical First Aid for officers
  • CoC and flag state endorsement for officers; watchkeeping or able seafarer certificates for ratings
  • ECDIS generic and type-specific training for deck officers, plus BRM/ERM as your company requires
  • A valid medical fitness certificate

The passenger ship training in STCW Regulation V/2 applies to passenger ships, including ro-ro passenger ships. A car carrier carrying cargo only is not a passenger ship, so it is not mandatory, although some operators value it.

What really counts is experience and company training. Expect operator courses or onboard familiarisation on:

  • cargo securing
  • the ship's fire-fighting systems
  • stability software
  • ramp operation
  • vehicle fire response

Requirements vary by operator and flag, so check with the manning agency.

Crew and life on board

Typical crew:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, 2nd and 3rd officers, and often cadets.
  • Engine: chief engineer, 2nd, 3rd and 4th engineers, and an ETO.
  • Ratings: bosun, ABs, OSs, fitter, oilers or motormen and wipers.
  • Catering: cook and steward or messman.

Exact numbers depend on flag safe-manning and the company.

Daily life:

  • Frequent ports, little shore time. With many calls and fast turnarounds, shore leave is often limited. Port days are long and busy for the chief officer and deck crew.
  • Physical work. Expect a lot of walking up and down ramps, lashing in hot or cold holds, and noise from fans and engines.
  • Sea passages are spent on maintenance: lashing gear inspection, ramp and deck-lift upkeep, fire equipment and ventilation.
  • Accommodation is usually forward, high above the waterline, so motion in a seaway can feel lively. Ship's stores and provisions run on the liner schedule.

Many seafarers like the variety of cargo and ports. Others find the constant port pressure tiring.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners ask about car carriers

Q: Fire is reported on deck 7 of your PCTC at sea. What are your first actions as OOW? Raise the general alarm and inform the master. Muster the fire party and start boundary cooling. Prepare to stop the ventilation and close openings for the affected space. Ensure everyone is accounted for before any fixed-system release, which is the master's decision under the SMS.

Q: Why is ventilation critical during cargo work? To remove exhaust gases, including carbon monoxide, and fuel vapour. It protects people and reduces fire risk.

Q: What document governs lashing of vehicles on board? The ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual, required under SOLAS, with guidance from the CSS Code.

Q: What can go wrong with a departure stability calculation on a car carrier? - Wrong cargo weights or vertical positions - Ballast figures not matching soundings - Ignoring free surface - Not checking wind heeling criteria

Q: What do you check before sailing on ramps and doors? That they are closed and secured, cleats or locks are engaged, indicator panels show closed, and the closure is recorded in the log.

How to get your first car carrier contract

  • Use licensed manning agencies that crew for car-carrier operators: DMW-licensed in the Philippines, RPSL-licensed in India. Ask directly whether they have PCC/PCTC fleets.
  • Highlight relevant experience. Ro-ro, ro-pax, cargo securing, fire-team leadership and stability software all count. For engine ranks, hydraulics and ventilation systems help.
  • Cadets: some car-carrier operators sponsor cadet programmes through their manning partners. That is often the cleanest route into the fleet.
  • Prepare for the interview. Expect questions on vehicle-space fire-fighting, CO2 release procedure, stability and lashing. The oral exam questions above are a good start.
  • Keep documents current. Have your CoC, endorsements, medical and visas valid. Liner trades to the US may need a C1/D visa.
  • Ask about the training matrix before joining, and take familiarisation seriously. The layout of a 12- or 13-deck car carrier takes days to learn properly.
Key points
  • PCCs and PCTCs are closed multi-deck ro-ro ships. PCTCs add strengthened, higher decks for trucks and heavy rolling cargo.
  • Port calls are frequent and short, and the chief officer runs cargo from a planner's stowage plan.
  • The main hazards are moving vehicles, exhaust fumes, cargo-space fire, falls and stability errors.
  • Ventilation, fire rounds and fixed-system knowledge are critical on vehicle decks.
  • No STCW ship-type endorsement exists for car carriers; standard rank certificates plus company training apply.
  • Base stability calculations on actual cargo weights and sounded ballast, and check wind heeling criteria.
  • Ro-ro, fire-fighting and cargo securing experience help you get a first contract.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a car carrier?

There is no STCW ship-type endorsement for car carriers as there is for tankers. You need the standard certificates for your rank. Operators add their own familiarisation and cargo or fire training. Exact requirements depend on the company and flag.

What is the difference between a PCC and a PCTC?

A PCC is designed mainly for cars, so most decks have low clearance. A PCTC has some strengthened, higher decks, often with hoistable sections, so it can also carry trucks, buses, machinery and other high-and-heavy rolling cargo.

Do crew lash the cars on a car carrier?

It depends on the port and operator. In many ports shore lashing gangs secure the cargo, while in others the ship's crew do part or all of it. Officers remain responsible for checking that securing follows the Cargo Securing Manual before sailing.

Is STCW ro-ro passenger ship training required on a PCTC?

The passenger ship training in STCW Regulation V/2 applies to passenger ships, including ro-ro passenger ships. A car carrier carrying cargo only is not a passenger ship, so it is not mandatory. Some operators may still ask for or value it.

Why are car carrier fires so dangerous?

Every vehicle carries fuel, a battery and combustible materials. The long open decks have limited subdivision and need large ventilation systems, so fire can spread quickly. Early detection, closing ventilation and correct use of the fixed system are vital.

Test yourself

What is the main reason ventilation fans run in vehicle decks during loading?

Running engines release carbon monoxide and other gases, and fuel vapour adds fire risk. Ro-ro space ventilation requirements sit in SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 20.

Which document should govern how vehicles are secured on board?

SOLAS requires an approved Cargo Securing Manual for ships carrying cargo units, including vehicles. The CSS Code gives supporting guidance.

Which STCW requirement applies to a car carrier carrying only cargo?

STCW has no car-carrier endorsement. Regulation V/2 covers passenger ships, including ro-ro passenger ships, not cargo-only PCTCs.

Investigations into the Hoegh Osaka and Golden Ray found which common factor?

Both investigations found the ships had departed with insufficient stability, which shows the importance of accurate cargo and ballast data (IS Code 2008 criteria).

Before releasing the fixed CO2 system into a burning vehicle deck, what must be done?

Fixed gas systems need the space closed and everyone accounted for before release. The master decides under the SMS, in line with SOLAS Chapter II-2 and the FSS Code.
