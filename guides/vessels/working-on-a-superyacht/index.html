Working on a Superyacht

Quick answer

A superyacht is a large private or charter yacht, usually taken as 24 m load line length and above, crewed by professionals whose job is to run the vessel safely and look after the owner and guests. You need STCW basic training and a recognised medical as a minimum. Officers and engineers need a CoC that the flag accepts for that size of yacht. Expect seasonal work, long guest-on hours, high presentation standards, and a hiring market driven by crew agencies and personal contacts in a few yachting hubs.

What a superyacht is and how it trades

A superyacht carries people for pleasure, not cargo for freight. There is no single legal definition. The industry and most flag rules treat yachts of 24 m load line length and above as "large yachts". Above that line, very different rules apply depending on how the yacht is used:

  • Private yachts are used only by the owner and their guests and are not engaged in trade. SOLAS and the Load Line Convention exclude pleasure yachts not engaged in trade, so much depends on flag requirements.
  • Commercial (charter) yachts are let to paying guests. These are regulated as commercial vessels. Many flags use the Red Ensign Group (REG) Yacht Code or their own equivalent.

The guest limit matters. A ship carrying more than 12 passengers is a passenger ship under SOLAS. That is why most charter yachts are certified for 12 guests or fewer. Larger "passenger yachts" carrying more than 12 guests fall under separate provisions, such as Part B of the REG Yacht Code.

The typical annual pattern runs like this:

  • Mediterranean in summer.
  • Caribbean or Bahamas in winter.
  • One or more shipyard periods for refit and survey.
  • Some yachts do world cruising or expedition voyages, including high-latitude trips.

You will spend a lot of time at anchor off beaches and in marinas. You will spend far less time on long ocean passages than on a merchant ship.

Layout, operations and the "cargo"

The "cargo" is the guests and their experience. Operations revolve around them.

A motor yacht typically has:

  • guest accommodation and saloons on the main and upper decks
  • the owner's suite, often forward on the main deck or on its own deck
  • crew accommodation and the crew mess low down, usually forward
  • a galley and laundry
  • a garage or lazarette for tenders and water toys
  • a swim platform aft

Larger yachts may also have a helideck, a beach club, a spa or a pool. Sailing yachts add rigs, sails and deck hardware that need constant work aloft.

Day-to-day operations include:

  • Tender operations – launching and recovering tenders by crane or from the garage, and running guest transfers ashore, often at night.
  • Water toys – personal watercraft, towables, dive gear and paddleboards, all with guests in the water close to the yacht.
  • Anchoring and stern-to mooring in crowded Mediterranean ports.
  • Bunkering of fuel and, for tenders, often petrol.
  • Maintenance to a very high cosmetic standard – varnish, paint, stainless steel and teak. Detailing is a large part of deck crew work.

Key hazards on board

The glamour hides real risk. The main hazards are:

  • Guests in the water. Swimmers near tenders, personal watercraft and the yacht's own propellers and thrusters are among the most serious risks. Never manoeuvre or start machinery until you have confirmed the water is clear. Exhaust outlets near the swim platform can expose swimmers to carbon monoxide.
  • Tender driving. Speed, darkness, other traffic and guests who have been drinking are a bad combination. Many serious yacht incidents involve tenders and small boats rather than the yacht itself.
  • Petrol storage and handling. Tender fuel is far more volatile than diesel. Ventilation, approved storage and no ignition sources are essential.
  • Fire. Interiors carry a heavy fire load of timber, fabric and furnishings. Detection, fixed systems and drills matter as much as on any ship.
  • Working aloft and overside. This means masts and rigs on sailing yachts, and hull detailing from tenders or stages. Use the permit to work and fall arrest the same as on a merchant ship.
  • Crane and passerelle operations. Hydraulic systems, swinging loads and guest walkways all need controlled operation.
  • Fatigue. Long guest-on days and night tender runs erode rest hours. Rest hour rules still apply where MLC 2006 and STCW are in force, and records must be honest.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Minimum for everyone:

  • STCW basic training: PST, FPFF, EFA and PSSR.
  • A seafarer medical certificate acceptable to the flag. Many employers specifically ask for the UK ENG1 because so many yachts are Red Ensign flagged. Check what the yacht's flag accepts.
  • Valid passport, seaman's book if your country issues one, and the visas for the cruising area.

Commonly requested extras:

  • Seafarers with Designated Security Duties (PDSD). Commercial yachts of 500 GT and above on international voyages operate under the ISPS Code.
  • Food hygiene and safety certificates for galley and interior crew. Chefs on commercial yachts may need a ship's cook qualification under the flag's MLC rules.
  • Powerboat or tender-driving qualifications for deck crew.

Officers and engineers:

Route What it means
Merchant STCW CoC Generally usable on yachts. You need a flag state endorsement and must work within the CoC's limits.
Yacht-restricted CoC (e.g. MCA Officer of the Watch / Master Yachts) Valid only on yachts within its tonnage limit. Not valid on merchant ships.
Yacht engineering route (e.g. MCA Approved Engine Course and yacht engineer certificates) This structure has been revised in recent years. Check the current MCA notices before planning training.

The tonnage limits, sea service rules and which flags accept which CoCs differ by administration. Confirm with the yacht's flag before accepting a position.

Typical crew and who does what

A 30 m yacht may have four or five crew. A 100 m yacht may have well over fifty.

  • Deck: captain, chief officer, second/third officers on larger yachts, bosun and deckhands. Deckhands handle mooring, tenders, water toys, guest transfers and exterior care.
  • Engineering: chief engineer, second engineer, sometimes a third engineer. Larger yachts may also carry an ETO and an AV/IT technician.
  • Interior: chief steward/stewardess (or purser on large yachts) and stewards/stewardesses. They cover service, housekeeping and laundry.
  • Galley: head chef, sous chef and crew chef on bigger yachts.
  • Specialists: some large yachts add a dive instructor, nurse, spa therapist or security team.

Compared with merchant ships, crews are mixed in nationality, gender and background. Many have no prior maritime career at all. A merchant-trained officer or engineer brings real safety and watchkeeping value. The interior and guest-facing skills you will have to learn on the job.

Life on board

  • Guest-on vs guest-off. With guests aboard, days are long and the schedule is set by the guests. Off-charter periods are steadier maintenance routine.
  • Presentation. Uniform, grooming and discretion are part of the job. Owners and guests expect privacy, and confidentiality agreements are common.
  • Accommodation. Crew cabins are usually shared and compact. Food is generally good because the crew eat what the galley produces.
  • Contracts and leave. These vary widely. Some large yachts run rotation; others employ crew year-round with leave entitlement. On commercial yachts, MLC 2006 applies, covering seafarers' employment agreements, rest hours, repatriation and complaints. A purely private yacht may sit outside some of these rules depending on flag. Read your contract carefully.
  • Turnover. Crew change yachts frequently. A stable record of seasons served helps your next application more than many short stints.

Getting your first superyacht contract

  1. Get your paperwork in order first. You need valid STCW basic training, an accepted medical, a passport with plenty of validity, and the visas for where the yachts are. For Indian and Filipino seafarers, the Schengen and US visa position is often the biggest barrier. Get proper advice on which visa category applies to your situation before you travel.
  2. Check your national overseas-employment rules. Some countries regulate how their seafarers are recruited abroad. Examples are the DMW processing requirements in the Philippines and DG Shipping's rules on licensed recruitment agencies in India. Comply with them.
  3. Write a yacht-style CV. Keep it to one or two pages with a professional photo. Highlight relevant skills such as tender handling, mooring, hospitality, cooking, carpentry, varnishing or technical trades.
  4. Register with established crew agencies and use the main hubs: Antibes, Palma and Fort Lauderdale are the best known. Hiring peaks before the Mediterranean and Caribbean seasons.
  5. Use your merchant background. Engineers and officers with STCW CoCs are often in demand. Get the flag state endorsement process moving early.
  6. Take daywork. Short-term deck or refit work is a common route to a permanent position because captains hire people they have seen working.

Watch for scams. Treat any "agency" that charges you to be placed with suspicion. MLC 2006 (Standard A1.4) requires that no fees or other charges for recruitment or placement are borne by the seafarer, directly or indirectly.

Oral exam style: questions an examiner might ask

Q: A charter yacht wants to carry 14 guests. What changes? She would exceed the 12-passenger threshold. On international voyages she becomes a passenger ship for SOLAS purposes, and in any case she needs certification under passenger yacht (e.g. REG Yacht Code Part B, up to 36 passengers) or passenger ship standards.

Q: You are deck officer and guests are swimming off the stern. The captain wants to reposition. What do you do? 1. Recall all swimmers and account for every person by name. 2. Check that tenders and toys are clear. 3. Post a lookout on the swim platform. 4. Only then confirm to the bridge that propellers and thrusters can be used.

Q: Your OOW (Yachts) CoC is valid up to a stated tonnage. Can you sail as OOW on a merchant bulk carrier? No. A yacht-restricted CoC is limited to yachts.

Q: What precautions apply to tender petrol? - Approved, ventilated storage away from ignition sources. - Controlled refuelling procedure. - Spill kit ready. - Fire-fighting arrangements as required by the flag.

Key points
  • Superyachts are usually taken as yachts of 24 m load line length and above, and are either private or commercial (charter).
  • More than 12 passengers makes a ship a passenger ship under SOLAS, so most charter yachts take 12 guests or fewer.
  • STCW basic training and a flag-accepted medical are the minimum; PDSD and tender qualifications are commonly asked for.
  • Merchant STCW CoCs generally work on yachts with flag endorsement; yacht-restricted CoCs do not work on merchant ships.
  • The biggest hazards are guests in the water, tender operations, petrol, fire load and fatigue.
  • Hiring runs through crew agencies, daywork and contacts in hubs such as Antibes, Palma and Fort Lauderdale.
  • MLC 2006 applies on commercial yachts; check your contract, and never pay to be placed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I work on a superyacht with my merchant navy CoC?

Generally yes. A merchant STCW CoC is widely accepted on yachts, but you will usually need a flag state endorsement from the yacht's flag. You must also work within the capacity and tonnage limits of your certificate. Check with the flag administration before joining.

Do I need an ENG1 medical to work on yachts?

Many yacht employers ask for the UK ENG1 because a large share of yachts are Red Ensign flagged. The legal requirement is a medical certificate accepted by the yacht's flag state, and some flags accept other national STCW medicals. Confirm with the employer and flag before paying for an extra medical.

Is a yacht CoC valid on cargo ships?

No. Yacht-restricted CoCs, such as the MCA Officer of the Watch (Yachts) or Master (Yachts), are limited to yachts within their stated tonnage. If you might return to merchant ships, keep your merchant CoC valid and revalidated.

Does the Maritime Labour Convention apply on superyachts?

MLC 2006 applies to ships ordinarily engaged in commercial activities, so commercial charter yachts are covered. Purely private yachts may fall outside it depending on how the flag applies the Convention. Read your employment agreement carefully either way.

What is the best way for a first-timer to get hired on a yacht?

Have your STCW basic training, medical, passport and visas sorted before you look. Then register with established crew agencies and be present in a yachting hub before the season. Taking daywork on refits and deliveries is a common way to get noticed by captains.

Test yourself

Above what number of passengers does a ship become a passenger ship under SOLAS?

SOLAS defines a passenger ship as one carrying more than twelve passengers, which is why most charter yachts are limited to 12 guests. Reference: SOLAS 1974, Chapter I, Regulation 2.

A deck officer holds an Officer of the Watch (Yachts) CoC. Where may they serve as OOW?

Yacht-restricted CoCs are limited to yachts and to the tonnage stated on the certificate; they are not valid on merchant ships. Reference: issuing administration's yacht CoC conditions (e.g. MCA yacht certification notices); limitations endorsed on the CoC per STCW Regulation I/2.

Guests are swimming off the swim platform. What is the main extra hazard from the yacht's own systems?

Exhaust outlets near the stern can expose swimmers to carbon monoxide, and propellers and thrusters must not be used until the water is confirmed clear. Reference: flag/REG Yacht Code guidance on swimming and water-sports operations and the yacht's safety management procedures.

Which commercial yachts normally have to comply with the ISPS Code?

The ISPS Code applies to ships of 500 GT and upwards on international voyages, which captures large commercial yachts; private pleasure yachts not in trade fall outside SOLAS. Reference: SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and ISPS Code Part A.

A crew 'agency' asks you for a placement fee before it will forward your CV. What does MLC 2006 say?

MLC 2006 requires that fees or other charges for recruitment or placement are not borne by seafarers, directly or indirectly. Reference: MLC 2006, Regulation 1.4 and Standard A1.4.
