Working on a Platform Supply Vessel (PSV)

Quick answer

A Platform Supply Vessel carries deck cargo, bulk liquids and dry bulk powders between a supply base and offshore oil, gas and renewables installations. It usually holds position alongside the installation on dynamic positioning (DP) while cargo is worked. Expect DP training, offshore-specific inductions and close-quarters deck work, with requirements that vary by region, client and company.

What a PSV does and where it trades

A PSV is the delivery truck of the offshore industry. It loads at a supply base, sails to drilling rigs, production platforms, FPSOs or construction sites, and works cargo while the installation's crane lifts deck cargo and hoses transfer bulk products.

Typical cargoes:

  • Deck cargo: containers, tubulars (drill pipe and casing), cargo baskets, chemical tanks, tools and provisions
  • Bulk liquids below deck: marine gas oil (fuel for the installation), drill water, potable water, liquid mud (water- or oil-based), base oil and brines. Some vessels are fitted to carry products such as methanol in dedicated tanks.
  • Dry bulk: cement, barite and bentonite carried in pressurised tanks and blown to the installation with compressed air
  • Backload: empty containers, waste skips, returned mud and slops, and sometimes contaminated equipment

A PSV is not an anchor handler. Anchor handling, towing and rig moves belong to the AHTS. Many PSVs also do standby, oil-recovery or fire-fighting work if they are equipped and certified for it.

PSVs work wherever there is offshore activity. The main areas are the North Sea, the Gulf of Mexico, Brazil, West Africa, the Middle East, India's western offshore fields and South-East Asia. Each region has its own client standards, and these often shape your daily work more than the flag does.

Layout and how the ship is driven

The layout is easy to recognise:

  • Accommodation and bridge are well forward.
  • A long, open, low-freeboard cargo deck runs aft.
  • Crash rails or cargo rails along both sides protect the deck crew and give them safe havens to step into.

The bridge has a forward conning position for passage and an aft-facing console for DP and joystick work. From the aft console the officer looks straight down onto the cargo deck and the installation.

Below deck you will find a large number of tanks with their own pumps and piping. There are separate systems for:

  • fuel
  • water
  • mud
  • brine
  • dry bulk, with dedicated air compressors

Many modern PSVs are diesel-electric. Several generator sets feed a switchboard that drives azimuth thrusters aft and tunnel or retractable thrusters forward. This gives the redundancy needed for DP. Some newer vessels run on LNG or carry battery hybrid systems.

DP equipment class is defined in the IMO DP guidelines:

  • Class 1: no redundancy
  • Class 2: a single fault in active components should not cause loss of position
  • Class 3: also covers loss of a compartment from fire or flood

Many PSVs are DP2, but clients specify what they require.

Working cargo at the installation: step by step

The exact procedure comes from the company SMS and the installation operator. The general sequence looks like this:

  1. Planning at the base. The deck cargo plan, bulk tank allocation and backload are agreed. Lifting points, cargo securing and dangerous goods documentation are checked before sailing.
  2. Pre-arrival checks. Before entering the installation's safety zone (normally up to 500 m around the structure), the bridge completes the 500 m zone checklist:
    • DP and thruster checks
    • Power and redundancy set-up
    • Communications with the installation
    • Weather assessment
    • Confirmation of the activity-specific operating guidelines (often called ASOG or WSOG), which state when to stop work or move away.
  3. Permission to enter. The installation control room grants entry. The vessel sets up on DP at a safe stand-off, verifies DP performance, then closes in to the working position.
  4. Choosing the side. Wherever possible the vessel works on the side where the environment would push her away from the installation (drift-off side) rather than towards it. Exceptions need a clear risk assessment.
  5. Lifting. The deck crew hook and unhook loads using approved methods such as hooks or push-pull sticks. Nobody stands under or beside a suspended load. Crew withdraw to a safe haven before each lift, and a single designated person talks to the crane.
  6. Bulk transfer. The installation lowers the hose, it is connected, and pumping starts only when both sides confirm line-up. Rate and quantity are monitored, with clear stop signals and emergency disconnection agreed.
  7. Departure. Hoses go back, the vessel moves out to stand-off and leaves the zone. The installation is informed when she is clear.

Key hazards on a PSV

  • Deck cargo work in a seaway. The crane is on a fixed platform but your deck is moving, so loads can swing, snatch or land unexpectedly. Line-of-fire injuries and crushing between cargo units are the classic PSV accidents.
  • Green water on deck. The low freeboard aft means seas can sweep the cargo deck in poor weather. Access to the deck during passage is controlled.
  • Loss of position. Drift-off (losing thrust) or drive-off (the system pushing the vessel the wrong way) close to an installation can cause a collision. This is why DP trials, ASOGs and alert levels exist.
  • Bulk products:
    • Oil-based mud, base oil and some brines and chemicals are harmful to skin and eyes.
    • Pressurised dry-bulk systems can fail violently.
    • Hose failures cause spills.
    • Check the safety data sheet for each product and follow your SMS on PPE.
  • Tank cleaning. Mud and brine tanks need regular cleaning. They are enclosed spaces and can contain toxic or oxygen-deficient atmospheres, so a full permit, atmosphere testing and standby arrangements apply.
  • Backload contents. Returned cargo can be contaminated or mis-declared. Treat unknown units with caution and report anomalies.
  • Fatigue. Short trips and repeated operations make rest difficult. Hours of rest rules under STCW and MLC still apply in full.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your STCW basics are the foundation:

  • CoC for your rank
  • Basic safety training
  • Security
  • Valid medical
  • Flag endorsement

On top of that, the following are commonly asked for. The precise list depends on the client, region and company.

Item Who usually needs it Notes
DP certificate Deck officers on DP watch The Nautical Institute scheme is the most widely recognised; others exist. DP is not a STCW CoC requirement, but clients normally demand it.
BOSIET/HUET or regional equivalent Often all crew in some regions Commonly required where helicopter transfers or client rules apply, e.g. parts of the North Sea. Not universal.
Offshore-specific medical Region-dependent Some sectors require a medical to their own standard in addition to the flag medical.
High voltage training Engineers and ETOs on HV diesel-electric plant STCW includes HV competences for engineers and ETOs; company requirements vary.
Rigging, slinging or banksman courses Deck ratings and officers Company or client requirement; content varies.

STCW Part B also contains guidance on training masters and officers on offshore supply vessels and DP personnel. Some administrations apply this guidance, so check what your flag expects.

Previous offshore experience matters a great deal. Clients often look at time on DP vessels, not just total sea time.

Crew and life on board

Crew numbers are small compared with deep-sea ships. A typical complement is:

  • Master
  • Chief Officer
  • One or two further deck officers, who are often also DP operators
  • Chief Engineer, plus one or two engineers and sometimes an ETO
  • A handful of ABs
  • A cook

Exact manning is set by the flag's minimum safe manning document and client requirements.

Watches. Many PSVs run two watches, with officers and ratings on 12-hour days split into two periods. This is a common offshore pattern, but the SMS and the rest-hour rules decide what is lawful on your ship.

Rotations. Rotations are usually shorter and more regular than on deep-sea trades, with crew change at the supply base. The pattern depends heavily on region and company.

Pace and team. Port calls are frequent and short. Weather windows can mean long waits at a field followed by intense working periods. The small crew means everyone pitches in, and your competence is visible to the whole ship and to the client.

Rules and inspections. Clients run their own inspections and audits. Expect strict PPE rules, toolbox talks before every operation, and stop-work authority that is genuinely expected to be used.

How to get your first PSV contract

  1. Get the DP induction early (deck officers). If you are aiming for DP work, take the induction course first. Then log DP sea time properly in a recognised logbook. Without logged time you cannot progress through the scheme.
  2. Get the regional basics. Check what the region you are targeting expects, for example BOSIET or an offshore medical. Do not pay for courses a company will not recognise. Ask first.
  3. Target offshore operators and manning agents. Look for agencies that crew offshore fleets in India, the Philippines, the Middle East and South-East Asia. Regional fleets often take officers with deep-sea CoCs and train them up.
  4. Use related experience. Tug, harbour, AHTS, crew boat or dredger time shows you can handle close-quarters work. Ratings with crane, rigging or cargo handling experience have an advantage.
  5. Accept a step down if needed. Many officers join as a junior or trainee DPO, or engineers join one rank lower, to get offshore time on the CV.
  6. Prepare for a practical interview. Offshore interviews focus on DP, 500 m zone procedures, cargo safety and hazard awareness rather than theory alone.

Oral exam and interview questions

Q: What do you check before entering the 500 m zone? Complete the company's 500 m checklist:

  • DP system and position reference status
  • Thrusters and power configuration for the required redundancy
  • Communications with the installation
  • Weather against the ASOG limits
  • Deck crew briefed

Then get permission to enter.

Q: Which side of the installation would you prefer to work? The drift-off side, where wind, sea and current would carry the vessel away from the structure if position were lost. Working the drift-on side needs extra assessment and is avoided where possible.

Q: What is the difference between drift-off and drive-off?

  • Drift-off: the vessel loses thrust and the environment moves her.
  • Drive-off: the thrusters actively drive her off position, for example because of a faulty position reference or control error.

Q: A load is coming down and your AB is between two containers. What do you do? Stop the lift immediately by the agreed signal. Get the AB to a safe haven, then resume only once the deck is clear and the person controlling the lift confirms.

Q: Why is mud tank cleaning treated as high risk? The tank is an enclosed space that may hold toxic vapours or lack oxygen. It needs a permit, ventilation, gas testing, a standby person and a rescue plan.

Key points
  • A PSV carries deck cargo, bulk liquids and dry bulk powders to offshore installations and returns backload.
  • Cargo is normally worked on DP inside the installation's safety zone, after a 500 m checklist and permission to enter.
  • Line-of-fire injuries during lifting are the classic PSV accident; always use safe havens.
  • Mud and brine tank cleaning is enclosed space entry and needs full permit control.
  • A DP certificate is usually expected for deck officers, and regional extras such as BOSIET depend on client and area.
  • Crews are small, rotations are often shorter than deep-sea, and hours of rest rules still apply in full.
  • Logged DP time and related close-quarters experience are what open the door to a first contract.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a PSV and an AHTS?

A PSV is built to carry cargo: deck cargo plus bulk liquids and powders. An AHTS has a powerful towing winch and stern roller for anchor handling, towing and rig moves. It also carries some supply cargo, but anchor work is its main role.

Do I need a DP certificate to work on a PSV?

DP is not a STCW certificate of competency requirement, but most clients expect deck officers on DP watch to hold a recognised DP certificate. Ratings and engineers do not normally need one. Check the specific company and client requirement before booking courses.

Do PSV crew need BOSIET and HUET?

It depends on the region and client. Where crew may travel by helicopter or client rules demand it, as in parts of the North Sea, it is commonly required. Many fleets that crew-change at the supply base do not ask for it.

Can a deep-sea officer move to PSVs?

Yes, many do. Your CoC remains valid, but you will usually need DP training, offshore inductions and sometimes a step down in rank to gain offshore experience first.

What are the watch hours like on a PSV?

Many PSVs operate two watches with crew working 12-hour days split into periods, but the actual pattern is set by the company SMS. Whatever the pattern, the STCW and MLC minimum hours of rest must be met and recorded.

Test yourself

Where is the bridge console used for DP and close-quarters cargo work on a typical PSV?

PSVs have an aft-facing bridge console so the officer can see the cargo deck and installation while on DP or joystick. Reference: typical OSV design practice.

When working alongside an installation, which side should a PSV normally prefer?

Working on the drift-off side means a loss of position carries the vessel away from the structure. Reference: industry offshore marine operations guidance and company/installation ASOG practice.

What does DP equipment Class 2 mean?

Class 2 tolerates single failures of active components; Class 3 additionally covers loss of a compartment. Reference: MSC.1/Circ.1580.

A crew member is asked to enter a liquid mud tank for cleaning. What applies?

Cargo tanks are enclosed spaces that may hold toxic or oxygen-deficient atmospheres. Reference: SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 and IMO enclosed space entry recommendations.

What is drive-off?

Drive-off is powered movement off position caused by a system fault, unlike drift-off, where thrust is lost. Reference: industry DP guidance (e.g. IMCA/MTS DP operations guidance).
