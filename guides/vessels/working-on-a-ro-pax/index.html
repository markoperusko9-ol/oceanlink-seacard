Working on a Ro-Pax: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

A Ro-Pax carries wheeled cargo and more than 12 passengers, usually on short, scheduled routes with fast turnarounds. Beyond your normal CoC and basic training, you will need the passenger ship training in STCW Regulation V/2 that matches your role, such as emergency familiarisation, crowd management, crisis management and, for key roles, passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training. Expect frequent port calls and drills, a large hotel department, and a strong focus on watertight integrity, vehicle-deck fire and stability.

What a Ro-Pax is and where it trades

A Ro-Pax is a roll-on/roll-off passenger ship. Vehicles drive on and off through bow, stern or side doors and ramps, and the ship carries more than 12 passengers, which makes it a passenger ship under SOLAS. In SOLAS terms it is a ro-ro passenger ship, and several regulations apply only to this type.

Typical trades:

  • Short-sea and strait crossings with a few hours' sailing and several round trips a day.
  • Overnight routes with passenger cabins, restaurants and shops, where the ship is part ferry and part small hotel.
  • Domestic inter-island services, which are common in the Philippines, Indonesia, Greece and Japan.
  • Freight-heavy routes, where most of the vehicle deck carries trailers and the passenger side is mainly lorry drivers.

The layout is broadly the same across the type. Vehicle decks sit low in the ship, often with hoistable mezzanine car decks. Passenger decks, accommodation and the bridge sit above them. The engine room is below the main vehicle deck, often with twin screws, controllable pitch propellers and bow thrusters so the ship can berth quickly without tugs. Many ships also have stern thrusters and high-lift rudders.

Cargo operations and the key hazards

Ro-Pax operations are a race against the timetable. Discharge, load, lash and sail can take as little as an hour. Most serious accidents start when that pressure beats procedure.

Hull openings and watertight integrity. The Herald of Free Enterprise capsized in 1987 after leaving port with her bow doors open. The Estonia sank in 1994 after her bow visor failed in heavy weather. Today's rules followed from both accidents:

  • Door indicators on the bridge.
  • Television surveillance and water-leakage detection for vehicle decks.
  • Damage stability standards.
  • Clear procedures for confirming that doors are closed and secured before sailing.

The exact equipment and checks depend on the ship's age, flag and company SMS.

Stability. A large undivided vehicle deck is very vulnerable to free surface effect. A small amount of water spread across it, whether from a leak, sea ingress or fire-fighting, can sharply reduce GM. You must understand the vehicle deck drainage, scuppers and the effect of drencher water.

Vehicle deck fire. Car and truck fires, including electric vehicles and reefer trailer units, are a recognised risk. Ro-ro spaces have fixed fire-extinguishing arrangements, usually water-based drenchers, along with fire detection and fire patrols at sea as the SMS requires. Many companies have extra procedures for electric vehicles and alternative-fuel vehicles.

Moving traffic and atmosphere. Common hazards on the vehicle deck include:

  • Being struck by vehicles or tugmasters.
  • Crush points at ramps and hoistable decks.
  • Exhaust fumes.
  • Falls from mezzanine decks.
  • Noise that drowns out warnings.

High-visibility clothing and controlled traffic marshalling are standard. Ventilation must run during loading.

Dangerous goods. Trailers can carry dangerous goods under the IMDG Code. Some regions apply special arrangements for short international voyages. Stowage and segregation must comply with the IMDG Code and the ship's Document of Compliance for dangerous goods, and the dangerous goods manifest or stowage plan must accurately record what is on board.

Passengers. Passengers are normally not allowed on vehicle decks at sea. Head counts must be accurate, and every crew member's emergency role in moving and controlling large numbers of people actually matters.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Start with your rank's normal documents: CoC or proficiency certificate, STCW basic training, security training, medical fitness and, for officers, the usual navigation or engineering courses.

On top of these, STCW Regulation V/2 and Section A-V/2 of the Code set out passenger ship training. V/2 applies to passenger ships engaged on international voyages; administrations decide whether and how it applies to domestic services. In summary:

Training Broadly who needs it
Passenger ship emergency familiarisation All personnel on board, appropriate to their capacity, duties and responsibilities, before being assigned to shipboard duties
Safety training for personnel providing direct service to passengers Crew serving passengers in passenger spaces
Crowd management Personnel designated on the muster list to assist passengers in emergencies
Crisis management and human behaviour Master, chief mate, chief engineer, second engineer and anyone designated with responsibility for passenger safety in emergencies
Passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity Master, chief mate, chief engineer, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for embarking or disembarking passengers, cargo loading or securing, or closing hull openings on ro-ro passenger ships

Some of these must be refreshed, or proof of continued competence shown, at intervals not exceeding five years. Your administration decides how course content and refreshers are delivered, so check with DG Shipping, MARINA or your flag state.

Other items you will often see:

  • Proficiency in Fast Rescue Boats. SOLAS requires ro-ro passenger ships to carry a fast rescue boat, so its crew need STCW VI/2 fast rescue boat training.
  • PSCRB and AFF for officers, with marine evacuation system (MES) familiarisation where the ship carries MES.
  • Pilotage exemption certificates for masters and sometimes chief officers on regular routes, where the port authority allows them. Requirements vary by port.
  • Flag endorsements and, on some routes, language requirements so crew can communicate with passengers in an emergency.

Previous Ro-Ro, ferry or passenger experience is a real advantage. Many operators prefer officers who have already handled a ship through frequent berthing.

Crew composition

Crew size varies hugely, from a small freight-focused ship to a large overnight cruise-ferry. The main departments are:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, second and third officers (some ships also carry a staff captain or extra mates), bosun, ABs and OSs. On some ships deck crew double as loading crew.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second engineer, junior engineers, ETO, motormen or oilers, fitters and wipers. Expect a lot of thruster, CPP, ramp hydraulics and HVAC work.
  • Hotel and catering: purser or hotel manager, receptionists, cooks, stewards, cabin attendants, shop and bar staff. On bigger ships this is the largest department.
  • Cargo or loading team: some operators employ dedicated loading officers or deck crew who marshal traffic. Others share the work across the deck department.

Every crew member, including hotel staff, has a muster list duty. Hotel staff are central to passenger control, so crowd management training applies well beyond the deck and engine departments.

Life on board

Ro-Pax life is busy and repetitive, but you are rarely far from land.

  • Many port calls. Officers may do several arrivals and departures per watch. Ship-handling skills develop quickly.
  • Hours of rest. These are a constant management issue. The STCW and MLC limits still apply, but drills, cargo work and port calls cut into rest. Record your hours honestly.
  • Frequent drills. Passenger ships hold abandon ship and fire drills more often than cargo ships. Expect regular door, damage control and passenger evacuation exercises.
  • Rotations and manning. These vary widely. Many domestic or flag-specific ferry services are crewed under national rules or collective agreements. Others use international crews on contracts similar to cargo ships.
  • Inspections. Flag, class and port state inspectors attend often, and passenger ships face extra flag and port state checks. Housekeeping, fire doors and paperwork need to be in order every day.
  • Passengers. People will ask you directions and complain to you, and in a real emergency they will follow you. Professional behaviour in uniform is part of the job.

Worked scenario: door indicator alarm on departure

You are OOW. The ship has just let go and is moving off the berth. The bow door indicator on the bridge changes from green to red.

  1. Tell the master at once. Do not assume the sensor is faulty.
  2. Stop increasing speed. Follow the master's orders, which may be to hold position or return alongside.
  3. Check every source: the TV monitor of the door area, the leakage detection, and a report from the crew member responsible for the door.
  4. Send someone to inspect the door and its securing devices in person.
  5. Do not proceed until the master is satisfied the door is closed and secured, as the SMS procedure requires.
  6. Log the event and report it through the company's near-miss or non-conformity system.

This is exactly the chain of assumptions that the Herald investigation exposed, so treat it seriously every time.

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask about Ro-Pax

  • What is a special category space? It is an enclosed vehicle space, above or below the bulkhead deck, that vehicles can drive into and out of and to which passengers have access.
  • Why is fire-fighting water on a vehicle deck dangerous? Free surface effect across a wide deck reduces GM, so you must manage drainage and watch the stability.
  • What happened to the Herald and the Estonia, and what changed afterwards? Discuss bow door and visor failures, then indicators, surveillance, leakage detection, damage stability and door procedures.
  • Who needs crisis management training, and why do hotel staff need crowd management? Answer from STCW A-V/2.
  • How would you fight a fire in a trailer on the vehicle deck? Cover raising the alarm, boundary cooling, drenchers, ventilation control, stability monitoring, passenger muster and communication.

How to get your first Ro-Pax contract

  • Get the V/2 courses early. Crowd management and crisis management make you far easier to place. Ratings and hotel staff need the courses that match their muster list duties.
  • Use domestic ferry fleets. In the Philippines especially, domestic Ro-Pax operators are a common way in. Check the requirements of your administration.
  • Target ferry-specialist manning agencies. Choose agencies that place crew with ferry or passenger operators, and confirm the agency is licensed by your administration.
  • Consider the hotel department. It is a realistic entry point for catering and service crew.
  • Build relevant experience. Ro-Ro, PCTC or cruise sea time is a stepping stone, because the cargo handling or passenger side is already familiar.
  • Prepare for the interview. Expect questions on stability, door procedures, vehicle-deck fire, passenger evacuation and working under timetable pressure.
Key points
  • A Ro-Pax is a ro-ro passenger ship carrying vehicles and more than 12 passengers, usually on scheduled short-sea routes.
  • Watertight integrity of bow, stern and side doors is the critical safety item, as the Herald and Estonia losses showed.
  • Free surface effect from water on wide vehicle decks can rapidly reduce stability.
  • STCW Regulation V/2 adds passenger ship training on top of your normal certificates, depending on your role and muster duties.
  • Ro-ro passenger ships must carry a fast rescue boat, so crews need fast rescue boat proficiency.
  • Expect frequent port calls, frequent drills and close attention to hours of rest.
  • Crowd management and crisis management certificates make a first ferry contract much easier to secure.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a Ro-Ro and a Ro-Pax?

Both load wheeled cargo through doors and ramps. A Ro-Pax also carries more than 12 passengers, so it is a passenger ship under SOLAS. That brings extra construction, life-saving and crew training requirements, including STCW Regulation V/2.

Which extra courses do I need to work on a Ro-Pax?

It depends on your role. Typical requirements are passenger ship emergency familiarisation, crowd management, crisis management and human behaviour, and, for senior officers and those handling cargo or hull openings, passenger safety, cargo safety and hull integrity training. Fast rescue boat training is also common. Your administration sets the approved courses and refresher arrangements.

Do hotel and catering crew need STCW training on a Ro-Pax?

Yes. All need passenger ship emergency familiarisation. Those with designated safety duties on the muster list, which on passenger ships is usually most crew, also need STCW basic training. Most also need the safety training for crew who serve passengers, and those with muster duties to assist passengers need crowd management training.

Is a Ro-Pax a good ship for a deck cadet or junior officer?

It gives excellent exposure to ship-handling, port approaches, frequent pilotage and emergency drills. The trade-offs are a heavy routine and pressure on rest hours. Check that the company can give you training record book tasks suited to your CoC route.

How often do Ro-Pax crews do drills?

More often than on cargo ships, because SOLAS sets stricter drill requirements for passenger ships. Exact schedules and extra exercises, such as damage control or door failure drills, are set by the flag state and company SMS.

Test yourself

What was the primary cause of the Herald of Free Enterprise capsize in 1987?

The ship left Zeebrugge with her bow doors open and water flooded the vehicle deck. This drove later SOLAS changes on door indicators and integrity for ro-ro passenger ships (SOLAS Chapter II-1).

Under STCW A-V/2, who must complete crisis management and human behaviour training?

STCW Code Section A-V/2 applies this training to the senior officers listed and to anyone designated with responsibility for passenger safety in emergency situations.

Which best describes a special category space?

SOLAS Chapter II-2 defines special category spaces as enclosed vehicle spaces that vehicles drive into and out of and to which passengers have access.

Why is drencher water accumulating on a vehicle deck a stability concern?

Water spread over a large undivided deck creates a large free surface effect and reduces effective GM, which is why drainage must be managed (SOLAS Chapter II-2 fire-fighting and drainage provisions).

Which extra rescue craft requirement applies specifically to ro-ro passenger ships?

SOLAS Chapter III requires ro-ro passenger ships to carry at least one fast rescue boat, and its crew need training under STCW Section A-VI/2.
