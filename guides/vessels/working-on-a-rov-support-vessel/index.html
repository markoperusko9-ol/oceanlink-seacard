Working on a ROV Support Vessel

Quick answer

A ROV support vessel holds position on dynamic positioning while a contractor's team launches and flies remotely operated vehicles for subsea inspection, survey and construction work. For marine crew this means DP watchkeeping, close-quarters work inside offshore safety zones, and sharing the ship with a large project team. Most operators expect offshore survival training and DP training for deck officers, and entry often comes through other offshore or DP tonnage.

What a ROV support vessel does

A remotely operated vehicle (ROV) is an unmanned underwater vehicle. It is powered and controlled from the surface through an umbilical. The ship's job is to put the ROV in the water, keep it there safely, and hold a steady position or track while the ROV team does the subsea work.

Typical jobs include:

  • Inspection, maintenance and repair (IMR) of pipelines, risers, wellheads and subsea structures.
  • Survey work such as pipeline route surveys, as-laid and as-built surveys, and debris clearance.
  • Construction support, including spool and structure installation, valve operation and monitoring of lifts made by the ship's crane or by another vessel.
  • Drilling support, including ROV assistance to rigs during riser and BOP operations.
  • Renewables work, including cable route and turbine foundation inspection.

ROV support is a role rather than a fixed ship design. Purpose-built ROV support vessels exist. Many PSVs, construction vessels and DSVs also carry ROV spreads, either permanently fitted or mobilised for a single contract. Ship type, certification and crew size therefore vary a lot between hulls.

Layout and equipment you will meet

Most ROV vessels share the following features:

  • DP system. Usually DP class 2, sometimes class 3. The ship has redundant thrusters, power generation and position reference systems. These typically include GNSS, hydroacoustic reference (HPR/USBL) and sometimes taut-wire or laser/radar references when working close to installations.
  • ROV hangar or deck spread. This holds work-class and/or observation-class ROVs, control vans or a dedicated control room, workshops and spares containers.
  • Launch and recovery system (LARS). This may be an A-frame over the side or stern, a cursor/guided system, or a launch through a moon pool amidships, which reduces motion in a seaway. Work-class ROVs are often deployed in a tether management system (TMS) or 'garage', which pays out a lighter tether below it.
  • Offshore crane. Often heave-compensated on construction and IMR vessels, for subsea lifts.
  • Large accommodation block. It must house the project team as well as the marine crew. Where the ship carries more than 12 special personnel, it may be certified under the Special Purpose Ships (SPS) Code, depending on the flag.
  • Helideck. Fitted on some vessels, typically the larger construction units.

Work-class ROVs run on high-voltage power. Electrical isolation on the ROV system is a specialised job, and the contractor's procedures and the ship's permit-to-work system will both apply.

Key hazards and how they are controlled

Most incidents on these ships involve position keeping, deck handling or the interface between ship and subsea team.

  • Loss of position. A drive-off or drift-off with an ROV deployed can damage the umbilical, the vehicle or subsea assets. It is worse near a platform. Control rests on the DP system's redundancy design and on clear activity-specific operating guidelines, often called ASOG or WSOG. These define green/advisory/yellow/red conditions and the action required at each stage.
  • Umbilical and thruster interaction. An umbilical drawn into a thruster or propeller is a classic serious event. Thruster use, ship heading and umbilical paths are agreed with the ROV supervisor. Some thrusters may be restricted while the ROV is close to the hull.
  • Launch and recovery. Swinging loads, snatch loads in a swell and crush points around the LARS are the main risks. Maximum weather limits for launch and recovery are set in advance by the system design and company procedures. The Master and the ROV supervisor can still postpone or stop an operation on the day if they judge conditions unsafe, even inside those limits.
  • Working inside safety zones. Installations normally have a 500 m safety zone, and entry follows the field operator's procedures and checklists. Collision with an installation is a top-tier risk.
  • High-pressure hydraulics and HV electrics on the ROV spread.
  • SIMOPS. You may be working at the same time as diving, crane lifts, drilling or other vessels. A missed communication between parties is a recurring factor in incident reports.

Under COLREG, a vessel engaged in underwater operations whose work restricts her ability to manoeuvre is a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre. She shows the appropriate lights and shapes, and the additional obstruction signals where applicable.

Your STCW certificate of competency and basic safety training are the foundation. Beyond those, the following are well established in the offshore sector:

Who is on board: marine crew and project crew

There are effectively two crews.

Marine crew, responsible for the ship:

  • Master
  • Chief Officer, often also the senior DPO
  • DPOs and other navigating officers
  • Chief Engineer and engine officers
  • ETO, who is critical on DP tonnage
  • Bosun, ABs and crane operator
  • Motormen or oilers
  • Catering staff

Project crew, usually contractor and client personnel:

  • ROV supervisor and ROV pilot-technicians
  • Surveyors and data processors
  • Offshore or party manager
  • Client representatives

The Master's overriding authority for safety and pollution prevention, as set out in the ISM Code, does not change because a client is paying for the day. A good ROV vessel has a clear daily planning meeting. It also has an agreed stop-work culture: anyone can halt an operation they believe is unsafe.

Life on board

Life on board differs from deep-sea trading in several ways:

  • Watches are often 12 hours, and DP desks are manned throughout operations. STCW and MLC rest-hour requirements still apply and are recorded.
  • Rotations are commonly shorter than deep-sea tours and are sometimes equal time on and off. The pattern depends entirely on the company and region.
  • Port calls may be rare during a campaign. Crew changes often happen by crew boat, helicopter or quick port turnarounds.
  • Weather downtime happens. You may sit waiting on weather, then work intensively once the window opens.
  • Accommodation is busy with project staff. Food and internet standards tend to be good because clients expect them, but this varies by company.
  • Skills. Deck officers gain strong DP and close-quarters ship-handling skills. Engineers gain experience with diesel-electric plant and redundancy concepts that transfer well across offshore tonnage.

Oral exam style questions you may face

Examiners and offshore interviewers often probe these points:

  • What lights and shapes do you show during ROV operations? As a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre:
    • By day, ball, diamond, ball in a vertical line.
    • By night, red-white-red all-round lights in a vertical line.
    • Where an obstruction exists, two balls or two all-round red lights on the obstructed side, and two diamonds or two all-round green lights on the side where another vessel may pass.
    • When making way through the water, masthead light(s), sidelights and sternlight as well. At anchor, a vessel engaged in underwater operations shows the obstruction lights or shapes instead of the anchor lights or shape (Rule 27(d)(iii)).
  • Explain DP equipment class 2 versus class 3. Under class 2, a single fault in an active component or system should not cause loss of position. Class 3 also covers loss of any one compartment through fire or flooding.
  • What does a red status in your ASOG mean? Stop the operation, make the ROV and any connected work safe, and follow the defined emergency actions.
  • What is a drift-off and a drive-off? A drift-off is loss of thrust, so the vessel drifts with the environment. A drive-off is thrust applied wrongly, which drives the vessel off position.
  • What do you do before entering a 500 m zone? Complete the field operator's checklist, confirm DP capability for the conditions, carry out communication checks with the installation, and confirm your approach and escape headings.
  • What is the Master's position if the client wants to continue in marginal weather? The Master has overriding authority on safety under the ISM Code.

How to get your first contract

  1. Get your offshore basics in order. Hold survival training accepted in your target region and an offshore medical if required. Check first, because courses and validity periods differ.
  2. Deck officers: start the DP route early. Complete the induction course. DP sea time usually has to be logged on DP vessels, so a first offshore job on a PSV or AHTS is a common stepping stone.
  3. Engineers and ETOs: highlight diesel-electric, HV, PMS and thruster experience.
  4. Ratings: offshore deck experience, crane tickets recognised by the operator, and clean safety records count.
  5. Apply to offshore-specialist companies and their manning agents, not only general deep-sea managers.
  6. Be honest on your CV about DP hours and equipment. These are checked, and DP logbook entries are verified.

Deck cadets and ratings sometimes ask about moving into ROV pilot roles. That is a separate contractor career with its own technical training and is not part of the STCW marine crew pathway.

Key points
  • A ROV support vessel holds position on DP while contractors launch and fly ROVs for IMR, survey and construction.
  • Most such vessels are DP class 2 or 3 and carry a LARS, often a moon pool, and a large project team.
  • The main hazards are loss of position, umbilical-thruster interaction, launch and recovery, and SIMOPS near installations.
  • Offshore survival training and DP certification for deck officers are widely expected, but exact requirements depend on client, region and flag.
  • The Master's overriding authority under the ISM Code applies whatever the client's schedule.
  • Under COLREG, a vessel whose underwater operations restrict her manoeuvrability shows RAM signals, plus obstruction signals where applicable.
  • A first job on a PSV or AHTS is a common route to gaining DP sea time.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a DP certificate to join a ROV support vessel?

Deck officers standing DP watches are normally expected to hold or be working towards the industry-recognised DPO certificate. Engineers and ratings do not normally need the DPO certificate, although some companies require DP-related training for engineers and ETOs. Some companies take junior officers who have completed DP induction and let them build sea time on board. Requirements are set by the company and client.

Is BOSIET compulsory for working on ROV vessels?

It is not an STCW requirement, but many offshore clients and regions require BOSIET with HUET or an accepted equivalent. This is especially common where crew changes happen by helicopter. Check with the manning agent which standard the contract area accepts before booking a course.

What is the difference between a ROV support vessel and a DSV?

A DSV carries diving systems, often saturation diving, to put divers on the seabed, and usually ROVs as well. A ROV support vessel relies on unmanned vehicles only. The marine skills overlap heavily, but diving adds its own hazards, signals and procedures.

Who is in charge on board, the Master or the client?

The client and offshore manager direct the work programme. The Master remains responsible for the safety of the ship, crew and environment and can stop any operation. Good vessels settle this through daily planning meetings and agreed operating guidelines.

Can a deck rating or cadet become an ROV pilot?

It is possible, but it is a different career run by subsea contractors with their own technical training and grading. It is not part of the STCW marine crew pathway. Experience on ROV vessels helps you understand the work and make contacts.

Test yourself

By day, a vessel engaged in ROV operations that restrict her ability to manoeuvre shows which shapes in a vertical line?

Ball-diamond-ball is the shape signal for a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre. Two balls indicate a vessel not under command. Reference: COLREG Rule 27(b).

Which statement best describes DP equipment class 2?

Class 2 is defined by single-fault tolerance in active components or systems. Fire and flood tolerance is what defines class 3. Reference: IMO MSC.1/Circ.1580.

Where does the STCW Code give guidance on training of personnel operating DP systems?

Section B-V/f is the non-mandatory guidance on DP training and experience. Section A-II/2 covers masters and chief mates, the tanker training standards are in A-V/1-1 and A-V/1-2, and Regulation VI/1 covers familiarization and basic safety training. Reference: STCW Code, Section B-V/f.

The client asks to continue ROV launch in weather the Master considers unsafe. Who has the final decision?

The Master's overriding authority to make decisions on safety and pollution prevention is established in the ISM Code. Reference: ISM Code, section 5.

Is a vessel engaged in underwater operations automatically a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre?

Underwater operations are listed as an example of RAM, but the definition depends on the work actually restricting manoeuvrability. Reference: COLREG Rule 3(g).
