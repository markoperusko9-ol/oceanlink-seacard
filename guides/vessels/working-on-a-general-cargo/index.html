Working on a General Cargo Ship

Quick answer

A general cargo ship carries breakbulk, project cargo, steel, timber and mixed loads, usually with its own cranes, into ports large and small. The work is hands-on: frequent port calls, cargo securing, lifting operations and stability checks matter more than on most ship types. No special STCW tanker-style endorsement is needed, and the type is a common first ship for many cadets and junior officers.

What a general cargo ship does

A general cargo ship carries goods that are not in containers and not loaded as a single homogeneous bulk. Think steel coils, plates and beams, bagged rice or cement, timber and logs, machinery, vehicles, pipes, wind turbine parts, project cargo and the occasional container on deck. Many modern ships are multipurpose (MPP) designs that can switch between breakbulk, containers, heavy lifts and some bulk cargoes.

Typical trades:

  • Tramp trading: the ship goes where the next charter takes it, often to ports that bulk carriers and container ships rarely visit.
  • Liner or semi-liner breakbulk services: regular routes, for example between Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Europe.
  • Short-sea and coastal trade: smaller ships with short passages and very frequent port calls.
  • Project and heavy-lift work: specialised MPP ships with heavy cranes, carrying one-off loads for energy and infrastructure projects.

Because many ships are geared (fitted with their own cranes or derricks), they can work ports with little or no shore equipment. That is a big part of why the type survives.

Layout and equipment you will work with

Layouts vary a lot, but expect most of the following:

  • Box-shaped holds on MPP ships, or older-style holds on traditional tweendeckers.
  • Tweendecks: often movable pontoon panels that can be shifted to different heights or stowed, which splits a hold into levels.
  • Hatch covers: folding hydraulic covers, or pontoon (lift-away) covers handled by the ship's cranes. Covers are often used as deck cargo stowage, so their strength limits matter.
  • Deck cranes or derricks: some ships can combine two cranes (tandem lift) for heavier loads. Heavy-lift ships may have dedicated cranes and a stability (anti-heeling) system.
  • Lashing gear: chains, wire, web lashings, turnbuckles, shackles, D-rings, twistlocks for containers, plus a lot of dunnage timber.
  • Ballast system that is worked hard, because trim and list change during every cargo operation.

The engine room is usually a conventional slow-speed or medium-speed diesel plant. On older or smaller tonnage, expect less automation and more manned watchkeeping, though this varies by ship and company.

Cargo work and the main hazards

Cargo is the heart of the job. The ship must carry a Cargo Securing Manual approved by the flag state, and stowage and securing follow the principles of the IMO Code of Safe Practice for Cargo Stowage and Securing (CSS Code). Shippers must give the master adequate information about the cargo before loading.

Key hazards and what experienced crew watch for:

  • Cargo shifting: steel coils, pipes and heavy units can break loose in a seaway. Coils are normally stowed on dunnage with their axes fore-and-aft, wedged and secured as a block. Lashings are checked before departure and again after heavy weather.
  • Lifting operations: suspended loads, tag lines, signalling, crane limit switches, safe working load and wind limits. Never stand under a load or between a load and a fixed structure.
  • Falls from height: open hatches, tweendeck edges and stacked cargo. Unguarded openings are a common cause of serious injury.
  • Enclosed spaces: cargo holds can become oxygen-depleted. Rusting steel, timber, some agricultural cargoes and fumigation can all affect the atmosphere. A closed hold must be treated as an enclosed space until tested and permitted.
  • Dangerous goods: packaged dangerous goods are carried under SOLAS Chapter VII and the IMDG Code. Segregation, documentation and emergency response information must be correct.
  • Timber deck cargo: this has its own code of safe practice. It brings stability, lashing and access hazards, and special loadline provisions may apply.
  • Stevedore activity: shore gangs of mixed experience, working fast. The duty officer and AB must actively supervise, not just watch.
  • Condensation and cargo care: sweat damage to steel and bagged cargo leads to claims. Ventilation decisions depend on dew points, not habit.
  • Hatch cover weathertightness: a leaking cover can ruin a cargo. Rubber packing, cleats and drainage need regular care.

Worked scenario: list during a heavy lift

This is a classic examination topic and a real risk. Once a load is lifted clear, its weight acts at the crane jib head (or derrick head), not where it sat on the quay. G rises immediately, GM reduces, and the outreach causes a list.

Illustrative figures only (not from any real ship):

  • Displacement before lift: 8,000 t; KG 7.0 m; KM 8.2 m (KM assumed unchanged for simplicity)
  • Load 100 t, jib head 25 m above keel, outreach 15 m from the centreline

Step 1: new KG with the load suspended (8,000 × 7.0 + 100 × 25) ÷ 8,100 = 58,500 ÷ 8,100 ≈ 7.22 m

Step 2: new GM 8.2 − 7.22 ≈ 0.98 m (down from 1.2 m)

Step 3: list tan θ = (100 × 15) ÷ (8,100 × 0.98) ≈ 0.189, so θ ≈ 10.7°

That list may exceed the crane's operating limits and can be dangerous for people on deck. In practice the chief officer plans counter-ballast or uses the anti-heeling system, slackens moorings appropriately, and checks the figures against the stability booklet and crane limits before the lift. Remember free surface effect from partly filled tanks, which reduces the effective GM further.

Certificates and experience usually expected

General cargo ships have no dedicated STCW endorsement of the kind required for tankers or gas carriers. You need the standard set for your rank:

  • STCW basic training and the relevant proficiency certificates (for example PSCRB, and Advanced Fire Fighting for officers)
  • Certificate of Competency and flag state endorsement for officers; watchkeeping rating certificates for ratings
  • Medical first aid or medical care, depending on rank
  • Security training appropriate to your duties
  • A valid seafarer medical certificate

What companies often look for in addition, depending on their own requirements:

  • Previous geared-ship experience, especially for chief officers and bosuns
  • Crane operating experience or a crane operator course. Some companies and flags expect formal training. It is not a single STCW requirement, so check what your employer demands.
  • Heavy-lift or project cargo experience for specialised MPP operators
  • Dangerous goods familiarity, often covered through company training

Lifting appliances must be tested, examined and certified according to flag and classification society rules. Newer SOLAS requirements for lifting appliances also apply. Officers should know where the register of lifting gear is kept and when examinations are due.

Crew and life on board

Crews are usually small. The minimum is set by the ship's Safe Manning Document, and companies add to it as they see fit. A typical line-up:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, 2nd and 3rd officers, often a deck cadet, bosun, ABs and OS
  • Engine: chief engineer, 2nd and 3rd engineers, sometimes a 4th engineer or ETO, fitter, oiler or motorman, sometimes a wiper or engine cadet
  • Catering: cook and messman or steward

What life is actually like:

  • Many port calls and short passages, especially in short-sea trades. Watch rotas are broken up by cargo watches, and fatigue management under the hours of rest rules is a real issue.
  • Hard physical work: lashing, unlashing, dunnage handling, hold cleaning and hatch maintenance.
  • Varied ports: river ports, anchorages with barge operations, and places few other seafarers see. Shore leave chances are often better than on tankers, but depend on the port, security level and the cargo programme.
  • Hands-on seamanship: a good school for cadets, who see more real cargo planning, securing and stability work than on many other types.
  • Older tonnage in parts of the fleet: expect more maintenance and repair work. Port state control attention also tends to be higher on older ships.

Oral exam corner: what examiners ask

Typical questions for junior and senior deck candidates, with the points they expect to hear:

  • Where does the weight of a suspended load act? At the jib or derrick head. Explain the rise of G and the resulting list.
  • What is the Cargo Securing Manual? A flag-approved document covering securing devices, their strength and how to use them. It is required for cargo units other than solid and liquid bulk.
  • How do you stow and secure steel coils? Stowed on dunnage with axes fore-and-aft, tightly blocked and wedged, with locking coils and lashings. Check that the tanktop load limits are not exceeded.
  • Before entering a hold that has been closed for days? Treat it as an enclosed space: risk assessment, permit, ventilation, atmosphere testing, a standby person and rescue arrangements.
  • What do you check before using a ship's crane? Certification and SWL, limit switches, wire condition, communications, wind and list limits, and that no one is under the load.
  • Ventilating a hold of steel cargo? Compare the dew points of the outside air and the hold air. Ventilate only when it will not cause condensation on the cargo.

Engineers can expect questions on crane hydraulics, hatch cover hydraulic systems and ballast pump operation during cargo work.

How to get your first contract

  1. Complete your mandatory documents first. These are your CoC or rating certificate, STCW courses, medical certificate, passport and seaman's book. Indian seafarers also need an INDoS number. Filipino seafarers need their national seafarer documents from MARINA.
  2. Apply through licensed manning agents only. In India, check that the agency is a recruitment and placement service licensed by the Directorate General of Shipping. In the Philippines, check that it is a licensed manning agency under the national migrant workers authority. Never pay unlawful placement fees.
  3. Target operators of geared and multipurpose ships. Many tramp and MPP companies recruit regularly through agents in Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi and Manila.
  4. Highlight practical skills on your CV, such as lashing, crane signalling, hold cleaning and cargo watch experience. Cadets should show their training record book progress.
  5. Prepare for the interview. Expect questions on cargo securing, enclosed space entry, crane safety and basic stability, as covered above.
  6. Be flexible on trade. Short-sea and older-tonnage ships are often where newcomers get their first chance. Good sea time there is valued when you move up.
Key points
  • General cargo ships carry breakbulk, project, steel, timber and mixed cargoes, usually with their own cranes.
  • There is no special STCW endorsement for the type; standard certificates for your rank apply.
  • The Cargo Securing Manual and the CSS Code principles govern stowage and securing.
  • A suspended load acts at the jib head, so lifts raise G, reduce GM and cause a list.
  • Closed cargo holds can be oxygen-depleted and must be treated as enclosed spaces.
  • Packaged dangerous goods are carried under SOLAS Chapter VII and the IMDG Code.
  • Frequent port calls mean hard physical work, fatigue risk and strong practical learning.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a general cargo ship?

No STCW endorsement is specific to general cargo ships, unlike tankers and gas carriers. You need the standard certificates for your rank and a valid medical certificate. Some companies ask for crane operator training or geared-ship experience, so check the employer's requirements.

Is a general cargo ship good for a deck cadet's first ship?

It is often a very good one. Cadets see real cargo planning, lashing, stability and port work, which helps with the training record book and later oral exams. The work is physical and the port calls are frequent, so expect long days.

What is the difference between a general cargo ship and a multipurpose vessel?

A multipurpose (MPP) ship is a type of general cargo ship designed to switch between breakbulk, containers, heavy lifts and some bulk cargoes. MPP ships usually have box-shaped holds, movable tweendecks and stronger cranes. Traditional general cargo ships and tweendeckers are less flexible.

Why do general cargo ships list during crane operations?

Once a load is lifted, its weight acts at the crane jib head, which raises the ship's centre of gravity. Swinging the load outboard then creates a heeling moment. The chief officer plans ballast or anti-heeling measures to keep the list within safe and crane-operating limits.

Can general cargo ships carry dangerous goods?

Yes. Packaged dangerous goods are commonly carried, in compliance with SOLAS Chapter VII and the IMDG Code. Correct documentation, stowage and segregation are essential, and the ship must be equipped and certified for the classes it carries.

Test yourself

When a ship's crane lifts a load clear of the quay, where is the load's weight considered to act for stability purposes?

A suspended weight acts at its point of suspension, which raises G and reduces GM. This is standard ship stability theory, as covered in STCW Code Section A-II/1 and A-II/2 competences.

Which document, approved by the flag state, sets out the securing devices and arrangements for cargo units on a general cargo ship?

SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5 requires cargo units to be secured according to the approved Cargo Securing Manual. The Cargo Record Book belongs to MARPOL Annex II.

A hold loaded with steel has been closed for several days. What is the main atmospheric danger on entry?

Rusting steel consumes oxygen, so the hold must be treated as an enclosed space and tested before entry. This follows the IMO recommendations for entering enclosed spaces and SOLAS requirements on enclosed space drills.

Which IMO instrument governs the carriage of dangerous goods in packaged form?

SOLAS Chapter VII Part A makes the IMDG Code mandatory for packaged dangerous goods. The IBC and IGC Codes cover bulk chemicals and gases, and the IMSBC Code covers solid bulk cargoes.

How are steel coils normally stowed in a general cargo hold?

Coils are stowed with their axes fore-and-aft on dunnage, tightly blocked, wedged and secured. This is in line with the CSS Code guidance on steel coils and the ship's Cargo Securing Manual.
