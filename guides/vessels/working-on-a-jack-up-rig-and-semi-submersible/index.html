Working on a Jack-up Rig and Semi-Submersible

Quick answer

Jack-ups and semi-submersibles are mobile offshore drilling units (MODUs). They drill or service oil and gas wells, mostly while stationary on location, so the marine job is about stability, ballast, station-keeping and rig moves rather than sea passages. Expect an offshore survival course such as BOSIET with HUET and an offshore medical on top of your STCW certificates. The exact certification depends on the flag state, the coastal state and the drilling contractor.

What jack-ups and semi-subs actually do

Both are mobile offshore units. They move from location to location, then stay put for weeks or months while a well is drilled, completed, tested, worked over or plugged and abandoned. Some units are converted or built as accommodation or construction units, but drilling is the core trade.

  • Jack-up: a buoyant hull with legs that are lowered to the seabed. The hull is then jacked up clear of the sea. Jack-ups work in relatively shallow water on the continental shelf. Most are not self-propelled and are towed between locations. A few have their own propulsion or propulsion assist.
  • Semi-submersible: a deck box sitting on columns that stand on submerged pontoons. On location the unit ballasts down to its operating draught and holds position with anchors and mooring lines, dynamic positioning (DP), or both. Semis go into much deeper water than jack-ups. Many are towed, and some are self-propelled.

For a seafarer, the big change is the drilling contractor and the oil company client. Their wellsite priorities shape every day on board. The marine crew keeps the unit safe, stable and on location so the drilling crew can work.

Layout and how the unit works

Jack-up essentials

  • Legs: usually three or four open-truss or tubular legs, with spudcans or a mat at the bottom.
  • Jacking system: normally rack-and-pinion, which climbs the legs.
  • Cantilever and drill floor: on most modern units these skid out over the stern so the rig can work over a fixed platform.
  • Installation sequence:
    1. Position on location.
    2. Lower the legs to the seabed.
    3. Lift the hull slightly.
    4. Preload by filling preload tanks. This loads the legs beyond the maximum expected storm loading so the foundation settles now, not during a storm.
    5. Dump the preload.
    6. Jack up to the planned air gap.
  • Leaving location reverses the sequence. Pulling legs out of sticky seabed soil can take time and may need jetting.

Semi-submersible essentials

  • Pontoons and columns: these give a small waterplane area. Ballasting down to operating draught puts most of the buoyancy below the worst wave action, which reduces motion.
  • Ballast control room: tanks, pumps and valves in the pontoons and columns are controlled from here. The ballast control operator watches draught, heel, trim, deck load and the remaining stability margin.
  • Station-keeping: anchor winches with chain and wire, usually laid and recovered with the help of anchor handling vessels. Alternatively, a DP system with thrusters and position references.
  • Variable deck load: drill pipe, mud, bulk and casing are constantly shifting. Every change has to be accounted for in the stability picture.

Who is on board

Crew size and titles vary by contractor, flag and coastal state. The main groups are:

Group Typical roles
Command Offshore Installation Manager (OIM), sometimes also a Master on self-propelled or DP units
Marine Barge master or barge supervisor, ballast control operator (semis), DPOs on DP units, deck foreman, crane operators, roustabouts
Drilling Toolpusher, driller, assistant driller, derrickman, floormen (roughnecks)
Maintenance Chief mechanic, chief electrician, mechanics, electricians, motormen, subsea engineer (BOP), ETOs on some units
Support Medic, radio operator, catering crew
Client and third parties Company man, mud engineers, cementers, well testing crews and other specialists

The OIM has overall responsibility for the installation. On some units the OIM is a marine-qualified person. On others the OIM comes from drilling, with a marine specialist alongside.

The chain of command in an emergency is laid down in the unit's emergency procedures. Learn it on day one.

Key hazards

Well control

  • A blowout is the worst case, involving gas release, fire or explosion.
  • H2S can be present in some fields. Know your muster point for gas alarms and the location of breathing apparatus.

Jack-up specific

  • Punch-through: during preload, a spudcan breaks suddenly through a stronger soil layer into weaker soil and the unit lurches or tilts.
  • Leg damage during going-on or coming-off location in a swell.
  • Overloading the legs.
  • Problems during the towed rig move itself.

Semi-submersible specific

  • Ballast errors and loss of stability.
  • Flooding of columns or pontoons.
  • Mooring line failure.
  • DP drive-off or drift-off.
  • Historical casualties such as the Alexander L. Kielland and Ocean Ranger capsizes are still studied for structural failure, ballast control and evacuation lessons.

Everyday hazards

  • Dropped objects from the derrick and cranes.
  • Pipe handling on the drill floor.
  • Heavy crane lifts to and from supply vessels.
  • Helicopter operations.
  • High-pressure lines.
  • Working at height.

Control comes from a strong permit to work system, SIMOPS planning, toolbox talks, dropped-object surveys and stop-work authority, which you are expected to use. In some coastal states, such as the UK and Norway, the unit also operates under a regulator-accepted safety case or equivalent regime.

Certificates and experience usually expected

The baseline

  • Your STCW basic training and a valid seafarer medical are the starting point.
  • Offshore work normally adds the items below. Exact requirements depend on the coastal state, flag state and contractor, so check the job specification.

Offshore survival and medical

  • BOSIET with HUET and, in many sectors, an emergency breathing system element. Refresh it with FOET or the equivalent on time.
  • In some regions a local equivalent survival course is accepted or required instead.
  • An offshore medical. Some sectors require a specific medical standard, such as a UK or Norwegian sector medical, in addition to or instead of your seafarer medical.

Role-specific

  • H2S training: common, and often mandatory in fields where H2S is expected.
  • DP certificate: for DPO roles on DP semis.
  • Crane operator, banksman and rigger certificates: for deck roles. Many contractors require industry-recognised offshore crane competence.
  • Command and stability roles: IMO recommendations for MOU personnel (Resolution A.1079(28)) cover OIM, barge supervisor, ballast control operator and maintenance supervisor training. Administrations apply them in different ways. Many contractors also run in-house competence schemes.

Experience that helps

  • Officers with stability-heavy cargo work, anchor handling or DP time are attractive for marine roles.
  • Engineers and ETOs with high-voltage, diesel-electric or thruster experience fit maintenance roles well.

Watch the paperwork: on non-self-propelled units, how STCW and MLC 2006 apply depends on the flag. The time you serve may or may not count as seagoing service towards your next CoC. Ask your administration, such as DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines, before you rely on it.

Life on board

Work pattern

  • Offshore life runs on 12-hour shifts, day and night tours.
  • Rotations vary by contractor and region. Equal time on and off (e.g. 2/2, 3/3 or 4/4 weeks) is common, but some sectors give more time off, such as 2 on/3 off in the UK or 2 on/4 off in Norway.
  • Crew change is mostly by helicopter. In some areas it is by crew boat and personnel transfer basket or gangway.

Accommodation and living

  • Bunks are often in shared cabins, with back-to-back occupancy on different shifts.
  • Food is generally good, with gyms and internet common.
  • Alcohol is banned, and drug and alcohol testing is routine.

Drills and inductions

  • Muster and evacuation drills are frequent.
  • Evacuation is by lifeboats (TEMPSC) and liferafts, with helicopter as the preferred route where possible.
  • Expect a detailed site induction and a probationary or green-hat period when you are new. During that time you are closely supervised.

What changes when you come from ships

  • On a jack-up there is very little motion once you are up, though you may feel some sway or vibration in heavy weather.
  • The pace is driven by well operations, not port schedules.
  • You will work alongside many non-seafarers and third-party specialists.
  • The safety culture is heavily paperwork-based. Permits, JSAs and handovers are taken seriously, and auditors from the client visit often.

Getting your first contract

  1. Decide your entry route.
    • Deck ratings often enter as roustabouts and progress to crane operator or into the drilling crew.
    • Engine ratings may join as motormen.
    • ETOs and electricians fit maintenance roles.
    • Deck officers aim for barge supervisor, ballast control or DPO roles.
    • Engineers aim for mechanic, maintenance supervisor or chief engineer roles on self-propelled units.
  2. Get the offshore tickets first. BOSIET/HUET, an offshore medical and H2S training where relevant make you deployable. Some employers fund these for new hires, so ask before paying.
  3. Target the right employers.
    • Drilling contractors and their approved manning agencies.
    • National oil company fleets and contractors working your home region's fields.
  4. Show transferable experience. Put stability work, crane hours, anchor handling, DP and HV work clearly on your CV.
  5. Use only licensed agents.
    • In India, check the agency holds a valid RPSL from DG Shipping.
    • In the Philippines, check the agency is licensed by the Department of Migrant Workers.
    • Never pay for a guaranteed rig job.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why do we preload a jack-up? To load the foundation beyond the worst expected environmental loading. Any settlement or punch-through then happens during controlled preloading with the hull low, not in a storm at full air gap.

Q: What is air gap and why does it matter? It is the clearance between the underside of the hull and the sea surface. It must be enough to keep design waves clear of the hull, or wave loads on the structure rise sharply.

Q: Why does a semi-sub ballast down on location? To put the pontoons below most wave action and reduce motion while keeping the required stability.

Q: What lights does a jack-up show under tow? The tow and towing vessel follow COLREG Rule 24. On location, the marking of the unit as an offshore structure follows coastal state requirements.

Q: Who has overall responsibility on board? The OIM, as set out in the unit's operations manual and emergency procedures.

Key points
  • Jack-ups stand on legs in shallow water; semi-subs float on pontoons and moor or use DP in deep water.
  • Most jack-ups and many semis are towed between locations, so rig moves are high-risk operations.
  • Preloading, air gap and punch-through are the core jack-up marine concepts; ballast and deck load control are the core semi-sub concepts.
  • BOSIET with HUET, an offshore medical and often H2S training are expected on top of STCW basics.
  • The OIM has overall command; marine, drilling and maintenance crews work alongside client staff.
  • Whether rig time counts as sea service for your CoC depends on your administration and the flag, so check first.
  • Use only licensed manning agents and never pay for a guaranteed offshore job.

Frequently asked questions

Is a jack-up rig considered a ship?

A jack-up is a mobile offshore unit. It is a vessel when afloat or under tow, but when jacked up it behaves as a fixed installation. How SOLAS, STCW and MLC 2006 apply depends on the flag state and on whether the unit is self-propelled. Coastal state offshore regulations also apply on location.

Do I need BOSIET to work on a rig?

In most offshore regions you need BOSIET with HUET, or an accepted equivalent survival course, before you can fly offshore. Some regions accept or require a different local course. Always check the job specification and the coastal state's requirement.

Does time on a rig count as sea service for my CoC?

Not automatically. It depends on your certifying administration's rules, the type of unit and whether it was self-propelled or underway. Get written confirmation from your administration before counting on it.

What is the difference between a jack-up and a semi-submersible?

A jack-up lowers legs to the seabed and lifts its hull clear of the water, so it is limited to shallower water. A semi-sub floats on submerged pontoons and columns and holds position by moorings or DP. That lets it work in much deeper water.

Can a deck rating get onto a drilling rig?

Yes. The usual entry route is as a roustabout, working on deck handling cargo and assisting crane lifts. With experience and the right certificates you can progress to crane operator or move into the drilling crew.

Test yourself

What is the main purpose of preloading a jack-up on location?

Preload deliberately overloads the legs while the hull is low, so any settlement or punch-through occurs in a controlled way. Reference: unit operations manual and site-specific assessment.

Why does a semi-submersible ballast down to a deeper operating draught on location?

The deeper draught places buoyancy below the wave zone and reduces the waterplane area exposed to waves, improving motion behaviour. Resting on the seabed would make it a bottom-supported unit, not a semi-sub.

Which statement about BOSIET is correct?

BOSIET is an offshore industry survival course that includes HUET. It is taken in addition to, not instead of, STCW basic training.

Who has overall responsibility for a mobile offshore drilling unit on location?

The OIM is in overall charge of the installation, as defined in the operations manual and emergency procedures. The company man represents the client's well programme, not command of the unit. Reference: IMO Resolution A.1079(28).

What is a punch-through on a jack-up?

Punch-through is a sudden foundation failure, most likely during preloading, that can cause rapid leg penetration and tilting. Site soil surveys and controlled preloading manage the risk.
