Working on an Anchor Handling Tug Supply (AHTS) Vessel

Quick answer

An AHTS moves and sets the anchors of drilling rigs and other floating units, tows rigs and barges, and carries supplies to offshore installations. It is one of the most demanding ship types for deck crew and bridge teams because of the heavy wire and chain loads, the open working deck and the stability risks. Most employers want offshore-specific training such as BOSIET/HUET and, for officers, DP certification, along with previous offshore or towing experience.

What an AHTS actually does

An Anchor Handling Tug Supply vessel combines three jobs in one hull:

  • Anchor handling: running out, setting, recovering and re-positioning the anchors of semi-submersible rigs, drilling units, accommodation units, barges and floating installations during a rig move.
  • Towing: towing rigs, barges and other units between locations, sometimes on long ocean tows.
  • Supply: carrying deck cargo such as pipe, containers and equipment, and bulk cargoes like fuel, drill water, potable water, liquid mud, brine, cement and barite to offshore installations.

Many AHTS are also kept on standby duty near installations, and some are fitted for oil recovery, firefighting (FiFi notation) or rescue work. The equipment and notations fitted vary a great deal between vessels, so check the class notations and charter scope before you join.

The work is concentrated where floating rigs operate: the North Sea, West Africa, Brazil, the Gulf of Mexico, the Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and Australia. The regional regime has a big effect on which certificates, medicals and procedures are expected.

Layout and key equipment

An AHTS looks like a supply vessel, with the accommodation and bridge forward and a long, low, open cargo deck aft. The difference is in the heavy gear at the stern.

Equipment What it does
Anchor handling / towing winch Large drum winch, usually with a work drum and one or more towing drums, plus spooling gear
Stern roller Heavy roller at the transom that the wire or chain runs over under load
Shark jaws (karm forks) Hydraulic stoppers in the deck aft that grip chain or wire so the crew can connect or disconnect while the load is held off the winch
Towing pins (guide pins) Hydraulic pins that keep the wire or chain inside a safe sector over the stern
Tugger winches and capstans Used to pull wires and gear around the deck
Chain lockers Store rig chain when it is recovered or delivered
Aft-facing control station Manoeuvring, winch and pin/jaw controls looking over the working deck

Propulsion is usually twin screw, often with controllable pitch propellers, high-lift or flap rudders and bow and stern tunnel thrusters. Many units are fitted with dynamic positioning. Vessels are advertised by bollard pull, and their main engine power is high for their size. That matters for engineers' certificates, which are explained further down.

Bulk tanks and pumps for liquid mud, brine, fuel and water, and pneumatic tanks for dry bulk, sit below the cargo deck. The engine department runs these, so expect them to be a big part of an engineer's working day.

How an anchor handling job runs: step by step

The detail comes from the rig move procedure, the anchor handling plan and your company SMS. In outline, running an anchor typically goes like this:

  1. Planning and toolbox talk: the master, officers, deck crew and engineers go through the rig move procedure, the anchor pattern, the expected tensions, the communication channels and the stop-work points.
  2. Stability check: the chief officer checks stability for the expected loads, including chain in the lockers, wire on the drums and deck cargo. It must stay within the vessel's approved stability information and the company's limits.
  3. Approach: the vessel backs up to the rig and the rig crane passes over the pennant. The deck crew connect it to the work wire, then clear the deck before any load is applied.
  4. Run-out: the vessel steams out along the planned bearing while paying out wire. The bridge watches tension, heading and the position of the wire between the towing pins.
  5. Set anchor: the anchor is lowered to the seabed in position, and the rig tensions up its mooring.
  6. Return: the pennant is returned to the rig or buoyed off, depending on the system. Work wire is recovered and the deck is made safe before the next anchor.

Recovery usually means chasing down the mooring line with a chaser or grapnel, or picking up a pennant buoy, and breaking the anchor out. Recovery often produces the highest loads of the whole job.

When anchor handling restricts her ability to manoeuvre, or a towing operation severely restricts her ability to deviate from course, the vessel shows the restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre signals under COLREG Rule 27, in addition to any towing lights required by Rule 24.

The real hazards on deck and on the bridge

Anchor handling has caused deaths, including the loss of entire vessels. Take the following hazards seriously.

  • Capsize from girting or tripping: if a heavily loaded tow line or work wire leads out on the beam, it can roll the vessel over in seconds. Towing pins, correct heading, an available emergency release on winch and jaws, and stability checks made in advance are the defences. The 2008 Intact Stability Code, as amended, includes guidance specific to vessels engaged in anchor handling and towing. Your vessel's own stability booklet sets the actual limits.
  • Snap-back and parting wire or chain: wire, chain or connecting gear under tension can fail without warning. Everyone on deck must stay out of the bight and out of the danger zones the bridge has identified.
  • Moving loads on an open deck: chain, anchors, shackles, sockets and pennants weigh tonnes and move with the vessel.
  • Green water over the stern: the aft deck is low and open to the sea. Waves boarding the stern have washed crew off their feet and over the side.
  • Heavy motion: an AHTS rolls quickly and hard in a seaway, which raises the risk of slips, crush injuries and fatigue.
  • Stored hydraulic energy: shark jaws, pins and winch brakes can release suddenly.

Most companies forbid anyone from entering the working deck while load is on the system unless the bridge has given clear permission. They also give the deck crew safe havens and radio contact with the bridge. Industry guidance such as GOMO (which replaced the earlier NWEA guidelines) and regional or client standards is widely used to set these practices.

Certificates and experience usually expected

No single international "AHTS certificate" exists. Expect a mix of mandatory STCW items and documents that the region or client requires.

For everyone:

  • STCW basic training, the security training certificate appropriate to your role (security awareness or designated security duties), and a valid seafarer medical.
  • BOSIET with HUET (or the regional equivalent) where crew change by helicopter. This is standard in the North Sea and common elsewhere offshore. Some regions also require a specific offshore medical on top of your flag medical.
  • Some clients require banksman/slinger or rigging competence, or certified crane operators. Requirements vary by region.

For deck officers:

  • A CoC with the correct tonnage endorsement. Many AHTS are under 3,000 GT, but larger units exist, so check the vessel.
  • DP certification under a recognised scheme. It is often needed even to be considered for officer roles on DP-fitted units.
  • Anchor handling experience is the main thing hiring managers look for. Some companies send officers on simulator-based anchor handling courses. These are company or client requirements rather than STCW.

For engineers:

  • AHTS have high propulsion power for their size. STCW sets engineer certificate levels by propulsion power (kW), not GT, so make sure your CoC covers the installed power.
  • Experience with CPP, thrusters, DP-related machinery and bulk cargo systems helps. High voltage training is relevant on diesel-electric units.

Crew and life on board

Crews are small compared with a deep-sea ship. Exact manning depends on the flag's minimum safe manning document, the client and the type of operation. A typical crew includes:

  • Deck: Master, Chief Officer, one or more officers who often double as DPOs, a Bosun or lead AB, ABs, and sometimes OS.
  • Engine: Chief Engineer, Second Engineer, often further engineers or motormen, and on many DP vessels an ETO.
  • Catering: Cook, and on larger crews a steward or messman.

What the job is like day to day:

  • Watches are often 6-on/6-off or 12-hour shifts. During a rig move the work can carry on around the clock. Hours of rest rules still apply, so log them honestly.
  • Rotations differ by region. Equal time on and off is common in some offshore markets, while other markets run contracts closer to deep-sea lengths.
  • Port calls are mostly at supply bases. You get quick turnarounds and little shore time.
  • Motion is a fact of life. Even seasoned deep-sea hands can get seasick on an AHTS at first.
  • Teamwork is tight. The bridge, the winch operator and the deck crew depend on each other's discipline. Using your stop-work authority is expected, not frowned on.

Ratings coming from bulkers or tankers usually find the seamanship far more hands-on: wire splicing, handling shackles and sockets, and working chain on deck.

Oral exam style: questions you should be able to answer

Masters and superintendents often test candidates informally before hiring. Examiners also like these topics. Be ready for:

  • What is girting and how do you prevent it? The vessel capsizes because a tow or work wire pulls from abeam. Prevention covers heading control, keeping the wire between the towing pins, watching tension, stability checks made in advance and being ready to use the emergency release.
  • Why check stability before anchor handling? Wire tension at the stern roller acts as a heavy load applied high and aft. Chain in the lockers and wire on the drums also change the vessel's condition.
  • Purpose of shark jaws and towing pins? The jaws hold the load so the crew can connect or disconnect safely. The pins keep the wire in a safe sector over the stern.
  • Where should the deck crew be when load is on the wire? Clear of the bight and the snap-back zones, in a safe haven, and in radio contact with the bridge.
  • What signals does an AHTS show while anchor handling? When the operation restricts her ability to manoeuvre, the RAM lights and shapes under Rule 27(b). When towing, she shows the Rule 24 towing lights. If the tow severely restricts her ability to deviate from course, she also shows the RAM signals under Rule 27(c).
  • When would you release the tow? When vessel safety is at risk, for example a heel developing that cannot be controlled. Know your own vessel's release system and the sequence for using it.

How to get your first AHTS contract

  • Get the documents first. Complete BOSIET/HUET for your target region. Officers should start the DP certification route early, because it takes time to finish.
  • Use offshore-specialist manning agents and owners. Many deep-sea agencies do not crew AHTS. Indian seafarers should check that the agent holds a valid RPSL from the DG Shipping. Filipino seafarers should check that the manning agency is licensed by the DMW (formerly POEA).
  • Start in a regional market. The Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and West Africa often have more entry openings than the North Sea, where previous anchor handling experience is usually expected.
  • Step across from related vessels. Time on PSVs, tugs or other OSVs makes a strong stepping stone. Many officers move from PSV DPO to AHTS.
  • Accept a lower rank to get in. Experienced deep-sea officers sometimes join as junior officer or trainee DPO to log anchor handling time.
  • Show seamanship in interviews. Talk confidently about rigging, wire care, stability under external loads and safe deck practice.
Key points
  • An AHTS handles rig anchors, tows offshore units and supplies installations, all from one hull.
  • Capsize by girting and snap-back from wire or chain under load are the defining hazards.
  • Stability must be checked for expected wire tension, chain in lockers and deck cargo before the job.
  • BOSIET/HUET and, for officers, DP certification are commonly expected; exact needs depend on region and client.
  • Engineer CoCs are limited by propulsion power in kW, and AHTS have high power for their size.
  • Crews are small, the motion is heavy and teamwork with stop-work authority is essential.
  • PSV, tug or OSV experience is the usual stepping stone to a first AHTS contract.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an AHTS and a PSV?

A PSV is built mainly to carry deck and bulk cargo to installations. An AHTS also has a heavy anchor handling and towing winch, a stern roller, shark jaws and towing pins, and high bollard pull. AHTS can do supply work, but PSVs cannot handle anchors or tow rigs.

Do I need a DP certificate to work on an AHTS?

It is not required for every rank or every vessel. However, many AHTS are DP-fitted, and charterers usually want bridge officers holding recognised DP certification. Ratings and engineers do not normally need it, though engineers benefit from DP familiarisation.

Is BOSIET mandatory for AHTS crew?

BOSIET is not an STCW requirement. It is set by the offshore industry and by regional rules, and it is standard where crew transfer by helicopter, such as the North Sea. Many employers in other regions also ask for it, so check the client's requirements before you book a course.

Can a deep-sea AB get a job on an AHTS without offshore experience?

Yes, but usually in a regional market and often after gaining offshore training such as BOSIET. Strong practical rigging and mooring skills help a lot. Some ratings get in through PSVs or harbour tugs first.

Why is my engine CoC an issue on a small AHTS?

STCW sets engineer certificate levels by the ship's propulsion power, not by gross tonnage. An AHTS can be small in GT but have very high installed power. A CoC limited to lower power will not cover it.

Test yourself

What is 'girting' on a tug or AHTS?

Girting is a sideways heeling force from a beam lead of the line that can capsize the vessel rapidly. Stability guidance for towing and anchor handling is in the 2008 IS Code as amended.

What is the main purpose of the shark jaws on an AHTS?

Shark jaws hold the load so connection work can be done safely. Keeping the wire within a safe sector is the job of the towing (guide) pins.

Which training is commonly required for AHTS crew changing by helicopter offshore?

Helicopter transfer offshore normally needs BOSIET/HUET, which is an industry and regional requirement rather than STCW.

For an engineer joining an AHTS, what mainly determines whether their CoC is valid for the vessel?

STCW Chapter III certificate levels are set by propulsion power. That is why high-powered but small AHTS catch out engineers whose CoC carries a power limitation.

Where should deck crew be while the work wire is under heavy tension?

Crew must stay out of danger zones while load is on the system and enter the deck only with bridge permission, as required by company SMS and industry guidance such as GOMO.
