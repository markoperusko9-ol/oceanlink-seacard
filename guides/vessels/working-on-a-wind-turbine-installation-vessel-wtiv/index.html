Working on a Wind Turbine Installation Vessel (WTIV)

Quick answer

A WTIV is usually a self-propelled jack-up vessel with a large crane. It loads turbine foundations or components in port, positions on DP at each offshore wind farm location, then jacks itself out of the water to lift with precision. Getting aboard usually takes offshore or DP experience, project-specific safety training such as GWO, and comfort with jacking, heavy lifts and long periods of waiting on weather.

What a WTIV does and how it is built

A wind turbine installation vessel carries offshore wind components from a marshalling port to the wind farm and lifts them into place. Depending on the project and the vessel, that means foundations (monopiles and transition pieces), tower sections, nacelles and blades.

Most purpose-built WTIVs are self-propelled jack-ups. Typical features:

  • Legs and jacking system. Usually four legs, sometimes three or six, often with spudcans at the feet. The jacking system lifts the hull clear of the sea so the crane works from a stable platform.
  • A main crane of very high capacity. It is often mounted around one of the legs to save deck space, and newer units are built for ever larger turbines.
  • Dynamic positioning. The vessel holds position on DP while lowering legs. Redundant DP (commonly equipment class 2) is normal on modern units, but always check the vessel's DP class notation.
  • Diesel-electric propulsion with high-voltage switchboards, thrusters and a large power demand from the jacking and crane systems.
  • Large open deck with project-specific grillages and seafastening for the components.
  • Accommodation well beyond normal ship crew size, because project and client personnel live on board.

Not every installation vessel is a jack-up. Some foundation work is done by floating heavy-lift and crane vessels. This guide focuses on the jack-up type, which is what most people mean by "WTIV".

Trade pattern and the project cycle

WTIVs work on project charters rather than regular liner or tramp trades. A typical cycle runs like this:

  1. Load-out at the marshalling port. Components are lifted aboard and seafastened. The vessel often jacks up alongside to load, so the crew must understand quay and seabed conditions there as well.
  2. Transit to the field. Distances are short compared with deep-sea work, but transit has its own weather limits because of high deck cargo and long legs.
  3. Positioning and jacking at each turbine or foundation location.
  4. Installation lifts, coordinated with the client's installation team.
  5. Jack down, move to the next location, and repeat until the deck is empty. Then return to port.

The main markets have been the North Sea and wider European waters. Activity has grown in East Asia and has started in the United States. Cabotage and local employment rules differ by country and can affect who is crewed on a particular project, so check before you assume a vessel is open to your nationality.

Weather drives everything. Crane lifts, blade handling, jacking and transit each have their own limits in the vessel's operations manual. Waiting on weather is a normal part of the job.

Jacking operations step by step

Jacking is the operation that most separates a WTIV from a normal ship. Exact limits and sequences come from the vessel's operations manual, the class-approved documentation and the project's site-specific assessment. The outline below is general.

  1. Pre-arrival checks. Review the site-specific assessment, which covers soil data, predicted leg penetration and any seabed hazards. Seabed hazards can include unexploded ordnance in some European waters, cables, boulders and old footprints. Confirm that the forecast is within jacking limits for the whole operation, not just the start.
  2. Approach and position on DP within the target box. Confirm DP status, thruster availability and position references.
  3. Lower the legs to touch-down. This phase, together with lifting the hull clear, is the most sensitive to waves and swell. Legs and hull can be hit by wave-induced impact loads before the vessel is firmly founded.
  4. Preload. The legs are loaded beyond the loads expected during the operation. On some units this is done leg by leg, on others with ballast. The aim is for the soil to have already taken more than it will see in service. Preloading reduces the risk of a sudden leg failure later.
  5. Monitor penetration against the predicted values. Unexpected rapid penetration means stop and assess.
  6. Raise the hull to a small air gap first, then to the planned working air gap. That air gap is chosen to keep waves clear of the hull.
  7. During operations, keep monitoring leg loads, levelness and weather.
  8. Jacking down and leg extraction reverse the process. Legs can be held by suction in some soils, and extraction can take time.

The biggest single geotechnical hazard is punch-through. A leg breaks through a stronger soil layer lying over a weaker one and drops suddenly. The result can be severe heel, structural damage or worse. Good site data, proper preloading and strict adherence to procedure are the defences.

Key hazards on board

  • Punch-through and leg/seabed problems. These are covered above. Never short-cut a preload.
  • Heavy and suspended loads. Dropped objects, swinging loads and taglines under tension are everyday risks. Blades catch the wind, so a blade lift can become a sail very quickly.
  • Working at height and overside. This applies to seafastening removal, rigging and access to the transition piece and turbine.
  • SIMOPS. Crane work, jacking, deck work, CTV or walk-to-work transfers and other vessels in the field often happen at the same time. Permit-to-work and clear communication are essential.
  • High voltage and stored energy. Diesel-electric plant, large thrusters and jacking drives make HV switching and lock-out/tag-out routine for engineers and ETOs.
  • Collision and allision. Fields contain partly built turbines, foundations, guard vessels and transfer craft. While on DP during positioning and leg lowering (and on floating installation vessels during lifts), the vessel is normally restricted in its ability to manoeuvre and must show the correct signals. Light and signal arrangements when jacked up depend on flag, coastal state and company requirements, so check the vessel's procedures.
  • Fatigue. Long shifts during project work and broken sleep while waiting on weather wear people down. Hours of rest rules still apply.

The statutory framework varies. Depending on design, flag and how many non-crew personnel are carried, a WTIV may be certified under the SPS Code, under the MODU Code, or as a cargo ship carrying industrial personnel under SOLAS chapter XV and the IP Code. Know which applies to your vessel, because it affects manning, life-saving appliances and the personnel you carry.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your STCW certificate of competency and basic training remain the foundation. Wind clients and operators commonly add the following. Exact requirements vary by company, client and flag.

Requirement Who usually needs it Notes
GWO Basic Safety Training Often all marine crew, especially anyone accessing turbines Industry standard from the Global Wind Organisation, not an STCW certificate. Modules typically include first aid, manual handling, fire awareness, working at heights and sea survival.
DP certificate (recognised DPO scheme) Deck officers, especially those standing DP watches Previous DP hours, often on DP2 vessels, are valued highly.
High voltage training Engineers and ETOs Diesel-electric HV plant is standard.
Crane operator certification Crane operators Must meet the vessel's flag and company requirements. Offshore crane experience is usually expected.
Rigging, slinging and banksman training Deck ratings, bosun, riggers Often company or industry certified.
BOSIET/HUET Only where helicopter transfers are used Many wind projects use port, CTV or walk-to-work crew changes instead.
Offshore medical Some operators Usually in addition to the STCW medical certificate.

There is no single STCW endorsement for jacking. Companies normally train jacking operators in-house, and some prefer officers with jack-up rig, liftboat or heavy-lift backgrounds.

Crew composition and life on board

A WTIV typically carries the following people:

  • Marine crew. Master, chief officer, DP-qualified officers, chief engineer, engineers, ETO(s), bosun, ABs and fitters. On units run under MODU-style arrangements, the command structure may include an offshore installation manager or a separate jacking role.
  • Crane and deck team. Crane operators, riggers and banksmen.
  • Hotel and catering crew. This team is large because of the number of people on board, and may include a medic.
  • Client and project personnel. Installation technicians, supervisors, surveyors and the client's representatives. Total persons on board often reach around a hundred or more on larger units.

What life is like:

  • Shift work, not watches. Twelve-hour shifts are common during projects, but follow the company roster and hours of rest records.
  • Rotations. European operators frequently use fixed rotations of a few weeks on and off. Arrangements vary widely, so read your contract.
  • Stable when jacked up. Once out of the water there is little motion. Transits in a short, steep North Sea swell are a different matter.
  • Close to shore. Internet and crew change access are often better than deep-sea, but visas for the coastal state or the crew change country must be sorted before joining.
  • A project culture. Daily toolbox talks, client audits, tight safety rules and heavy paperwork are normal. People who are disciplined and communicate well do well here.

How to get your first WTIV contract

  1. Build relevant sea time. The usual stepping stones are offshore support vessels, AHTS, PSV, jack-up rigs, heavy-lift ships and other DP work. Deep-sea bulk or tanker experience alone is rarely enough for officer roles.
  2. Get DP certified and log DP hours if you are a deck officer. Engineers should build experience with diesel-electric plant, HV systems and thrusters.
  3. Take GWO Basic Safety Training at an accredited centre if your target employers ask for it. Check before paying, because some companies arrange it themselves.
  4. Target the right employers. These are WTIV owners and operators, offshore wind contractors and the manning agencies that crew for them. Many vessels are on European registers, and nationality, cabotage and visa rules can limit openings on some projects.
  5. Present your CV the offshore way. List DP class and hours, vessel types, crane and lifting experience, HV work and any jack-up time clearly at the top.
  6. Be ready to step down a rank for a first trip. Some officers join as junior DPO or junior engineer to break in.
  7. Keep everything valid. Clients check certificate expiry dates closely, and an expired GWO module or medical can cost you a joining date.

Oral exam: questions you should be ready for

Interviewers and senior officers on these vessels often test practical understanding.

  • Why do we preload, and what are you watching during it? Preloading pushes the legs to a soil capacity above the loads expected in service. You watch penetration against prediction, leg loads and hull levelness.
  • What is punch-through and how do you reduce the risk? It is a sudden leg drop through a strong layer over a weak one. You reduce the risk with site assessment, proper preload, a small initial air gap and following the procedure.
  • When is jacking most sensitive to the sea state? At leg touch-down and while lifting the hull clear, before the vessel is firmly founded.
  • What is air gap and why does it matter? It is the clearance between the sea surface and the hull underside. It must keep waves off the hull in the design conditions.
  • What is your status under the COLREGs while positioning on DP and lowering legs? Normally restricted in its ability to manoeuvre, showing the appropriate lights or shapes.
  • What do you do on a DP degradation during leg lowering? Follow the vessel's DP and jacking procedures and the activity-specific operating guidelines. That usually means stopping the operation and moving to a safe condition.
Key points
  • Most WTIVs are self-propelled jack-ups with a heavy-lift crane, DP and diesel-electric HV plant.
  • Jacking is the defining operation: preload properly and watch for punch-through.
  • Leg touch-down and lifting the hull clear are the phases most sensitive to sea state.
  • GWO Basic Safety Training, DP certification and HV training are commonly requested in addition to STCW.
  • Prior offshore, DP, jack-up or heavy-lift experience is the usual route in.
  • The statutory framework (SPS Code, MODU Code or SOLAS XV/IP Code) depends on vessel and flag.
  • Expect shift work, project discipline, a large non-crew complement and time waiting on weather.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need GWO training to work on a WTIV?

GWO is not a legal requirement under STCW, but many wind clients and operators require it, particularly for anyone who may access turbines or foundations. Requirements differ by company and project. Confirm with the employer before booking, as some arrange the training for new joiners.

Can I join a WTIV without DP experience?

Officers without DP experience can sometimes join, mainly in non-DP roles. Deck officers standing DP watches are normally expected to hold a recognised DP certificate and logged DP hours. Engineers are usually judged more on diesel-electric, HV and thruster experience.

Is a WTIV a ship or an offshore unit?

It depends on design and flag. Some are certified under the SPS Code, some under the MODU Code, and others carry industrial personnel under SOLAS chapter XV and the IP Code. The vessel's certificates and safety management system will tell you which framework applies.

Can Indian and Filipino seafarers get WTIV jobs?

Yes, particularly those with offshore DP or jack-up backgrounds, but openings vary by project. Cabotage rules, coastal state employment conditions and visa requirements for the crew change country can limit some contracts. Check those points with the employer early.

How are crew changes done on a WTIV?

Crew changes commonly happen in the marshalling port, by crew transfer vessel or by walk-to-work gangway in the field. On some projects they are done by helicopter, which is when BOSIET/HUET-type training is needed. Rotations are set by the company and often follow fixed on/off periods.

Test yourself

What is the main purpose of preloading the legs of a jack-up WTIV?

Preloading makes the foundation soil take more than it will see during operations, guarding against punch-through. Exact methods come from the vessel's class-approved operations manual.

What is punch-through?

Punch-through is a geotechnical failure that can cause severe heel or structural damage. Site assessment and proper preloading are the main defences, as set out in the vessel's operations manual.

During which phase is a jacking operation generally most sensitive to waves and swell?

Before the vessel is firmly founded, legs and hull are exposed to wave-induced impact loads. Limits are set in the vessel's jacking procedures and site-specific assessment.

A WTIV is holding position on DP while lowering its legs (or a floating installation vessel is lifting on DP) and cannot readily manoeuvre. What is its usual COLREG status?

The definition of a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre is not limited to the listed examples, and work of this nature fits it. COLREG 1972 as amended, Rules 3(g) and 27(b).

Which statement about GWO Basic Safety Training is correct?

GWO is a wind industry standard, not part of STCW, and many operators require it for marine crew as well. STCW basic training under STCW Code Section A-VI/1 is still required.
