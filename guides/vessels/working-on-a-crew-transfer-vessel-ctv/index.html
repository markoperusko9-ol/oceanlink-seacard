Working on a Crew Transfer Vessel (CTV)

Quick answer

A crew transfer vessel is a small, fast workboat, usually a catamaran, that carries technicians and light cargo from port or a mothership to offshore wind turbines and platforms. Crews are small, typically a master plus one or two deckhands or an engineer. The core skill is putting the bow safely against a turbine so people can step across. Entry depends heavily on the flag and coastal state, because many CTVs operate under national small-vessel codes and national certificates rather than standard STCW deep-sea manning.

What a CTV does and where it works

A CTV's job is simple to describe and hard to do well. It moves people, tools and small spares between shore, or a service operation vessel (SOV), and offshore structures, then waits nearby or does a circuit of turbines before bringing everyone back.

Most CTVs work in offshore wind. The established markets are the North Sea, the Irish Sea and the Baltic, with fleets in the UK, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands and Belgium. Newer work is in Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and the US East Coast. Some CTVs also support oil and gas platforms, port projects and survey work.

A typical day:

  • Early technician briefing and toolbox talk
  • Loading tools and bags, often from a pontoon or quayside crane
  • A fast passage out
  • A series of turbine transfers
  • Standby close to the field
  • Pick-ups in reverse order
  • Return before dark or at the end of the shift

In construction phases and on far-offshore fields, CTVs may work from an SOV or a hotel base instead of returning to port daily.

Typical design and layout

Most CTVs are aluminium or composite catamarans in roughly the 20–30 m range. A smaller number are monohulls, SWATH designs or surface-effect craft. Propulsion is usually twin high-speed diesels driving waterjets or propellers. Some newer vessels are hybrid or designed for alternative fuels.

The usual layout is:

  • Bow fendering and transfer deck. A heavy rubber fender on the bow is pushed against the turbine's boat-landing tubes. A raised, non-slip platform with handrails sits behind it, and some vessels fit a motion-compensated gangway or step.
  • Wheelhouse forward or midships. It gives good visibility over the bow, with engine and jet controls at both forward and aft-facing stations on some designs.
  • Passenger cabin. It has seating for technicians, often suspension seats, plus lockers, a mess area and toilets. Many CTVs carry no more than 12 technicians. The SOLAS definition of a passenger ship applies above 12 passengers, and national small-vessel codes use the same threshold, so this limit keeps the vessel outside passenger-ship rules.
  • Foredeck and aft deck cargo areas. They take tool boxes, small containers and fuel or water tanks, sometimes with a small deck crane.
  • Limited crew accommodation. Day-boat designs may have only a bunk or two. Vessels built for longer deployments have proper cabins.

The rules a CTV is built and operated under vary by flag and size. Some are certified under the High-Speed Craft (HSC) Code. Many smaller ones fall under national small commercial vessel codes, such as the UK's Workboat Code. Rules on carrying industrial personnel have also developed in recent years: SOLAS chapter XV and the IP Code (in force 1 July 2024) apply to cargo ships and high-speed cargo craft of 500 GT and upwards carrying more than 12 industrial personnel on international voyages. Check your vessel's actual certificates rather than assuming.

Operations and key hazards

The turbine transfer is the defining operation.

  1. The master approaches the boat landing at slow speed and puts the bow fender on the landing tubes.
  2. The master holds the vessel there with steady forward thrust (the push-on method), so the fender grips without sliding.
  3. The deckhand checks the fender is stable and gives the go-ahead.
  4. The technician, wearing a harness and lifejacket, steps across from the transfer deck to the ladder at the moment the master and deckhand judge right. They connect to the turbine's fall-arrest system exactly as the operator's transfer procedure specifies, then climb.
  5. Bags and tools are lifted by the turbine davit or crane. They are not carried or passed across by hand unless the procedure allows it.
  6. The vessel backs off once the technician is clear of the danger zone.

Whether a transfer goes ahead is usually decided against a significant wave height (Hs) limit. The vessel's design and the operator's procedures set that limit. The master also makes a dynamic assessment on the spot, and the master can always say no. Do not quote a fixed Hs figure from one company as if it were universal.

Main hazards:

  • Crush and fall between the vessel and the structure. The fender slips or the vessel surges, and a person can be caught between the hull and the landing.
  • Person overboard. This risk exists during transfers and on open decks at speed. Quick recovery with a recovery net, sling or davit is essential in cold northern waters.
  • Collision and allision. Fields are dense with structures, other CTVs, SOVs, guard vessels, fishing gear and cable-lay or jack-up spreads. Speed must be safe under COLREG Rule 6, and a proper look-out under Rule 5 matters even more at 20+ knots.
  • Motion and fatigue. Hours of slamming bring seasickness, back and neck strain and whole-body vibration. Long standby periods also affect alertness.
  • Dropped objects and lifting. Davit and crane lifts at the landing are made over people and a moving deck.
  • Fuel and refuelling. CTVs often bunker little and often, or deliver fuel to turbines and SOVs.
  • Weather turning. Getting technicians back matters most. A forecast that allowed the morning drop-off may not allow the afternoon pick-up.

Certificates and experience usually expected

The flag and coastal state decide what CoC you need. In practice this is the biggest barrier for foreign officers.

  • Master and mate. Many CTV flags accept national certificates for smaller vessels, such as a code-vessel master or a near-coastal master certificate limited by tonnage. Some employers want a full STCW deck CoC. If your CoC is from another administration, you may need the flag's endorsement or recognition first.
  • Engineer. Where an engineer is carried, a national small-vessel or STCW engineering certificate appropriate to the propulsion power is usually required. Some CTVs carry no dedicated engineer and the master or deckhand covers basic engineering.
  • Deckhand. STCW basic safety training is expected. Some flags also require a rating certificate or a national workboat qualification.
  • HSC type rating. This is required where the craft is certified under the HSC Code, and it is specific to the craft type and route.
  • Wind-industry training. Wind operators widely expect crew to hold GWO (Global Wind Organisation) Basic Safety Training modules alongside STCW. Sea survival is the most common. Offshore oil and gas clients may ask for BOSIET/HUET instead.
  • Valid seafarer medical acceptable to the flag. Some clients add an offshore medical.

Employers usually value:

  • Small-boat handling experience, for example tugs, pilot boats, workboats, rescue boats and fast rescue boats
  • Twin-engine and waterjet handling
  • Cold-water and North Sea experience

Crew composition and life on board

Crews are small. A common pattern is:

Role Typical duties
Master / skipper Navigation, transfers, go/no-go decisions, client liaison, paperwork
Mate or deckhand Transfer deck, mooring, cargo, technician safety, MOB recovery, cleaning, daily checks
Engineer (on some vessels) Engines, jets, fuel, generators, maintenance, defect reporting

Some vessels work with two crew, others three. It depends on the safe manning document and the client contract.

Life on board is very different from deep-sea work:

  • Day shifts. You often sleep ashore in a hotel or rented accommodation, or on an SOV.
  • Rotations. Two weeks on, two weeks off is common, but rotations vary by employer and project.
  • Constant motion. Small catamarans move a lot, and sea legs take time.
  • High client contact. You work closely with the client and technicians. The wind farm operator's marine coordination centre tracks every movement.
  • Lots of paperwork. Expect daily logs, technician manifests, transfer records, fuel records and incident reporting under the company SMS.
  • Weather days. Weather stand-downs can mean long periods in port doing maintenance.

How to get your first CTV contract

For Indian and Filipino seafarers, be realistic. Most CTV fleets are flagged and crewed locally in Europe, and coastal-state rules, CoC recognition and work permits all apply. In the US, cabotage law generally means CTVs serving wind farms are US-flagged and US-crewed. Newer Asian markets each have their own national rules on crewing.

Steps that actually help:

  1. Decide which market you are targeting. Find out which flag and certificate that market's CTVs need. Ask the administration and the operators, not social media.
  2. Check whether your CoC can be recognised or endorsed by that flag, and what extra assessment is needed.
  3. Get the wind-industry training clients ask for, especially GWO sea survival or full GWO BST, from an accredited provider.
  4. Build small-craft handling time. Tugs, pilot boats, rescue boat duty, workboats and offshore support vessels all count. Log your hours carefully.
  5. Consider SOVs and larger offshore vessels. They recruit internationally more often and put you inside the wind sector, where CTV contacts are made.
  6. Apply to CTV operators and wind-specialist crewing agencies directly. Be honest about your certificates. Deckhand roles are a common way in, even for officers.
  7. Have the right to work in the country of operation sorted before you expect an offer.

Oral exam style: questions an examiner or client might ask

Q: Before a transfer, what do you check? Check the wind, swell and Hs against limits, and the tidal stream at the landing. Confirm the fender condition, that the transfer deck is clear and the deckhand is ready. Check technician PPE, the lifejacket and harness. Confirm comms with the technician and the turbine, the MOB equipment is ready, and the boat landing and ladder are in good condition.

Q: During a push-on the bow starts to slide on the landing. What do you do? Stop the transfer immediately. Nobody steps across. Adjust thrust and heading or back off, then reassess. If the vessel cannot hold steady, the transfer does not happen.

Q: A technician falls in between the vessel and the turbine. Your actions? Back off and keep the propulsion clear of the casualty. Raise the alarm and inform the marine coordination centre. Keep sight of the casualty, deploy the recovery equipment and recover them per your MOB procedure. Then treat for cold shock and hypothermia, and report.

Q: The forecast is marginal for the afternoon pick-up. Do you take technicians out this morning? Discuss it with the client and the coordination centre, and plan for the return leg, not just the outbound. If you cannot be confident of getting people back off the turbines, do not drop them off. Weather is a master's decision under the company SMS.

Key points
  • CTVs are small, fast workboats, mostly catamarans, carrying technicians to offshore wind turbines.
  • The bow push-on transfer to the turbine boat landing is the core skill and the biggest crush and MOB risk.
  • Many CTVs carry no more than 12 technicians, keeping them outside passenger-ship rules under SOLAS and national codes.
  • Required certificates depend on flag and coastal state; many vessels use national small-vessel codes.
  • Wind clients widely expect GWO Basic Safety Training on top of STCW basic safety training.
  • Crews are typically two to three people working day shifts on rotation.
  • For foreign officers, CoC recognition and the right to work locally are usually the main hurdles.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an STCW CoC to work on a CTV?

It depends on the flag and size of the vessel. Many CTVs operate under national small commercial vessel codes that accept national masters' or engineers' certificates, while some employers and larger craft require a full STCW CoC. Always check with the flag administration and the operator.

Is GWO training mandatory for CTV crew?

GWO is an industry standard, not an IMO requirement. Wind farm operators and CTV companies widely ask crew to hold GWO modules, especially sea survival, in addition to STCW basic safety training. Treat it as expected rather than optional if you want wind work.

Why do many CTVs carry only 12 technicians?

Under SOLAS, a ship carrying more than 12 passengers is a passenger ship, with much heavier construction and certification requirements, and national small-vessel codes use the same threshold. Keeping to 12 or fewer keeps most CTVs out of that category. Newer IMO and flag rules on industrial personnel also affect larger vessels.

Can Indian or Filipino officers get CTV jobs in Europe?

It is possible but harder than deep-sea work. You generally need your CoC recognised or endorsed by the vessel's flag and the right to work in the country of operation. Many people enter the wind sector first through larger offshore vessels such as SOVs, which recruit internationally more often.

Do you live on board a CTV?

Usually not for long periods. Most CTVs work day shifts and crew sleep ashore in hotels or accommodation, or on a mothership on far-offshore fields. Some larger CTVs have cabins for extended deployments.

Test yourself

During a push-on transfer, the bow fender begins sliding on the boat landing. What is the correct action?

A transfer only proceeds when the vessel is stable against the landing, and the risk of crushing between hull and structure is the main danger. This reflects company SMS transfer procedures and the master's overriding authority under the company's safety management system.

Why are many CTVs limited to carrying no more than 12 technicians?

SOLAS defines a passenger ship as one carrying more than 12 passengers, and national small-vessel codes use the same threshold. COLREG, STCW and MARPOL set no such limit (SOLAS Chapter I, Regulation 2).

Who decides what certificate of competency a CTV master needs?

Certification and manning are set by the flag administration. For smaller vessels this is often under national codes rather than a single international CTV certificate (STCW Convention, Article I and Regulation I/2).

A CTV is transiting a wind farm at speed in moderate visibility. Which COLREG requirement is most directly relevant to choosing that speed?

Rule 6 requires a safe speed that allows proper action and stopping, taking account of factors such as visibility, traffic density and navigational hazards (COLREG 1972 as amended, Rule 6).

The morning forecast allows the outbound transfer, but conditions are expected to worsen in the afternoon. What should drive the decision?

Dropping technicians off without a reliable plan to recover them is a key hazard. Go/no-go decisions rest with the master (company SMS; on vessels where the ISM Code applies, Section 5 sets out the master's overriding authority).
