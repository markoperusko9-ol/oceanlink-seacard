Working on an Accommodation Vessel (Flotel and Walk-to-Work)

Quick answer

An accommodation vessel houses large numbers of offshore workers next to an oil, gas or wind installation. Personnel usually cross by gangway, helicopter or crane transfer. Expect DP operations, a big hotel department, strict muster and evacuation discipline, and offshore-industry certificates such as BOSIET/HUET on top of your STCW tickets. Most people get in through offshore support vessel experience, DP training or, on the hotel side, catering experience.

What an accommodation vessel does

An accommodation vessel, often called a flotel, provides beds, meals, offices and welfare facilities for offshore workers. These workers cannot all be housed on the installation they are working on.

Typical jobs:

  • Hook-up and commissioning of new platforms, when hundreds of extra workers are needed for months.
  • Maintenance campaigns and shutdowns on existing platforms.
  • Decommissioning, where the platform's own accommodation may already be out of use.
  • Offshore wind construction and service work, often as walk-to-work (W2W) vessels.
  • Coastal and nearshore construction, where moored accommodation barges house project labour.

The ship's job is to stay safely in position, keep a large number of people fed, rested and accounted for, and get them to and from the worksite. The marine crew are the service provider. The client's project dictates the schedule.

Types of unit and typical layout

The term covers several quite different designs:

Type Station keeping Typical use
DP monohull accommodation / W2W vessel Dynamic positioning, often with a motion-compensated gangway North Sea, offshore wind, field support
Semi-submersible flotel DP, mooring spread, or both Large hook-up and shutdown campaigns
Accommodation barge Moored, usually non-propelled Sheltered or shallow waters, coastal projects
Jack-up accommodation unit Legs on the seabed Shallow-water fields

Common layout features:

  • A large accommodation block with many cabins. Shared cabins are common for project personnel.
  • Galley, mess rooms, laundry, gym, cinema or recreation rooms and client offices.
  • A gangway or bridge connection to the installation. On DP units this is usually motion-compensated.
  • A helideck on many units.
  • Deck cranes for stores and sometimes personnel transfer.
  • Lifeboats and liferafts sized for the full complement. Lifesaving capacity is a hard limit on how many people can be on board.

The regulatory basis depends on design, flag and coastal state:

  • SOLAS chapter XV and the IP Code apply to many cargo ships carrying industrial personnel.
  • Some vessels are certified under the Special Purpose Ships (SPS) Code.
  • Semi-submersibles and jack-ups often fall under the MODU Code.
  • Many coastal states add their own offshore safety regime.

Check which regime applies to your unit during familiarisation.

Operations and key hazards

There is no cargo in the usual sense. The 'cargo' is people, plus the stores, fuel and water to support them.

Gangway operations

  • Connection, disconnection and keeping the gangway within its operating envelope are the core marine tasks.
  • Most systems use staged alarms. A warning stage stops new transfers. An emergency stage means clearing the gangway and disconnecting.
  • Weather and motion limits are set by the manufacturer, the vessel's procedures and the client. They are not something to stretch.

Dynamic positioning

  • Drift-off or drive-off while connected to a platform is the main marine risk. The consequences include collision with the installation and injury to people on the gangway.
  • Operations follow the vessel's DP operations manual, activity-specific operating guidelines and the client's requirements.

Working inside the installation's safety zone

  • SIMOPS (simultaneous operations) with platform cranes, supply vessels and helicopters are routine.
  • A gas release or general alarm on the installation can trigger an emergency disconnect and move-off.

Large numbers on board

  • POB (persons on board) control is critical. Every arrival and departure must be logged so musters reconcile.
  • Fire risk is concentrated in galleys, laundries (lint in dryer ducts) and cabins.
  • Food hygiene and infectious illness spread fast in a crowded hotel, so outbreak control matters.
  • Fresh water, sewage and garbage volumes are high, and MARPOL compliance gets real attention.

Helicopter and crane transfers bring their own procedures, helideck team and weather limits.

As an underway vessel engaged in an operation that restricts her ability to manoeuvre, a DP unit connected by gangway would normally show the Rule 27(b) signals. A barge riding to its own anchors or mooring spread would normally show the Rule 30 anchor lights and shape. Units made fast alongside, or jacked up, follow the requirements of the flag, coastal state and port.

Worked scenario: alarm while the gangway is connected

Situation: You are the DPO on watch. The vessel is connected to a platform by gangway, and a shift change is under way with workers crossing. A thruster trips. Position and heading start to wander, and the gangway's telescopic stroke heads towards its limit.

A sound response, in outline (your vessel's procedures govern the detail):

  1. Recognise and announce. Call out the failure on the bridge. Check that the DP system is compensating with the remaining thrusters, and see whether the vessel is still inside its operating guidelines.
  2. Stop transfers. Raise the gangway warning so nobody else steps on. Tell the gangway operator and the installation's control room.
  3. Clear the gangway. If the vessel is outside its guidelines or the gangway is reaching its limits, escalate to the emergency stage. Get everyone off the gangway to whichever end the vessel's and installation's procedures (bridging document) specify. This is often the nearer, safer end.
  4. Disconnect and move off. Lift or retract the gangway and move to a safe position away from the structure.
  5. Inform and record. Notify the Master, the client and the installation. Log events and keep DP data for the investigation.

Examiners and DP assessors want to see that you protect people first, communicate early, and never try to 'save the operation' outside the agreed limits.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your STCW base stays the same for your rank: CoC, endorsements, STCW basic training, medical fitness and the rank-specific courses. On top of that, employers commonly expect the following:

Offshore safety and survival

  • BOSIET with HUET (and later the FOET refresher) is commonly required wherever crew change or travel by helicopter. These are offshore-industry standards, not STCW certificates.
  • Some coastal states and clients also require their own offshore medical in addition to your flag-state seafarer medical.

Deck and navigation

  • DP certification for DPOs, usually through the Nautical Institute scheme or a recognised equivalent, and DP experience on similar vessel types.
  • Advanced Fire Fighting, Medical First Aid or Medical Care on board, and GMDSS, as required by STCW for your rank.

Specialist roles

  • Gangway operators, crane operators, HLOs and helideck crew need role-specific training. This is often set by the client, the coastal state or the equipment maker.

Hotel and catering staff

  • Hotel staff with safety duties on the muster list need STCW basic training.
  • Cooks usually need a ship's cook or food-hygiene qualification acceptable to the flag and employer.

Crowd management and crisis management training are mandatory under STCW for passenger ships. Some flotel operators require them anyway because of the numbers on board. Check the company's matrix rather than assuming.

Typical crew composition

The marine crew is usually similar to an offshore support vessel:

  • Master, Chief Officer and DPOs/OOWs.
  • Chief Engineer, engineers and an ETO.
  • Bosun, ABs, and motormen or oilers.
  • On barges, a barge master or equivalent may lead.

On some units, especially MODU-type flotels, an OIM leads under the coastal state's regime. The division of authority between OIM and Master is set in the unit's safety management documents and the applicable regime.

The hotel department is often the largest group on board, and frequently employed by a catering contractor:

  • Camp boss or chief steward.
  • Cooks, bakers and stewards.
  • Laundry and cleaning staff.

Support roles may include:

  • Medic.
  • Radio operator or POB coordinator.
  • HLO and helideck team.
  • Gangway operators and crane operators.
  • Client representatives.

Everyone, including contractor and client personnel, appears on the muster list and attends drills. Muster discipline with several hundred people is the defining safety culture of the vessel type.

Life on board and getting your first contract

Life on board

  • Rotations follow offshore patterns in many regions. Equal-time rotations are common in some sectors, while others run longer trips. It depends on the company, region and contract.
  • Crew change may be by helicopter, crew boat or in port.
  • Food and recreation facilities are usually good, because the vessel exists to keep workers rested.
  • Internet access is generally available. Bandwidth and policy vary.
  • Strict drug and alcohol policies are normal offshore. Expect testing.
  • You may spend weeks stationary next to the same platform. The work is procedural, audited and client-facing, with frequent drills, toolbox talks and permit-to-work activity.

Getting a first contract

  • Deck officers: Build DP experience on OSVs, PSVs or similar first. A trainee DPO route with sea time logged under the DP scheme is the usual path.
  • Engineers and ETOs: Experience with DP-class power plants, thrusters and high voltage helps.
  • Ratings: OSV or offshore deck experience, plus BOSIET/HUET, makes you more attractive to offshore manning agencies.
  • Hotel staff: Shipboard or offshore catering experience, food-hygiene qualifications and STCW basic training are the usual entry points.

Apply through established offshore operators, catering contractors and licensed manning agencies. Indian seafarers should check DG Shipping-approved RPSL agencies. Filipino seafarers should check that the agency is licensed by the DMW (formerly POEA). Never pay for a 'guaranteed' offshore job.

Oral exam style questions

Q: What lights and shapes does a DP accommodation vessel show when connected to a platform by gangway? She is restricted in her ability to manoeuvre. By day she shows ball-diamond-ball. By night she shows all-round red-white-red, plus masthead and sidelights when making way through the water.

Q: Are offshore workers on board passengers? Not normally. Industrial or special personnel are a defined category. They are covered by the IP Code under SOLAS chapter XV or the SPS Code, depending on the vessel and certification.

Q: What limits the maximum POB? The vessel's certificated complement, mainly lifesaving appliance capacity and accommodation as approved by the flag. The client cannot override it.

Q: What do you do on a general alarm from the installation while connected? Follow the agreed bridging document and procedures. Typically you stop transfers, clear the gangway, disconnect and move to a safe position, keeping the installation informed.

Q: Who must attend drills? Everyone on board, including catering, contractor and client personnel, according to the muster list.

Key points
  • Accommodation vessels house offshore workers beside installations, typically using gangway, helicopter or crane transfer.
  • Main hazards: DP drift-off or drive-off while connected, gangway incidents, SIMOPS in the safety zone, and fire and illness with high POB.
  • Workers on board are industrial or special personnel, not passengers. The SPS Code, IP Code or MODU Code may apply depending on the unit.
  • BOSIET/HUET and an offshore medical are often required on top of STCW tickets. Requirements vary by coastal state and client.
  • The hotel department is often the largest group, and every person on board is on the muster list.
  • Never exceed the gangway or DP operating limits to keep an operation going.
  • OSV and DP experience is the usual route in for officers. Catering experience is the usual route in for hotel staff.

Frequently asked questions

What is a flotel?

A flotel is a floating hotel: a vessel or unit that accommodates offshore workers near an installation. It can be a DP monohull, a semi-submersible, a jack-up or a moored barge. Workers usually reach the installation by gangway.

Do I need BOSIET to work on an accommodation vessel?

It is commonly required where crew or personnel travel by helicopter, especially in the North Sea and other regulated offshore regions. BOSIET is an offshore-industry standard and does not replace STCW basic training. Check the operator's training matrix for the specific contract.

Can a hotel steward work on an accommodation vessel without sea experience?

Some catering contractors take people with shore hotel or catering backgrounds. They will still need STCW basic training, a seafarer medical and usually food-hygiene qualifications, and offshore survival training if the job involves helicopter travel. Previous shipboard or offshore catering experience makes selection much easier.

Is DP certification needed for deck officers on accommodation vessels?

On DP units, officers operating the DP system are normally expected to hold DP certification, commonly under the Nautical Institute scheme, plus relevant DP experience. Moored barges and jack-ups may not need DPOs. Requirements are set by the company, the client and industry guidance rather than by STCW alone.

Are accommodation vessels treated as passenger ships?

Generally not, because offshore workers are industrial or special personnel rather than passengers. Such vessels are typically certified under the SPS Code or the IP Code (SOLAS chapter XV). Semi-submersible and jack-up units often fall under the MODU Code. The exact certification depends on design, flag and coastal state.

Test yourself

A DP accommodation vessel is connected to a platform by gangway. Which day signal should she show?

Her ability to manoeuvre is restricted by the operation, so she shows ball-diamond-ball. Two balls is not under command, a cylinder is constrained by draught, and one ball is at anchor. COLREG Rule 27(b).

Offshore workers accommodated on an accommodation vessel are normally classed as:

SOLAS chapter XV and the IP Code (or the SPS Code, depending on certification) treat them as a distinct category, not passengers. They must still be mustered and accounted for.

A steward is assigned to direct personnel at muster on an accommodation vessel. What STCW training applies as a minimum?

Seafarers with designated safety or pollution-prevention duties in the muster list need basic training, not just familiarisation. STCW Code, Section A-VI/1.

Which statement about BOSIET/HUET is correct?

BOSIET with HUET is an industry (OPITO) standard commonly required for offshore and helicopter travel. It sits alongside the STCW Section A-VI/1 requirements rather than replacing them.

While personnel are crossing the gangway, the vessel moves outside its DP operating guidelines after a thruster failure. What is the priority?

Protecting people on the gangway comes first. Once outside the operating guidelines, the vessel stops transfers, clears the gangway and disconnects. This follows the vessel's DP and gangway procedures under its ISM Code safety management system.
