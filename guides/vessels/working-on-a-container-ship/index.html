Working on a Container Ship

Quick answer

Container ships carry standardised boxes on fast liner schedules. Port stays are short and cargo work is done mostly by shore gantries. Crew work centres on lashing, reefer monitoring, dangerous goods segregation, stability and keeping to schedule. There is no mandatory STCW cargo endorsement for this vessel type, so a clean set of standard certificates, solid watchkeeping and good company references are what get you aboard, although LNG- or methanol-fuelled ships also require IGF Code training.

What a container ship does and how she is laid out

A container ship carries cargo in standard ISO containers, mostly 20 ft and 40 ft. Capacity is measured in TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units). Most ships run on liner services. They follow a fixed rotation of ports on a published schedule, so the ship keeps the same rhythm voyage after voyage. Feeder ships shuttle boxes between hub ports and smaller ports on short coastal or regional runs.

Typical layout on a cellular container ship:

  • Holds fitted with cell guides. Vertical guide rails hold the stacks in place below deck, so boxes in the holds are generally not lashed.
  • Hatch covers. These are usually pontoon (lift-away) covers handled by the shore gantry. Containers are stowed on top of them.
  • Deck stacks. These are secured with twistlocks, lashing rods and turnbuckles. Larger ships have lashing bridges between bays so the crew can reach the upper tiers.
  • Accommodation and bridge. These may be aft or set forward of the engine room on larger ships. A bridge set further forward improves visibility over the deck cargo.
  • Reefer sockets on deck and in some holds supply power to refrigerated containers.

Stowage positions follow a bay–row–tier system. Odd bay numbers usually mark 20 ft positions, and even numbers mark a 40 ft box spanning two 20 ft bays. Rows are counted outward from the centreline, odd to starboard and even to port. Tiers are numbered upward from the bottom of the hold, with deck tiers given a separate higher series. Check the exact convention in your ship's bay plan.

Cargo operations: what the crew actually does

The terminal plans the stow and drives the cranes. The ship's job is to check the plan, monitor the operation and secure the cargo. Expect these duties:

  • Checking the stowage plan. The chief officer checks the terminal's bay plan for stability, stack weights, lashing forces, visibility and dangerous goods segregation, using the approved loading computer. Arrival and departure conditions are checked against stability criteria and the Cargo Securing Manual.
  • Lashing and unlashing. On many trades shore lashing gangs do this work. On others the crew does it, or checks it before sailing. Deck ratings check twistlocks (manual, semi-automatic or fully automatic), tension turnbuckles and confirm that bridge fittings are in place.
  • Reefer watch. Plugging in, unplugging and recording temperatures, and reporting faults. The ETO or a designated engineer usually leads reefer fault-finding. Many ships also have remote reefer monitoring.
  • Dangerous goods. Checking the DG manifest against the stow for segregation, stowage category and placarding, under the IMDG Code and the ship's Document of Compliance for dangerous goods.
  • Damage and leakage checks. Watch for damaged or leaking boxes, and make sure every exception is recorded before departure.
  • Hatch cover and gangway watch. Shore gantries travel along the quay throughout the stay, so the accommodation ladder and moorings need constant tending.

Since SOLAS required a verified gross mass (VGM) for every packed export container, a box without a VGM should not be loaded. In practice cargo weights still differ from the declared figures. Keep a sceptical eye on stack weights shown in the loading computer.

Key hazards on container ships

Lashing work. Lashing work is one of the main sources of injury on container ships. The main risks are:

  • falls from lashing bridges and from the tops of hatch covers;
  • falling twistlocks and lashing gear;
  • crush injuries between stacks;
  • working under cranes that are moving boxes overhead.

Wear your helmet and high-visibility clothing, use fall protection where the SMS requires it, and never stand under a suspended load.

Quayside traffic. Straddle carriers, terminal tractors and reach stackers do not watch for seafarers on the quay. Use the designated walkways and follow terminal rules.

Fire. Misdeclared or undeclared dangerous goods have caused major container ship fires. A fire deep in a deck stack is very hard to reach. Ships built from 2016 that carry containers on deck must carry a water-mist lance, and those designed for five or more tiers on deck also need mobile water monitors. Know where these are stowed and how to rig them.

Parametric and synchronous rolling. Ships with fine hulls, pronounced bow flare and wide flat sterns can build up violent rolls. Parametric rolling occurs in head or following seas when the wave encounter period is about half the natural roll period. Synchronous rolling occurs when the encounter period is close to the natural roll period, typically in beam, quartering or following seas. The result can be lashing failure and lost stacks. The IMO adverse-weather guidance to masters covers changing course and speed to break the resonance.

Restricted visibility from the bridge. High deck stacks create a large blind sector ahead. SOLAS sets the maximum blind sector ahead and the loading plan must respect it. A sharp lookout matters even more on these ships.

Electrical. Reefer sockets carry high current, and many large ships have high-voltage main switchboards. Follow lock-out tag-out.

Fatigue. Short port stays, back-to-back ports and pilotage at all hours make rest-hour compliance a real challenge on these ships. Record your rest hours honestly.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Container ships do not need an STCW cargo-specific endorsement of the kind tankers and gas carriers require. Expect the following:

  • Everyone: STCW basic safety training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR), security awareness or designated security duties as appropriate, a valid medical fitness certificate, a seaman's book and passport. A US C1/D visa is needed on many services.
  • Deck officers:
    • the CoC for your rank;
    • Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (PSCRB);
    • ECDIS generic training, plus type-specific familiarisation for the equipment fitted;
    • Radar/ARPA, GMDSS GOC and BRM;
    • Advanced Fire Fighting and Medical First Aid or Medical Care as your level requires;
    • Ship Security Officer where assigned.
  • Engineers:
    • the CoC for your rank;
    • Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (PSCRB);
    • ERM;
    • high voltage training at management level where the plant operates above 1,000 V, which is common on large container ships.
  • Ratings: Rating forming part of a navigational or engineering watch, or Able Seafarer Deck/Engine, plus PSCRB where designated to survival craft or rescue boat duties.

Alternative-fuel ships. On ships using gases or other low-flashpoint fuels (e.g. LNG- or methanol-fuelled container ships), IGF Code basic training is mandatory under STCW Regulation V/3. Advanced training is also required for those with immediate responsibility for fuel systems.

What employers add on top varies. It may be a company IMDG Code or dangerous goods course, reefer training, a loading computer course or in-house lashing training. These are company or charterer requirements, not STCW endorsements, and they differ between operators. Some flags also want their own endorsement of your CoC. Check with the company and flag before joining.

Crew composition and life on board

A typical complement looks like this:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, second and third officers, sometimes a fourth officer, plus deck cadets.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second, third and fourth engineers, ETO and engine cadets.
  • Ratings: bosun, ABs and OS on deck; fitter, motormen or oilers and a wiper below.
  • Catering: cook and messman.

Exact numbers depend on the ship's Minimum Safe Manning Document and company policy. Larger ships with heavy reefer loads usually carry an ETO, and sometimes a dedicated reefer technician.

Life on board is shaped by the schedule:

  • Short port stays. Port stays are often from several hours to a day or two, depending on ship size and the number of moves, so shore leave is often limited or impossible. Feeder runs can mean several ports a week.
  • Long sea passages on deep-sea services give a steadier watch routine and time for maintenance.
  • Weather routing and speed. Schedule pressure is real, but the master's authority to reduce speed or alter course for safety comes first.
  • Modern equipment. Integrated bridges, ECDIS, large electronically controlled engines on newer ships, and plenty of connectivity on many fleets.
  • Steady liner routine. The same rotation repeatedly means you get to know the pilots, ports and terminals well.

For cadets, container ships give strong exposure to navigation, stability work and busy pilotage waters. Hands-on cargo work is less varied than on bulk carriers or general cargo ships, because shore staff handle most of it.

Worked scenario: departure stability and lashing check

You are the chief officer. Final cargo is completing, and the terminal sends the final bay plan two hours before departure.

  1. Load the final plan into the approved loading computer. Check GM, shear forces, bending moments and torsion against the limits. Compare drafts with the actual readings once cargo is complete.
  2. Check stack weights and lashing forces bay by bay against the Cargo Securing Manual or the lashing software. Look hardest at heavy boxes stowed high in the outer rows.
  3. Check bridge visibility against the SOLAS blind-sector limit with the final deck heights.
  4. Cross-check the DG manifest against the stow for segregation, stowage category and any position-specific prohibitions under the ship's DG Document of Compliance.
  5. Physical round with the bosun. Confirm twistlocks are locked, rods fitted and turnbuckles tight, especially on the outboard stacks and in the forward bays exposed to green water.
  6. Reefer list. Confirm every reefer is plugged in and running at its set temperature, and note any faults on the hand-over list.
  7. Weather. Review the forecast for the passage. If heavy head or following seas are expected, brief the master and the bridge team on the risk of parametric rolling.

If any check fails, tell the master before the pilot boards. Restowing a box costs minutes in port. Losing a stack at sea costs far more.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like container topics. Be ready for these:

  • What is the Cargo Securing Manual and who approves it? It is a ship-specific manual, required under SOLAS for ships carrying cargo units, and approved by the Administration. It covers securing devices, stowage and lashing arrangements and the limits that apply.
  • What is VGM and who is responsible? It is the verified gross mass of a packed container. The shipper must provide it before loading, and a container without one should not be loaded.
  • Explain parametric rolling and how you avoid it. It is a resonant build-up of roll in head or following seas, linked to changing waterplane stability as waves pass along the hull. You avoid it by altering course and/or speed to change the encounter period, using the IMO adverse-weather guidance.
  • How do you fight a fire in a deck stack? Raise the alarm and muster. Identify the box and its contents from the DG manifest and the IMDG Code. Cool the surrounding containers with boundary cooling, and use the water-mist lance or mobile monitors where fitted. Consider changing course to put the fire downwind.
  • What limits bridge visibility, and what is the rule? Deck cargo creates the blind sector ahead. SOLAS limits it, and the loading plan must comply.

How to get your first container ship contract

Container fleets are dominated by large liner companies and big third-party managers. Many of them recruit through their own manning offices in India and the Philippines. Practical steps:

  • Get your documents in order. You need a valid CoC or rating certificate, STCW courses, medical, passport, seaman's book and, where relevant, an INDoS number. Many container services call at US ports, so a valid US C1/D visa helps you a lot.
  • Apply to licensed manning agencies only. In India check DG Shipping recruitment and placement service licensing. In the Philippines check that the agency is properly licensed with the DMW. Never pay to be placed on a vessel.
  • Cadets should look at sponsorship schemes run by container operators and managers. Many fleets take their cadets from their own training programmes.
  • Crossing over from another vessel type? Highlight your ECDIS and BRM training, busy-traffic and pilotage experience, and any stability or loading computer work. Engineers should point to experience with large two-stroke engines, HV systems and reefer plant.
  • Ask about company courses such as IMDG and loading computer training, and complete them before joining if the company offers them.
  • Be honest about your experience. Container operators check references. A clean appraisal record and good conduct reports from your last ship often count more than an extra short course.
Key points
  • Container ships run fixed liner schedules with short port stays, and shore gantries do most cargo handling.
  • Crew focus areas are lashing, reefer monitoring, dangerous goods segregation, stability and schedule.
  • Lashing work, quayside traffic and fires in deck stacks are the main hazards.
  • SOLAS requires a verified gross mass for every packed container before it is loaded.
  • Parametric rolling in head or following seas can cause lashing failure and lost stacks.
  • No STCW cargo endorsement is required, but companies often add IMDG or loading computer courses, and LNG- or methanol-fuelled ships require IGF Code training.
  • A valid US C1/D visa and clean references help a lot when seeking a first contract.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a container ship?

No. STCW has no container-specific endorsement like the tanker or gas carrier endorsements. You need the standard certificates for your rank. On LNG- or methanol-fuelled ships, IGF Code training under STCW Regulation V/3 is mandatory. Companies often add their own IMDG Code, loading computer or reefer training, so check with the operator.

Do seafarers lash containers themselves?

It depends on the trade and the port. In many ports shore lashing gangs do the work. On some trades and feeder services the crew lash or unlash, and the crew always check the securing before sailing.

Is there shore leave on container ships?

Often less than on other vessel types. Port stays are short, often from several hours to a day or two depending on ship size and the number of moves. Shore leave also depends on port immigration rules, the terminal's location and your watch schedule.

Is a container ship good for a deck cadet's first ship?

It is good for navigation, busy pilotage waters, stability calculations and modern bridge equipment. Hands-on cargo handling is less varied than on general cargo or bulk ships, because shore staff do most of it. Many cadets value both kinds of experience during their training.

What does an ETO do on a container ship?

The ETO maintains the ship's electrical, electronic and automation systems. On container ships they also usually lead reefer fault-finding and maintenance of the reefer sockets. On large ships this can be a significant share of the workload.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS, what must happen before a packed export container is loaded?

The shipper is responsible for providing the verified gross mass, and a container without one should not be loaded (SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 2).

In the usual bay numbering system, an even bay number indicates:

Odd bays usually mark 20 ft positions, and even numbers mark a 40 ft box occupying two adjacent 20 ft bays. Port and starboard are shown by the row number, not the bay.

What does SOLAS limit regarding the view of the sea surface ahead from the conning position?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 22 sets the blind sector limit at two ship lengths or 500 m, whichever is less. Fitting a camera does not remove the requirement.

Parametric rolling is most likely when:

Parametric roll develops in head or following seas when the encounter period is about half the natural roll period, and synchronous rolling occurs when the two periods are similar. The remedy is to change course and/or speed, as set out in the IMO revised guidance to the master on adverse weather (MSC.1/Circ.1228).

Who approves a ship's Cargo Securing Manual?

SOLAS requires a Cargo Securing Manual approved by the Administration, drawn up to at least the standard of IMO guidelines (SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5 and Chapter VII, Regulation 5).
