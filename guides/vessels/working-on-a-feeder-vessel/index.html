Working on a Feeder Vessel: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

A feeder vessel is a small container ship that carries boxes between large hub ports and smaller regional ports. The routes are short and the port calls frequent, so the work is mostly cargo operations, mooring, pilotage and stability calculations, and fatigue is the biggest hazard. No special STCW certificate is required beyond what your rank needs, but companies prefer container experience, good ECDIS and stability skills, and people who can handle a fast pace.

What a feeder vessel does

Large container ships call at only a handful of hub ports. Feeder vessels collect containers from smaller ports and bring them to the hub. They also take transhipment boxes from the hub back out to the regional ports. Some feeders run their own short-sea services and are not linked to one hub.

There is no single legal definition of "feeder". The term usually means a container ship at the small end of the size range. Where the cut-off sits depends on the trade and on who you ask. Typical trading areas include:

  • Intra-Asia and Gulf services
  • North Europe and the Baltic
  • The Mediterranean and Black Sea
  • The Caribbean, West Africa and the Indian subcontinent coast

A service usually loops through a fixed rotation, and some loops repeat weekly. You may visit the same berths many times in one contract. That helps with familiarity. It also breeds complacency.

Layout and typical equipment

Most feeders follow the standard cellular container ship layout:

  • Holds with cell guides, closed by hatch covers. Many smaller feeders use pontoon-type hatch covers, which are lifted off by crane.
  • On-deck stowage on the hatch covers, secured with lashing rods, turnbuckles and twistlocks. Larger vessels have lashing bridges.
  • Reefer sockets on deck, and sometimes in the holds, for refrigerated containers.
  • Accommodation, which may be aft or forward depending on design.

Geared and gearless ships

  • Gearless feeders rely on shore gantry cranes.
  • Geared feeders carry their own deck cranes. This lets them call at ports with little shore equipment. On geared ships, crane maintenance becomes a major deck and engine job.
  • Who drives the ship's cranes in port varies. It may be stevedores or the crew, depending on the port and the company.

Engineering plant

  • Main engine, auxiliary generators, purifiers and the usual auxiliaries.
  • A larger electrical load than a bulk carrier of similar size, because of the reefers.
  • Many feeders have a bow thruster, and some have a controllable pitch propeller. Both are useful for frequent berthing.
  • Feeders that trade in Emission Control Areas switch fuels, or run scrubbers, to meet MARPOL Annex VI sulphur limits. Fuel changeover procedures and record keeping get a lot of attention from inspectors.

Cargo operations and key hazards

Cargo planning and stability

  • The stowage plan usually comes from the operator or a shore planner.
  • The Chief Officer checks the plan on the loading computer for stability, stack weights, lashing forces and visibility from the bridge.
  • With short port stays, the final figures may arrive late. Do not let time pressure push you into sailing on figures you have not checked.
  • Under SOLAS Chapter VI, a packed container should not be loaded without a verified gross mass (VGM). Misdeclared weights still happen, so treat stack-weight alarms seriously.

Lashing

  • Lashing must follow the vessel's approved Cargo Securing Manual.
  • In some trades and ports, crew do the lashing and unlashing. In others, shore lashers do it.
  • The main risks are:
    • falls from hatch covers or lashing bridges;
    • being struck by swinging boxes or spreaders;
    • hand injuries from lashing gear;
    • working under crane loads.
  • Stay out of the line of fire. Wear proper PPE and high-visibility clothing. Agree clear communication with the crane driver and the foreman.

Dangerous goods

  • Feeders regularly carry IMDG Code cargo.
  • Officers must check the dangerous goods manifest against the stowage plan for segregation.
  • Know where DG boxes are stowed, and keep the Emergency Schedules (EmS) to hand.
  • Damaged or leaking containers must be reported, not ignored.

Reefers

  • Reefers are plugged in, monitored and logged. The ETO, electrician or a nominated rating often does this. The detail depends on the company.
  • Alarms and power failures need prompt action. Know which socket groups are fed from which switchboard sections.

Navigation and mooring

Frequent port entries bring their own risks:

  • dense coastal traffic;
  • many pilot boardings and landings;
  • tug work;
  • mooring operations several times a week.

Mooring line snap-back and pilot ladder accidents are real risks on these ships, mainly because the jobs are repeated so often.

Fatigue: the defining hazard

On a feeder, the problem is rarely the sea. It is the clock.

Why fatigue builds up

  • A short sea passage is followed by arrival stations, pilotage, mooring, cargo watch, departure and another pilotage.
  • On smaller crews, the same officers do bridge watches and cargo watches.

Minimum rest under STCW and MLC 2006

  • At least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period.
  • At least 77 hours of rest in any 7-day period.
  • Rest may be split into no more than two periods, and one of them must be at least 6 hours long.
  • The interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours.

Your flag state may allow limited exceptions. The details depend on the administration and must be in line with the Convention.

What this means in practice

  • Record your hours honestly. Falsified records hide the problem and leave you exposed if something goes wrong.
  • Tell the Master early if the port rotation makes compliance impossible. A fatigued OOW on a coastal passage is a well-known accident pattern.
  • Use BNWAS properly, and never disable it.

Crew composition and life on board

Crew numbers are set by the vessel's Minimum Safe Manning Document and by company policy. Crews on feeders are often smaller than on deep-sea ships. A typical complement includes:

Department Usual ranks
Deck Master, Chief Officer, 2nd Officer, often a 3rd Officer, Bosun, ABs, OS, sometimes a deck cadet
Engine Chief Engineer, 2nd Engineer, 3rd Engineer, sometimes a 4th Engineer, motorman/oiler, sometimes a fitter
Electrical ETO or electrician on many ships (reefer load makes this common)
Catering Cook, sometimes a messman or steward

On some smaller ships, the watch system differs from the classic three-watch pattern. Ask about this at interview.

Life on board

  • Port calls are short. Time ashore depends on the rotation, port security rules, shore leave rules and how the cargo plan runs. Do not count on it.
  • On the plus side, coastal trading often means mobile signal near the coast, regular crew change ports and a predictable routine.
  • Port State Control and terminal inspections are frequent. A well-kept ship keeps paperwork, drills and records up to date as normal practice, not as a scramble before each port.

Certificates and experience usually expected

No container-specific STCW certificate exists. You need the certificates required for your rank and flag:

  • Everyone:
    • STCW basic safety training;
    • security training at the appropriate level;
    • a valid seafarer medical certificate;
    • flag state endorsement where required.
  • Deck officers:
    • the appropriate CoC;
    • GMDSS;
    • Radar/ARPA;
    • BRM;
    • ECDIS generic training;
    • type-specific ECDIS familiarisation for the installed system, as required by the flag and the company.
  • Engineers: the appropriate CoC and ERM. High-voltage training may also be required, depending on your CoC level, the ship's electrical installation and your flag administration's requirements.
  • ETO: an ETO CoC where the flag requires one. Reefer experience is valued.

What employers look for beyond certificates

  • Previous container ship experience.
  • Confidence with loading computers.
  • Familiarity with IMDG segregation.
  • For engineers, comfort with frequent manoeuvring, fuel changeovers and reefer power loads.

Some companies run their own in-house courses on container lashing, reefer handling or dangerous goods. The content and duration vary by company.

Worked scenario: a typical port call

Here is how a 14-hour port call on a feeder might run for the deck department. The details vary by company SMS.

  1. Before arrival:
    • Pass the pre-arrival checklist.
    • Test steering gear, engines astern and the thruster.
    • Rig the pilot ladder and have an officer check it.
    • Brief the mooring parties.
  2. Pilot boarding: the duty officer meets the pilot. The Master–pilot exchange is completed, including the berthing plan, tugs and the line sequence.
  3. Mooring:
    • Keep stations manned and in communication with the bridge.
    • Keep everyone clear of snap-back zones.
    • Report lines when they are fast.
  4. Cargo:
    • The Chief Officer confirms the final plan and stability.
    • Lashing begins, either by crew or shore.
    • Reefers are unplugged and plugged in as boxes move.
    • DG stowage is checked.
    • Draughts are read and stability is recalculated at the end.
  5. Departure:
    • Lashing is checked, hatch covers are secured, and gangway and ladders are stowed.
    • Engine and steering tests are logged.
    • Pilot disembarks.
    • Hours of rest are reviewed before setting sea watches.

The weak points in this sequence are steps 4 and 5. Pressure to sail can lead to skipped lashing checks or unverified final stability.

Oral exam angle and getting your first contract

Questions examiners like on container and feeder work

  • Why must the master not load a container without a VGM? Because SOLAS Chapter VI requires a verified gross mass, and wrong weights threaten stack strength, lashing and stability.
  • What document governs how you lash containers? The vessel's approved Cargo Securing Manual.
  • How do you check DG segregation? Compare the DG manifest with the stowage plan, using the IMDG Code segregation requirements.
  • What are the hours-of-rest minimums? 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days, under STCW and MLC.

Getting the first contract

  • Apply through licensed manning agents. In India, use agencies holding a valid DG Shipping RPS licence. In the Philippines, use agencies licensed by the Department of Migrant Workers (formerly POEA).
  • Target feeder and short-sea operators. Container line feeder arms and regional ship managers are worth approaching directly.
  • Highlight your experience. Show any container, coastal or high-port-frequency sea time on your CV.
  • Cadets and juniors: feeders give a lot of pilotage, mooring and cargo experience in a short time, which looks good in your training record book.
  • Prepare for interview. Expect questions on stability, lashing, IMDG basics and how you manage fatigue.
  • Protect yourself. Never pay unofficial fees for a job. MLC 2006 prohibits charging seafarers for recruitment and placement, with limited exceptions such as certain documents.
Key points
  • Feeder vessels are small container ships that link hub ports with regional ports on short, port-heavy rotations.
  • Frequent port calls make fatigue, mooring, pilot boarding and lashing the main hazards.
  • No container-specific STCW certificate exists; you need the standard certificates for your rank and flag.
  • Under SOLAS Chapter VI, packed containers need a verified gross mass before loading.
  • Lash only in line with the ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual, and check DG segregation against the IMDG Code.
  • Hours of rest under STCW and MLC are at least 10 in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days; record them honestly.
  • Use licensed manning agents and never pay unofficial recruitment fees.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a feeder vessel and a mainline container ship?

A feeder is a smaller container ship that moves boxes between hub ports and smaller regional ports. A mainline ship runs long deep-sea routes between major hubs. Feeders make far more port calls and spend less time at sea. The size cut-off is not legally defined and varies by trade.

Do I need a special certificate to work on a feeder vessel?

No STCW certificate is specific to container or feeder ships. You need the certificates required for your rank and flag, such as basic safety training, security training and your CoC with the related courses. Some companies add in-house training on lashing, reefers or dangerous goods.

Is working on a feeder vessel tiring?

It often is. Frequent arrivals, departures and cargo watches can cut into rest. STCW and MLC 2006 set minimum rest hours that must still be met. If the rotation makes compliance difficult, raise it with the Master rather than altering your records.

Do crew have to lash containers on feeder ships?

It depends on the port, the trade and the company. In some places crew do the lashing, and in others shore lashers do it. Either way, lashing must follow the ship's approved Cargo Securing Manual, and officers remain responsible for checking it before sailing.

Is a feeder vessel good for a cadet's first ship?

It can be very good for practical experience. Cadets see frequent pilotage, mooring, cargo operations and stability work. The pace is fast, so a supportive officer team matters. Make sure the company's training programme and the record book requirements can be met on that rotation.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS Chapter VI, what is required before a packed container is loaded?

SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 2 as amended requires a verified gross mass before a packed container is loaded. Officers do not survey each box, and flag state approval is not needed per container.

Which document sets out how containers must be secured on a particular vessel?

SOLAS Chapters VI and VII require cargo to be secured in accordance with the approved Cargo Securing Manual. The other documents cover security, manning and safety management, not lashing.

What are the minimum hours of rest under STCW and MLC 2006?

STCW Code Section A-VIII/1 and MLC Standard A2.3 set 10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days. Being in port does not remove the weekly limit.

A container on the DG manifest is found stowed next to an incompatible class. What governs whether this stowage is acceptable?

Segregation of dangerous goods in packaged form, including containers, is governed by the IMDG Code under SOLAS Chapter VII. Booking notes, yard plans and voyage length do not override it.

Who may lawfully charge a seafarer for finding them a job under MLC 2006?

MLC 2006 Standard A1.4 prohibits charging seafarers recruitment or placement fees, apart from limited costs such as certain national documents. A receipt or a wage deduction does not make the fee lawful.
