Working on a Bulk Carrier

Quick answer

Bulk carriers move unpackaged dry cargo such as iron ore, coal, grain and fertiliser in large holds, usually on tramp trades with irregular ports and long anchorage waits. No special STCW tanker-style endorsement is required, but you need sound knowledge of the IMSBC Code, cargo liquefaction, hull stress and enclosed space hazards. For many deck and engine cadets, a bulk carrier is the most common first ship.

What a bulk carrier does and how it trades

A bulk carrier loads homogeneous dry cargo straight into its holds without packaging. The main cargoes are iron ore, coal and grain. Other common cargoes include bauxite, alumina, cement, fertilisers, sugar, nickel ore, petcoke and scrap. Some ships also lift steel products and logs.

Most bulkers work the tramp market. They go wherever the next charter sends them, so you rarely know your ports more than a voyage ahead. A typical pattern is a long ballast passage, then a few days loading at a mine or grain terminal, then a long laden passage and days or weeks waiting at anchor for a discharge berth. Some larger ore and coal carriers run on long-term contracts between the same load and discharge ports.

The common size classes are:

Class Typical use
Handysize / Handymax / Supramax Often geared (own cranes and grabs). Flexible trading to small ports. Wide cargo mix.
Panamax / Kamsarmax Usually gearless. Coal, grain, ore.
Capesize / VLOC Gearless. Iron ore and coal between major terminals.

The boundaries between classes are industry conventions. They are not regulatory definitions, and companies draw them differently.

Layout: holds, hatches, tanks and the engine room

A conventional bulker has the accommodation and engine room aft and a long cargo deck forward. The deck is divided into five to nine holds, depending on size. Each hold has a hatch cover. These are typically side-rolling or folding steel covers, hydraulically or electrically driven, and they seal by rubber packing on the cover panels pressing against a compression bar on the coaming.

Key structural features:

  • Topside tanks (upper wing tanks) and hopper tanks (lower wing tanks) give the hold its sloped shape, which helps cargo trim itself and limits grain shift.
  • Double bottom tanks under the holds hold ballast and, on some designs, fuel.
  • On many ships one hold is designated a ballast hold. It is flooded during heavy weather or to get a lower air draught.
  • Fore and aft peak tanks are used for trim.

Geared ships carry deck cranes, usually four, placed between hatches, often with grabs. Gearless ships rely entirely on shore equipment.

The engine room is usually a single slow-speed two-stroke main engine with diesel generators. Ballast pumps are a big part of engine room work in port. Fast-loading terminals demand that deballasting keeps pace with the shiploader. Fitting of ballast water treatment systems has added to the plant the engineers must run and maintain.

Cargo operations and the hazards that matter

Bulk carriers have a hard safety history, and SOLAS Chapter XII exists because of structural losses. These are the hazards you must understand.

1. Cargo liquefaction. Some fine, moist cargoes can behave like a liquid when the ship moves, shift suddenly and capsize her. The IMSBC Code classes them as Group A. Nickel ore, some iron ore fines and some bauxite cargoes are well-known examples. The shipper must declare the moisture content and the Transportable Moisture Limit (TML) before loading. A cargo with moisture above its TML is not safe to carry in a normal ship.

2. Chemical hazards (Group B). Coal can emit methane and self-heat. Some fertilisers decompose. Some cargoes deplete oxygen or give off toxic gases. Monitoring and ventilation follow the cargo's IMSBC schedule.

3. Hull stress. Fast loading combined with a poor sequence can overstress the hull through excessive shear forces and bending moments. The loading and deballasting sequence is planned and checked on the approved loading instrument and agreed with the terminal. The BLU Code sets out this ship–terminal cooperation.

4. Water ingress. Hatch cover leaks or hull damage can flood a hold. SOLAS Chapter XII requires water level detectors in cargo holds and other forward spaces, with alarms on the bridge.

5. Enclosed spaces. Holds, ballast tanks and adjoining spaces can be oxygen-deficient or toxic. Causes include rusting steel, coal, wood products and fumigants. Grain is often fumigated in transit, and the holds must then stay closed. Many seafarer deaths on bulkers involve people entering holds or forecastle stores without testing the air.

6. Everyday physical risk. Other daily hazards include:

  • Working at height during hold cleaning.
  • Crane and grab operations.
  • Dust exposure.
  • Gangway and mooring lines that need constant attention as draught changes by metres within hours at ore terminals.

Worked scenarios from real cargo watches

Scenario 1: suspected wet cargo. You are OOW during nickel ore loading in the rainy season. You notice free water on the cargo surface in the hold and wet-looking stockpiles ashore. Take these steps:

  1. Ask for loading to be stopped and inform the Master and Chief Officer at once.
  2. Photograph the cargo and stockpile, and record the time and conditions in the log.
  3. The Master may carry out the supplementary can test described in the IMSBC Code. Free moisture or a fluid surface is a warning sign. The test does not replace proper laboratory results, and the Master can still refuse cargo that appears unsafe.
  4. Check the shipper's declaration and moisture/TML certificates.
  5. The Master informs owners and the P&I club.

Never let commercial pressure override this.

Scenario 2: basic draught survey. The Chief Officer, often with the 2nd or 3rd Officer, runs draught surveys before and after cargo work.

  1. Read six draughts: forward, midships and aft, on both sides.
  2. Measure the dock water density with a hydrometer.
  3. Sound all ballast, fresh water and bunker tanks.
  4. Average port and starboard readings to remove the effect of list, then apply corrections for trim (to the perpendiculars and to displacement), hog or sag, and dock water density.
  5. Deduct the ship's light weight, liquids and the ship's constant to get the cargo weight.

Small reading errors become large tonnage errors on a big ship, so read carefully, preferably from a boat or the quay rather than over the rail.

Training and experience usually expected

Unlike tankers and gas carriers, bulk carriers have no specific STCW cargo endorsement. What you need:

  • STCW basic training, a valid medical certificate and your CoC or watchkeeping certificate for your rank, as for any seagoing ship.
  • Security training appropriate to your duties.
  • GMDSS, ECDIS and BRM for deck officers, and ERM for engineers, as required by your administration and company.

Companies will also look for:

  • Working knowledge of the IMSBC Code, the Grain Code and the BLU Code. These come up in CoC orals and company interviews.
  • Stability competence. This covers grain heeling moment calculations and the loading instrument for officers.
  • Crane operation on geared ships. Training and authorisation are usually company-specific. Requirements differ by company SMS and sometimes by port or flag, so check yours.
  • Enclosed space entry procedures and gas detector use. SOLAS requires regular enclosed space entry and rescue drills.

Prior bulk experience is valued for senior officers, especially for Group A cargoes, grain and fast-loading ore trades. Many owners also prepare for dry bulk vetting inspections, such as RightShip, and for port state control. Crews are therefore expected to keep the ship and paperwork in good order.

Crew composition and life on board

Manning is set by the flag state's Minimum Safe Manning Document plus company policy, so numbers vary. A typical crew is:

  • Deck: Master, Chief Officer, 2nd Officer, 3rd Officer, often one or two deck cadets, Bosun, ABs and OSs.
  • Engine: Chief Engineer, 2nd, 3rd and 4th Engineers, sometimes an ETO, Fitter, Motormen/Oilers, Wiper and engine cadets.
  • Catering: Cook and Messman.

Crews are often mixed nationality, with Indian and Filipino officers and ratings very common.

Life on board is shaped by the tramp trade:

  • Irregular schedule. Ports are often remote mine or grain terminals with limited shore leave. Long anchorages are common.
  • Hold cleaning. This is the hardest physical work aboard: sweeping, washing down, chipping, and sometimes painting hold structure between cargo grades. A grain cargo after coal demands spotless holds and a surveyor's pass.
  • Port intensity. At ore terminals you may load tens of thousands of tonnes in a day. Deck and engine teams are busy with deballasting, draughts, moorings and gangway, and rest hours need active management under MLC and STCW.
  • Sea passages are calmer and are when most maintenance gets done.

It is honest, hands-on seamanship. Many senior officers say a few bulker contracts teach more practical ship handling and cargo work than any classroom.

How to get your first bulk carrier contract

Bulkers are one of the most common first ships, especially for cadets.

  1. Go through licensed recruitment.
    • In India, use a DG Shipping-approved RPSL agency, and keep your INDoS and CDC current.
    • In the Philippines, use a manning agency licensed by the DMW.
    • Under MLC, 2006, seafarers should not be charged fees for recruitment or placement. Be wary of anyone asking for money for a guaranteed berth.
  2. Cadets: many bulk operators run sponsored cadet programmes linked to maritime institutes. Applying through these is often easier than chasing a berth after graduation.
  3. Ratings: having basic training complete and your watchkeeping or able seafarer certificate in order makes you deployable quickly.
  4. Prepare for the interview. Expect questions on enclosed space entry, liquefaction, hatch cover checks, mooring safety and, for officers, the loading instrument and draught surveys.
  5. Keep a clean record. Good appraisals and complete handovers on your first contract are what get you a re-hire on the same fleet.

Oral exam style questions on bulk carriers

Examiners like these questions. Practise answering them aloud.

  • What is the TML, and why does it matter? It is the maximum moisture content considered safe for carrying a cargo that may liquefy. It is derived from the cargo's Flow Moisture Point.
  • What are IMSBC Groups A, B and C?
    • Group A: may liquefy.
    • Group B: chemical hazard.
    • Group C: neither.
    • A cargo can be both A and B.
  • What extra safety measures does SOLAS Chapter XII bring? Structural standards for bulk carriers, water level detectors in holds, and a loading instrument on larger ships.
  • Why must grain stability be checked? Grain settles and can shift, creating a heeling moment. The ship must meet the Grain Code criteria and hold a document of authorisation, unless exempt.
  • What do you check before entering a hold after a coal cargo? Follow the permit-to-work procedure:
    • Ventilation.
    • Atmosphere testing for oxygen, flammables, CO and other toxic gases as appropriate.
    • A standby person and communications.
    • Rescue equipment ready.
Key points
  • Bulk carriers carry unpackaged dry cargo such as iron ore, coal and grain, mostly on irregular tramp trades.
  • Liquefaction of Group A cargoes is a major cause of bulk carrier losses; never accept cargo that looks wet without escalating.
  • Loading and deballasting must follow an agreed sequence checked on the loading instrument to avoid overstressing the hull.
  • Holds and adjacent spaces can be oxygen-deficient or toxic; test the atmosphere and use the permit system every time.
  • No special STCW cargo endorsement is required, but IMSBC, Grain Code and BLU Code knowledge is expected.
  • Hold cleaning and fast port turnarounds make rest hour management critical.
  • Use only licensed recruitment agencies; MLC, 2006 bars charging seafarers recruitment fees.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a bulk carrier?

No. There is no STCW cargo endorsement for bulk carriers as there is for tankers. You need the standard certificates for your rank, such as basic training, medical and CoC or watchkeeping certificate. Companies may add their own training, for example crane operation or familiarisation courses.

Is a bulk carrier a good first ship for a cadet?

For many cadets it is the most common first ship and gives solid practical experience in cargo work, stability, mooring and maintenance. The work is physical, especially hold cleaning. Ports can be remote with little shore leave.

What is cargo liquefaction on bulk carriers?

Some fine, moist cargoes can turn into a fluid state under the ship's motion and shift, which can capsize her. The IMSBC Code calls these Group A cargoes. It requires the shipper to declare moisture content and the Transportable Moisture Limit before loading.

What is the difference between a geared and gearless bulk carrier?

A geared bulker has its own deck cranes, often with grabs, so it can load and discharge at ports without shore equipment. Gearless ships, usually the larger Panamax and Capesize types, rely entirely on shore loaders and unloaders.

Why are holds on bulk carriers dangerous to enter?

Cargoes like coal, wood products and fumigated grain, along with rusting steel, can consume oxygen or release toxic or flammable gases. A hold can look harmless and still kill. Entry must follow the company's enclosed space procedure, with ventilation, atmosphere testing and a rescue plan.

Test yourself

Which IMSBC Code group covers cargoes that may liquefy?

Group A cargoes may liquefy if shipped above their TML. Group B covers chemical hazards, and Group C covers cargoes with neither hazard. IMSBC Code, Section 1 (definitions).

During ore loading you see free water on the cargo surface. What should you do first?

Visible free moisture is a liquefaction warning sign. Stop and escalate so the Master can assess, including a can test and a document check. IMSBC Code, Sections 4 and 8.

Which SOLAS chapter contains the additional safety measures specific to bulk carriers?

Chapter XII covers bulk carrier structural standards, hold water level detectors and loading instruments. Chapter VI covers carriage of cargoes generally. SOLAS 1974 as amended, Chapter XII.

What is the main purpose of the agreed loading and deballasting sequence?

Fast loading combined with a poor sequence can overstress the hull. The plan is checked on the loading instrument and agreed with the terminal. BLU Code; SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 7.

Before entering a hold after a coal cargo, which action is essential?

Coal can deplete oxygen and emit methane and CO, and none of these can be judged by eye or by brief ventilation. SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 and Chapter III, Regulation 19.
