Working on a Drillship: A Practical Guide for Seafarers

Quick answer

A drillship is a self-propelled, ship-shaped drilling unit that holds position over a deepwater well, almost always on dynamic positioning, while the drill crew works through a moonpool. Marine crew need their normal STCW certificates plus offshore survival training (usually BOSIET with HUET), and DP, high-voltage or crane experience depending on rank. Exact requirements depend on the flag, the coastal state and the drilling contractor.

What a drillship does and where it works

A drillship is a mobile offshore drilling unit (MODU) built on a ship-shaped hull with its own propulsion. It sails to location under its own power, sets up over the well and holds position while the drilling department runs a marine riser and blowout preventer (BOP) down to the seabed.

The main market is deep and ultra-deep water, where a jack-up cannot stand on the bottom and anchoring becomes impractical. Typical work includes:

  • Exploration and appraisal wells
  • Development drilling and well completions
  • Well testing and some intervention work

The unit is owned or managed by a drilling contractor and hired by a client (the oil company, usually called the operator). Contracts can be a single well or several years long. Between contracts the unit may transit long distances, or it may be warm-stacked or cold-stacked when the market is weak.

On regulation, the unit is built and certified to the IMO MODU Code and class rules under its flag. The coastal state where it drills also applies its own offshore safety regime. So two drillships with the same flag can work under quite different rules in Brazil, the US Gulf, West Africa or the North Sea.

Layout: what is different from a normal ship

Step on board and the first impression is that the ship exists to carry a drilling plant. The main features are:

  • Derrick over the moonpool. The moonpool is an opening through the hull, roughly amidships, through which the riser and drill string pass. Some modern units have a dual-activity derrick with two working centres.
  • Pipe and riser storage. There are large racks for drill pipe, casing and riser joints, with automated pipe-handling equipment.
  • BOP and LMRP. The blowout preventer stack is stored and tested on deck before being run through the moonpool.
  • Mud system. This includes mud pits, high-pressure mud pumps, shale shakers and bulk tanks for barite, cement and brine.
  • Thrusters. There are several azimuthing thrusters (six is common on modern units) and no conventional single propeller and rudder arrangement.
  • Diesel-electric power plant. Multiple main generators feed high-voltage switchboards, split into redundant groups so that a single failure does not lose position. DP equipment class 3 also requires physical separation of engine rooms and switchboards by fire and watertight divisions.
  • Helideck, large accommodation and survival craft sized for a persons-on-board figure far above any cargo ship.
  • Hazardous area zoning around the drill floor, mud handling and well-test areas, with gas detection and emergency shutdown (ESD) systems.

Operations and key hazards

Your marine job is to give the drill crew a stable, powered, correctly positioned platform. That means the hazards overlap with theirs.

Hazard Why it matters Typical controls
Well control / blowout Hydrocarbons or gas reach surface; fire and explosion risk BOP, mud weight, kick detection, well-control drills, ESD
H2S Toxic gas from some reservoirs Fixed and personal detectors, H2S drills, breathing apparatus, safe briefing areas
Loss of position Drift-off or drive-off can damage the riser, BOP or wellhead DP redundancy, well-specific operating guidelines, emergency disconnect
Dropped objects and drill floor Heavy rotating and moving equipment overhead Red zones, dropped-object surveys, restricted access
Lifting and supply boat work Constant crane traffic alongside on DP Lift plans, permit to work, weather limits
Helicopter operations Routine crew change and freight Helideck team, fire cover, HUET-trained passengers
Weather Squalls, cyclones and hurricanes Weather routing, disconnect criteria, move-off and evacuation plans

The DP side is structured around a well-specific operating guideline (WSOG), a drilling-specific form of the activity-specific operating guideline (ASOG). The details are company-specific. Commonly, a set of colour-coded status levels runs from normal operations, through an advisory stage, to a stop-and-prepare-to-disconnect stage. At the final stage the vessel initiates an emergency disconnect sequence (EDS). In an EDS the lower marine riser package (LMRP) unlatches from the BOP, which seals the well, and the vessel can then move off.

Service companies also bring radioactive logging sources and explosives for perforating. These are handled under strict permits and are not marine crew jobs. You still need to know where they are stowed and what restrictions apply.

Worked scenario. You are a DPO and a generator trips during drilling. The system redistributes load and the vessel holds position, but your WSOG says this failure moves you to advisory status. Follow it step by step:

  1. Inform the driller and toolpusher using the agreed wording.
  2. Inform the Master and the engine room.
  3. Log the event.
  4. Watch position and power margins closely.
  5. Escalate further if the WSOG conditions for the next level are met.

You do not wait for the excursion to grow before talking to the drill floor.

Crew composition and who is in charge

Persons on board on a drillship is often well over a hundred. The crew falls into three broad groups.

Marine department - Master - Chief Mate - Senior DPOs and DPOs - Chief Engineer and engineers - ETOs and electricians - Bosun, ABs and crane operators (on some units the crane operators sit in the deck or drilling department instead)

Drilling department - Senior toolpusher and toolpushers - Drillers and assistant drillers - Derrickmen - Floorhands (roughnecks) and roustabouts - Subsea engineers who maintain the BOP - Drilling mechanics and electricians

Client and third parties - The client representative (often called the company man) - Mud engineers, cementers, logging and ROV crews - Catering, which is often contracted out

Command structure varies. On some units the Master is also the Offshore Installation Manager (OIM). On others the OIM comes from the drilling side and the Master keeps responsibility for marine matters such as navigation, stability, DP and some emergencies. The split depends on the flag, the coastal state and the contractor's safety management system. Read the operations manual and emergency response plan in your first days and know exactly who gives which order.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your STCW certificate of competency, endorsements and basic safety training still apply to marine crew. On top of that, the following are well established, although the exact list is set by the contractor, client and coastal state:

  • Offshore survival with helicopter underwater escape. Most commonly this is OPITO BOSIET with HUET, kept current with the further offshore emergency training (FOET) refresher. A tropical version (T-BOSIET) is used in some regions. Some areas run their own equivalent schemes, and compressed-air emergency breathing system (CA-EBS) training is now widely expected.
  • H2S training. This is commonly required by the contractor or client before you go offshore.
  • DP certificate for DPOs. The Nautical Institute scheme is the most widely recognised. Clients often want unlimited certification plus logged experience on DP class 3 units or drilling operations specifically.
  • High-voltage training for engineers and ETOs, because the plant is diesel-electric at high voltage.
  • Offshore medical. Some regions require a region-specific offshore medical in addition to your seafarer medical certificate.
  • Well-control certification (IWCF or IADC WellSharp) for the drilling department, not for marine crew.

Some flag administrations issue MODU-specific endorsements for certain positions. Check with the flag, not with forum posts. Expect company inductions, permit-to-work training and competence assessments before you are signed off to work alone.

Life on board

  • Rotations. Rotations are usually equal time on and off, such as 21/21 or 28/28. The pattern depends on the contractor and region, which suits many seafarers who are used to long contracts.
  • Shifts. Shifts are typically 12 hours. Drilling runs around the clock, so night shift is normal.
  • Crew change. Crew change is usually by helicopter from a shore base. Sometimes it is by crew boat or during a transit.
  • No port calls. You may sit on one location for months.
  • Facilities and rules. Accommodation is generally larger than on cargo ships, with gym and recreation spaces. Alcohol is almost always prohibited under company and client policy.
  • Safety culture. Expect a heavy safety culture with a lot of paperwork:
    • frequent drills (fire, H2S, abandon unit, well control)
    • toolbox talks and observation cards
    • client audits
    • a clear stop-work authority that you are expected to use
  • Mixed crews. Crews are multinational, and local nationals are present in many regions.

Getting your first drillship contract

Drilling contractors recruit directly and through crewing agencies. Realistic entry routes are:

  • Deck officers. Build DP hours on offshore support vessels first. Trainee DPO time on a PSV, AHTS or other DP vessel is the usual stepping stone. A drillship DPO role without DP experience is rare.
  • Engineers. Diesel-electric and high-voltage experience counts most. Experience on DP-class vessels, cruise ships or LNG carriers with electric propulsion is a good match.
  • ETOs. Show high-voltage, variable-frequency drive (VFD), PLC and DP control system experience.
  • Ratings. AB and crane operator roles exist, and roustabout is a common entry point into the drilling department.
  • Cadets. Cadet berths on drillships are rare.

Practical steps:

  1. Keep your BOSIET/HUET, medical, passport and DP logbook current before you apply.
  2. Ask the employer about region-specific work permits or visas. The employer normally arranges them, but lead times can be long.
  3. Understand that local-content rules in some coastal states limit the number of expatriate positions.
  4. Check with your administration whether time on a drillship, especially while stationary on location, counts as seagoing service for your next CoC. Practice differs by administration.
  5. Never pay a recruitment fee. MLC 2006 prohibits charging seafarers for recruitment and placement.

The drilling market is cyclical. Units get stacked when demand falls, so keep your OSV or conventional fleet options open.

Oral exam style: questions you should be ready for

Offshore interviewers and some examiners like these questions:

  • What is the moonpool and why is it there? It is an opening through the hull through which the riser and drill string pass, located near midships to minimise motion.
  • Drift-off versus drive-off? Drift-off is loss of thrust, where the vessel drifts with wind and current. Drive-off is thrust pushing the vessel off position, often from a reference or control fault.
  • What happens in an EDS? The LMRP unlatches from the BOP after the BOP shuts in the well, so the vessel can move off without parting the riser.
  • What lights and shapes do you show when drilling on DP? Normally those for a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, plus any signals required by the coastal state.
  • Why split the switchboards? So that a single failure cannot black out the whole plant and lose position.
  • Who has authority in an emergency on this unit? Answer from the specific unit's emergency response plan, not a general rule.
  • What is a safety zone? Coastal states commonly establish a zone of up to 500 m around offshore installations. Vessels need permission to enter it.
Key points
  • A drillship is a self-propelled MODU that holds position over deepwater wells, usually on DP.
  • Loss of position and well control are the defining hazards; the WSOG and EDS link the bridge to the drill floor.
  • BOSIET with HUET (or a regional equivalent) and H2S training are standard entry tickets.
  • DPOs need a recognised DP certificate and logged experience; engineers and ETOs need high-voltage and diesel-electric experience.
  • The OIM and Master split differs by unit, flag and coastal state, so read the emergency response plan.
  • Equal-time rotations and 12-hour shifts are typical, with no port calls.
  • Check with your administration whether drillship time counts towards your next CoC.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a job on a drillship with no offshore experience?

It is possible but uncommon for officers. Ratings sometimes join as roustabouts. Most deck officers first gain DP experience on offshore support vessels, and engineers and ETOs are usually hired on the strength of diesel-electric and high-voltage experience.

Do deck officers on a drillship need a DP certificate?

DPO positions require a recognised DP certificate, most commonly under the Nautical Institute scheme. Clients often also ask for experience on DP class 3 units or in drilling operations. Exact requirements are set by the contractor and client.

Is BOSIET mandatory for working on a drillship?

It is not an STCW requirement, but offshore survival training with helicopter underwater escape is almost always required by the contractor, the client or the coastal state. OPITO BOSIET/HUET is the most common standard. Some regions use their own equivalents or a tropical version.

What is the usual rotation on a drillship?

Equal-time rotations such as 21/21 or 28/28 are common, with 12-hour shifts while on board. The exact pattern depends on the contractor, the region and your contract.

Does drillship sea time count for my next certificate of competency?

It depends on your administration. Some treat time on a unit stationary on location differently from time on a vessel making passages. Confirm with your issuing administration, such as DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines, before you rely on it.

Test yourself

How does a modern deepwater drillship normally keep station over the well?

Deepwater drillships rely on DP with redundant power and thrusters, because anchoring is impractical at those depths.

What is the main purpose of the moonpool on a drillship?

The moonpool is a vertical opening through the hull, normally near midships, allowing well equipment to pass to the seabed (drilling unit design practice; class rules for drilling units).

What does an emergency disconnect sequence (EDS) achieve?

The EDS shuts in the well at the BOP and disconnects the riser package so the unit can leave position safely. The unit's well-specific operating guidelines set when it must be initiated.

A control fault makes the thrusters push the drillship away from the well while power is still available. What is this called?

A drive-off is position loss caused by thrust acting in the wrong direction. A drift-off is loss of thrust, with the vessel moving under environmental forces (industry DP guidance, e.g. IMCA/MTS).

When drilling on DP, which status does a drillship normally show under COLREG?

A unit engaged in drilling cannot readily keep out of the way, so it is normally treated as restricted in her ability to manoeuvre. Coastal states may require additional signals (COLREG 1972 as amended, Rules 3(g) and 27(b)).
