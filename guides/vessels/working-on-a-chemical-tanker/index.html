Working on a Chemical Tanker: Trade, Hazards, Certificates and Your First Contract

Quick answer

Chemical tankers carry bulk liquid chemicals, vegetable oils and often clean petroleum products in many segregated tanks. Cargoes may be toxic, flammable, corrosive or reactive, so the work is technical and port-intensive. Everyone needs STCW Basic Safety Training. Anyone with cargo duties also needs Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations, and most operators expect it from all deck and engine crew. Senior officers and anyone directly responsible for cargo also need Advanced Training for Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations, which depends on chemical tanker sea time.

What a chemical tanker does and where it trades

A chemical tanker is built or adapted to carry the liquid products listed in Chapter 17 of the IBC Code. SOLAS Chapter VII makes that Code mandatory, and MARPOL Annex II controls the pollution side. Ships built before 1 July 1986 were covered by the older BCH Code, but very few remain in service.

Typical cargoes include:

  • Bulk chemicals: methanol, monoethylene glycol (MEG), caustic soda, sulphuric and phosphoric acid, styrene, benzene, xylene, acetone.
  • Vegetable oils and fats: palm oil, soybean oil and similar. Most are Category Y under MARPOL Annex II and need a chemical tanker of the appropriate ship type.
  • Clean petroleum products: many ships are dual-certified as chemical/product tankers and switch between trades.

The trade splits into three broad segments:

  • Deep-sea parcel tankers with dozens of tanks, carrying many grades for many shippers on one voyage.
  • Regional and coastal chemical tankers on short runs with frequent port calls.
  • Dedicated ships that carry one or a few cargoes, such as acid or molten sulphur carriers.

The main chemical hubs include the US Gulf, Northwest Europe (the ARA range), the Middle East Gulf, Singapore, China, Korea and India, with palm oil loading out of Malaysia and Indonesia.

Layout: ship types, tanks and pumps

The IBC Code sets out three ship types based on how hazardous the cargo is:

IBC ship type Intended for Protection
Type 1 Cargoes with the most severe environmental and safety hazards Maximum: cargo tanks furthest from the shell
Type 2 Cargoes with appreciably severe hazards Significant preventive measures
Type 3 Cargoes with sufficiently severe hazards Moderate containment

Most of the modern fleet is Type 2 or Type 3. Some ships have a few Type 1 tanks. The ship's Certificate of Fitness lists exactly which products may go in which tanks. Treat that list as law on board.

Features you will notice when you join:

  • Many small, segregated tanks. Each tank usually has its own line and its own submerged deepwell pump, often hydraulically driven, with a cofferdam purge arrangement. This keeps grades apart and allows efficient stripping.
  • Tank materials. Tanks are either stainless steel or coated (zinc silicate, epoxy, phenolic epoxy and others). The coating resistance list decides which cargo can go where. A wrong cargo can damage a coating permanently.
  • Heating coils or heat exchangers for cargoes such as vegetable oils and some chemicals.
  • Vapour control. This includes closed loading, vapour return lines, high-velocity P/V valves and closed gauging.
  • Nitrogen. Nitrogen from shore or an onboard generator is used for padding, purging and drying, either because the cargo requires it or because the shipper does.
  • Deck safety equipment. This includes decontamination showers, eyewash stations and, for many cargoes, emergency escape breathing and eye protection for everyone on board, as the IBC Code requires.

Cargo operations and tank cleaning

The chief officer plans and runs cargo work, supported by the duty officers and deck ratings. On ships that carry one, the pumpman also helps. The engine department runs the hydraulic power packs, heating, nitrogen plant and, on many ships, tank-cleaning heaters.

A typical cycle looks like this:

  1. Stowage planning. Check the Certificate of Fitness, coating resistance lists and compatibility. Many operators use the USCG compatibility chart for reactive groups. Apply segregation between incompatible grades, including adjacent tanks, lines and vent systems. Also check stability, stress and heating needs.
  2. Pre-loading. Hold the ship/shore safety checklist meeting and collect cargo data sheets. Confirm the inhibitor certificate for inhibited cargoes such as styrene, the pad or inerting requirements and the line-up checks.
  3. Loading and discharge. Use closed operations wherever possible, monitor rates and pressures, and keep a gas and vapour watch. Sampling and line clearing (blowing, pigging) follow terminal and charterer procedures.
  4. Tank cleaning. Methods include fixed or portable cleaning machines with hot or cold water, cleaning chemicals, steaming, ventilation and drying. The method depends on the last cargo and the next one, guided by the company's cleaning tables and the P&A Manual.
  5. Inspection. Sensitive cargoes such as methanol or MEG often need a wall-wash test by a surveyor. Typical checks are for chlorides, hydrocarbons and colour, depending on the specification. A failed wall wash means re-cleaning and delays.

Under MARPOL Annex II:

  • Products are classed as Category X, Y or Z (or Other Substances).
  • After unloading a Category X cargo, a prewash is mandatory before the ship leaves the port of unloading, with residues discharged to a reception facility. Some high-viscosity or solidifying Category Y substances also need a prewash.
  • Every operation, including loading, unloading, internal transfer, cleaning, ventilation and any permitted discharge, must be entered in the Cargo Record Book. Port State Control officers read it closely.
  • The Procedures and Arrangements (P&A) Manual is the approved document that governs how residues and tank washings are handled on that ship.

Key hazards and how they are controlled

Chemical tankers carry a wider spread of hazards than almost any other ship type:

  • Toxicity. Some cargoes, such as benzene, can harm you through inhalation or skin contact and are chronic health hazards. Exposure limits and required PPE come from the cargo data sheet.
  • Corrosivity. Acids and caustics cause severe burns. A splash during hose connection or sampling is a classic accident.
  • Flammability and static. Many cargoes are flammable. Bonding, controlled loading rates in the initial stage and correct sampling and gauging practice all matter.
  • Reactivity. Cargoes can react with each other, with water or with air. Some can polymerise if they lose inhibitor or are overheated. Segregation errors can be catastrophic.
  • Nitrogen and enclosed spaces. Nitrogen is odourless, and a nitrogen-rich atmosphere can cause collapse with almost no warning. Tanks, cofferdams, pump trunks and the spaces around nitrogen plant all need full enclosed-space entry procedures. SOLAS requires suitable portable atmosphere-testing instruments to be carried.
  • Pressurised lines. Lines and hoses that are not properly drained or depressurised can spray cargo when flanges are opened.

The controls are what you would expect, but they are applied with real discipline:

  • permit to work
  • gas testing
  • chemical-resistant PPE matched to the cargo
  • breathing apparatus where required
  • the Medical First Aid Guide (MFAG) for chemical exposure
  • regular cargo-specific drills

Industry vetting also keeps standards high. Charterers rely on CDI and OCIMF SIRE inspections, and poor inspection findings can lead charterers to reject the ship, costing it business.

Certificates and experience you need

The tanker certificates come from STCW Regulation V/1-1 and Section A-V/1-1 of the STCW Code. Course titles and the issuing process vary by administration, such as DG Shipping in India or MARINA in the Philippines.

Who What is required
Anyone with specific duties related to cargo or cargo equipment Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations (plus STCW basic safety training)
Master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer, and anyone with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit, handling of cargo, tank cleaning or other cargo operations Advanced Training for Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations

To qualify for the advanced certificate you need the basic certificate plus either:

  • at least three months of approved seagoing service on chemical tankers, or
  • at least one month of approved onboard training on chemical tankers as a supernumerary, including at least three loading and three unloading operations.

The oil and chemical advanced certificates are separate. Holding the oil endorsement does not make you a qualified chemical tanker senior officer.

You will also need:

  • your Certificate of Competency, or rating certificate, with the flag state endorsement
  • a valid medical fitness certificate
  • security training
  • any company-specific courses

Some companies also want an inert gas course, or a nitrogen generator or cargo-system course. These requirements differ between operators, so check the company's own list.

Crew and life on board

Crew numbers are set by the flag state's safe manning document and the company's own policy. A typical complement includes:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, second and third officers, and often deck cadets.
  • Engine: chief, second, third and fourth engineers, an ETO and often engine cadets.
  • Ratings: bosun, ABs, OS, fitter, oiler or motorman, cook and steward or messman.

Whether a pumpman is carried depends on the ship's design and the company. Ships with individual deepwell pumps often do without one. Some fleets carry a dedicated cargo engineer instead.

What to expect day to day:

  • A busy port schedule. You will have short sea passages, several berths in one port, berth shifts at night and cargo work around the clock.
  • Tank cleaning at sea. Cleaning between cargoes is heavy, wet and hot work, often under time pressure to be ready for the next load.
  • Paperwork and inspections. Expect cargo documents, Cargo Record Book entries, surveyor visits, vetting inspections and PSC.
  • Rest hours. STCW (for watchkeepers and those with safety duties) requires at least 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days. Most flag states apply the same minimum rest limits for all seafarers under MLC 2006. Record your hours honestly. Fatigue is a recognised risk in this trade, and accurate records protect you.
  • The upside. The learning curve is steep and well regarded. Officers with solid chemical tanker experience are generally sought after across the tanker sector.

Oral exam and interview questions to prepare

Examiners and company interviewers routinely ask questions like these:

  • Where would you find which cargoes this ship may carry, and in which tanks? The Certificate of Fitness, together with the cargo tank coating resistance list.
  • What are the IBC ship types and what distinguishes them? Types 1 to 3, based on how hazardous the cargo is and the degree of containment required.
  • What happens after discharging a Category X cargo? A mandatory prewash before leaving the port of unloading, with washings sent to a reception facility, recorded in the Cargo Record Book and endorsed as required.
  • Why is an inhibitor certificate needed? To confirm the inhibitor type, amount and effective life, so the cargo will not polymerise in transit.
  • What is the specific danger of nitrogen? It displaces oxygen without any warning signs. Spaces must be tested for oxygen and toxic or flammable gas before anyone enters.
  • What would you do if cargo splashed in someone's eyes? Flush immediately at the eyewash or shower and keep flushing. Raise the alarm, consult the cargo data sheet and MFAG, and seek radio medical advice.

Getting your first chemical tanker contract

  1. Get basic tanker training early. Without Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations, few tanker operators will consider you, even as a junior rating or cadet.
  2. Target companies with chemical fleets. Cadetships and junior officer posts with chemical tanker operators are the cleanest route. Product tanker experience transfers well, and dual-certified ships are a common bridge into chemicals.
  3. Join as a supernumerary or trainee if offered. One month of approved onboard training with three loads and three discharges can count toward the advanced certificate. Make sure it is properly documented.
  4. Keep sea time records clean. Collect discharge book entries, sea service testimonials and training record books. Your administration will scrutinise these when you apply for the advanced certificate.
  5. Prepare for a technical interview. Know the IBC ship types, the MARPOL Annex II categories, the P&A Manual, the Cargo Record Book, segregation, wall-wash basics and enclosed space entry.
  6. Use proper channels only. Go through licensed manning agencies and verify their licence with your national authority. Never pay for a certificate that skips the training or the sea time it claims to represent.
Key points
  • Chemical tankers carry bulk liquid chemicals, vegetable oils and often clean products under the IBC Code and MARPOL Annex II.
  • The Certificate of Fitness and coating resistance list decide which cargo may go in which tank.
  • Key hazards are toxicity, corrosivity, flammability, reactivity and nitrogen asphyxiation.
  • Everyone with cargo duties needs Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations.
  • Senior officers and those directly responsible for cargo need the separate chemical tanker advanced certificate.
  • Advanced training needs three months' chemical tanker sea time, or one month supernumerary with three loads and three discharges.
  • Expect frequent ports, heavy tank cleaning and strict vetting by CDI and SIRE inspectors.

Frequently asked questions

Can I work on a chemical tanker with only the oil tanker advanced certificate?

You can work there in a role that needs only basic tanker training. To be master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer or anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations, you need Advanced Training for Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations. The oil and chemical advanced certificates are separate under STCW Regulation V/1-1.

What is the difference between a chemical tanker and a product tanker?

A product tanker carries refined petroleum products under MARPOL Annex I. A chemical tanker is certified under the IBC Code to carry noxious liquid substances listed in its Certificate of Fitness. Many modern ships are dual-certified chemical/product tankers and can switch between the two trades.

How do I get sea time for the chemical tanker advanced course?

STCW accepts either three months of approved seagoing service on chemical tankers, or one month of approved onboard training as a supernumerary that includes at least three loading and three unloading operations. Your administration decides what counts as approved, so check its requirements and keep your documentation in order.

Do chemical tankers carry a pumpman?

It depends on the ship and the company. Ships with individual submerged deepwell pumps in each tank often have no pumpman, and cargo work is shared between deck officers, ratings and engineers. Some fleets carry a pumpman or a dedicated cargo engineer.

Is working on a chemical tanker more dangerous than other tankers?

The range of hazards is wider. Cargoes can be toxic, corrosive or reactive as well as flammable, and nitrogen is widely used. With proper segregation, PPE, permits and enclosed space procedures the risks are well controlled, but the trade does not forgive shortcuts.

Test yourself

Which IBC Code ship type is intended for cargoes with the most severe environmental and safety hazards?

Type 1 requires maximum preventive measures, Type 2 significant measures and Type 3 moderate containment. Reference: IBC Code, Chapter 2.

Besides holding the basic tanker certificate, which sea-time route qualifies a candidate for Advanced Training for Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations?

The sea time must be on chemical tankers, or an approved one-month supernumerary period with at least three loads and three discharges. Reference: STCW Regulation V/1-1.

After unloading a Category X noxious liquid substance, what does MARPOL Annex II require?

Category X cargoes require a mandatory prewash in the port of unloading, with residues to a reception facility, recorded in the Cargo Record Book. Reference: MARPOL Annex II, Regulation 13 and Appendix VI.

Who must hold Advanced Training for Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations?

STCW requires advanced training for these senior officers and any person with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit, cargo handling or tank cleaning. Reference: STCW Regulation V/1-1.

Why is nitrogen particularly dangerous around chemical tanker cargo spaces?

Nitrogen is inert and odourless, so an oxygen-deficient atmosphere gives no warning. Spaces must be tested before entry. Reference: SOLAS Regulation XI-1/7 and IMO enclosed space entry recommendations.
