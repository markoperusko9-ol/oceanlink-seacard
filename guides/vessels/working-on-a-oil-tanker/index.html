Working on an Oil Tanker

Quick answer

Oil tankers carry crude oil or refined products in bulk. They are among the most closely regulated and inspected ships afloat. To be assigned cargo duties you need STCW Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations, and senior officers and anyone in immediate charge of cargo also need Advanced Oil Tanker training backed by qualifying tanker service. Most people get their first tanker contract as a cadet or junior rank with a tanker operator, then build type-specific experience, which charterers and vetting inspectors check closely.

What an oil tanker does and where it trades

An oil tanker carries liquid petroleum cargo in bulk. There are two broad trades, and they feel quite different on board.

  • Crude carriers (VLCCs, Suezmaxes, Aframaxes) run long-haul voyages from producing regions to refineries. They load at terminals, often offshore at single point moorings, and may discharge by ship-to-ship (STS) transfer or lightering.
  • Product tankers carry refined cargoes such as gasoline, naphtha, jet fuel, gasoil and fuel oil. Voyages are shorter, port calls more frequent, and several grades may be carried at once with strict segregation.

Size categories are trade terms, not regulatory ones, and the dividing lines differ between sources. The regulatory distinctions that matter to you are set by MARPOL Annex I and SOLAS Chapter II-2. These include whether the ship is a crude or product carrier, its deadweight and its date of build, and they decide which equipment the ship must have.

Many tankers are on time charter to oil majors or traders. Many are also inspected under the OCIMF SIRE programme before a charterer will use them. That vetting culture shapes daily life more than almost anything else.

Layout: what is where on board

A typical modern oil tanker has:

  • Accommodation, bridge and engine room aft, clear of the cargo area.
  • Cargo tanks along most of the ship's length, usually arranged in pairs or sets across the beam, plus slop tanks aft for tank washings and oily residues.
  • Double hull protection, with segregated ballast tanks in the wing and double-bottom spaces. MARPOL Annex I requires this for oil tankers built to current standards, and single-hull oil tankers have been largely phased out under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Cargo pump room between the engine room and the cargo tanks on most crude carriers. These ships typically use large centrifugal pumps, often steam turbine driven. Many product tankers have no pump room and use submerged deepwell pumps, one per tank.
  • Cargo control room (CCR), where valves, pumps, tank gauging, the inert gas plant and alarms are monitored.
  • Manifolds at midships on both sides, where shore hoses or loading arms connect.
  • An inert gas system and, on crude carriers above the MARPOL threshold, a crude oil washing (COW) system.

On joining, walk the deck with the plans in hand. Find the manifolds, P/V valves, high-velocity vents, mast riser, deck seal or non-return arrangement, emergency stops and fire-fighting monitors. You will need all of them in an emergency.

Cargo operations in brief

The chief officer normally plans and runs cargo operations under the master's authority. Junior officers and ratings stand cargo watches.

Typical cycle:

  1. Pre-arrival: cargo and ballast plan, stress and stability checks, tank inerting and O2 checks, cargo equipment tests.
  2. Ship-shore safety: the ship/shore safety checklist is completed jointly with the terminal before operations and rechecked at agreed intervals.
  3. Loading: start slowly into the nominated tanks, check for leaks overside and at the manifold, then increase to the agreed rate. Deballast in step, then top off carefully with reduced rates.
  4. Passage: maintain positive inert gas pressure, monitor tank pressures and temperatures, and heat cargo if required.
  5. Discharge: inert gas is supplied to the tanks as cargo is pumped out. Crude carriers may crude oil wash during discharge. Tanks are stripped and the ship ballasts.
  6. Tank cleaning and gas-freeing: only when needed, for grade changes, inspection or repair, under the company procedure.

Oily ballast and slops are handled under MARPOL Annex I. Discharges at sea pass through the oil discharge monitoring and control system (ODME) and must meet the Annex I criteria. Outside special areas these include being more than 50 nautical miles from the nearest land, proceeding en route, an instantaneous rate of discharge not exceeding 30 litres per nautical mile, and a limit on the total quantity discharged. Cargo and ballast operations are recorded in the Oil Record Book Part II. PSC officers and vetting inspectors read that book closely.

Key hazards and how they are controlled

Main hazards:

  • Fire and explosion from hydrocarbon vapour. Inert gas keeps cargo tank oxygen low enough that the atmosphere cannot burn. The commonly applied limit is no more than 8% oxygen by volume in the tank.
  • Toxic gases: hydrogen sulphide (H2S) in sour crudes and some products, benzene in many cargoes, and hydrocarbon vapour itself. Personal H2S monitors and closed operations are standard where H2S is present.
  • Static electricity: restricted initial loading rates, bonding of equipment, and care with sampling and ullaging in non-inerted tanks.
  • Oxygen-deficient atmospheres in cargo tanks, ballast tanks, cofferdams and the pump room. Inerted tanks will kill without warning.
  • Pressure and vacuum damage to tanks if venting or inert gas supply is mismanaged.
  • Overflow and spills at topping off or through leaking valves.
  • Mooring line failure at terminals and during STS operations.

Controls come from SOLAS, MARPOL, the company SMS under the ISM Code, and industry guidance, chiefly ISGOTT.

Worked scenario: high O2 alarm on discharge

You are 3rd Officer on cargo watch on a crude carrier, discharging four centre tanks. The inert gas oxygen analyser alarms high, and the reading at the main line is rising past the set limit.

  1. Inform the chief officer immediately and follow the CCR instructions. Many plants automatically divert or vent inert gas that is off-spec.
  2. Do not let tanks be supplied with gas above the oxygen limit. If the plant cannot deliver inert gas of the required quality or quantity, cargo discharge must be stopped. Notify the terminal and stop pumps in a controlled way.
  3. Watch tank pressures closely. Continuing to pump without inert gas supply pulls the tanks towards vacuum.
  4. The engine room investigates the cause, for example boiler combustion or scrubber problems on a flue gas plant, or a generator fault.
  5. Resume only when quality is restored and the chief officer authorises it. Log the event.

Tank and pump room entry: entry follows the enclosed space entry procedure and permit. As a widely applied industry standard, the tank should be gas-free with 21% oxygen, hydrocarbon at or below 1% of the LFL, and toxic gases below 50% of their occupational exposure limits (OEL). Monitoring continues throughout, with rescue equipment ready. Company procedures may be stricter.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Beyond your normal CoC or rating certificate and STCW basic training, the core requirement is STCW Regulation V/1-1.

Training Who needs it
Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations Officers and ratings assigned specific duties and responsibilities related to cargo or cargo equipment
Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations Master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit, handling of cargo, tank cleaning or other cargo operations

For the advanced certificate, STCW requires qualifying experience on top of the course. You need either a set period of approved seagoing service on oil tankers, or a shorter supernumerary onboard training period that includes a minimum number of loading and discharging operations, documented in a training record book. Your administration's rules set out how this service must be documented. Check them before you plan your sea time.

Commonly expected by companies, varying by operator and charterer:

  • Ship security, medical and survival-craft certificates for your rank, as on any ship.
  • Company or manufacturer courses on inert gas, COW and cargo handling simulators.
  • Type-specific tanker experience for senior officers. Charterers apply officer experience matrices during vetting, so time in rank and time on tankers counts heavily for masters, chief officers, chief engineers and second engineers.

Chemical tanker and gas carrier endorsements are separate. An oil tanker endorsement alone does not qualify you for those ships.

Crew and life on board

Manning is set by the ship's minimum safe manning document, issued by the flag, plus whatever extra the company adds. A typical complement includes:

  • Deck: master, chief officer, 2nd and 3rd officers, often a junior officer or deck cadets, bosun, ABs and OSs.
  • Engine: chief engineer, 2nd, 3rd and 4th engineers, ETO on many ships, fitter, oilers or motormen, wiper, and engine cadets.
  • Cargo: a pumpman on many crude carriers with pump rooms. Product tankers with deepwell pumps often do not carry one.
  • Catering: cook and steward or messman.

What life is actually like:

  • Short port stays, often at remote terminals or SBMs with little or no shore leave. Long waits at anchorage are common.
  • Intense port periods. Cargo watches, frequent rounds, sampling, manifold watch and paperwork add up, and rest-hour compliance needs active management.
  • Strict rules: smoking only in designated places, no naked lights, and controls on mobile phones and non-intrinsically safe equipment in hazardous areas.
  • Constant inspection: SIRE vetting, terminal inspections, PSC, flag and company audits. Housekeeping, records and crew knowledge are all tested.
  • Strong safety culture and good training. Well-run tanker operators are often seen as excellent places to learn the profession properly.

Getting your first tanker contract

Cadets: the most direct route is a cadetship with a tanker-owning or tanker-managing company. Many operators recruit cadets in India and the Philippines through sponsorship programmes. Tanker sea time as a cadet also counts towards the experience you will later need.

Ratings: hold your watchkeeping certificate and get Basic Oil and Chemical Tanker training before applying. Tanker managers generally want it before joining if you will have cargo duties.

Officers moving from dry cargo:

  1. Complete the basic tanker course.
  2. Expect to join at a junior rank, or at your rank on a less demanding trade, because charterer matrices limit how many inexperienced officers a ship can carry.
  3. Plan your route to the advanced certificate. A supernumerary training trip is one option.
  4. Keep your sea service and training records clean and verifiable.

Avoid: agents who charge fees for placement. Under MLC 2006, seafarers should not be charged for recruitment or placement. Also be wary of offers of tanker endorsements without the required service.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

Expect questions like these at an officer CoC oral or a company interview:

  • Why do we inert cargo tanks, and what oxygen level do we keep? To keep the atmosphere non-flammable. Tank oxygen is kept at or below 8% by volume, with positive pressure maintained.
  • What do you do if the IG plant fails during discharge? Stop discharge, prevent vacuum, inform the terminal and follow the SMS contingency plan.
  • Explain crude oil washing. The cargo itself is used as the washing medium to dissolve and remove residues during discharge. It is done only with an inerted tank and under an approved COW operations manual.
  • What goes in the Oil Record Book Part II? Cargo and ballast operations, tank cleaning, slop handling and discharges through the ODME.
  • How do you prepare a cargo tank for entry? Isolation, gas-freeing, atmosphere testing, a permit, communication, a standby person and rescue arrangements.
  • Why restrict the initial loading rate? To limit static generation and splashing until the tank inlet is submerged.
Key points
  • Oil tankers split into crude carriers on long-haul trades and product tankers with frequent ports and multiple grades.
  • STCW Regulation V/1-1 requires basic tanker training for cargo duties and advanced training for senior officers and cargo-responsible staff.
  • Advanced oil tanker training needs qualifying tanker service or documented supernumerary training as well as the course.
  • Inert gas keeps tank oxygen at or below 8% by volume, and loss of the IG supply means stopping discharge.
  • H2S, benzene, static, oxygen deficiency and overflow are the hazards you manage every port call.
  • SIRE vetting and officer experience matrices strongly influence who gets hired at senior ranks.
  • The easiest entry is a cadetship or junior rank with a tanker operator, never through fee-charging agents.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join an oil tanker without a tanker certificate?

If you will have any duties related to cargo or cargo equipment, STCW requires Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations. In practice, most tanker companies want every crew member to hold it before joining. Check the specific requirement with your company and flag.

What is the difference between basic and advanced oil tanker training?

Basic training covers safe cargo operations, hazards and emergency response for anyone with cargo-related duties. Advanced training is for the master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone in immediate charge of cargo operations. It requires qualifying tanker sea service or documented onboard training as well as the course.

Is a crude tanker or a product tanker better for a first contract?

Both give valid oil tanker experience. Product tankers usually mean more port calls, grade changes and tank cleaning, which is busy but builds skills quickly. Crude carriers offer longer passages, COW operations and terminal or STS work. The quality of the operator matters more than the type.

Does oil tanker experience count for chemical tankers or gas carriers?

Not for the advanced endorsements. Chemical tanker and liquefied gas tanker advanced training are separate STCW endorsements, each with its own experience requirement on that ship type. The basic oil and chemical course does cover both oil and chemical tankers at basic level.

Why are tanker companies so strict about officer experience?

Charterers vet tankers before use, and inspectors check officer experience against matrices covering time in rank, time with the company and time on the tanker type. A ship that fails the matrix may not get chartered. Operators therefore manage crew combinations carefully, especially for senior ranks.

Test yourself

Under STCW, which group must hold Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations?

STCW Regulation V/1-1 lists the master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and others with immediate responsibility for cargo as needing advanced oil tanker training.

What is the commonly applied maximum oxygen content in an inerted cargo tank?

Tank atmospheres are kept at no more than 8% oxygen by volume under positive pressure. The requirements for inert gas systems are in SOLAS Chapter II-2 and the FSS Code, Chapter 15.

During discharge, the inert gas plant can no longer deliver gas of the required quality. What should happen?

If the IG supply fails, cargo discharge must be stopped so that air is not drawn into the tanks. This follows SOLAS Chapter II-2 and ISGOTT practice.

What is the washing medium in crude oil washing?

COW uses the crude oil cargo to dissolve and remove residues during discharge. It is required on crude carriers above the threshold in MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 33.

What is the main purpose of the oil discharge monitoring and control system (ODME)?

The ODME records and controls oily water discharges from the cargo area against the limits in MARPOL Annex I, Regulations 31 and 34.
