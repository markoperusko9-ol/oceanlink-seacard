Working on a Research Vessel

Quick answer

Research vessels carry scientists and their equipment to sea to collect samples and data from the water, seabed, atmosphere and marine life. Ship's crew handle the navigation, station-keeping, overside equipment, power and hotel services. Skilled winch and crane handling, dynamic positioning experience (where DP is fitted), small-boat competence and good teamwork with the science party count for more here than cargo experience.

What a research vessel actually does

A research vessel is a working platform for scientists. It does not carry cargo. There are several common types:

  • Oceanographic and multidisciplinary vessels take water samples, measure temperature and salinity profiles, core the seabed and deploy moorings, floats and gliders.
  • Fisheries research vessels run survey trawls and acoustic surveys to assess fish stocks.
  • Hydrographic and geophysical survey vessels map the seabed with multibeam echo sounders. Some also tow seismic equipment.
  • Polar research and supply vessels are often ice-strengthened or icebreaking. They support stations in the Arctic or Antarctic as well as doing science.

Most are owned by governments, national research institutes, universities or navies. They are run either by an in-house marine department or by a ship manager under contract. A smaller number are commercial survey ships working for the offshore and cable industries.

The ship's job is to put the science package in the right place at the right time and keep it safe. Then it brings the data and the people home.

Trade pattern, layout and equipment

Voyages are usually called cruises or legs. A leg can be a few days on a coastal survey or several weeks in the open ocean or polar regions. Port calls are mainly for science party crew changes, fuel and stores. They are not for cargo.

Typical layout features:

  • Large working deck aft and/or on the side, with tie-down points for containerised laboratories and portable equipment.
  • Overside handling gear: a stern A-frame, side gantries or cranes, and dedicated winches. These include CTD (conductivity-temperature-depth) wire winches, trawl winches and coring winches, often with tension and payout monitoring.
  • Laboratories: wet, dry and sometimes temperature-controlled labs, with their own clean water and seawater supplies.
  • Hull-mounted acoustics: multibeam and single-beam echo sounders, ADCPs and fisheries sonars, often in a drop keel or gondola.
  • Propulsion: very often diesel-electric, with azimuth thrusters or bow/stern tunnel thrusters. Many ships have DP so they can hold station over a sampling point.
  • Accommodation for a science party. This can be larger than the crew itself.

On many fisheries and acoustic ships, the design aims to keep underwater radiated noise low. This affects how machinery is run during surveys. Expect standing orders about which pumps or generators may be online while acoustic work is under way.

Operations and key hazards

Many of the most serious hazards on research ships are found on the working deck rather than in cargo spaces. The main hazards are:

  • Overside lifting and wire work. Heavy packages such as rosettes, corers, trawl doors and mooring anchors swing as the ship rolls. A parting wire under tension, or a package coming back aboard uncontrolled, can kill. Snap-back zones, clear communication between winch driver and deck, and a single person in charge of the evolution are essential.
  • Working near an open stern or side. Crew and scientists often work at the edge of an open transom or with rails removed. Lifejackets, fall-arrest equipment and permits apply under the company SMS.
  • Station-keeping and low-speed manoeuvring. The ship may be stopped or moving slowly for hours with gear in the water. Its ability to manoeuvre is genuinely restricted.
  • Small boat and rescue boat work for sampling, instrument recovery or transfers.
  • Laboratory hazards. These include preserving chemicals, compressed and cryogenic gases such as liquid nitrogen, and sometimes radioactive tracers. These are controlled under the vessel's SMS and the institute's own rules. The bridge and engine room must know what is stowed where.
  • Remote and polar areas, with ice, cold, long medical evacuation times and limited SAR cover.
  • Mixed experience on deck. Scientists may be highly qualified ashore but new to ships. Watch out for them.

COLREG point: a vessel engaged in surveying or underwater operations can be a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre under Rule 3(g). This only applies when the nature of the work actually restricts her. She then shows the Rule 27 lights or shapes. A ship that is just steaming between stations is an ordinary power-driven vessel. Showing RAM signals when you are not restricted is wrong and misleads other ships.

Regulatory point: many research ships are certified under the IMO Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships. Under this code, scientists are special personnel. They are neither passengers nor crew. How many special personnel are carried affects which safety standards apply. The training and familiarisation they need is set by the flag Administration and the SMS.

Certificates and experience usually expected

All the normal STCW certificates for your rank apply. These include your CoC, basic training, PSCRB, medical certificate and security training. The following are often requested on top. The exact list depends on the ship, the flag and the operator:

Requirement When it matters
DP certificate (industry scheme, not STCW) Ships fitted with DP, especially for deck officers
Proficiency in Fast Rescue Boats Ships that carry a fast rescue boat
Polar Code basic or advanced training Ships trading in polar waters
High Voltage training Engineers and ETOs on diesel-electric ships with HV systems
ECDIS type-specific Officers, for the make fitted
Crane, A-frame or winch familiarisation Bosun and ABs, often trained by the company

Experience that operators value:

  • Deck ratings and bosun: hands-on winch and crane driving, wire splicing and terminations, mooring and anchor work.
  • Deck officers: DP, offshore or survey experience, precise low-speed ship handling and small-boat operations.
  • Engineers and ETOs: diesel-electric propulsion, power management, thrusters and hydraulics. Fault-finding on mixed ship and scientific electrical systems is a real asset.

Government or institute operators may add national requirements. These can include citizenship, security clearance or their own medical standard. Check each vacancy rather than assuming.

Crew composition and the science party

The marine crew looks like a smaller version of any merchant ship's crew:

  • Deck: Master, Chief Officer, 2nd and 3rd Officers, Bosun, ABs and OS.
  • Engine: Chief Engineer, 2nd and 3rd Engineers, often an ETO, plus motormen or oilers and sometimes a fitter.
  • Catering: Cook, steward and messman, feeding a ship's company that can double when the scientists are aboard.

On top of the crew, larger operators often carry marine or technical support staff. These are technicians who look after the shared scientific equipment and act as the link between the crew and the scientists.

The science party is led by a Principal or Chief Scientist. The Chief Scientist decides what science is done. The Master decides whether and how it can be done safely. Good ships agree each day's programme at a daily science meeting. That clear split of responsibility is the heart of a well-run research ship.

Life on board

  • Round-the-clock work. Stations come up day and night. Deck ratings may be called for a deployment outside their normal watch. Rest-hour limits under MLC 2006 and STCW still apply, and the Master must plan manning to respect them.
  • Variety. No two legs are the same. You may be coring in the morning, deploying a glider at noon and recovering a mooring at midnight.
  • Mixed company. You will share the mess with students, professors and technicians from many countries. Most enjoy talking about their work, and many crew find this one of the best parts of the job.
  • Remote areas and weather. Science often goes where the weather is worst. Expect long periods away from land on some legs and port stays that are busy with crew changes.
  • Standards. Institute ships are often closely inspected by their owners. Expect strong emphasis on housekeeping, permits to work and drills.

Contract patterns vary widely. Some operators run fixed rotations, others work cruise by cruise. Ask before signing.

Oral-exam style questions to prepare

Examiners and interviewers for these ships often ask the following.

Q: You are holding station with a CTD on the wire in clear weather. What do you show? If the operation really restricts your ability to keep out of the way, show the Rule 27 RAM signals: red-white-red all-round lights, or ball-diamond-ball by day. Add the usual lights for making way if you are moving through the water. If the wire or gear forms an obstruction, also show the Rule 27(d) signals: two all-round red lights (by day two balls) in a vertical line on the side of the obstruction, and two all-round green lights (by day two diamonds) on the side on which another vessel may pass. These are shown in addition to the red-white-red lights or ball-diamond-ball.

Q: A man goes overboard while the trawl is down. What are your first actions? Raise the alarm, release the lifebuoy and keep a lookout on the casualty. Then follow the MOB procedure. You must weigh up whether to recover or slip gear, because a fouled propeller or thruster may make the situation worse.

Q: Who can stop a deployment? Anyone who sees danger should call a stop. The Master has overriding authority. The deck officer in charge of the evolution normally controls it on the day.

Q: What are special personnel? Under the Special Purpose Ships Code, they are persons carried for the ship's special purpose who are not crew or passengers. Scientists and technicians are the usual example.

Q: Why might the Chief Engineer refuse to start a second generator during a survey? One possible reason is acoustic noise limits. If so, the conflict between power needs and science needs must be resolved through the Master, never just ignored.

How to get your first contract

  1. Identify the operators. In your country, look for national research institutes, fisheries departments, hydrographic offices, polar programmes and the ship managers who run their ships. Abroad, many institute ships are crewed through manning agencies.
  2. Build relevant experience first. OSV, AHTS, cable, dredger or tug service gives you DP, winch, crane and deck-handling skills that transfer directly.
  3. Get the add-ons that fit the ship. DP for officers, fast rescue boat proficiency, and HV for engineers and ETOs. Add Polar Code training if you are targeting polar ships.
  4. Write a CV that shows what you did, not just ranks. List the types of winches and cranes you have driven, the DP class and hours, and the small-boat work you have done.
  5. Watch government recruitment notices. State-owned operators often recruit through formal advertisements with fixed eligibility rules and selection procedures.
  6. Show the right attitude in interview. Operators want people who stay patient with non-mariners, communicate clearly, and stop a job when it becomes unsafe.
Key points
  • Research vessels carry scientists and equipment, not cargo, so deck skills centre on winches, A-frames and cranes.
  • Most serious hazards involve overside lifting, wires under tension and work near open sterns.
  • Show Rule 27 RAM signals only when the work genuinely restricts manoeuvrability.
  • Scientists are usually 'special personnel' under the Special Purpose Ships Code, not passengers or crew.
  • DP, fast rescue boat, HV and Polar Code training are common add-ons, depending on the ship.
  • The Chief Scientist sets the programme; the Master decides whether it is safe.
  • OSV, offshore and tug experience transfers well and is a common route into the sector.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a research vessel?

There is no single 'research vessel certificate'. You need the normal STCW certificates for your rank. Operators often add requirements suited to the ship, such as DP, fast rescue boat proficiency, high voltage or Polar Code training. Check each vacancy and the flag's rules.

Are scientists on board treated as passengers?

On ships certified under the Special Purpose Ships Code, scientists and technicians are 'special personnel'. That is a separate category from passengers and crew. They still need safety familiarisation and must take part in musters and drills as the flag Administration and company SMS require.

Is DP experience necessary for deck officers?

Only if the ship is fitted with DP and the operator uses it for station-keeping. Many research ships have DP, so it is a strong advantage, but some smaller or older ships hold position manually. Read the job description.

Is work on a research vessel harder than on a cargo ship?

It is different rather than simply harder. There is no cargo watch, but stations run day and night and overside work is physically demanding. Rest hours are still limited under MLC 2006 and STCW, and the Master must plan manning to comply.

Can a cadet or fresher join a research vessel?

Some government and institute operators take cadets or trainees, especially on national programmes, but many prefer experienced crew. It depends on the operator and whether the ship has approved training berths. Offshore or general cargo service first is a common path.

Test yourself

A research vessel is steaming at full speed between two sampling stations with no gear deployed. Which lights should she show?

She is not restricted in her ability to manoeuvre while transiting. She shows normal power-driven vessel lights (COLREG Rules 3(g), 23 and 27).

Under the Special Purpose Ships Code, how are scientists carried for the ship's purpose classified?

The SPS Code creates a separate category, special personnel, who are neither passengers nor crew (Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships, 2008).

Who has overriding authority to stop an overside deployment for safety reasons?

The Master keeps overriding authority for safety. The Chief Scientist sets the scientific programme (ISM Code, Section 5).

Which training is most relevant for an ETO joining a diesel-electric research ship with high-voltage switchboards?

Diesel-electric ships with HV systems call for HV competence. Operation and maintenance of HV installations is part of the ETO competence table (STCW Code, Table A-III/6).

Which hazard is most characteristic of the working deck on a research vessel?

Overside lifting with A-frames, cranes and winches is the dominant deck hazard. It is controlled through risk assessment and permits under the SMS (ISM Code, Section 7).
