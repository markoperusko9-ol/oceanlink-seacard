Working on a Dredger

Quick answer

Dredgers deepen channels and ports, reclaim land and move seabed material. Work runs around the clock in short, repetitive cycles close to shore and traffic. Expect hands-on deck and engine work, heavy wear-part maintenance, and COLREG restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre status while actually dredging. Your normal STCW certificates are the base, and mechanical, welding, hydraulic and close-quarters shiphandling skills are what get you hired.

What a dredger does and where it works

A dredger removes material from the seabed or riverbed and moves it somewhere else. The work falls into a few broad types:

  • Maintenance dredging keeps existing channels, berths and port approaches at their charted depth as silt builds up.
  • Capital dredging creates new channels, basins and harbours, or deepens existing ones for larger ships.
  • Land reclamation pumps sand ashore to build new land for ports, airports, industrial zones and islands.
  • Coastal and offshore work covers beach nourishment, trenching for pipelines and cables, and rock placement over subsea structures.

The largest international fleets belong to a small number of mainly Belgian and Dutch contractors, with large Chinese state-owned fleets as well. In India, the Dredging Corporation of India operates its own fleet. Many smaller national and port-owned dredgers also work locally.

The work is project-based. A vessel may spend weeks or many months on one contract and then mobilise across an ocean to the next. That means long ocean passages followed by long periods working in one small area.

Main dredger types and layout

Dredger types differ more than most ship types do, so find out exactly what you are joining.

Type How it works Self-propelled?
Trailing suction hopper dredger (TSHD) Trails one or two suction pipes with dragheads along the bottom while under way. It loads the mixture into its own hopper, then sails to discharge. Yes
Cutter suction dredger (CSD) Stays fixed on spuds. A rotating cutter head on a ladder loosens material, which is pumped away through a pipeline. Often not; some are
Backhoe / grab dredger A large excavator or grab digs material and loads it into barges. Usually a spud pontoon; grab hopper dredgers are self-propelled
Split hopper barge Carries dredged material and dumps it by opening its hull along the centreline. Varies
Fall-pipe / rock placement vessel Places rock precisely on the seabed through a fall pipe. Often uses DP. Yes

Typical TSHD layout:

  • Accommodation and bridge, usually forward.
  • A large open hopper amidships, with bottom doors or valves and an adjustable overflow.
  • Suction pipes on gantries along the side, with swell compensators.
  • Dredge pumps in a pump room low in the hull.
  • A bow coupling for pumping ashore and a bow nozzle for "rainbowing".

Typical CSD layout:

  • A ladder and cutter at the bow.
  • A spud carriage and spuds at the stern.
  • Swing winches and anchors at either side.
  • A heavy dredge pump installation.

Both types carry a well-equipped workshop, because wear parts are changed constantly.

How the work actually runs: two worked cycles

Scenario 1: TSHD on maintenance dredging in a port approach

  1. The vessel arrives on the dredging area, slows to trailing speed and lowers the suction pipes.
  2. The dredge pumps start and the dragheads are set on the bottom. The swell compensator keeps contact pressure steady.
  3. The mixture fills the hopper. Excess water leaves through the overflow, within whatever limits the project's environmental permit sets.
  4. When the vessel reaches its loaded draught or target load, the pipes are raised and stowed.
  5. The vessel sails loaded to the disposal site, a rehandling pit, or a shore connection.
  6. It discharges the load by one of three methods:
    • opening the bottom doors;
    • pumping ashore through a floating pipeline connected at the bow coupling;
    • rainbowing the mixture through the bow nozzle.
  7. The vessel returns empty and repeats the cycle.

This cycle can run many times a day, every day. The bridge team handles traffic, VTS calls and the loading process together.

Scenario 2: CSD on land reclamation

  1. The dredger is positioned with its working spud down. The swing anchors are laid out by a support vessel.
  2. The cutter ladder is lowered and the dredger swings from side to side on its swing wires, pivoting on the spud. The cutter cuts in arcs.
  3. After each set of swings, the dredger "steps" forward on the spud carriage. It resets the spud when the carriage reaches its end.
  4. The mixture is pumped through the pipeline to shore, sometimes via booster stations.
  5. Downtime comes from several sources:
    • pipeline extensions;
    • changing cutter teeth;
    • moving anchors;
    • blockages.

Much of the crew's effort goes into these tasks.

COLREG status. Rule 3(g) lists a vessel engaged in dredging as an example of a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre (RAM), but only when the work actually restricts her. A TSHD on passage with pipes stowed is an ordinary power-driven vessel and shows normal lights.

Key hazards on dredgers

  • Stability. A hopper full of a liquid sand-water mixture has a large free surface and a shifting load.
    • Loading and discharging change stability quickly.
    • Some hopper dredgers are assigned a special dredging freeboard under IMO dredging-freeboard guidelines applied by their administration, with conditions on the area and weather. Know what your vessel's load line certificate and stability booklet actually permit.
  • Wires and winches under tension. Gantry wires, swing wires, anchor wires and spud hoists all carry heavy loads. Keep out of the snap-back zone, as you would in mooring operations.
  • Pipelines and pumps. Dredge pipelines carry abrasive mixture under pressure.
    • Coupling failures and blockages can release it suddenly.
    • Clearing a blocked pump or pipe needs proper isolation (lock-out tag-out) before anyone opens it.
  • Wear-part maintenance. Dragheads, cutter teeth, pump casings and impellers wear fast. That means frequent hot work, heavy lifts and confined working spaces such as the pump room and inside pipework, under the permit to work system.
  • Close-quarters navigation.
    • You work in busy channels at low speed with limited manoeuvrability and shallow water.
    • Squat and bank effects matter.
    • Floating pipelines and anchor wires are themselves hazards to other traffic.
  • Dredged material.
    • Material from industrial or harbour areas can be contaminated, or release gas from organic matter.
    • In some areas, unexploded ordnance turns up in the dredged material or on the seabed. Follow the project's specific procedures.
  • Small-boat transfers. Crew changes and pipeline work often involve workboats, multicats and boat transfers in exposed conditions.
  • Noise, vibration and fatigue. Continuous 24-hour operation and repetitive cycles make fatigue management and proper rest-hour records essential.

Certificates and experience usually expected

There is no single international "dredger endorsement". What you need depends on the flag, the vessel and the project.

  • Base STCW certificates. On a sea-going, self-propelled dredger, you need the same certificates as any ship of that size, power and trading area:
    • your Certificate of Competency;
    • basic safety training;
    • PSCRB;
    • advanced fire fighting and medical training at officer level;
    • security training;
    • a valid medical certificate.
  • Non-self-propelled and inland craft. Manning and certification for these are often set nationally and can differ a lot. Check with the flag administration or the company rather than assuming STCW applies one way or the other.
  • Flag state endorsement. Officers serving under a flag other than the one that issued their CoC need the usual flag endorsement.
  • Dynamic Positioning. DP training and certification is commonly expected for fall-pipe, trenching and some offshore project vessels. It is not standard for most hopper or cutter dredgers.
  • Offshore survival. BOSIET/HUET or similar may be demanded on projects in offshore oil and gas or wind areas, depending on the client and the location.
  • Company training. Dredging operation, such as running the dredge console or a cutter, is usually taught in-house and on the job. The large contractors run their own programmes.

The experience that counts most:

  • Close-quarters shiphandling for deck officers.
  • Hydraulics, diesel-electric or heavy diesel plant, and pump maintenance for engineers.
  • Coded or practical welding and fitting skills for ratings.

Crew composition and life on board

Crews are usually compact, practical and heavily mechanical. A typical sea-going dredger carries:

  • Master. On some hopper dredgers the master also takes an active role in dredging operations.
  • Deck officers. They keep navigation watches and also run the dredging console during loading.
  • Chief engineer and engineers. They look after propulsion, generators, high-power dredge pump drives and a lot of hydraulics.
  • Electrician or ETO. Large diesel-electric plant and pump drives make this role important.
  • Bosun, ABs and deck ratings. They handle pipes, wires, anchors, couplings and boat work.
  • Fitters and welders. On dredgers these are core crew, not an extra. Wear parts keep them busy.
  • Cook and steward.
  • Project staff. Superintendents, surveyors or hydrographers may join for the project.

Cutter dredgers often run with a larger workforce on shift. Some project crew may live ashore or on an accommodation vessel nearby.

What daily life is like:

  • Shifts, not traditional watches. Many dredgers work two 12-hour shifts. Your rest-hour records must still comply with the flag's rules under MLC 2006 and STCW.
  • Repetition. The same loading, sailing and discharging cycle all day, in the same area, for weeks.
  • Close to land, but not always ashore. Phone signal is often good, but shore leave depends on the port, the project and the work schedule.
  • Frequent crew changes. Crew changes and stores runs tend to come more often than on deep-sea tonnage, and are often done by launch.
  • Rotation. Lengths and patterns vary widely by company and nationality of crew. Some European contractors are known for regular, equal-time style rotations, but confirm this in your own contract.

Many seafarers like dredging for the hands-on work and the stable project crews. Others find the noise, dirt and repetition hard after deep-sea trades.

How to get your first dredging contract

  1. Target the right employers.
    • The large international contractors recruit through their own career portals and, for Asian crew, through their appointed manning agents.
    • Indian seafarers can also watch for Dredging Corporation of India openings.
    • National port authorities sometimes run their own dredgers.
  2. Lead with transferable experience. Experience from these sectors reads well to dredging recruiters:
    • tugs;
    • offshore support vessels and anchor handlers;
    • heavy-lift ships;
    • coastal trades with frequent manoeuvring.
  3. Show the practical skills. Mention these specifically in your CV if you have them:
    • welding certificates;
    • hydraulic systems experience;
    • pump overhauls;
    • diesel-electric plant.
  4. Keep documents complete and valid. Projects mobilise at short notice. A valid passport, seaman's book, medical, STCW certificates and any visas the project country needs give you an edge.
  5. Be flexible on entry rank. Joining one rank lower to learn the dredging side is not unusual.
  6. Learn the COLREG lights, shapes and sound signals for dredging vessels before the interview.

Oral exam style questions on dredgers

Q: What does a dredger show when engaged in dredging and restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, with an obstruction on one side?

She shows:

  • the RAM lights of Rule 27(b): red-white-red all-round in a vertical line, plus masthead lights, sidelights and sternlight when making way;
  • when an obstruction exists, two all-round red lights in a vertical line on the side of the obstruction;
  • two all-round green lights in a vertical line on the side on which another vessel may pass.

By day, she shows ball-diamond-ball, with two balls on the obstructed side and two diamonds on the passable side.

Q: And if she is at anchor while dredging?

She shows the Rule 27(d) lights and shapes instead of the anchor lights and shape.

Q: What is her fog signal while dredging?

One prolonged blast followed by two short blasts, at intervals of not more than two minutes, under Rule 35(c). Rule 35(d) says she gives the same signal, instead of the anchor signal, when carrying out her work at anchor.

Q: Why does a partly loaded hopper concern a stability examiner?

The fluid mixture creates a large free surface. This reduces effective GM, and the load can shift as she rolls. The condition must stay within the stability booklet and the assigned freeboard.

Key points
  • The main types are the trailing suction hopper dredger (sails while loading) and the cutter suction dredger (stationary on spuds, pumps through a pipeline).
  • A dredger counts as RAM under COLREG only while her work actually restricts her ability to manoeuvre.
  • When dredging as RAM, she shows two reds or two balls on the obstructed side and two greens or two diamonds on the passable side.
  • Hopper stability, wires under tension, pressurised pipelines and constant hot work are the hazards you will meet most often.
  • There is no universal dredger endorsement: normal STCW certificates apply to sea-going ships, and non-propelled craft rules vary by flag.
  • Welding, hydraulics, pump work and close-quarters shiphandling are the skills recruiters value most.
  • Expect 24-hour shift work in repetitive cycles close to shore, with project-based moves between countries.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a dredger?

There is no single international dredger certificate. On a sea-going self-propelled dredger you need the normal STCW certificates for your rank, plus a flag endorsement where required. Dredging operation is usually taught in-house. DP or offshore survival training may be required for specific projects.

Is a dredger a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre?

Only while she is engaged in dredging and that work restricts her ability to manoeuvre. Rule 3(g) lists dredging as an example. On passage with dredging gear stowed, she is an ordinary power-driven vessel and shows the normal lights.

What is the difference between a hopper dredger and a cutter dredger?

A trailing suction hopper dredger loads material into its own hopper while under way, then sails to discharge. A cutter suction dredger stays fixed on spuds, cuts the seabed with a rotating cutter head and pumps the material away through a pipeline. Many cutter dredgers have no propulsion of their own.

Is dredging good experience for a deck or engine officer?

It gives strong close-quarters shiphandling for deck officers. Engineers get heavy plant, hydraulics and pump experience. Watch your sea-time records: time on non-propelled craft, or on vessels below certain size or power, may not count fully towards your next CoC. Check with your administration.

Do dredger crews get shore leave?

It depends on the project, the port and the work schedule. Dredgers often work close to land with good communications and frequent crew changes. Continuous 24-hour operations, and working away from berths, can still mean limited time ashore.

Test yourself

A dredger engaged in dredging, RAM, has an obstruction on her port side. What does she show on that side at night?

Two all-round reds mark the side where the obstruction exists. Two all-round greens mark the side where another vessel may pass (COLREG Rule 27(d)(i)).

In restricted visibility, what sound signal does a vessel engaged in dredging and RAM give?

RAM vessels sound one prolonged followed by two short blasts at intervals of not more than two minutes (COLREG Rule 35(c)).

A dredger is carrying out dredging work while at anchor and is RAM. What does she exhibit?

When at anchor while dredging, she shows the Rule 27(d) lights and shapes instead of the anchor lights or shape (COLREG Rule 27(d)(iii)).

What is the main stability concern with a partly loaded hopper of sand-water mixture?

A large free surface in the hopper reduces effective GM, and the load can shift as the vessel rolls. Conditions must stay within the approved stability booklet.

By day, which shapes does a dredging vessel show on the side where another vessel may pass?

Two diamonds mark the passable side and two balls mark the obstructed side, in addition to ball-diamond-ball (COLREG Rule 27(d)(ii)).
