Working on a Diving Support Vessel (DSV)

Quick answer

A diving support vessel is a dynamically positioned offshore ship that keeps commercial divers, often saturation divers, working safely on the seabed. The marine crew's main job is to hold position and protect the divers. That means DP experience, offshore safety training and strict discipline matter more here than on almost any other ship. Most officers and ratings get there through PSVs, AHTS or other DP tonnage, not straight from deep-sea trades.

What a DSV does and where it works

A DSV is a platform for subsea construction, inspection, maintenance and repair (IMR) work done by divers. Typical jobs include:

  • tying in pipelines and risers
  • installing and changing out subsea valves and spool pieces
  • inspection and repair of structures and pipelines
  • decommissioning
  • support to salvage or emergency repair work

There are two broad types of diving spread:

  • Saturation (sat) diving. Divers live under pressure in a chamber complex on board for an extended period. They are transferred to the worksite in a closed diving bell. Decompression happens only once, at the end of the period, and takes several days.
  • Surface-supplied air or mixed-gas diving. Divers are deployed from the surface in a basket or wet bell using a launch and recovery system (LARS). This is for shallower work.

Many DSVs also carry work-class ROVs. Where cranes are big enough, they also take on light construction. Main areas include the North Sea, the Middle East and India's western offshore, West Africa, the Gulf of Mexico, Brazil and South-East Asia. The work is project-based, so the ship moves between fields and sometimes between regions.

Layout and key equipment

Compared with a PSV, a DSV looks crowded. The diving spread dominates the ship.

Area What you'll find
Midships Moon pool for the diving bell, sometimes a second moon pool for an ROV
Inside the hull or deckhouse Saturation system: living chambers, transfer-under-pressure trunking, bell handling system
Dive control and life support control The dive supervisor and life support teams, with direct comms to the DP desk
Deck Gas storage (quads and bundles of helium, heliox and oxygen), gas reclaim plant, air-diving LARS, offshore crane(s)
Ship's side Hyperbaric rescue unit or self-propelled hyperbaric lifeboat (SPHL) for evacuating divers still under pressure
Engine room Usually diesel-electric with several generator sets, redundant switchboards and multiple thrusters

DP is central. Industry guidance generally expects diving to be carried out from vessels of DP equipment class 2 or 3. The class is set out in the ship's DP documentation. In practice, a single failure must not cause loss of position.

Many DSVs carry far more than 12 people who are not crew, such as divers, project staff and client reps. The SPS Code covers ships of 500 gross tonnage and above carrying more than 12 special personnel, so DSVs are often certified under the Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships. Check the ship's Safety Certificate and the SPS certificate to see exactly how it is certified.

Key hazards on a DSV

The divers are the most vulnerable people on board. Every system on the ship is arranged around protecting them.

  • Loss of position (drift-off or drive-off). A DP failure can drag a bell or diver's umbilical, or put a diver near a thruster. Umbilical lengths are deliberately limited so divers cannot physically reach thrusters or other hazards.
  • Thrusters and intakes. Thrusters may be deselected or restricted depending on where the bell or basket is. The bridge must know which ones are safe to use.
  • Hyperbaric emergencies. If the ship must be abandoned with divers in saturation, they cannot simply be brought to surface pressure. The SPHL or hyperbaric rescue unit takes them, still under pressure, to a reception facility. Marine crew often have defined roles in launching it.
  • Gas hazards. Large volumes of helium and nitrogen can vent into enclosed spaces and drop oxygen levels without any warning sign. Oxygen-enriched atmospheres around gas storage and chambers greatly increase fire risk. Treat gas rooms and the area around the sat system with the same caution as an enclosed space.
  • High-pressure gas. Damaged quads, hoses or valves can fail violently.
  • Lifting operations. Crane lifts over the side and over the moon pool often happen while divers are in the water. This needs tight control of what goes over which area.
  • Simultaneous operations (SIMOPS). These are common near platforms, other vessels and live pipelines.
  • Weather limits. Bell and LARS launch and recovery have sea-state limits set by the diving contractor's procedures.

Crew composition: marine crew and dive team

A DSV usually carries three groups. The split of responsibility is set out in the company's procedures and the project's bridging documents.

Marine crew (ship owner or operator)

  • Master, chief officer, DP officers (DPOs) and junior officers
  • Chief engineer and engineers, an ETO (often more than one on large diesel-electric ships)
  • Bosun, ABs, often dedicated crane operators, motormen and catering staff

Dive team (diving contractor)

  • Diving superintendent and dive supervisors
  • Life support supervisors and life support technicians, who run the chamber atmosphere around the clock
  • Saturation and air divers, dive technicians

Project and client

  • Offshore construction manager, client representatives, engineers, ROV team, surveyors

The master keeps overriding authority for the safety of the vessel. The dive supervisor controls the dive. Either can stop the job. Clear, closed-loop communication between the DP desk and dive control is non-negotiable. Expect a dedicated DP-to-dive control line and strict reporting of any change in DP status.

Worked scenario: DP alert with divers in the water

Your ship is on DP at a manifold with two sat divers out of the bell. A generator trips and the DP system loses some redundancy. Position is still held, but the vessel's activity-specific operating guidelines (ASOG) now call for an advisory or yellow condition.

What the DPO does depends on the vessel's own ASOG and the dive procedures. The usual pattern is:

  1. Inform dive control immediately on the dedicated line, using the agreed status (for example, yellow). State the cause in plain words.
  2. The dive supervisor recalls the divers to the bell and prepares to recover it. The DPO does not wait to see whether the problem gets worse.
  3. Call the master and the engineer on watch. The ECR starts working on restoring the generator or switchboard configuration.
  4. Keep station and avoid any unnecessary moves or thruster changes while the bell is still deployed.
  5. Agree next steps. Bell recovery goes ahead as the dive supervisor directs, without waiting for the fault to be cleared. Diving only resumes once redundancy is confirmed restored and the ASOG conditions are green again.
  6. Log everything. Record the times, the status changes and the communications.

A red condition means position may not be kept. The divers return to the bell and it is secured, or the emergency response in the dive procedures is followed. The DPO's job is to give the dive team as much time as possible. Never move the ship without dive control knowing.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your STCW certificate of competency, endorsements and basic safety training remain the foundation. On top of that, operators commonly look for the following. Exact requirements depend on the company, the client, the flag and the offshore region.

  • DP certificate. Essential for deck officers in DP roles. The Nautical Institute scheme is the most widely recognised. Many DSV operators want an unlimited certificate plus logged DP time, ideally on DP2/DP3 tonnage.
  • Offshore survival and helicopter training (BOSIET/HUET or equivalent). Needed where crew change by helicopter or work in sectors that require it, such as the North Sea. Refresher training applies.
  • Offshore-specific medical. Some sectors and clients require one in addition to your flag-state medical.
  • High voltage training. Often expected for engineers and ETOs on diesel-electric DSVs.
  • Proficiency in Fast Rescue Boats. Asked for on vessels carrying an FRC.
  • Crane operator and rigging/banksman qualifications. For deck ratings handling offshore cranes. Requirements vary by company and region.
  • Company and client inductions. These cover permit to work, SIMOPS and project-specific training.

On many DSVs, engineers and ETOs are also expected to understand power management, blackout prevention and the DP FMEA (failure modes and effects analysis) of their ship.

Life on board

  • Rotation. Offshore DSVs commonly run equal-time rotations, often with crew changes by helicopter or crew boat. Patterns vary by operator and region, so check your contract.
  • Watches. These are usually 12-hour shifts, because diving runs 24 hours.
  • Pace. Expect long periods on DP at one site, with frequent toolbox talks, permits and audits by the client.
  • Accommodation. Ships are generally modern with good food and internet. They are also busy, with many more people than a cargo ship, so cabins are often shared.
  • Culture. It is safety-heavy and procedure-driven, with a stop-work culture taken seriously. You will work alongside several contractors and nationalities.
  • Downtime. There is little port time. When the ship is waiting on weather, maintenance and drills fill the day.

Getting your first DSV contract and oral-check questions

Typical routes in

  • Deck officers. Build DP time on PSVs, AHTS or construction vessels, complete your DP certificate, then apply to DSV operators or their manning agents. Junior officers sometimes join as trainee DPOs.
  • Engineers and ETOs. Diesel-electric experience, HV training and thruster or PMS familiarity are what get you noticed.
  • Ratings. OSV experience, crane tickets and clean offshore safety training are your strongest assets.
  • Keep your documents, DP logbook and offshore training in date. Gaps are a common reason for rejection.
  • Only use licensed manning agents and verify the company. Never pay for a "guaranteed" offshore job.

Questions an interviewer or master may ask a new DPO

  • What is the ASOG, and what triggers a yellow or red condition on this ship?
  • Why are diver umbilicals limited in length?
  • Which thrusters may be restricted when the bell is deployed, and why?
  • What is your first action on a DP alarm with divers in the water? (Inform dive control.)
  • How are saturation divers evacuated if the ship must be abandoned?
  • What is the critical activity mode of operation (CAMO), and why does it matter for redundancy?
Key points
  • A DSV exists to support divers, and keeping position on DP is the marine crew's core job.
  • Diving is generally done from DP class 2 or 3 vessels so that no single failure causes loss of position.
  • Saturation divers cannot be quickly decompressed, so evacuation is by hyperbaric lifeboat or rescue unit.
  • Helium and nitrogen can create oxygen-deficient atmospheres, and oxygen-enriched areas raise fire risk.
  • Any DP status change must be reported to dive control immediately.
  • Deck officers usually need a DP certificate, engineers and ETOs benefit from diesel-electric/HV experience, and offshore survival training is commonly required.
  • Most people reach DSVs via PSVs, AHTS or other DP tonnage, not directly from deep-sea trades.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a DP certificate to work on a diving support vessel?

Deck officers standing DP watches need a DP certificate, and the Nautical Institute scheme is the most widely recognised. Engineers and ratings do not normally need one. DP familiarity, HV training and offshore experience still help them get selected.

Can a deep-sea officer move directly to a DSV?

It happens but is uncommon, because DSV operators want logged DP time and offshore experience. The usual path is a PSV, AHTS or other DP vessel first. You then move to a DSV once your DP certificate and offshore record are established.

Are marine crew involved in the diving itself?

No. Diving is run by the diving contractor's supervisors and life support team. Marine crew support it by holding position, running the power plant, handling cranes and deck work, and taking part in emergency response such as launching the hyperbaric lifeboat.

Is BOSIET/HUET mandatory for DSV crew?

It depends on the region and the client. It is commonly required where crew change by helicopter or work in sectors such as the North Sea. Some other regions accept equivalent offshore survival courses, so check the operator's requirements before joining.

What is the biggest safety difference between a DSV and a PSV?

On a DSV, people are in the water or under pressure beneath the ship, so loss of position or a thruster mistake can be fatal. There are also large quantities of breathing gases on board. Procedures, communications and redundancy are therefore much stricter.

Test yourself

During a saturation dive, the DP system gives an advisory due to a generator trip. What should the DPO do first?

Dive control must be told of any change in DP status at once so divers can be recalled to the bell. This is set out in the vessel's ASOG and the diving contractor's procedures.

Why are diver umbilical lengths deliberately limited on a DSV?

Umbilical management keeps divers clear of thrusters, intakes and other hazards. This is a basic principle of diving from DP vessels in industry guidance and the dive procedures.

The ship must be abandoned with divers in saturation. How are they evacuated?

Saturation divers cannot be quickly decompressed, so they are evacuated under pressure to a reception facility. Hyperbaric evacuation arrangements are addressed in the IMO Code of Safety for Diving Systems (current edition).

What is the main atmospheric hazard from helium leaks into enclosed spaces on a DSV?

Helium is inert and non-flammable, but it displaces oxygen and gives no warning. Treat gas-affected spaces under enclosed space entry precautions and the company SMS.

Why are many DSVs certified under the Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships?

The SPS Code covers ships of 500 GT and above carrying more than 12 special personnel, which DSVs typically do. How a particular ship is certified depends on its flag and survey regime.
