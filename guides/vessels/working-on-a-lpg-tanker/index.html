Working on an LPG Tanker

Quick answer

An LPG tanker carries liquefied petroleum gases, and often ammonia and petrochemical gases, kept liquid by pressure, refrigeration or both, under the IGC Code. Anyone given specific cargo duties needs STCW Basic Training for Liquefied Gas Tanker Cargo Operations. Senior officers and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo also need the Advanced certificate, which requires gas tanker sea service. Most newcomers get in as cadets with gas-fleet companies or by moving across from oil and chemical tankers.

What an LPG tanker does and where it trades

LPG carriers move propane, butane and mixtures of the two. Many are also certified for ammonia and petrochemical gases such as propylene, butadiene and vinyl chloride monomer (VCM). What the ship may carry is set by its IGC Certificate of Fitness. Check that certificate, not the ship's nickname.

The industry groups LPG ships by how they keep the cargo liquid:

Type How cargo is kept liquid Typical use
Fully pressurised Pressure only, at ambient temperature Smaller ships, coastal and regional trades, many short voyages
Semi-refrigerated / semi-pressurised Some pressure plus refrigeration Flexible petrochemical and LPG trades; ethylene carriers are a specialised branch
Fully refrigerated Refrigeration at near-atmospheric pressure Large ships including VLGCs on long-haul routes

Typical trades are shaped by where gas is produced and where it is used:

  • Long-haul LPG: fully refrigerated ships and VLGCs load in the Middle East Gulf and the US Gulf. They discharge mainly in Asia, including India, China, Japan and Korea.
  • Short-sea work: pressurised and semi-refrigerated ships run regional and coastal trades, often with several ports a week.

For scale, propane boils at roughly −42 °C at atmospheric pressure and butane at around 0 °C. That is why a fully refrigerated propane cargo is carried very cold. It is also why a pressurised ship can carry the same product at sea temperature.

Layout and cargo equipment you will work with

Cargo tanks. The IGC Code defines independent tank types. The type decides how much secondary barrier the ship needs:

  • Type A: usually prismatic, found on fully refrigerated ships. Needs a full secondary barrier.
  • Type B: needs a partial secondary barrier.
  • Type C: pressure vessels, cylindrical or bilobe, found on pressurised and semi-refrigerated ships. Needs no secondary barrier.

Hold or interbarrier spaces are kept inerted or filled with dry air, depending on the tank type and the cargo, as the Code requires.

Main systems on deck and in the cargo machinery space:

  • Cargo pumps: deepwell or submerged pumps in each tank. Booster pumps and cargo heaters are used when refrigerated cargo is discharged into pressurised shore tanks.
  • Reliquefaction plant: compressors, condensers and sometimes a cascade system. It handles boil-off, controls tank pressure and cools cargo during loading.
  • Liquid and vapour lines: these run to the manifold. A vapour return line goes to shore where the terminal requires it.
  • Inert gas and nitrogen: an inert gas generator and/or nitrogen supply for inerting and purging.
  • Pressure relief valves: fitted on each tank. Their maximum setting (MARVS) is stated on the ship's documents.
  • Emergency shutdown (ESD) system: fusible elements near tank domes and manifolds, plus manual push-buttons. It is usually linked to the shore ESD.
  • Fixed gas detection: covers cargo machinery spaces, hold spaces, air locks and accommodation intakes.
  • Fire protection: a water-spray system covers tank domes, manifolds and exposed boundaries. A dry chemical powder system protects the cargo deck area.

Cargo operations step by step: from dry dock to first loading

A typical sequence after dry dock shows most of what makes gas carrier work different. Exact steps, limits and gas concentrations come from the ship's cargo manual and the company's SMS.

  1. Drying and inerting. Tanks are dried to avoid ice and hydrates later. They are then inerted with inert gas or nitrogen to bring oxygen low enough that a flammable mixture cannot form.
  2. Gassing up. Cargo vapour, from shore or from the ship's own liquid, displaces the inert gas. This matters because inert gas is largely non-condensable: the reliquefaction plant cannot condense it, so it raises condenser and tank pressure. Its CO₂ and moisture would also contaminate the product, and on colder cargoes such as ethylene the CO₂ can freeze.
  3. Cooling down. Liquid cargo is sprayed slowly into the tanks. The cool-down rate is limited by the tank and steel design, so watch tank temperatures and pressures closely.
  4. Loading. Boil-off is controlled with the reliquefaction plant or vapour return to shore. You must keep within the tank filling limits stated in the loading limit tables, and the ESD is tested with the terminal before transfer.
  5. Loaded passage. The reliquefaction plant controls boil-off, and the ship keeps a cargo log of tank pressures and temperatures.
  6. Discharge. Cargo pumps are used, with booster pumps and cargo heater if the receiver needs warmer cargo. Vapour is returned to the tanks or supplied to them to hold pressure.
  7. Changing grade or going to dry dock. The tanks are warmed up, the cargo vapour is inerted out, and the tanks are aerated before anyone enters.

Ammonia is a special case. Ammonia reacts with the CO₂ in combustion inert gas and forms solid carbamates. Ammonia tanks are therefore not inerted with that gas. Ships typically use nitrogen, or purge directly with ammonia vapour, as the cargo manual specifies.

Hydrates. Water in hydrocarbon cargo can form ice-like solids that block pumps, valves and gauges. Prevention starts with good drying. Freezing-point depressants such as methanol are injected only where the cargo specification and charterer permit.

Key hazards on an LPG tanker

  • Flammability. Propane and butane vapour is heavier than air. It collects in low points, drains and enclosed areas, and can travel a long way before finding an ignition source. This is why the ship enforces strict hot work permits, smoking areas, and intrinsically safe torches and radios.
  • Cold burns and brittle fracture. Refrigerated liquid and cold-flashing pressurised cargo cause frostbite on contact. Spilled cold liquid can make ordinary hull steel brittle. Wear gloves and face protection at the manifold, and never treat a cold pipe as safe to touch.
  • Toxicity. Ammonia, VCM and butadiene are toxic, and some are carcinogenic. Where toxic cargoes are carried, the IGC Code requires:
    • respiratory and eye protection for emergency escape for everyone on board;
    • decontamination showers and eye washes on deck.
  • Asphyxiation. Inerted tanks, hold spaces and nitrogen-filled lines will kill without warning. Enclosed space entry procedures, gas testing and permits are non-negotiable.
  • Pressure. Trapped liquid between closed valves warms up and expands. Relief valves on lines and tanks protect against this, but only if nobody isolates them.
  • Reactivity and compatibility. Some cargoes polymerise or react with other cargoes, water, air or certain materials. Inhibitor certificates and compatibility charts must be checked before loading.

Certificates and experience usually expected

STCW training (Regulation V/1-2):

  • Basic Training for Liquefied Gas Tanker Cargo Operations. Required for officers and ratings with specific duties related to cargo or cargo equipment. Some companies require all crew to hold it, so ask yours. Oil and chemical tanker training does not replace it.
  • Advanced Training for Liquefied Gas Tanker Cargo Operations. Required for the master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations. You need the basic certificate first, plus one of these:
    • at least three months of approved seagoing service on liquefied gas tankers; or
    • at least one month of approved onboard training in a supernumerary capacity covering at least three loading and three unloading operations.
  • Who issues it: your administration issues the endorsement or certificate of proficiency.
    • India: the DG Shipping Dangerous Cargo Endorsement.
    • Philippines: a MARINA certificate of proficiency.
  • Revalidation: STCW Regulation I/11 requires masters and officers to re-establish continued professional competence for tankers at intervals not exceeding five years, through approved tanker sea service or an approved course. Check your administration's exact requirements, including for ratings, before the certificate lapses.

Everything else: you still need the full STCW set for your rank. That means CoC, basic safety training, AFF for officers, medical fitness, ECDIS, BRM/ERM and so on.

Experience matters as much as paper. Charterers' vetting (for example under the OCIMF SIRE programme) looks at officers' time in rank, time on the ship type and time with the operator. Companies therefore often prefer officers with previous gas experience, or tanker experience at least. Exact expectations vary by company and charterer.

Crew composition and life on board

Typical crew. Most LPG carriers carry:

  • a master, chief officer, second and third officers, and often deck cadets;
  • a chief, second and third engineer, and on larger ships often a fourth engineer and an ETO;
  • deck and engine ratings.

The chief officer normally runs cargo operations. Some companies carry a dedicated cargo or gas engineer for the reliquefaction plant and cargo machinery, and some carry a pumpman. Manning depends on ship size, flag and the company's safe manning document.

What daily life is like depends heavily on the ship type:

  • VLGCs and fully refrigerated ships have long sea passages and fewer port calls. You will spend steady time on plant monitoring, cargo logs and maintenance.
  • Small pressurised and semi-refrigerated ships call frequently, sometimes changing grade between voyages. Workload is heavy around the clock, and rest hours under STCW and MLC need real management.

Some things are common to all gas carriers:

  • strict control of ignition sources;
  • limits on mobile phones and non-certified electronics in gas-dangerous zones;
  • frequent terminal and vetting inspections;
  • regular ESD, gas detection and emergency drills.

Vetting and terminal inspections put strong pressure on operators to keep gas carriers well maintained.

Oral exam corner: questions examiners like to ask

Have a confident one-line answer ready for each:

  • Why must tanks be gassed up after inerting with combustion inert gas? Inert gas is non-condensable, so the reliquefaction plant cannot handle it, and its CO₂ and moisture would contaminate the cargo. On very cold cargoes such as ethylene, CO₂ can also freeze.
  • Why not use combustion inert gas with ammonia? It reacts with CO₂ to form carbamates.
  • Which tank type needs no secondary barrier, and why? Type C, because it is designed as a pressure vessel.
  • What is a hydrate and how do you prevent it? An ice-like solid of water and hydrocarbon. Prevent it by drying tanks and lines, and by using a permitted depressant.
  • What activates the ESD apart from the push-buttons? Fusible elements near domes and manifolds, among other trips listed in your ship's ESD matrix.
  • Where does propane vapour go if it leaks on deck? It sinks and spreads to low points, because it is heavier than air.
  • What limits how full you may load a tank? The filling limits in the IGC-based loading limit tables. These depend on the relief valve setting, cargo density and the reference temperature.

Examiners will also expect you to link these points to enclosed space entry, the permit-to-work system and the ship's cargo manual.

How to get your first LPG tanker contract

  • Cadets. Apply to companies or managers that run gas fleets and sponsor cadets. Cadetship on a gas carrier is the most direct route into the trade.
  • Officers and ratings from oil or chemical tankers. This is the most common transfer route:
    • complete the basic gas tanker course ashore at an approved institute;
    • ask your company whether it runs a supernumerary or trainee berth, which can also count towards the advanced certificate.
  • Ratings. Your best chance of being picked comes from holding the basic gas course and AB or Able Seafarer certificates, plus a clean tanker record.
  • Show readiness. Know the IGC basics, your tank types and your PPE. Many gas operators run in-house cargo simulator courses and test candidates before deployment.
  • Use only licensed manning agents. MLC 2006 Regulation 1.4 means seafarers should not be charged for recruitment or placement.
    • India: use agencies holding a DG Shipping RPSL.
    • Philippines: use agencies licensed by the DMW (formerly POEA).
    • Treat any offer that asks you to pay for a 'gas tanker job' as a red flag.
Key points
  • LPG tankers carry propane, butane and often ammonia and petrochemical gases, kept liquid by pressure, refrigeration or both.
  • The IGC Certificate of Fitness, not the ship's name, decides which cargoes the ship may carry.
  • Anyone with specific cargo duties needs STCW basic gas tanker training; oil and chemical tanker training does not cover gas.
  • Senior officers and anyone with immediate cargo responsibility need advanced gas training, which requires gas tanker sea service or supervised supernumerary training.
  • The main hazards are heavier-than-air flammable vapour, cold burns, toxic cargoes and inerted spaces.
  • Ammonia is never inerted with CO2-bearing inert gas because it forms carbamates.
  • The usual entry routes are gas-fleet cadetships or a transfer from oil and chemical tankers, through licensed manning agents only.

Frequently asked questions

Is my oil and chemical tanker course valid for an LPG tanker?

No. Liquefied gas tankers have their own STCW basic and advanced training under Regulation V/1-2. You need the gas tanker certificate or endorsement from your administration before taking specific cargo duties on an LPG ship.

Can I join an LPG tanker without any gas experience?

Yes, at junior or rating level, provided you hold the basic gas tanker certificate and the company accepts you. Senior positions need the advanced certificate, which requires gas tanker sea service or supervised supernumerary training. Charterer vetting also tends to favour officers with previous gas time.

Is working on an LPG tanker more dangerous than on an oil tanker?

The hazards are different rather than simply greater. You add cold burns, pressurised systems, heavier-than-air vapour and, on many ships, toxic cargoes such as ammonia. Gas carriers have strong engineering safeguards like ESD, relief valves and gas detection, but they depend on disciplined procedures.

What is the difference between an LPG tanker and an LNG tanker?

LNG carriers carry methane-rich natural gas at far lower temperatures and usually use the boil-off as fuel. LPG carriers carry heavier gases such as propane and butane, which can be kept liquid by pressure or by milder refrigeration. They normally reliquefy boil-off rather than burn it. Both fall under the IGC Code and the same STCW gas tanker training.

Do galley and catering crew need the gas tanker course?

STCW requires the basic gas tanker certificate for personnel with specific duties related to cargo or cargo equipment. Catering crew do not normally have such duties. All crew still receive ship-specific familiarisation, and some companies set their own wider requirements, so check with your manager.

Test yourself

Under STCW, who must hold advanced training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations?

Advanced gas training is required for the senior officers listed and for any person with immediate responsibility for cargo operations (STCW Regulation V/1-2).

Why are ammonia cargo tanks not inerted with combustion-generated inert gas?

CO2 reacts with ammonia to form carbamates, so nitrogen or direct ammonia vapour purging is used instead (ship's cargo manual; SIGTTO/industry gas-carrier practice).

A small propane leak occurs on deck in calm weather. Where is the vapour most likely to accumulate?

Propane and butane vapour is heavier than air, so it spreads low and can travel to distant ignition sources (IGC Code hazard basis; STCW Section A-V/1-2 competence).

Which independent cargo tank type is designed as a pressure vessel and needs no secondary barrier?

Type C tanks are pressure vessels and the IGC Code does not require a secondary barrier for them, unlike Type A (full) and Type B (partial).

What fixed fire-extinguishing arrangement does the IGC Code require for the cargo deck area of a gas carrier carrying flammable products?

Gas carriers carrying flammable cargoes are fitted with dry chemical powder systems for the cargo area, plus water-spray protection (IGC Code, Chapter 11).
