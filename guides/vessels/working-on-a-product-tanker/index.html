Working on a Product Tanker

Quick answer

A product tanker carries refined petroleum products such as gasoline, naphtha, jet fuel, diesel and fuel oil, usually several grades at once on short voyages with frequent port calls. To work on one you need the STCW tanker endorsement for your role: basic oil and chemical tanker training for anyone with cargo duties, and advanced oil tanker training for senior officers and those in charge of cargo operations. You should also expect heavy inspection and vetting scrutiny and a demanding work pattern.

What a product tanker does and how it trades

Crude tankers carry crude oil from oilfields to refineries. Product tankers carry what comes out of the refinery, from refinery to storage terminals, other refineries and import hubs. The cargo is usually split into two families:

  • Clean petroleum products (CPP): gasoline (mogas), naphtha, jet fuel/kerosene, gasoil and diesel.
  • Dirty petroleum products (DPP): fuel oils and other black oils.

A ship normally stays in either clean or dirty trade. Moving from dirty back to clean takes extensive tank cleaning, and charterers will look closely at the last cargoes.

The trade is regional and fast-moving: coastal and short-sea runs, refinery-to-terminal shuttles, and longer arbitrage voyages between regions. Ships are commonly described by commercial size classes such as Handysize, MR (Medium Range), LR1 and LR2 (Long Range). Brokers do not all use the same deadweight boundaries for these classes. The MR is the workhorse of the clean trade.

Many MR-size ships are built as dual-purpose chemical/product tankers, certified under the IBC Code to carry certain chemicals and vegetable oils as well as oil products. Find out which kind of ship you are joining, because chemical cargoes bring their own certification and hazards.

Layout and cargo equipment

The fittings on a product tanker reflect its multi-grade work:

  • Many cargo tanks with independent segregations, so several grades can be loaded and discharged without mixing. The number of segregations varies by ship.
  • Coated cargo tanks (for example epoxy or zinc silicate). The coating type limits which cargoes may be carried. Check the coating resistance list before accepting a nomination.
  • Cargo pumps: many modern product and chemical/product tankers have a deepwell (submerged) pump in each tank, with hydraulic or electric drive. Older and larger ships often have a pump room with centrifugal pumps. Whether a pumpman is carried often depends on which arrangement the ship has.
  • Inert gas system: SOLAS requires a fixed inert gas system on oil tankers above a size threshold that depends on the ship's build date. Many smaller ships fit one anyway. Check the ship's certificates rather than assuming.
  • Slop tanks and an oil discharge monitoring equipment (ODME) for handling tank washings under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Closed loading and gauging: radar or float gauges, high-level and overfill alarms, and vapour return connections where terminals require them.
  • Cargo heating coils on ships in the dirty trade.

The cargo control room (CCR) is the centre of cargo operations. The chief officer plans the operation, and the watch officers run it from the CCR while ratings carry out deck checks.

Cargo operations and the main hazards

A typical port call runs: pre-arrival checks and ship/shore safety checklist, gauging and sampling, loading or discharging several grades, line draining and stripping, final ullages and documents, then sailing. Tank cleaning and line flushing between grades often happen on passage. That is why rest-hour planning on product tankers is hard.

Key hazards every crew member must understand:

  • Flammability. Gasoline and naphtha give off flammable vapour at normal temperatures. Control of ignition sources, hot work permits and keeping inert gas in its correct condition are basic disciplines on board. On ships fitted with an inert gas system, cargo tanks are kept inerted to an oxygen content not exceeding 8% by volume and at positive pressure.
  • Static electricity. Jet fuel, gasoil and similar products are static accumulators. When loading static accumulators into non-inerted tanks, initial loading rates are restricted until the tank inlet is covered. A settling period applies before conductive dipping or sampling equipment goes into a non-inerted tank. Bonding requirements apply. ISGOTT and your company procedures set the detail.
  • Toxic components. Gasoline and naphtha can contain benzene, which is a carcinogen. Some products, especially certain fuel oils, can release hydrogen sulphide (H2S). Use personal gas monitors and the correct PPE, and stand upwind when gauging or sampling.
  • Enclosed spaces. Cargo tanks, pump rooms, void spaces and cofferdams can be oxygen-deficient or toxic. Entry is only under a permit with atmosphere testing. Never go in to rescue a collapsed person without breathing apparatus.
  • Contamination. A grade mix or poorly flushed line can ruin a cargo worth a great deal. Valve line-ups are checked twice, and jet fuel in particular calls for scrupulous cleanliness.
  • Pollution. Discharges of oily water from the cargo area are only allowed under strict MARPOL Annex I conditions, with the ODME working. Every cargo and ballast operation is recorded in Oil Record Book Part II.

Certificates and experience expected

Tanker certification comes from STCW regulation V/1-1 and Section A-V/1-1 of the Code:

Requirement Who needs it
Basic training for oil and chemical tanker cargo operations Officers and ratings assigned specific duties and responsibilities related to cargo or cargo equipment
Advanced training for oil tanker cargo operations Master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit or handling of cargo, tank cleaning and related operations

To qualify for the advanced certificate you need to hold the basic qualification and complete the approved advanced course. You also need either at least three months of approved seagoing service on oil tankers, or at least one month of approved onboard training as a supernumerary that includes at least three loading and three unloading operations. Your administration decides how that service is evidenced. Collect signed tanker service testimonials from every ship.

The name of the document varies by administration. India issues a Dangerous Cargo Endorsement (DCE). Other administrations, including the Philippines, issue a certificate of proficiency or endorsement. On a chemical/product tanker carrying chemicals, the chemical tanker endorsements also apply.

Beyond STCW, oil majors and charterers vet ships through OCIMF's SIRE inspection programme. They also check the officer matrix, which records each officer's time in rank, time on tankers and time with the operator. Matrix requirements differ between charterers and are not set by any convention. They are a big reason companies are careful about who they promote or put on a given ship.

Typical crew and who does what

Crew size and structure vary by company, ship size and flag manning. A typical MR product tanker carries:

  • Deck: master, chief officer (cargo officer), second officer, third officer, often deck cadets, bosun, ABs, OSs and sometimes a pumpman.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second engineer, third and fourth engineers, an ETO on many ships, motormen/oilers, fitter and wiper.
  • Catering: cook and messman/steward.

Cargo work is shared. The chief officer plans and supervises. Junior officers keep the cargo watch in the CCR. ABs handle hoses, manifolds, gauging, sampling and deck rounds. Engineers run the cargo pump power packs, inert gas plant, boilers and heating. On deepwell-pump ships the engine department also carries a large maintenance load for the hydraulic system.

Life on board: what to expect

  • Short, busy voyages. Some runs are only days long, so port calls, tank cleaning and inspections come close together. Sleep is often broken.
  • Rest hours matter. STCW requires watchkeepers and those with safety, security and pollution-prevention duties to have at least 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. MLC 2006 also limits hours, with flag states applying either minimum hours of rest or maximum hours of work. Record your hours honestly. Vetting and port state inspectors check them, and fatigue causes cargo incidents.
  • Limited shore leave. Terminals are often remote, turnarounds are fast, and terminal rules may restrict movement.
  • Constant inspection. SIRE, terminal, port state and company audits are routine. Expect frequent drills and paperwork, and high standards for housekeeping, PPE and checklists.
  • Strong safety culture. Tanker operators tend to run strict systems: permits, stop-work authority and tight control of smoking and mobile phones on deck. People who adapt to that discipline progress quickly.

Oral exam style questions

Expect questions like these at tanker course assessments, company interviews and CoC orals:

  1. What is a static accumulator and what do you do about it? It is a product with low electrical conductivity that holds a charge, such as jet fuel or gasoil. When loading into a non-inerted tank, you restrict the initial loading rate, avoid splash loading, observe settling times before dipping or sampling, and make sure equipment is bonded.
  2. What oxygen content must be maintained in an inerted cargo tank? Not more than 8% by volume, with the tank at positive pressure.
  3. Which record book covers cargo and ballast operations on a tanker? Oil Record Book Part II.
  4. Who needs advanced oil tanker training? The master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for cargo operations.
  5. Walk me through how you would prevent a grade mix. Use the cargo plan and pipeline diagram, check the valve line-up independently, use blinds or double-valve segregation as the plan requires, compare initial ullages and flow against expected rates, and stop if anything does not add up.

How to get your first product tanker contract

  1. Complete basic oil and chemical tanker training at an approved institute and get the endorsement or certificate of proficiency from your administration.
  2. Target tanker operators and manning agents that run product or chemical/product fleets. Cadetships with tanker companies are one of the most reliable ways in.
  3. Accept the entry rank. Experienced officers from dry cargo ships are often asked to step down a rank, or to sail as a supernumerary, to build tanker time. Policy varies by company.
  4. Document everything. Keep tanker service testimonials, logs of loading and discharge operations, and your record of rest hours. You will need these for the advanced course and for charterers' matrices.
  5. Prepare for the interview. Revise inert gas, static, enclosed space entry, MARPOL Annex I and the ship/shore safety checklist.
  6. Stay with one operator if you can. Time with the company counts on the officer matrix and makes promotion easier.
Key points
  • Product tankers carry refined oils (clean or dirty), usually several grades at once on short, port-intensive voyages.
  • Many MR tankers are dual chemical/product ships, so check whether chemical endorsements also apply.
  • Basic oil and chemical tanker training is needed for anyone with cargo duties; advanced oil tanker training is needed for senior officers and those in charge of cargo.
  • Static electricity, flammable vapour, benzene and H2S exposure, enclosed spaces and cargo contamination are the core hazards.
  • On ships with an inert gas system, inerted cargo tanks are kept at no more than 8% oxygen by volume and at positive pressure.
  • SIRE vetting and charterers' officer matrices shape crewing, so tanker time and time with one company are valuable.
  • Collect signed tanker service records from every contract, because you will need them for the advanced endorsement and for promotion.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join a product tanker without a tanker course?

Not if you will have any cargo-related duties. STCW requires basic oil and chemical tanker training for officers and ratings with specific cargo duties. In practice almost every company requires it from all deck and engine crew before joining. Catering staff may have different requirements depending on company policy and flag.

What is the difference between a product tanker and a chemical tanker?

A product tanker carries refined petroleum products under MARPOL Annex I. A chemical tanker carries noxious liquid substances under MARPOL Annex II and the IBC Code. Many ships are dual chemical/product tankers that can do both. When they carry chemicals, the chemical tanker training and endorsements apply.

How much tanker service do I need for the advanced oil tanker course?

STCW requires at least three months of approved seagoing service on oil tankers, or at least one month of approved supernumerary training that includes at least three loading and three unloading operations. You must also hold the basic tanker qualification. Your administration sets how the service is verified, so keep signed testimonials.

What is SIRE and why does it affect me?

SIRE is OCIMF's ship inspection programme, which oil companies and charterers use to vet tankers. Inspectors interview crew, check rest hours, drills and procedures, and look at officers' experience. A poor inspection can stop a ship trading with major charterers, so every crew member's performance matters.

Do product tanker crews get shore leave?

Less than on many other ship types. Terminals are often far from town, port stays are short, and terminal security rules may restrict movement. Shore leave depends on the port, the operation and the master's assessment, and seafarers' rights to it are recognised under MLC 2006.

Test yourself

A non-inerted tank is being loaded with jet fuel, a static accumulator. What is the correct precaution at the start of loading?

Low initial rates limit static generation until the inlet is submerged. Dipping during or right after loading without a settling period is a known ignition risk (ISGOTT and company SMS).

What is the maximum oxygen content in an inerted cargo tank?

Inerted tanks are kept at not more than 8% oxygen by volume and at positive pressure. The 5% figure applies to the gas delivered by the inert gas plant, not to the tank atmosphere (SOLAS II-2 and FSS Code Chapter 15).

Which record book do you use to record cargo and ballast operations on an oil tanker?

Part II covers cargo and ballast operations on oil tankers. Part I covers machinery space operations, and the Cargo Record Book is for noxious liquid substances under Annex II (MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 36).

Who must hold advanced training for oil tanker cargo operations?

Advanced training is required for senior officers and those directly in charge of cargo handling. Ratings with cargo duties need basic training (STCW Regulation V/1-1).

What is the minimum rest a product tanker crew member must get?

The minimum is 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. Exceptions apply only in emergencies or as provided in the Code (STCW Code Section A-VIII/1; see also MLC 2006 Regulation 2.3).
