Working on a Heavy Lift Vessel

Quick answer

Heavy lift vessels carry oversized and very heavy project cargo such as transformers, reactors, cranes, yachts and offshore modules. Cargo is usually loaded with the ship's own heavy cranes, by float-on/float-off, or by roll-on/skidding onto deck carriers. The work depends on stability, ballast and lifting discipline. There is no single mandatory 'heavy lift' STCW certificate, so companies value multipurpose or general cargo experience, strong stability knowledge and their own in-house crane and lift training.

What a heavy lift vessel does

Heavy lift ships move cargo that will not fit in a container or does not suit normal breakbulk handling. Typical cargo includes:

  • Power plant and refinery equipment: transformers, generators, reactors, columns, boilers
  • Port and construction plant: STS cranes, RTGs, locomotives, tunnel boring machines
  • Offshore and renewables items: jackets, modules, monopiles, transition pieces
  • Yachts, small craft, barges and sometimes other vessels

The trade is project-driven, not liner-driven. A voyage is built around project schedules. Sometimes it carries a single shipper's cargo, and sometimes several parcels are combined. Load ports are often fabrication yards or small river ports. Discharge may be at a remote site with limited shore facilities. Itineraries change often, and port stays can be long while cargo is prepared, surveyed and secured.

Main ship types and layout

"Heavy lift" covers several designs. Know which one you are joining.

Type How cargo is loaded Typical features
Heavy lift / project carrier (lift-on/lift-off) Ship's own cranes, often working in tandem Box-shaped holds, pontoon hatch covers usable as tween decks or weather-deck stowage, large ballast capacity
Multipurpose heavy lift Ship's cranes, smaller lifting capacity Mixes project cargo with breakbulk, bulk or containers
Semi-submersible heavy transport Float-on/float-off: the ship ballasts down until the deck is submerged and cargo is floated over Open flush deck, very large ballast system, accommodation forward or to one side
Deck carrier / open-deck vessel Lift-on, roll-on (SPMT trailers) or skidding Strengthened open deck, often no holds

On crane ships the cranes are usually mounted along one side. A combined tandem lift can far exceed the rating of any single crane. Many such ships have a stabilisation (anti-heeling) system, a sponson or pontoon, or rely on rapid ballast transfer to counter the heeling moment during an outboard lift.

How a heavy lift is done: step by step

Procedures come from the company SMS and the lifting plan for that cargo. A typical crane lift from quay to ship goes like this:

  1. Engineering and lift plan. The office engineering department prepares rigging drawings, crane configuration, sling and spreader arrangement, stability calculations for each stage, and stowage and seafastening drawings.
  2. Pre-lift meeting. Master, chief officer, chief engineer, crane operators, bosun, riggers, the company superintendent or supercargo, and often the shipper's and insurer's surveyors attend. Everyone confirms the weight, centre of gravity of the cargo, wind and swell limits, communications, and stop criteria.
  3. Ship preparation. Ballast is set as planned. On many ships this means pre-heeling the vessel away from the lift side so the heel never becomes excessive when the load comes over the side. Free surface is kept to a minimum. Moorings are tended and fenders checked. Hatch covers or tween decks are positioned.
  4. Equipment checks. Check crane limits and overload protection, wires, hooks, spreader beams, shackles and slings against their certificates and markings. Check the tandem crane synchronisation if fitted.
  5. Test lift. Raise the load just clear of the quay and hold it. Confirm weight, heel, list and rigging behaviour before committing to the lift.
  6. Lift and slew. Hoist and slew slowly with tag lines manned. Counter-ballast or anti-heeling transfer runs in step with the plan. One person gives signals, and the radio channel is kept clear.
  7. Landing and seafastening. Land the load on the planned grillage or dunnage, then lash it and/or weld stoppers and seafastening. Hot work needs a permit and a fire watch.
  8. Final condition. Correct ballast to the departure condition, then complete the stability check and the seafastening inspection before sailing.

The chief officer normally owns the stability and ballast side. The chief engineer and ETO typically look after crane power, drives and ballast pumps, although each company's SMS defines who is responsible for crane maintenance.

Key hazards

  • Stability during lifting. Once a load is lifted, its weight acts at the crane head. This effectively raises the ship's centre of gravity and creates a large heeling moment as the boom slews outboard. Any error in cargo weight, free surface or ballast state shows up immediately as heel.
  • Ballast system failure mid-lift. A pump, valve or remote control failure during an outboard lift is a serious emergency. Contingency plans and manual backups matter.
  • Rigging failure and dropped loads. Causes include uncertified or damaged gear, sharp edges, wrong sling angles, and side-loading the crane.
  • Weather. Wind on large-area cargo and swell at open anchorages reduce lifting limits. Stop when the plan says stop.
  • Cargo shift at sea. Tall, high-CoG cargo on deck sees large accelerations in heavy weather. Seafastening must match the calculated forces and be re-checked on passage.
  • Hot work and working at height. Welding seafastening, working on top of cargo, and access to crane booms all require permits and fall protection.
  • Float-on/float-off. Deep submergence of a semi-submersible needs tight control of trim, heel and stability margins throughout, and positioning of the cargo over the deck.
  • Personnel under loads and in pinch points. Do not stand under a suspended load or between the load and a fixed structure.

Certificates and experience usually expected

There is no specific STCW certificate for heavy lift ships. Expect the normal requirements for your rank plus what the company and flag ask for:

  • Valid CoC and endorsements for your rank, Basic Safety Training and refreshers, medical certificate, security training
  • For officers: ECDIS (generic and type-specific), radar/ARPA and BRM/ERM as applicable to the rank
  • Crane training: most operators run in-house crane and lift courses. Some flag states, ports or charterers require formal crane operator certification. The rules differ, so check yours.
  • DP certification may be required on semi-submersibles or vessels fitted with DP. It depends on the ship and the company.
  • Experience that helps: multipurpose or general cargo ships, stability and ballast handling, lashing and securing, and welding skills for ratings and fitters

Companies screen deck officers hard on stability. Be ready to work through heel, GM and free-surface problems by hand, not only with the loading computer.

Crew composition and life on board

Crews are usually compact but skilled:

  • Deck: Master, Chief Officer, 2nd and 3rd Officers, sometimes cadets; Bosun, ABs, OS. Crane operators may be dedicated ratings or officers, depending on the company.
  • Engine: Chief Engineer, 2nd/3rd Engineers, ETO (crane drives and controls make this role busy), fitter or welder, motorman/oiler.
  • Visitors: supercargo or lifting superintendent, port captain, cargo and warranty surveyors, and sometimes riggers from the shipper.

Life on board:

  • Intense port periods. Lifts, lashing and welding can run long hours. Rest hours must still be planned and recorded under MLC and STCW.
  • Quiet sea passages, spent on maintenance, checking seafastening and preparing for the next cargo
  • Varied, often remote ports with limited shore leave at project sites
  • Steep learning. Rigging, crane work and stability on these ships are skills you keep for the rest of your career, including for offshore or installation vessels.

Getting your first contract

  • Build relevant sea time first. Service on general cargo or multipurpose ships is the most common route in.
  • Target the right crewing offices. Heavy lift operators recruit through their own crewing departments or through a limited number of contracted manning agencies. Find out which crewing offices the operators you are targeting use and approach them directly. Check that any agency is properly licensed by your national authority.
  • Highlight practical skills. Put crane operation, rigging, lashing, ballast handling and welding (for ratings) clearly on your CV.
  • Cadets: some operators run cadet programmes, which are often the easiest way in.
  • Prepare for a technical interview. Expect stability questions, crane safety, cargo securing and a scenario about a lift going wrong.

Typical interview/oral questions

  • Why does the ship's centre of gravity rise when a load is lifted off the quay?
  • How do you plan ballast for an outboard heavy lift, and what would you do if a ballast pump fails mid-lift?
  • What information must you confirm before lifting? (Weight, cargo CoG, lifting points, gear certificates, crane radius and SWL, wind limits.)
  • What is the Cargo Securing Manual and when do you use it?
  • How do you check seafastening at sea after heavy weather?
Key points
  • Heavy lift ships carry project cargo loaded by their own heavy cranes, by float-on/float-off, or by roll-on/skidding onto deck carriers.
  • Stability is the core skill: a lifted load acts at the crane head and creates a large heeling moment.
  • Every lift follows a written plan, a pre-lift meeting, a test lift and agreed stop criteria.
  • There is no specific STCW heavy lift certificate; companies use in-house crane and lift training.
  • Crane certification and DP requirements vary by flag, port, charterer and ship.
  • General cargo or multipurpose experience is the usual route into heavy lift.
  • Port periods are intense, but rest hours must still be managed and recorded.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a heavy lift ship?

No STCW certificate exists specifically for heavy lift vessels. You need the normal certificates for your rank, and most companies add their own crane and lifting training. Some flag states, ports or charterers require formal crane operator certification, so check the rules that apply to your ship.

Can I join a heavy lift vessel straight from a container ship or tanker?

It is possible, but operators prefer candidates with general cargo or multipurpose experience because of the lifting, lashing and stability work. If you come from another trade, strong stability knowledge and a willingness to learn crane work will help your application.

Who decides how the cargo is lifted and secured?

The company's engineering department usually prepares the lift plan, stability calculations and seafastening design. The Master keeps overriding authority for safety on board. For project cargo, a marine warranty surveyor acting for the insurers often has to approve the operation before it goes ahead.

Is the ETO important on heavy lift ships?

Yes. Heavy cranes depend on large electrical drives, control systems and power management, and a fault during a lift is serious. The ETO and engineers keep the cranes, generators and ballast systems reliable during cargo operations.

What is life like on a heavy lift vessel compared with a liner trade?

Schedules are less predictable and ports are often remote yards or project sites. Port stays involve long, demanding cargo work, while sea passages are usually quieter. Many seafarers find the work varied and the skills useful for offshore and installation vessels later.

Test yourself

Once a heavy load is lifted clear of the quay by the ship's crane, where does its weight effectively act for stability purposes?

A suspended load acts at its point of suspension, so it raises the ship's G and creates a heeling moment as the boom slews. This is basic ship stability, reflected in the guidance for vessels engaged in lifting operations in Part B of the 2008 IS Code, as amended.

Why is a heavy lift vessel often pre-heeled away from the lift side before an outboard lift?

Pre-heeling, combined with counter-ballasting, keeps the heel within limits as the heeling moment builds during the lift. Pre-heeling does not increase GM.

Under SOLAS, cargo units on board must be loaded, stowed and secured in accordance with which document?

SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 5 requires cargo units to be secured per the Cargo Securing Manual approved by the Administration.

For project cargo, who commonly reviews and approves the lift and seafastening on behalf of insurers?

Marine warranty surveyors act for the cargo or project insurers. PSC officers inspect compliance with conventions, not project lifts.

Which statement about heavy lift training requirements is correct?

STCW sets no heavy lift-specific certificate. Crane and lifting training come from the SMS and the flag, port or charterer requirements, and the ISM Code requires the company to ensure personnel are trained for their duties.
