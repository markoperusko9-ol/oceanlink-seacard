Working on a Pilot Boat

Quick answer

Pilot boats carry marine pilots between shore or a pilot station and ships at the boarding ground, then put them on board or take them off at sea. The work is short-sea, shift-based and boat-handling heavy, and the main risk is the transfer alongside a moving ship. Certificate requirements are set nationally and differ widely by administration and operator, so check your local rules before applying.

What a pilot boat does

A pilot boat, often called a pilot launch or pilot cutter, takes pilots out to inbound ships at the pilot boarding ground. It brings pilots back from outbound ships. Between those runs it may wait at a station, return to a shore base or shuttle between several ships on one shift.

The operator might be a port authority, a pilotage service or pilots' association, or a private contractor working for one of them. Most pilot boats are small, fast craft built to come alongside a ship's hull in a seaway. In some areas, pilotage services also run larger station vessels that stay offshore and use smaller daughter boats for the actual transfer.

Under the COLREGs, a pilot vessel engaged on pilotage duty has its own lights and sound signal. At night you will be showing and recognising these constantly:

  • White over red all-round lights at or near the masthead (Rule 29).
  • Sidelights and a sternlight in addition when underway.
  • Anchor lights in addition when at anchor.
  • When not on pilotage duty, the boat shows the lights of an ordinary vessel of her length.
  • In restricted visibility, a pilot vessel on duty may add an identity signal of four short blasts to her normal fog signals (Rule 35).

Layout and typical operation

Designs vary, but most transfer boats share the same features:

  • Wheelhouse with good all-round visibility, often with a view onto the foredeck where the transfer takes place.
  • Heavy fendering around the hull so the boat can lean on a ship's side.
  • Transfer deck forward or amidships, with guardrails and handholds. There are usually harness points for the deck crew.
  • Man overboard recovery equipment, such as a recovery device, ladder or scramble net. Some boats have a low recovery area near the waterline.
  • Twin engines on most boats, giving redundancy and manoeuvrability. Water jets, conventional propellers or other propulsion depend on the boat.
  • Seating for pilots inside the wheelhouse or a cabin, often shock-mitigating seats on faster boats.

A typical boarding runs like this. Your local procedures and the pilotage service's instructions always take precedence.

  1. Brief with the pilot station. Confirm the ship, her ETA, the boarding position and the side for the ladder.
  2. Pre-arrange speed and lee. The ship is asked to make a lee on the agreed side and to hold a steady course and speed suitable for the transfer.
  3. Check the ladder from a distance. Look at height above the water, condition, securing and lighting, and whether a responsible officer is at the embarkation point. Report defects before anyone climbs.
  4. Make the approach. Come in from aft, parallel to the ship and clear of her quarter. Close the hull gradually rather than crossing ahead of her bow.
  5. Match speed and hold steady. Keep the boat in position alongside with the ladder at the transfer point.
  6. Make the transfer. The pilot steps onto the ladder when the boat is near the top of a rise. The pilot then climbs promptly above the reach of the boat before it rises again on the next swell.
  7. Break away. Move off clear of the ship's side. Keep watching until the pilot is on deck and the ship confirms it.

Disembarking runs in reverse. It is often considered harder, because the pilot steps down onto a moving deck.

Key hazards

Pilot transfers have caused deaths and serious injuries to pilots and boat crews. Treat every transfer as a high-risk operation, however routine it feels.

  • Falls from the ladder. These are caused by defective, wrongly rigged or poorly secured ladders, and by climbs that are too long. SOLAS limits a single pilot ladder climb to between 1.5 m and 9 m. Higher freeboards need a combination arrangement with an accommodation ladder, or other equally safe and convenient means of access.
  • Crushing. A pilot or deckhand can be crushed between the boat and the ship's hull, or by the boat rising on a swell under the ladder.
  • Hydrodynamic interaction. The ship's bow wave, pressure fields and suction near the quarter can push or pull the boat. The stern and propeller area is a danger zone.
  • Man overboard. Recovery of a person in the water between a moving ship and the boat must be planned and drilled, not improvised.
  • Heavy weather and darkness. Many transfers happen at night, in swell or in poor visibility. Spray, motion and wet decks all add risk.
  • Fatigue. Shift work, irregular calls and repeated high-concentration manoeuvres take their toll. Hours of rest still apply, and they are worth taking seriously.
  • High-speed motion. Slamming at speed can cause spinal and other injuries to crew and pilots.

Good practice on most boats includes these habits:

  • Standing orders set clear weather and sea-state limits.
  • The skipper can abort. Any transfer that looks wrong can be stopped.
  • Deck crew are secured. Lifejackets, or the boat's specified PPE, are worn and harnesses are clipped on.
  • Communication is constant between the wheelhouse, the deckhand and the ship's bridge.

Certificates and experience usually expected

There is no single international pilot boat certificate. Requirements depend on the flag or coastal administration, the boat's size and operating area, and the operator. Many pilot boats are certified under national small-vessel or workboat rules rather than the full STCW framework for seagoing ships.

Role Commonly expected (varies by administration and operator)
Skipper / coxswain / master A national certificate of competency covering the boat's size and area, a radio operator certificate, radar training and first aid. Strong boat-handling experience is essential.
Deckhand / boatman Basic safety training, often the STCW Basic Safety Training modules. Some operators also want survival craft or rescue boat proficiency.
Engineer (larger boats) A national engineering certificate appropriate to the propulsion power.

Other points to check:

  • High-speed craft rules. Where the administration treats the boat as a high-speed craft, additional type-specific training may be required.
  • Recruiting criteria. Many operators prefer people with local knowledge of the port and pilotage area, and some recruit only from residents or nationals.
  • Medical fitness. A valid seafarer medical certificate is normally required. Some operators set their own fitness standards because of the physical demands of deck work.
  • Prior experience. A deep-sea officer's CoC is valued for seamanship and COLREG knowledge. It is not automatically a licence to command a small fast boat. Check what your administration will accept, and whether it offers a route to convert or endorse it.

Crew composition and life on board

A transfer boat is usually crewed by two or three people:

  • Coxswain or skipper. Handles the boat, makes the go/no-go call and is in charge on board.
  • Deckhand or boatman. Assists the pilot on deck, handles lines, keeps lookout and runs MOB recovery.
  • Engineer (on some larger boats). Looks after the machinery and sometimes acts as a second deckhand.

Larger station vessels carry bigger crews, sometimes including cooks and stewards for pilots waiting offshore.

Life is very different from deep-sea trading:

  • Shift-based work. Often you go home at the end of a shift or rotation instead of spending months away.
  • Short, intense runs. Long waits can be broken by several transfers in quick succession, at any hour.
  • Weather-driven days. Bad-weather shifts can be exhausting. Pilotage may be suspended or moved to a sheltered boarding position when limits are exceeded.
  • Limited accommodation. You rarely live aboard a small transfer boat. Facilities are basic.
  • Hands-on maintenance. Crews often do their own routine checks on engines, fenders, recovery gear and safety equipment.

The working relationship with pilots is close. You will get to know their preferences, and a good crew earns a lot of trust.

How to get a first job on a pilot boat

Pilot boat jobs are mostly filled locally, not through deep-sea manning agencies. A practical route looks like this:

  1. Find out who runs the boats in your target port: the port authority, a pilots' association or a contractor.
  2. Check the local certificate requirements with the administration and the operator. Do this before spending money on courses.
  3. Build small-craft and boat-handling experience. Tugs, harbour launches, workboats, crew transfer vessels and offshore support vessels are all useful.
  4. Keep your basics valid: basic safety training, medical, radio certificate and first aid.
  5. Apply as a deckhand or boatman first if you have no command experience on small fast craft. Many coxswains came up this way.
  6. Expect a probation period. Operators commonly require supervised transfers before letting a new coxswain operate independently.

For officers coming ashore from deep-sea service, a pilot boat is one route to staying in seagoing work while living at home. Be ready to learn small-boat handling from scratch, even with years on large ships.

Oral exam style questions

These are questions an examiner or interviewing skipper might ask:

  • Q: What lights does a pilot vessel on duty show underway at night? A: White over red all-round at or near the masthead, plus sidelights and a sternlight.
  • Q: What does a pilot vessel on duty show at anchor? A: White over red all-round, plus anchor lights.
  • Q: You see a ladder whose bottom rung sits well above the water and whose side ropes look frayed. What do you do? A: Do not let the pilot climb. Report the defect to the ship through the pilot or VHF and have it corrected or replaced. Inform the pilot station as required by your procedures.
  • Q: Why approach from aft and not across the bow? A: Crossing the bow risks being run down or caught in the bow wave and pressure field. Approaching parallel from aft gives better control and an escape route.
  • Q: The pilot falls between the boat and the ship. First actions? A: Shout the alarm and tell the ship to stop engines. Keep the person in sight and get clear of the hull and propeller. Then start recovery with the boat's MOB equipment and alert the pilot station or VTS.
Key points
  • Pilot boats carry pilots to and from ships at the boarding ground; the transfer alongside is the main hazard.
  • A pilot vessel on duty shows white over red all-round lights, plus sidelights and sternlight underway, or anchor lights at anchor.
  • In restricted visibility a pilot vessel on duty may add an identity signal of four short blasts.
  • SOLAS limits a single pilot ladder climb to between 1.5 m and 9 m; higher freeboards need a combination arrangement or other equally safe and convenient access.
  • Certificate requirements are set nationally and vary by administration, boat size, area and operator.
  • Jobs are mostly recruited locally; small-craft and boat-handling experience counts heavily.
  • Any crew member who sees an unsafe ladder or condition should stop the transfer.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an STCW certificate of competency to work on a pilot boat?

Not always. Many pilot boats are certified under national small-vessel or workboat rules, and the required certificate depends on the administration, boat size and operating area. Deckhands are usually expected to hold basic safety training, and skippers need a certificate valid for that boat. Confirm with your administration and the operator.

Can a deep-sea officer become a pilot boat coxswain?

Often yes, but a large-ship CoC does not automatically cover command of a small fast boat. Some administrations offer conversion or endorsement routes, and operators normally require supervised training transfers. Expect to learn close-quarters boat handling as a new skill.

Is working on a pilot boat the same as being a marine pilot?

No. Pilot boat crews operate the transfer craft, while marine pilots are licensed separately to con ships in pilotage waters. Pilot licensing has its own experience and examination requirements, set by the pilotage authority.

Do pilot boat crews live on board?

Usually not on small transfer boats. Crews normally work shifts or rotations from a shore base and go home afterwards. Larger offshore station vessels, where they exist, do carry crew on board for a period.

Who is responsible for the pilot ladder, the ship or the pilot boat?

SOLAS places the duty on the ship to provide and rig compliant pilot transfer arrangements, supervised by a responsible officer in contact with the bridge. The pilot boat crew and the pilot should still check the ladder before the climb. They should refuse it if it is unsafe.

Test yourself

A pilot vessel engaged on pilotage duty and underway at night shows which lights?

Rule 29 requires white over red at or near the masthead, plus sidelights and a sternlight when underway. Red over white is a vessel engaged in fishing other than trawling, and two reds is not under command (COLREG Rules 26, 27 and 29).

Under SOLAS, what is the maximum climb a pilot may be required to make on a single pilot ladder?

The climb on a pilot ladder must be not less than 1.5 m and not more than 9 m. Beyond that, a combination arrangement or other equally safe and convenient means of access is used (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 23).

In restricted visibility, what identity signal may a pilot vessel on duty sound in addition to her normal fog signals?

A pilot vessel on duty may add four short blasts. One prolonged and two short is used by several special-category vessels, five short is the doubt signal, and two prolonged is underway but stopped (COLREG Rules 34 and 35).

On which side of the ship does the pilot boat normally come alongside for a transfer?

The ship makes a lee on an agreed side so the boat can lie alongside in calmer water. No COLREG or SOLAS rule fixes the side, and a weather-side approach increases boat motion (local pilotage procedures and industry guidance).

Who is required to supervise the rigging of the pilot ladder and the embarkation of the pilot?

SOLAS requires the rigging and embarkation to be supervised by a responsible officer of the ship with means of communication with the navigation bridge (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 23).
