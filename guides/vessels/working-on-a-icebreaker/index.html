Working on an Icebreaker

Quick answer

Icebreakers open and maintain channels through ice, escort and tow merchant ships, and support Arctic and Antarctic projects and research. Many are state-owned and crewed by nationals of the operating country, so for most Indian and Filipino seafarers the realistic route is through commercial ice-class and icebreaking offshore or project vessels. Polar Code training under STCW Regulation V/4 (for deck officers) and documented ice experience are the qualifications that most distinguish candidates.

What an icebreaker actually does

An icebreaker is built to make headway through ice that would stop or damage an ordinary ship. In practice the work falls into a few categories:

  • Escort and convoy work: leading one or more merchant ships through a broken channel, often in winter ports and approaches such as the Baltic, Gulf of St Lawrence and Great Lakes.
  • Channel and harbour maintenance: keeping fairways, berths and turning areas open so port traffic keeps moving.
  • Assistance: freeing ships that are beset, towing in ice, and supporting search and rescue in ice-covered waters.
  • Project and offshore support: icebreaking supply, standby and escort vessels working for Arctic oil, gas and LNG projects.
  • Polar logistics and science: resupplying research stations and carrying scientists in the Arctic and Antarctic.

The operator might be a coast guard, a state-owned company, a navy, a research institute or a commercial ship manager. That distinction decides almost everything about who gets hired, as covered later.

Layout, propulsion and machinery

Icebreakers look short, beamy and heavy for their size, and there are good reasons for that.

  • Hull: thick, heavily framed shell plating with an ice belt around the waterline, and a bow shaped to ride up and break ice by weight rather than cut it. Ice class notations vary. Under IACS Polar Class rules the range runs from PC 1 (most capable) to PC 7, and classification societies also assign dedicated icebreaker notations. Baltic traffic uses the Finnish-Swedish ice classes.
  • Propulsion: modern units are usually diesel-electric, often with azimuthing podded propulsors. These give full torque at low propeller speed and let the ship work astern or sideways to widen a channel. Some cargo ships are "double acting", meaning they run ahead in open water and astern in heavy ice. A few Russian icebreakers are nuclear-powered and are crewed by their state operator.
  • Heeling and trim systems: large pump or tank arrangements that rock the ship from side to side or fore and aft to break free when stuck. Some designs also use air-bubbling systems to reduce friction.
  • Winterisation: heat tracing on deck lines, heated sea chests or ice boxes with warm cooling-water recirculation to stop intakes clogging with slush, heated bridge windows, and enclosed or protected workstations.
  • Towing gear: many icebreakers have a towing winch, and some have a notched stern so an escorted ship's bow can be pulled in close for "close-coupled" towing.

For engineers this means a lot of electrical plant, a power management system that is constantly working, high-load thrusters and drives, and sea water systems that need constant watching for ice ingestion.

Key hazards and how they are managed

The ice is only one of the hazards. On the vessel these are the ones that matter day to day:

  • Ice pressure and besetting: wind and current can close an open lead around the ship. Watch the ice drift and pressure ridges, and keep the heeling system and power ready.
  • Hull and appendage damage: propellers, rudders and pods are vulnerable. Speed in ice is governed by the ice regime and the ship's capability, not the schedule.
  • Escort collisions: the escorted ship can run into the icebreaker's stern if the icebreaker stops suddenly in heavy ice. Distance, speed and communications are set and controlled by the icebreaker. The International Code of Signals contains a dedicated set of signals for icebreakers and assisted vessels.
  • Topside icing: freezing spray and snow build up on decks, rails and masts. This raises the centre of gravity and reduces GM. Ice accretion has to be allowed for in stability, and ice is removed manually or with steam/hot water when necessary.
  • Cold injury: hypothermia, frostbite, cold-water immersion, and slips on iced decks. Dress in layers, use the buddy system and limit how long people work outside.
  • Isolation: search and rescue, medical evacuation and spares may be days away. Medical care on board becomes very important.
  • Light and fatigue: continuous daylight or darkness for weeks at a time upsets sleep. Watch schedules and rest-hour records under MLC and STCW still apply.

Ships certified under SOLAS working in polar waters must carry a Polar Ship Certificate and a Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM). The operational limits in ice are commonly assessed with the IMO-accepted POLARIS methodology.

Certificates and experience usually expected

Your normal CoC, STCW basic training and medical are the foundation. On top of that:

Requirement Who usually needs it
Polar Code Basic Training (STCW Reg. V/4) Master, chief mate and navigational watch officers on ships operating in polar waters (requirement depends on ship type and ice conditions; e.g. not mandatory for cargo ships other than tankers in open waters)
Polar Code Advanced Training (STCW Reg. V/4) Master and chief mate on ships operating in polar waters other than open waters, with approved polar seagoing service (or approved equivalent) required before it is issued
Ice navigation / ice handling courses Often required by the company, even outside polar waters (e.g. Baltic)
Advanced fire fighting, medical first aid / medical care on board According to rank, as normal
DP, towing or anchor handling experience Common for icebreaking offshore and project vessels

Points to note:

  • The Polar Code applies to polar waters as defined (Arctic and Antarctic areas). The Baltic and St Lawrence are not polar waters, but flag states and companies set their own winter navigation requirements there.
  • Government icebreakers that are not engaged in commercial service may be outside SOLAS and the Polar Code. The operator's own rules then apply.
  • Requirements for engineers and ratings are mostly set by the company rather than STCW. Experience with diesel-electric and pod or azimuth propulsion, high voltage training and winterised machinery carries real weight.
  • Check with your administration whether your polar training is endorsed or recognised for the flag concerned. Recognition differs between administrations.

Crew composition and the hiring reality

A typical icebreaker carries a bigger crew than a merchant ship of the same size. There is a full deck and engine officer complement and often an extra ice-experienced master or ice advisor. Electrical staff are usually strong because of the diesel-electric plant. There are deck and engine ratings, a catering department, and sometimes helicopter staff, scientists or project personnel.

You need to be honest about who hires:

  • State and coast guard icebreakers are often crewed by civil servants or nationals of that country, and may require citizenship, language skills and national certificates. Foreign seafarers rarely get these posts.
  • Commercial icebreaking offshore and project vessels and ice-class merchant ships (Arctic tankers, LNG carriers, bulk carriers and general cargo in ice trades) are crewed through ship managers. International crew are much more common on these.
  • Research icebreakers may be run by institutes or by commercial managers on their behalf, so check who the actual employer is.

For most Indian and Filipino officers, the realistic path is ice-class merchant or offshore service first. A move onto a dedicated icebreaker comes later, once the ice experience is in the discharge book.

Life on board

Work is seasonal and intense. The Baltic and Canadian winter seasons, the Arctic summer navigation season and the Antarctic austral summer each have their own rhythm. Out of season, vessels lie up for maintenance or do other work.

What to expect in practice:

  • Constant noise and vibration when breaking ice, including at night. Good earplugs and discipline about rest are essential.
  • Long periods with no port calls and limited connectivity in high latitudes.
  • A lot of outdoor work in cold weather, such as clearing ice, handling tow gear and making mooring arrangements in frozen conditions. Work is planned in short spells.
  • Close teamwork on the bridge. Ice navigation relies on visual lookout, ice radar, ice charts and constant communication with escorted ships. It is very different from open-ocean watchkeeping.
  • Engine rooms where load changes are frequent and sudden, and sea water and heating systems need constant monitoring.

People who do well are calm, patient, and comfortable with isolation and a slower, more deliberate pace of work.

Oral exam corner: questions an examiner may ask

If you have ice experience in your record, expect questions like these at orals or in a company interview:

  1. "You are beset and the pressure is increasing. What do you do?" Inform the master, stop putting power against heavy pressure, and use the heeling and trim systems. Assess drift and look for leads. Report and request icebreaker assistance, check hull and tanks for damage, and keep a record of events.
  2. "How is ice concentration reported?" In tenths of the sea surface covered, using WMO sea ice nomenclature. Ice charts typically use the "egg code".
  3. "What does the PWOM contain?" Ship-specific operational capabilities and limitations in ice, procedures for normal and emergency operations, and risk-management guidance for polar voyages.
  4. "Why is icing a stability problem?" Added weight high up raises KG and reduces GM. It also increases windage and can block freeing ports and vents.
  5. "Escort: who controls speed and distance?" The icebreaker. The escorted ship follows instructions and reports immediately if it slows or gets stuck.

How to get your first icebreaker or ice contract

A realistic step-by-step route:

  1. Complete Polar Code Basic Training at an approved centre. Deck officers aiming for senior rank should plan for Advanced once they have the qualifying polar service.
  2. Target ice-class merchant or offshore vessels with managers whose fleets trade in the Baltic, Arctic or other winter ice areas. Winter Baltic service is a common first step for deck and engine officers.
  3. Build a documented record: get your ice transits, escorts and polar voyages recorded in your sea service testimonials, not just in your memory.
  4. Strengthen relevant skills: diesel-electric, pod and azimuth propulsion, HV training, DP and towing all count on project icebreakers.
  5. Apply to managers of icebreaking offshore and research vessels once you have ice seasons behind you. Be ready to answer technical questions on ice handling and winterisation.
  6. Check flag and recognition: confirm your CoC and polar certificates can be endorsed by the vessel's flag state before you sign.

Ignore anyone offering guaranteed placement on a state icebreaker in exchange for a fee. Those berths are not filled that way.

Key points
  • Icebreakers escort ships, keep channels open, assist beset vessels and support polar projects and research.
  • Many icebreakers are state-owned and crewed by nationals; international crew mostly enter via commercial ice-class and offshore vessels.
  • STCW Regulation V/4 sets Polar Code Basic and Advanced training for masters, chief mates and navigational watch officers in polar waters, depending on ship type and ice conditions.
  • The Baltic and St Lawrence are not polar waters; winter ice requirements there come from flag states and companies.
  • Topside icing raises KG and cuts GM, so ice accretion must be managed and allowed for in stability.
  • Diesel-electric plants, azimuth/pod propulsion and winterised sea water systems are central to engine-side work.
  • Documented ice seasons in your sea service record are the strongest asset for an icebreaker application.

Frequently asked questions

Can Indian or Filipino seafarers work on icebreakers?

Yes, but mostly on commercially managed icebreaking offshore, project and research vessels and on ice-class merchant ships. Many state and coast guard icebreakers recruit only their own nationals or civil servants. Building ice experience on commercial ships first is the usual route.

Is Polar Code training mandatory to work on an icebreaker?

Under STCW Regulation V/4, depending on ship type and ice conditions, masters, chief mates and navigational watch officers on ships in polar waters need Basic training, and masters and chief mates need Advanced training when operating outside open waters. Outside polar waters, or on non-commercial government ships, the requirement comes from the flag state or operator instead. Most ice operators expect it regardless.

Do engineers and ratings need polar certificates?

STCW's polar training requirements are aimed at the deck officers listed above, not engineers or ratings. However, companies often ask engine staff and ratings to complete ice or cold-climate familiarisation. Check the company's SMS and the flag's requirements.

What is the difference between an icebreaker and an ice-class ship?

An ice-class ship has a strengthened hull and machinery to navigate in certain ice conditions, often with icebreaker assistance. An icebreaker is designed to break ice for other ships, carry out escorts and work independently in heavier ice. It usually has a higher ice class or a dedicated icebreaker notation.

What is the PWOM?

The Polar Water Operational Manual is a ship-specific document required by the Polar Code for ships certified under it. It sets out the ship's capabilities and limitations in ice and the procedures for normal and emergency polar operations. Officers are expected to know its contents for their vessel.

Test yourself

Which STCW regulation sets the training requirements for officers on ships operating in polar waters?

Regulation V/4 and Section A-V/4 of the STCW Code cover basic and advanced training for ships operating in polar waters. V/1-1 is for oil and chemical tankers, VI/1 for basic safety training, and II/1 for the OOW CoC.

What is the main stability effect of heavy topside icing?

Ice added high on the structure raises the centre of gravity and reduces the metacentric height. This is why the Polar Code requires ice accretion to be considered in stability.

Under SOLAS Chapter XIV, where does the Polar Code apply?

SOLAS Chapter XIV applies the Polar Code to ships operating in the defined Arctic and Antarctic polar waters. The Baltic is ice-affected but is not polar waters.

During an icebreaker escort, who sets the speed and distance between ships?

The icebreaker controls escort speed and distance, and escorted ships follow its instructions. The International Code of Signals includes dedicated signals for icebreakers and assisted vessels.

What does it mean when a vessel is 'beset'?

Beset means the ship is held fast by surrounding ice and cannot move under its own power. This term is used in WMO sea ice nomenclature.
