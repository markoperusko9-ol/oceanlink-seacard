Working on an LNG Tanker: A Practical Guide for Officers, Ratings and Cadets

Quick answer

LNG carriers move natural gas as a liquid at about -162°C and atmospheric pressure, usually on long-term charters between export and import terminals. Anyone with cargo duties needs STCW basic training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations. Senior officers and those directly responsible for cargo also need advanced training backed by gas tanker sea service. Most people get in through an LNG operator's cadet programme, by crossing over from oil, chemical or LPG tankers, or by joining as a supernumerary.

What an LNG carrier does and where it trades

An LNG carrier moves liquefied natural gas, which is mostly methane, from liquefaction plants to regasification terminals. Cooling the gas to around -162°C shrinks it to roughly 1/600th of its gaseous volume. That is what makes shipping it economic.

The trade is unlike the tramp tanker market:

  • Long-term charters are common. Many ships are built for a specific export project and serve the same few terminals for years.
  • Load ports are large export plants in countries such as Qatar, Australia, the USA, Malaysia and Nigeria. Discharge ports are typically in Japan, Korea, China, India and Europe.
  • Ship sizes for conventional large carriers commonly fall in the 125,000-180,000 m³ range. The Q-Flex and Q-Max classes are larger.
  • Related units include FSRUs (floating storage and regasification units), FSUs, small-scale carriers and LNG bunker vessels. All of them need the same core gas skills.

The construction and equipment of these ships is governed by the IGC Code, which SOLAS Chapter VII makes mandatory for gas carriers.

Layout, containment systems and propulsion

You will meet three main containment types:

Type How to recognise it Key point
Membrane (GTT Mark III or NO96 families) Flush trunk deck, tanks built into the inner hull Thin primary barrier, insulation and a full secondary barrier. Insulation spaces are kept inerted with nitrogen.
Moss spherical (independent Type B) Large domes above deck Self-supporting tanks with a partial secondary barrier
SPB (independent prismatic Type B) Flush deck, prismatic tanks Self-supporting, fewer in service

Small-scale and bunker ships often use independent Type C pressure tanks.

Cargo is handled by submerged electric cargo pumps inside the tanks. These are supported by spray pumps and stripping arrangements. A cargo compressor room and heaters/vaporisers sit on deck. The engine room is supplied with gas through a fuel gas system. There is no pumproom of the oil tanker kind.

Some heat always leaks through the insulation, so some cargo boils off. This boil-off gas (BOG) is used as fuel. Propulsion types you are likely to see:

  • Steam turbine (older fleet): BOG and fuel oil burnt in boilers.
  • Dual-fuel diesel-electric (DFDE/TFDE): medium-speed dual-fuel generators feeding high-voltage electric propulsion.
  • Two-stroke gas engines: high-pressure (ME-GI type) or low-pressure (X-DF type).

Excess BOG goes to a reliquefaction plant if one is fitted, or is burnt in a gas combustion unit (GCU). Engineers should expect high-voltage switchboards on electric-propulsion ships.

The cargo cycle, step by step

A typical cycle on a ship coming out of dry dock runs as follows. Exact procedures always follow the ship's cargo operations manual and company SMS.

  1. Drying. Dry air is circulated to remove moisture, which would otherwise freeze in the system.
  2. Inerting. Inert gas or nitrogen is used to bring oxygen below the safe limit set in the procedures.
  3. Gassing-up. Warm natural gas vapour, supplied from shore or produced on board by vaporising LNG supplied from shore, displaces the inert gas. This is needed because the CO₂ in inert gas would freeze at cargo temperature.
  4. Cool-down. LNG is sprayed into the tanks through spray lines at a controlled rate until tank temperatures reach loading condition. Rushing this risks thermal stress.
  5. Loading. This is done with a vapour return to shore and the ship-shore ESD link connected and tested. Filling limits are checked against the IGC Code and the ship's loading limit tables.
  6. Laden voyage. Tank pressure and BOG use are managed, and cargo temperature and level are monitored.
  7. Discharge. Vapour is returned from shore, or generated on board, to keep tank pressure up. Custody transfer measurements are taken before and after.
  8. Ballast voyage. A heel of LNG is usually kept to hold the tanks cold for the next loading and to supply fuel.

Before dry dock the cycle runs in reverse: stripping, warm-up, inerting, and then aeration so the tanks are safe for entry. Membrane ships also have filling restrictions to limit sloshing damage. Know your ship's permitted fill bands.

Key hazards and how they are controlled

  • Cryogenic burns and brittle fracture. LNG at about -162°C freezes skin instantly. It also makes ordinary hull steel brittle enough to crack. Controls include cryogenic PPE, stainless-steel drip trays and a water curtain running along the ship's side during manifold operations.
  • Flammability. Methane burns in air between roughly 5% and 15% by volume. Cold vapour stays low and spreads along the deck until it warms and becomes lighter than air. Fixed gas detection, ignition control and hot work permits are essential.
  • Asphyxiation. Nitrogen and inert gas kill without warning. Insulation spaces, the compressor room and tanks must be treated as enclosed spaces. Test the atmosphere, ventilate and work under a permit.
  • Rapid phase transition. LNG spilled onto water can flash to vapour violently. This is a physical explosion with no combustion, and is another reason spills over the side must be prevented.
  • Overpressure. A blocked vapour path, a failed BOG consumer or delayed discharge can raise tank pressure. Pressure relief valves are the last line of defence and must never be gagged.
  • High voltage. Electric-propulsion ships run HV switchboards and motors. Only authorised, trained persons should work on them, under a lock-out/tag-out procedure.
  • ESD. The emergency shutdown system closes the manifold valves and stops the pumps. Trips are tested before every cargo operation. Know your ship's ESD stages and what triggers each one.

Certificates and experience usually expected

STCW requirements (Regulation V/1-2):

  • Basic training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations is required for officers and ratings with specific cargo or cargo equipment duties. The standard is in STCW Code Section A-V/1-2-1.
  • Advanced training for liquefied gas tanker cargo operations is required for the master, chief engineer, chief mate, second engineer and anyone with immediate responsibility for loading, discharging, care in transit, handling of cargo, tank cleaning or other cargo-related operations. Candidates must already hold the basic liquefied gas tanker certificate. The standard is in Section A-V/1-2-2.
  • To qualify for the advanced certificate, STCW requires approved gas tanker sea service. This means either at least three months on liquefied gas tankers, or at least one month of supervised supernumerary onboard training that includes at least three loading and three discharging operations.

How the certificate is issued depends on your administration. India issues a Dangerous Cargo Endorsement, while the Philippines (MARINA) issues certificates of proficiency. Your flag state may also want its own endorsement. Check the current rules with your administration rather than relying on another seafarer's experience.

Other training employers commonly ask for:

  • High voltage training, especially for engineers and ETOs on electric-propulsion ships.
  • Type-specific courses, such as membrane or Moss systems, dual-fuel engines and cargo simulator courses.
  • In-house familiarisation programmes.

Also note:

  • IGF Code training is not normally the requirement here. The IGF Code does not apply to gas carriers; their use of cargo as fuel falls under the IGC Code.
  • Experience matrices. Charterers and terminals often check officer experience on type and time with the company, so time on gas ships carries real weight.

Crew composition and life on board

Typical crew

  • Deck: master, chief officer, second and third officers. Some companies also carry junior officers and cadets.
  • Engine: chief engineer, second engineer, third and fourth engineers, one ETO, and on some electric-propulsion ships a second ETO or electrical assistant, plus fitter, motormen and wipers. Some companies also carry a gas or cargo engineer.
  • Ratings and catering: bosun, ABs, OS, cook and stewards.

There is no pumpman role as on crude carriers. Exact manning follows the flag's minimum safe manning document and company policy.

What life is like

  • Procedure-heavy. Expect detailed checklists, frequent drills, cargo meetings and close attention from vetting inspections and terminals.
  • Clean and well maintained. Engineers spend a lot of time on gas handling, the fuel gas system, HV equipment and automation.
  • Short port stays at remote jetties. Shore leave is limited, and terminal security rules can restrict it further.
  • Long voyages on steady routes. Watch patterns become routine, and rest hour discipline under MLC and STCW really matters during cargo days.
  • Stable careers. Companies with long charters tend to keep crews and promote from within.

How to get your first LNG contract

Routes in

  1. Cadet programmes. Several LNG operators and their manning agents run their own cadetships. Applying to these as a pre-sea cadet is the most direct route.
  2. Crossing over from tankers. Oil, chemical and especially LPG experience shows you understand cargo discipline. Complete the gas basic training before applying.
  3. Supernumerary or trainee berth. Some companies place experienced officers on board in a supernumerary capacity. This builds gas service and can count towards the advanced certificate if properly documented.
  4. Engineers and ETOs. HV and dual-fuel engine experience from other ship types, such as cruise ships, ferries or offshore vessels, is a strong selling point.

Tips

  • Keep your training record book and sea service letters in order.
  • Use only recognised, licensed manning agencies.
  • Expect a technical interview, and be ready to explain the cargo cycle above in your own words.

Oral exam style questions you should be able to answer

  • Why do we rig a water curtain during cargo operations? To protect the hull steel from brittle fracture if LNG leaks at the manifold.
  • Why can't we gas-up directly from inert gas to LNG liquid? The CO₂ and moisture in inert gas would freeze. Warm vapour must first displace the inert gas.
  • Why keep a heel on the ballast passage? To keep the tanks cold so they are ready to load, and to provide boil-off for fuel.
  • Tank pressure is rising on passage. What do you check? BOG consumers and the GCU, the reliquefaction plant, the vapour line-up, cargo temperatures, and the trend against the relief valve settings.
  • What does the ship-shore ESD link do? It allows either side to stop transfer safely by closing the valves and stopping the pumps.
Key points
  • LNG is carried at about -162°C near atmospheric pressure; boil-off gas is normally used as fuel.
  • Main containment types are membrane, Moss spherical and SPB, with Type C tanks on small-scale ships.
  • STCW Regulation V/1-2 requires gas tanker basic training for cargo duties and advanced training for senior and cargo-responsible officers.
  • Advanced training needs documented gas tanker sea service: three months, or one month supernumerary with three loadings and three discharges.
  • The main hazards are cryogenic burns, brittle fracture, flammable vapour, asphyxiation from nitrogen or inert gas, and high voltage.
  • The IGC Code governs LNG carriers; IGF Code training is generally not the requirement on gas carriers.
  • Common entry routes are operator cadet programmes, crossing over from tankers, and supernumerary berths.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join an LNG carrier with only an oil tanker endorsement?

Not for duties involving cargo. STCW treats gas tankers separately, so you need the liquefied gas tanker basic training certificate. Advanced gas certification is needed for senior and cargo-responsible roles. Oil tanker experience still helps your application.

How do I get the sea service for the advanced gas certificate if I have never sailed on gas?

STCW allows either at least three months of approved service on liquefied gas tankers or at least one month of approved supernumerary onboard training. The supernumerary route must include at least three loading and three discharging operations recorded in an approved training record. Many people get this through a company that places them on board after basic training.

Do I need IGF Code training to work on an LNG carrier?

Generally no. The IGF Code applies to ships using gas or other low-flashpoint fuels that are not gas carriers. LNG carriers fall under the IGC Code, so gas tanker training is the relevant requirement, although a company may ask for extra courses of its own.

Is LPG experience useful for getting on LNG ships?

Yes. LPG and LNG are both covered by the same STCW liquefied gas tanker training. Gas service on LPG ships counts towards the experience operators look for, but you will still need familiarisation on LNG cryogenics and containment systems.

Why do engineers on LNG ships need high voltage training?

Many LNG carriers use diesel-electric propulsion with high-voltage generators, switchboards and motors. STCW includes HV competence for engineers at management level and for ETOs. Companies usually expect HV training before you join such a ship.

Test yourself

Why is a water curtain run along the ship's side during LNG cargo operations?

Ordinary hull steel becomes brittle at cryogenic temperatures, and the water curtain protects it from leaked LNG. Reference: IGC Code provisions on protection of the hull from cryogenic spills.

Which STCW regulation sets the training requirements for officers and ratings on liquefied gas tankers?

Regulation V/1-2 covers liquefied gas tankers. V/1-1 covers oil and chemical tankers and VI/1 covers basic safety training. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation V/1-2.

Why is warm natural gas vapour used to displace inert gas before cool-down?

The CO₂ and water vapour in inert gas would freeze in the tanks and lines. Gassing-up with warm vapour removes them first. Reference: IGC Code and ship cargo operations manual.

Which code governs the construction and equipment of LNG carriers?

The IGC Code applies to ships carrying liquefied gases in bulk and is made mandatory by SOLAS Chapter VII. The IGF Code covers other ships using gas as fuel. Reference: SOLAS Chapter VII Part C; IGC Code.

What is the main purpose of keeping a heel of LNG on board during the ballast voyage?

The heel keeps the tanks near cargo temperature, avoiding a full cool-down before loading, and its boil-off supplies fuel. Reference: ship cargo operations manual under the IGC Code.
