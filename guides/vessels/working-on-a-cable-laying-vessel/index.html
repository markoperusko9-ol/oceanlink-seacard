Working on a Cable Laying Vessel

Quick answer

Cable laying vessels lay, bury and repair subsea telecom and power cables, usually as DP ships with a large mixed crew of marine staff and project personnel. Owners usually want DP experience on the bridge, high-voltage diesel-electric experience in the engine room, and offshore-style deck discipline. Most people join after time on OSVs, AHTS or other DP tonnage, not straight from deep-sea trades.

What a cable laying vessel does

A cable layer loads hundreds or thousands of tonnes of subsea cable and pays it out along a surveyed route at slow, controlled speed. The work falls into three main trades:

  • Telecom cables. These are long fibre-optic systems crossing oceans or linking islands. They involve long lay campaigns, plus repair ships kept on standby under maintenance agreements. When a cable is damaged, the repair ship sails, recovers the ends, joins in new cable and relays it.
  • Power cables. These include interconnectors between countries, export cables from offshore wind farms to shore, and array cables between turbines. The cables are heavy, often high-voltage AC or DC, and are frequently buried by plough, jetting tool or trenching ROV.
  • Oil and gas umbilicals and cables. Some vessels overlap with the construction and pipelay sector.

At sea, the ship's job is to hold position or track very precisely while cable leaves the stern under controlled tension. One poor manoeuvre can damage a cable worth far more than a typical cargo.

Typical layout and equipment

No two cable ships are identical. Most share these features:

  • Cable tanks or carousels. These are large circular storage spaces below or on deck. Some are static tanks where cable is coiled by hand around a central cone. Power cable ships often have rotating turntables (carousels).
  • Cable engines. These are linear (tracked) engines or drum engines that grip the cable and control its tension during lay and recovery.
  • Cable path and overboarding. A route along the deck through guides and sheaves to a stern chute, sheave or overboarding wheel. Some vessels also have bow sheaves for repair work.
  • Burial equipment. Ploughs, jetting tools or trenching ROVs, launched by A-frame or crane.
  • Jointing facilities. Clean rooms or containers where specialist jointers splice cable sections or make repairs.
  • DP and propulsion. Most modern cable ships are diesel-electric. They have azimuth and tunnel thrusters and a DP system, commonly DP2 class and sometimes DP3. High-voltage switchboards are common.
  • Accommodation. It is sized well beyond the marine crew, because project personnel live on board.

Many cable ships carry more than twelve special personnel. Depending on the flag, they may be certified under the Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships (SPS Code) rather than as ordinary cargo ships. Check the ship's certificates when you join.

Operations and key hazards

Under COLREG Rule 3, a vessel laying, servicing or picking up a submarine cable is a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre (RAM). The bridge must:

  • show Rule 27(b) lights or shapes;
  • sound the Rule 35 signal in restricted visibility;
  • expect, but never rely on, other vessels keeping out of the way under Rule 18 (which applies only when vessels are in sight of one another).

Being RAM does not remove Rule 2 or Rule 8 responsibilities.

Main hazards:

  • Cable under tension. Snap-back if a stopper, grip or rigging fails. Bights forming on deck. Nip points at cable engines, sheaves and the chute. Exclusion zones and clear communication are non-negotiable.
  • Cable tank work. Falls into deep tanks and crush risk while coiling. Some companies treat tanks as enclosed spaces in certain conditions. Follow your company's permit system.
  • DP failure. A drive-off or drift-off with cable connected can overload or damage the cable and the equipment. Know the DP alarm philosophy and the agreed response for each operation.
  • Launch and recovery. Ploughs and ROVs are recovered over the stern in a seaway.
  • Shore-end landings. Cable is floated ashore with small boats and divers in shallow water, with heavy small-boat traffic.
  • Third-party traffic. Fishing vessels, guard vessels and other contractors working nearby (SIMOPS).
  • Unexploded ordnance. In some sea areas, route surveys deal with UXO. Follow project procedures.
  • Electrical hazards. HV plant on board, and on power cable jobs the test and termination work handled by specialists.

Worked scenario. You are on watch during a power cable lay at slow speed, showing RAM signals. A fishing vessel trawling is closing on the port bow. Under Rule 18, a vessel engaged in fishing must, so far as possible, keep out of the way of a RAM vessel. You cannot rely on that. Take these steps:

  1. Confirm risk of collision by radar/ARPA and compass bearing.
  2. Call the fishing vessel by VHF using AIS details, and use the guard vessel if one is assigned.
  3. Inform the Master and the project lead early.
  4. Agree the contingency in advance: whether you can stop the lay, hold position on DP, or alter the route within cable limits.
  5. If close quarters develops, take whatever action best avoids collision, then deal with the cable consequences.

The ship comes before the cable.

Certificates and experience usually expected

On top of your normal STCW certificate of competency, basic safety training and medical, owners commonly look for the following. Exact requirements depend on the company, the client and the flag.

Role Commonly expected
Master, Chief Officer, DPOs DP certificate under a recognised scheme (the Nautical Institute scheme is widely used); offshore DP watchkeeping experience
Engineers Diesel-electric and HV experience; high-voltage training; DP vessel engine-room familiarity
ETO STCW ETO competency; strong HV, drives and PMS experience
ABs, Bosun, crane operators Offshore deck work, rigging, crane experience; company or client crane qualifications where required
All BOSIET/HUET only where the project uses helicopter transfers or the client demands it

DP sea-time and course rules are set by the certifying body and they change. Check the current scheme requirements directly. Do not rely on what someone told you years ago.

Clients in the offshore wind and oil and gas sectors may also require their own inductions and medicals. The company usually arranges these.

Typical crew composition

Expect two groups on board, whose relative sizes vary with the vessel and the project.

Marine crew - Bridge: Master, Chief Officer and DP-qualified watchkeepers (senior DPOs and DPOs). 2nd and 3rd Officers are often DP trainees or DPOs. - Engine: Chief Engineer, 2nd Engineer and further engineers, plus one or more ETOs. On cable ships, electrical staff are central to the operation. - Deck: Bosun, ABs, crane operators, sometimes riggers and fitters. - Catering: Cooks, stewards and messmen, enough to feed a large complement.

Project and client personnel - Offshore or project manager and client representatives - Cable engineers and cable hands who work the tanks and cable engines - Jointers - ROV and trenching supervisors and pilots - Survey and positioning staff

The Master keeps overriding authority for safety of the ship. The project team runs the cable work. Daily meetings align the two groups.

Life on board

This is closer to offshore life than to a liner or tramp trade:

  • Shift work. It is often 12 hours on and 12 off during operations, rather than traditional 4-on-8-off watches. Rest hours still apply under MLC 2006 and STCW, and must be recorded honestly.
  • Long periods on location. Port calls are mainly for cable loading, mobilisation and crew change. Repair ships may sit in port or at anchor on standby for weeks, then sail at short notice.
  • Paperwork and meetings. Expect toolbox talks, permits, risk assessments, client audits and frequent drills. The safety culture is generally strict.
  • A busy accommodation. There are many nationalities and trades, and cabins may be shared depending on the ship.
  • Weather windows. Work stops and starts with the sea state. Waiting on weather is normal.
  • Rotations. Lengths vary by company and contract. Offshore-style equal-time rotations exist, but are not universal.

The work is varied. If you like seamanship, DP, rigging or electrical systems, you will learn more here than on most cargo ships.

How to get your first contract

Few people step straight from a bulk carrier to a cable ship. Common routes are:

  1. Build DP or offshore experience first. Time on OSVs, PSVs, AHTS or construction vessels makes you credible. For deck officers, starting the DP certification process is usually the key step.
  2. Electrical officers and engineers: gain HV and diesel-electric experience. Passenger ships, OSVs and LNG carriers with electric propulsion all help.
  3. Ratings: offshore deck work, crane and rigging experience and a clean safety record count heavily.
  4. Target the right employers. A limited number of companies own or manage cable ships, and several crew them through manning agencies. Apply directly and through reputable licensed agencies. Treat any agency asking for 'placement fees' with suspicion. MLC 2006 restricts charging seafarers for recruitment.
  5. Write a CV for the role. List DP hours and class, thruster types, HV voltages, cranes and A-frames operated, and offshore projects.
  6. Be flexible on your first job. A junior post, or a repair ship on standby, is a realistic way in.

Oral exam corner

Examiners like cable ships because they test several COLREG rules at once.

  • Q: Lights for a cable layer making way at night? A: Three all-round lights in a vertical line, red-white-red, plus masthead light(s), sidelights and sternlight (Rule 27(b)).
  • Q: Day shape? A: Ball-diamond-ball in a vertical line.
  • Q: At anchor while working? A: As RAM, she shows the Rule 27(b) signals plus the anchor light(s) or ball of Rule 30.
  • Q: Fog signal? A: One prolonged blast followed by two short, at intervals of not more than two minutes (Rule 35(c)).
  • Q: Does RAM status mean you never manoeuvre? A: No. Rules 2 and 8 still apply, and good seamanship may require action.
  • Q: Difference between DP class 2 and 3? A: Class 2 should not lose position from a single fault in an active component or system. Class 3 also covers loss of a compartment to fire or flood.
Key points
  • Cable layers lay, bury and repair subsea telecom and power cables, usually on DP.
  • While cable working they are RAM under COLREG Rule 3 and show Rule 27(b) signals.
  • The biggest deck hazards are cable under tension, cable tanks, and launch and recovery of burial tools.
  • DP certification, HV experience and offshore deck experience are the usual extra expectations.
  • Crew is mixed: marine staff plus a project team, with the Master keeping safety authority.
  • Offshore experience on OSVs, AHTS or other DP vessels is the most common route in.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a DP certificate to work on a cable laying vessel?

Usually yes for DP watchkeeping officers and Masters, because most cable ships operate on DP. Engineers, ETOs and ratings do not need one, though DP vessel experience helps. Requirements depend on the company and client.

Can a cadet or junior officer join a cable ship?

Some operators take cadets and DP trainees, but places are limited. A junior officer post or DP trainee role on a cable ship or other DP vessel is a realistic first step. Your certificate and the flag's manning requirements decide which post you can fill.

What COLREG signals does a cable laying vessel show?

When restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, she shows three all-round lights red-white-red, or by day the shapes ball-diamond-ball (Rule 27(b)). Making way, she adds the normal underway lights. In restricted visibility she sounds one prolonged blast followed by two short.

Is BOSIET/HUET required for cable ships?

Not automatically. It is required where crew change or transfers are by helicopter, or where the client, often an oil, gas or wind farm operator, demands it. Many cable ships change crew in port, so check the specific contract.

Why are ETOs so important on cable ships?

Most cable ships are diesel-electric with HV switchboards, variable-speed drives, thrusters and complex cable machinery. Electrical faults directly affect DP and cable handling. Strong HV and drives experience is therefore highly valued.

Test yourself

At night, a cable laying vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre and making way shows which lights?

A RAM vessel making way shows red-white-red all round plus masthead, side and stern lights. Two reds is NUC, and three reds is the Rule 28 constrained-by-draught signal. Reference: COLREG Rule 27(b)(i) and (iii).

What day shapes does a cable laying vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre show?

Ball-diamond-ball indicates RAM. Two balls is NUC, a cylinder is constrained by draught, and a single diamond is a tow exceeding 200 m. Reference: COLREG Rule 27(b)(ii).

In restricted visibility, what sound signal does a cable layer at work (RAM) give?

RAM vessels use one prolonged followed by two short, whether making way or not. The other options are for power-driven vessels making way, power-driven vessels stopped, and vessels at anchor. Reference: COLREG Rule 35(c).

A power-driven vessel crossing from the cable layer's starboard side meets the cable layer, which is showing RAM signals. Who must keep out of the way?

Rule 18(a)(ii) requires a power-driven vessel underway to keep out of the way of a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre, overriding the Rule 15 crossing rule (subject to Rules 9, 10 and 13). The RAM vessel is still bound by Rules 2, 8 and 17. Reference: COLREG Rule 18(a)(ii).

What distinguishes DP equipment class 3 from class 2?

Class 2 covers single faults in active components or systems. Class 3 extends this to fire or flooding of any one compartment. Reference: IMO MSC.1/Circ.1580, Guidelines for vessels and units with dynamic positioning systems.
