Working on a Salvage Vessel: A Seafarer's Guide

Quick answer

Salvage vessels respond to ships in trouble. They tow disabled ships, fight fires, refloat groundings, control pollution and support wreck removal. Most crews come from tug and anchor-handling backgrounds, and companies expect strong towing, firefighting and seamanship experience on top of your STCW certificates. The work is irregular and demanding, and it is never routine.

What a salvage vessel actually does

A salvage vessel exists to help ships, cargo and the environment when something has gone wrong. The term covers several types:

  • Salvage tugs and emergency towing vessels (ETVs). These are high bollard-pull tugs kept on station or on short notice to reach a disabled ship and take it in tow.
  • Salvage support vessels. These carry salvage gear, workshops, compressors, pumps and dive spreads to a casualty.
  • Multi-role offshore units. These are anchor handlers and similar vessels chartered for a specific salvage or wreck-removal job.

Typical jobs include:

  • Emergency towing of ships that have lost propulsion or steering.
  • External firefighting.
  • Refloating grounded ships.
  • Patching and pumping out flooded compartments.
  • Removing bunkers and cargo from casualties.
  • Long-term wreck removal projects.

The legal framework sits mainly in the International Convention on Salvage, 1989. It keeps the traditional idea that a salvage reward depends on a useful result. It also places a duty on salvors to act with due care, including care to prevent or minimise environmental damage. It adds special compensation (Article 14), covering at least the salvor's expenses, for operations on a ship or cargo that threatened environmental damage where the normal reward would not cover those expenses. An increase is available if the salvor actually prevented or minimised that damage. Commercial contracts such as standard open forms sit on top of the Convention. As crew, you will hear these discussed, but the master and the salvage master handle them.

Layout and key equipment

Salvage tugs look like oversized harbour or ocean tugs: a high, forward accommodation block and a long, clear working deck aft. Expect to see:

  • Towing winch(es) with large drums of wire, often a main and a secondary drum, plus stretchers and spare towlines.
  • Stern roller, towing pins and a shark jaw or similar stopper. These are used for handling heavy chain and wire.
  • A gob (gog) rope arrangement that holds the towline down aft and moves the effective towing point aft, reducing the risk of girting, plus an emergency release on the winch or towing hook.
  • External firefighting monitors and pumps. Many vessels hold a class notation for fire-fighting capability. The level and equipment depend on the classification society's rules.
  • Salvage stores: portable pumps, generators, compressors, hydraulic power packs, cutting and welding gear, timber, patching materials, oil boom and skimmers.
  • Deck crane or A-frame. Some vessels also carry a workboat, a dive station or a ROV.

The engine room is built for sustained high power and redundancy. Propulsion is commonly twin screw, often controllable pitch or azimuthing, with bow thrusters. Engineers spend a lot of time keeping portable salvage machinery ready alongside the ship's own plant.

Operations and key hazards

Salvage combines the hazards of towing, firefighting, heavy lifting, diving and enclosed spaces, often in bad weather and with a damaged ship. The main ones:

  • Girting (girding). The towline leads abeam and the tow pulls the tug over. A tug can capsize in seconds. Prevention comes from:
    • correct use of the gob rope or towing pins
    • keeping the tow astern
    • a working emergency release
    • a bridge team that knows when to slip the line
  • Snap-back. A parting wire or synthetic line can recoil with lethal force. Stay out of snap-back zones and keep the after deck clear when tension comes on.
  • Connecting to a casualty. You may be passing a messenger by line-throwing apparatus or approaching a rolling, drifting hull. You may also be boarding by ladder, workboat or helicopter.
    • Tankers of 20,000 dwt and above must carry emergency towing arrangements at both ends (SOLAS II-1/3-4.1).
    • Passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and above must carry ship-specific emergency towing procedures (SOLAS II-1/3-4.2).
    • Even so, the arrangement may be damaged or the crew may have abandoned ship.
  • The casualty itself. Dangers on board include:
    • unstable stability and progressive flooding
    • fire re-ignition
    • toxic or flammable atmospheres
    • shifted cargo
    • dangerous goods

Treat every space as an enclosed space until tested. Gas detection, permits and the company procedures for hot work and entry apply exactly as on your own ship. - Pollution response. Bunker and cargo removal carry fire, toxic vapour and spill risks. - Fatigue. Jobs run around the clock and crew levels are finite.

Under COLREG, a vessel towing shows the towing lights and shapes of Rule 24. If the towing operation severely restricts the ability of the tug and her tow to deviate from their course, she also shows the restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre signals under Rule 27. A disabled casualty may herself be showing not-under-command signals.

Certificates and experience usually expected

There is no single international salvage certificate for ship's crew. You need the normal STCW certificates for your rank, plus what the company and flag require. Commonly expected:

Requirement Why it matters
CoC appropriate to rank, with flag state endorsement where needed Legal basis to serve
STCW basic training (PST, FPFF, EFA, PSSR) Mandatory for everyone
Advanced Fire Fighting Officers lead firefighting on casualties
PSCRB and, where a fast rescue boat is carried, Fast Rescue Boat training Workboat and rescue operations
Medical first aid / medical care on board, by rank Injuries far from shore
GMDSS certificate for deck officers Distress and coordination traffic
Valid seafarer medical certificate Physically demanding work

Other training often looked for, depending on company and trade:

  • tanker basic training
  • ship security courses
  • DP
  • offshore survival and helicopter escape (BOSIET/HUET) if the vessel works offshore

Divers need commercial diving qualifications, which sit outside STCW and are set by national and industry schemes.

Requirements differ between flag states and between companies. Check the specific vessel's crewing requirements before you apply.

Experience counts more than paper here. Companies look for:

  • towing and anchor-handling sea time
  • confident deck work with heavy wire and chain
  • engineers who can repair anything with what is on board

Typical crew composition

Crew size varies widely with vessel size and role. A typical salvage tug or support vessel carries:

  • Deck:
    • master and chief officer, both usually with extensive towing experience
    • one or two further deck officers
    • a bosun
    • several ABs who are expert in wire, chain and rigging
  • Engine:
    • chief engineer and second engineer
    • often a further engineer
    • an ETO on larger units
    • motormen or oilers, often a fitter or welder
  • Catering: cook and sometimes a steward or messman.

For a salvage job, a salvage team usually joins. It may include:

  • a salvage master
  • salvage engineers
  • divers
  • riggers
  • specialists such as naval architects, often working from ashore

The salvage master directs the salvage operation. The ship's master remains responsible for the safety of his own vessel and crew. Clear communication between the two is essential.

Life on board

Salvage life swings between long waits and intense bursts of work:

  • On station. Days or weeks of standby in port or at sea. Time goes on maintenance, drills, gear checks and keeping towlines and pumps ready to go at short notice.
  • Mobilisation. You may sail within a short time of a call. Stores and specialist gear are loaded fast, and plans are made on passage.
  • On the job. Long hours, bad weather, heavy physical work, and often boarding a damaged ship in uncomfortable conditions.

Rest hour rules still apply: the STCW and MLC minimums and the requirement to record them. Both instruments allow the master to suspend the schedule when it is needed for the immediate safety of the ship, persons or cargo, or to assist other ships or persons in distress. Once the situation is normal, seafarers who worked during scheduled rest must get adequate compensatory rest. A professional company plans watches so this does not become a permanent state of exhaustion.

Contract lengths, rotation and leave depend on the company and the flag. The crews are small and close, and the work is varied.

How to get a first contract

Salvage is a small, specialist sector. Few companies operate dedicated salvage vessels, and they rarely take people without relevant experience. A practical route:

  1. Build towing experience first. Harbour tugs, ocean towage and AHTS work teach the wire handling, girting awareness and ship handling that salvage needs.
  2. Get strong firefighting and rescue credentials. Advanced Fire Fighting, rescue boat training and good drill experience all count. Officers should be comfortable leading a team.
  3. Develop practical skills. For engineers and fitters this means welding, cutting, portable pump and generator maintenance, and hydraulics. For deck ratings it means rigging, splicing and heavy lifting.
  4. Target the right employers. Apply to towage and salvage operators, emergency towing contractors and offshore companies that take salvage work. Manning agents that crew offshore tonnage are a better bet than container or bulk agencies.
  5. Present your CV properly. Show your sea service on each vessel type, any towing or casualty jobs, and your equipment experience.
  6. Accept a junior or relief slot. A first contract as AB, junior engineer or junior officer on a tug in a salvage fleet is a normal way in.

Cadets occasionally train on large tugs. Most people enter salvage after several years at sea elsewhere.

Oral exam style questions you should be able to answer

Examiners and towage masters like to test these points:

  • What is girting and how do you prevent it?
    • The tow pulls the towline abeam and capsizes the tug.
    • Prevent it by keeping the tow astern and using the gob rope or towing pins.
    • Know the emergency release and use it without hesitation.
  • What lights does a tug show when the towing operation severely restricts her ability to deviate?
    • The Rule 24 towing lights, plus the Rule 27 restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre lights.
  • What must be done before entering a space on a casualty?
    • Assess and test the atmosphere and ventilate.
    • Issue a permit with a standby person and rescue arrangements.
    • Treat the space as an enclosed space regardless of how it looks.
  • Who is responsible for your vessel when a salvage master is embarked?
    • Your master remains responsible for the vessel and her crew.
  • Why must salvors take care of the environment?
    • The Salvage Convention places a duty of due care on salvors to prevent or minimise environmental damage.
    • It also recognises that effort in the reward and special compensation provisions.
Key points
  • Salvage vessels tow disabled ships, fight fires, refloat groundings, control pollution and support wreck removal.
  • Girting and snap-back are the classic killers on salvage tugs; know your gob rope and emergency release.
  • There is no single salvage certificate for crew; you need your STCW certificates plus what the company and flag require.
  • Towing and anchor-handling experience is the usual entry route; direct entry from deep-sea trades is rare.
  • The salvage master directs the operation, but your master stays responsible for your vessel and crew.
  • Rest hour rules still apply, with suspension allowed only for genuine emergencies and compensatory rest afterwards.
  • The International Convention on Salvage 1989 underpins rewards, special compensation and the salvor's environmental duty.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join a salvage vessel straight from a bulk carrier or container ship?

It is possible but uncommon. Salvage operators usually want towing, anchor-handling or heavy deck-work experience first. Most people get there via tugs or AHTS, so a contract on that type is the practical stepping stone.

Do I need a special salvage certificate to work on a salvage tug?

Ship's crew do not need a separate international salvage certificate. You need a CoC and STCW certificates appropriate to your rank, plus any training the company or flag requires. Advanced Fire Fighting and rescue boat training are commonly expected. Divers need separate commercial diving qualifications.

Who is in charge during a salvage operation?

The salvage master directs the salvage work on behalf of the salvor. The master of each vessel remains responsible for the safety of his own ship and crew. Good practice is a clear agreed plan and constant communication between them.

Are rest hours ignored during salvage jobs?

No. STCW and MLC rest hour minimums still apply and must be recorded. The master may suspend the schedule for the immediate safety of ship, persons or cargo, or to assist others in distress. Compensatory rest must follow once the situation is under control.

What is the difference between a salvage tug and an ordinary harbour tug?

A salvage tug is built for ocean work. It has much greater endurance and seakeeping, large towing winches and spare towlines. It also carries external firefighting equipment and salvage stores such as pumps, generators and pollution gear, and it can stay at sea for long periods with a casualty.

Test yourself

Under the International Convention on Salvage 1989, which article provides special compensation where the salvor has dealt with a threat of environmental damage?

Article 14 provides special compensation, while Article 13 sets the criteria for the normal reward and Article 8 the duties of salvor and owner. Reference: International Convention on Salvage 1989, Articles 8, 13 and 14.

What is 'girting' (girding) on a tug?

Girting is when the towline pull acts abeam and heels the tug until she capsizes; it is prevented by controlling the towline lead and using the emergency release. Reference: established towing practice.

A tug's towing operation severely restricts her and her tow's ability to deviate from their course. What lights must she show?

Rule 27(c) requires the restricted-in-ability-to-manoeuvre lights or shapes in addition to the Rule 24 towing signals. Reference: COLREG 1972 as amended, Rules 24 and 27(c).

During a salvage operation the master suspends the rest hour schedule. What must follow?

The master may suspend the schedule for emergencies, but seafarers who worked in scheduled rest must receive adequate compensatory rest afterwards. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/1; MLC 2006, Standard A2.3.

What is the main purpose of the gob (gog) rope on a towing vessel?

The gob rope moves the effective towing point aft and holds the towline down, reducing the heeling lever if the tow sheers. Reference: established towing practice.
