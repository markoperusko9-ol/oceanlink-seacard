Working on a Training Ship

Quick answer

A training ship carries trainees so that they can complete the approved seagoing service and onboard training their administration requires for a certificate. The ship may be an academy vessel, a trading ship with extra cadet berths, or a sail training vessel. For officers and ratings the seamanship is familiar, but the job adds supervising, teaching and assessing large numbers of inexperienced people, so selection favours steady, well-documented officers.

What a training ship is and the main types

"Training ship" covers several quite different vessels. Before you apply anywhere, find out which kind you are dealing with.

  • Dedicated institutional training ships are owned or operated by a maritime academy, government or training trust. Their main purpose is training. They may make long training cruises, port visits and short coastal passages, and many carry no commercial cargo.
  • Trading ships with training berths are ordinary cargo ships, often bulk carriers, tankers or container ships. They have extra cabins or a cadet deck so a company can put a large group of its own cadets on board at once. The ship trades normally, and training runs alongside the cargo work.
  • Sail training vessels are tall ships and smaller sailing vessels. They train cadets and sometimes members of the public in traditional seamanship, and much of the work happens aloft and on deck.

In IMO terms, trainees are normally treated as special personnel, meaning neither crew nor passengers. Many such ships are dealt with under the Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships. The Code is aimed at ships of 500 gross tonnage and above carrying more than 12 special personnel, though flag states may apply it more widely. How that code is applied, and which requirements scale with the number of trainees carried, is decided by the flag state, so check the ship's certificates rather than assuming.

Layout, trade and daily operations

A dedicated training ship looks more like a small passenger ship than a cargo ship inside. It typically has:

  • multi-berth trainee messdecks or cabins
  • classrooms
  • a training bridge or a second navigation position
  • workshops and sometimes an engine-room simulator
  • a larger galley, laundry and sanitary system
  • a hospital with more capacity than a cargo ship would have

Trading ships with training berths keep their normal cargo layout and add accommodation and a study area.

Typical operations include:

  • watchkeeping with trainees understudying the officer or rating on watch
  • structured lessons and practical sessions, such as celestial and coastal navigation, chartwork, rules of the road, engine-room rounds, machinery overhaul and mooring
  • frequent drills, often well above the SOLAS and SMS minimum frequency
  • task assessments signed off in each trainee's approved training record book
  • port visits with large shore-leave groups, which bring their own headcount and conduct issues

On a trading ship all of this has to fit around cargo operations, bunkering and port turnarounds. Planning by the Chief Officer and Chief Engineer matters a great deal.

Key hazards on a training ship

The hazards are the normal ship hazards multiplied by numbers and inexperience.

  • Supervision gaps. One officer cannot properly watch ten cadets on a mooring station or in an engine room. A ratio of competent people to trainees for each task is good practice. Where the SMS sets one, follow it.
  • Enclosed spaces and permits. Trainees should never be used as "spare hands" to enter a tank. Every entry follows the permit system, and trainees enter only as part of a supervised and planned job.
  • Working aloft and overside. This is a constant risk on sail training ships. Harnesses, briefings and fitness checks are not optional.
  • Mustering and headcount. With many people on board, getting an accurate count at abandon ship and fire drills is hard. Muster lists and headcount methods must be rehearsed.
  • Life-saving capacity. Survival craft and lifejacket provision must cover everyone the ship is certified to carry. Never embark more trainees than the certificate allows.
  • Accommodation fire and hygiene. Crowded messdecks bring fire load, electrical misuse (chargers, extension leads), illness spread and pressure on fresh water and sewage plants.
  • Fatigue and welfare. Watches plus classes plus drills can push trainees past rest-hour limits. Bullying and harassment of young trainees is a real risk and must be reported under the company's procedures.
  • Seasickness and medical events. These are common early in a voyage and can take a trainee out of a watch rotation.

Typical crew composition

Exact manning comes from the ship's minimum safe manning document and the operator's training plan. A dedicated training ship commonly carries the following.

Group Typical roles
Deck Master, Chief Officer, 2nd and 3rd Officers, often an additional officer for training, Bosun, ABs, OS
Engine Chief Engineer, 2nd/3rd/4th Engineers, ETO, Fitter, Motormen/Oilers
Training Training officer(s) or instructors for deck and engine, sometimes academy faculty sailing for the cruise
Catering and medical Cook(s), Stewards, Messmen. On larger training ships, a doctor or medical staff
Trainees Deck and engine cadets, sometimes ratings under training

Under MLC 2006, a ship to which the Convention applies that carries 100 or more persons and is ordinarily engaged on international voyages of more than three days must carry a qualified medical doctor. Training ships not engaged in commercial activities may fall outside MLC, so their medical staffing is set by the flag state.

On a trading ship with training berths, the normal crew is usually reinforced by a dedicated training officer or superintendent and a larger catering department.

Certificates and experience usually expected

You need your normal STCW certificate of competency or proficiency for your rank, plus the usual basic safety training, medical fitness and flag endorsement. Beyond that, operators commonly look for the following.

  • Experience in rank and a clean record. You will be teaching by example.
  • Instructor and assessor competence. STCW Regulation I/6 requires anyone who conducts in-service training or assessment to be qualified for it. Many administrations and companies require a recognised instructor or assessor course. Which course counts is set by your administration, so check.
  • Medical training to the level your rank requires. On ships without a doctor, the person in charge of medical care needs Medical Care on board.
  • Vessel-type training if the ship is a tanker or gas carrier with training berths. The usual tanker basic and advanced endorsements apply.
  • Sail training experience for tall ships. This is usually built up as deckhand or watch leader time rather than through a single certificate.

For trainees, the point of the ship is approved sea time. For an officer of the navigational watch, STCW Regulation II/1 requires 12 months of approved seagoing service within an approved training programme documented in a training record book, or otherwise 36 months. For an engineer officer in charge of a watch, STCW Regulation III/1 requires not less than 12 months of combined workshop skill training and approved seagoing service, of which at least 6 months is seagoing service, as part of an approved training programme documented in a training record book. Your administration decides whether time on a particular training ship counts in full, so confirm before you sign.

Life on board

For officers and senior ratings:

  • Days are long and structured. You keep your watch, run sessions, check record books and attend debriefs.
  • You are on show all the time. Trainees copy habits, good and bad, so sloppy log entries or skipped PPE spread quickly.
  • There is real job satisfaction in watching a cadet take a first sun sight or handle a first mooring confidently.
  • Accommodation for permanent crew is usually normal. The ship as a whole is busier and noisier than a cargo ship.

For cadets and trainees:

  • Expect shared berths, a strict timetable, uniforms, inspections, and rules on phones, alcohol and shore leave that are tighter than on a trading ship.
  • Cleaning and "ship's husbandry" duties are part of the training, not punishment.
  • You will do more drills and practical tasks in a few months than many cadets see in a year on a trading ship.
  • Your rest hours are protected by STCW (Regulation VIII/1) for watchkeeping duties and, where the ship falls under MLC 2006, by MLC Regulation 2.3. Report any pattern of breaches through the proper channel.

How to get a first contract

Officers and ratings:

  1. Identify the operator. It may be an academy, a government body, a training trust or a shipping company with a cadet programme. Academy ships often recruit through the institution rather than through manning agents.
  2. Build evidence of training ability. That means mentoring cadets on your current ship, keeping well-maintained record book sign-offs, and holding an instructor or assessor course if your administration recognises one.
  3. Keep your documents fully in date, including medical, flag endorsements and any Medical Care certificate.
  4. At interview, expect questions on supervision, drills and handling a trainee welfare problem, as well as technical knowledge.

Cadets:

  1. Join an approved pre-sea course or a company sponsorship scheme. The training ship berth usually comes through that route.
  2. In India, make sure your INDoS number and DG Shipping-approved course details are correct. In the Philippines, follow MARINA's requirements for shipboard training.
  3. Beware of anyone selling "guaranteed training ship berths" for a fee. Verify the ship, the operator and the approval with your administration.

Oral exam style: questions you should be ready for

Q: Who is responsible for a trainee's safety during a mooring operation? A: The Master has overriding responsibility. The officer in charge of the station is directly responsible for everyone on it, trainees included. The training officer does not replace the station officer.

Q: A cadet's record book shows tasks signed off on days he was ashore. What do you do? A: Do not countersign. Raise it with the Master or training officer. False entries put the cadet's certificate and the company at risk.

Q: During an abandon ship drill your headcount is two short. Action? A: Report to the bridge at once. The search starts from the cabin and work-area lists while the remaining personnel stay mustered. Treat it as real until both are accounted for.

Q: The ship is certified for a set number of persons and the academy wants to embark extra trainees. Response? A: Refuse. The safety certificate and the life-saving capacity set the limit. Only the flag state can change it.

Key points
  • "Training ship" can mean an academy vessel, a trading ship with cadet berths, or a sail training vessel; find out which.
  • Trainees are usually treated as special personnel, and how the flag state applies the Special Purpose Ships Code matters.
  • Supervision ratios, mustering and headcount are the biggest day-to-day safety issues.
  • STCW Regulation I/6 requires those conducting onboard training and assessment to be qualified.
  • Under MLC 2006, ships it covers with 100 or more persons on international voyages of more than three days need a qualified medical doctor.
  • Confirm with your administration that sea time on the specific training ship counts towards your CoC.
  • Mentoring experience and clean, honest record book practice help officers win training ship berths.

Frequently asked questions

Does sea time on a training ship count for my CoC?

Usually yes, if it is part of a training programme approved by your administration and documented in your training record book. Some administrations treat time on non-trading training vessels differently, so check with yours before joining.

Do I need a special certificate to be a training officer?

STCW requires anyone who conducts onboard training or assessment to be suitably qualified. Many administrations and companies expect a recognised instructor or assessor course. The specific course depends on your administration and the operator.

Are cadets on a training ship counted as crew or passengers?

Normally neither. They are usually treated as special personnel under the Special Purpose Ships Code framework. The flag state decides how this is applied, and the ship's certificates show the maximum number of persons it may carry.

Is life on a training ship harder than on a cargo ship for cadets?

It is more structured, with timetables, inspections, frequent drills and tighter rules ashore. Many cadets find they gain more practical exposure in a shorter time. Rest-hour limits under STCW and, where it applies to the ship, MLC 2006 still protect you.

How do I find a training ship berth as a cadet?

Through an approved pre-sea institute or a company sponsorship programme, which arranges the berth as part of your training. Avoid agents who charge fees for guaranteed berths, and verify any offer with your administration.

Test yourself

Under MLC 2006, when must a ship carry a qualified medical doctor?

On ships covered by MLC 2006, the doctor requirement is triggered by 100 or more persons and international voyages of more than three days, whatever the ship type. Reference: MLC 2006, Standard A4.1.

Which STCW provision requires that people conducting onboard training and assessment are qualified?

Regulation I/6 covers training and assessment and requires qualified trainers and assessors. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation I/6 and STCW Code Section A-I/6.

How are trainees on a training ship normally classed in IMO terms?

Trainees are typically special personnel, neither crew nor passengers, and the flag state decides how the code is applied. Reference: Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships, 2008.

What does STCW require for a deck cadet's sea time towards an OOW certificate under an approved training programme?

The requirement is 12 months within an approved programme documented in a training record book; 36 months applies only outside such a programme. Reference: STCW Convention, Regulation II/1.

The academy asks the Master to embark more trainees than the ship's certificate allows for a short cruise. What is correct?

Only the flag state can change the certified number of persons, and the life-saving appliances must cover everyone on board. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III; Code of Safety for Special Purpose Ships, 2008.
