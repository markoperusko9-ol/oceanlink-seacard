Working on a Livestock Carrier

Quick answer

Livestock carriers move live cattle, sheep, goats and other animals between export and import ports, often on short, intensive voyages. Your normal STCW certificates are the entry ticket. What sets the job apart is around-the-clock animal care, heavy demand on ventilation, fresh water and fodder systems, and specific hazards such as ammonia, effluent gases, stability problems and zoonotic disease.

What a livestock carrier does and where it trades

A livestock carrier is a cargo ship fitted to carry live animals in pens on multiple decks. It also carries the fodder, water and ventilation needed to keep them alive for the whole passage.

Typical cargoes are cattle, sheep and goats. Some trades also carry buffalo, camels or breeding stock.

Well-known trade patterns include:

  • Australia to South-East Asia, China and the Middle East
  • South America, especially Brazil and Uruguay, to Turkey, the Middle East and North Africa
  • Europe, from Mediterranean and Black Sea ports and Ireland, to North Africa, the Middle East and Turkey

Voyages are usually days to a few weeks laden. The return leg is normally in ballast after a thorough washdown.

Much of the fleet consists of older ships converted from ro-ro, container or general cargo tonnage. There are also some purpose-built vessels, which tend to have better ventilation, drainage and automation. When you are offered a contract, the ship's age and conversion history matter a great deal.

Layout and key systems

Picture a cargo hull (often a converted ro-ro, container or general cargo ship) filled with pen decks, with a high, boxy superstructure built over the original deck. The main features are:

  • Pen decks. Fenced pens with non-slip flooring, ramps between decks, and alleyways for handlers.
  • Mechanical ventilation. Banks of supply and exhaust fans for every enclosed livestock deck. This is the most critical system on board: if ventilation fails in hot weather, animals can die within hours.
  • Fodder handling. Pellet silos or bins, conveyors or manual distribution, and stowage for hay or chaff.
  • Fresh water. Drinking water demand is very large. Ships rely on fresh water generators, reverse-osmosis plants and big storage tanks, with automatic drinkers or troughs in the pens.
  • Drainage and effluent. Scuppers, drain lines and effluent or manure tanks. Washdown pumps and hoses for cleaning the pens.
  • Lighting and power. Livestock decks are lit around the clock. Ventilation and water plants add a big, continuous electrical load, so generator availability and redundancy are watched closely.
  • Hospital pens. Set aside for sick or injured animals.

For engineers, this means a lot of fans, motors, pumps, water-making plant and electrical distribution. It is closer to a large HVAC and utilities job than a typical cargo ship engine room.

Regulation and approvals

There is no single IMO code dedicated to livestock carriers. The ship must comply with SOLAS, MARPOL, Load Lines and the other conventions like any cargo ship. On top of that, the flag state and the export states apply their own rules.

  • Australia. AMSA regulates livestock ships through Marine Order 43. A ship needs an Australian Certificate for the Carriage of Livestock before it can load there. Australian export rules also govern stocking densities, fodder and water reserves, and the carriage of accredited stockpersons and, on some voyages, veterinarians.
  • European Union. EU animal transport rules require livestock vessels loading in EU ports to be inspected and approved, with conditions on ventilation, water, fodder and alarms.
  • Other states. Exporting and importing states, such as South American countries, may set their own conditions.

Garbage and carcasses. Dead animals count as garbage under MARPOL Annex V. Disposal at sea is tightly restricted, depends on location and must follow the ship's Garbage Management Plan.

Effluent and washings. Discharge of manure and washdown water is also controlled by coastal state and export conditions. Never assume; check the company procedures and the voyage instructions.

The exact requirements differ by flag, by export state and by company SMS. Learn the ones that apply to your ship rather than relying on what you heard from another vessel.

Hazards you must take seriously

This trade has a poor safety record. The capsizes of Queen Hind in the port of Midia, Romania, in 2019 and Gulf Livestock 1 in a typhoon off Japan in 2020 are reminders that stability and heavy weather are life-and-death issues here.

Stability

  • The high superstructure and multiple loaded decks raise the centre of gravity and the windage.
  • Large volumes of water, fodder and effluent create free surface effects, and these change continuously during the voyage.
  • Loading and discharge plans must be followed strictly, because animals do not stay exactly where the plan put them.

Gases and enclosed spaces

  • Ammonia builds up from urine and manure, especially if ventilation is weak.
  • Hydrogen sulphide and oxygen depletion can occur in effluent tanks, drain wells and bilges.
  • Fodder silos are enclosed spaces with engulfment and dust hazards.
  • Treat every one of these as an enclosed space under the permit-to-work system: test the atmosphere, ventilate, and keep a standby person.

Animals

  • Kicks, crushing and goring injuries happen during loading, discharge and pen work.
  • Wet, manure-covered decks and ramps are very slippery.

Health

  • Zoonotic infections such as Q fever, ringworm and leptospirosis are a risk.
  • Wear PPE: gloves, boots, eye protection and respiratory protection where provided. Keep good hygiene and report skin lesions or fever.

Heat and fatigue

  • Hot-climate trades combined with heavy manual work cause heat stress.
  • Manage hours of rest carefully when the workload peaks.

Crew composition and certificates

The marine crew is the usual set: Master, Chief Officer, watchkeeping officers, Chief Engineer and engineers, ETO on some ships, Bosun, ABs, OSs, oilers, fitters and catering staff.

The difference is a livestock crew of dedicated hands. They feed, water, clean pens and care for sick animals. Some ships use the deck ratings for part of this work. Exporters may also place an accredited stockperson or veterinarian on board, depending on the export state's rules.

Certificates. The essentials are the same as on any cargo ship:

  • A valid CoC or watch-rating certificate for your rank
  • STCW basic training and the other training required for your duties
  • A medical fitness certificate
  • Security training

There is no internationally mandated livestock-specific certificate for ship's crew. Companies may run their own familiarisation on animal handling and welfare. Some ask for particular vaccinations, such as tetanus; this depends on the company.

Experience that helps:

  • Deck officers: ro-ro or general cargo background, and solid stability and loading computer skills.
  • Engineers: experience with fresh water generators, RO plants, large ventilation and electrical systems.
  • Livestock hands: farm or animal-handling background.

Life on board

Be honest with yourself before you sign.

The smell. It is strong and gets into clothing and cabins. Most people adapt within days, but some never do.

The workload. The cargo is alive, so there is no quiet sea passage. Feeding, watering, pen cleaning and checking for sick animals go on day and night. Fans and water plants need constant attention.

Port time. Loading and discharge are intense and supervised by exporters, importers and authorities. Expect inspections, vets and paperwork.

Mortality. Animal deaths have to be recorded and reported. Carcasses must be handled and disposed of correctly. This can be unpleasant work.

Turnaround. Voyages can be short and port calls frequent, so the routine is repetitive. Shore leave varies with the port.

Strengths. Many crews are tight-knit and the work builds strong practical skills. Engineers get broad auxiliary-plant experience.

Worked scenario: ventilation failure on a livestock deck

It is a hot, humid night passage in the Red Sea. An alarm shows that two supply fans on a lower livestock deck have tripped, and the livestock crew report animals panting heavily.

  1. Bridge OOW. Call the Master and the duty engineer at once. Consider altering course or speed to bring the relative wind onto the openings that help natural airflow, where the design allows it.
  2. Duty engineer. Check the breaker and the cause of the trip: motor overload, a starter fault or a supply problem. Start standby fans and confirm that generator capacity is sufficient.
  3. Livestock crew. Check that the drinkers are working, hose down the decks if procedures allow, and monitor the animals' condition.
  4. Ammonia. Monitor levels on the affected deck. Do not send people into a poorly ventilated deck without checking the atmosphere first.
  5. Master. Log the event and inform the company and the exporter's representative as required. Record any mortality accurately.

Lesson: on this ship type, ventilation and water plant faults are treated with the same urgency as a steering or propulsion failure.

Getting your first contract and oral-exam style questions

Getting started

  • Go through licensed manning agents only:
    • India: use an agency holding a DG Shipping RPSL licence.
    • Philippines: use an agency licensed by the DMW.
  • Livestock operators often recruit through specialist agents. Ro-ro, general cargo or reefer experience strengthens your CV.
  • Before signing, look up the ship's age, flag and port state control detention history. Ask about the ventilation and water plant redundancy and the crew numbers.
  • Never pay recruitment fees to a manning agent. This is not permitted under MLC, 2006.

Typical questions an examiner or Master may ask

  • Why is GM on a livestock carrier harder to predict than on a bulk carrier? Fodder and water are consumed and effluent builds up throughout the voyage, which changes weights and free surface. The animals can also shift.
  • What gases would you expect in an effluent tank? Hydrogen sulphide, ammonia and possibly a depleted oxygen atmosphere.
  • What is the most critical auxiliary system on board? Livestock deck ventilation, closely followed by fresh water production.
Key points
  • Livestock carriers move cattle, sheep and goats mainly from Australia, South America and Europe to Asia, the Middle East, Turkey and North Africa.
  • Ventilation, fresh water and fodder systems are life-critical; a fan failure in heat can kill animals within hours.
  • High superstructure, windage and changing free surface make stability a serious hazard on this ship type.
  • Effluent tanks, drain wells and fodder silos are enclosed spaces with H2S, ammonia, low-oxygen or engulfment risks.
  • Standard STCW certificates apply; extra approvals come from flag and export states such as Australia and the EU.
  • Expect a larger crew including livestock hands, round-the-clock work and a strong smell.
  • Check ship age and PSC record, and only use licensed manning agents.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special certificate to work on a livestock carrier?

There is no internationally mandated livestock-specific certificate for ship's crew. You need the normal STCW certificates for your rank and a valid medical. Companies may run their own animal-handling familiarisation and may ask for certain vaccinations.

Is working on a livestock carrier dangerous?

It carries extra risks: stability problems, toxic gases from effluent, animal-related injuries and zoonotic disease. Several serious casualties have occurred in recent years. Good ships with sound stability practice, enclosed space discipline and proper PPE manage these risks, so check the ship's condition and record before signing.

Do deck ratings have to feed and clean animals?

It depends on the company. Many ships carry a dedicated livestock crew, but deck ratings may help with washdowns, loading and discharge. Ask the manning agent exactly what your duties will be before you sign.

What experience helps engineers get a livestock carrier job?

Experience with fresh water generators, reverse-osmosis plants, large fan and motor installations, and electrical distribution is valued. Ro-ro and reefer backgrounds also transfer well, because ventilation and power reliability are central to the job.

How are dead animals disposed of at sea?

Carcasses are garbage under MARPOL Annex V. Disposal at sea is restricted by location and must follow the ship's Garbage Management Plan, together with any export or coastal state conditions. All mortality must be recorded and reported as the company and exporter require.

Test yourself

Which system is generally considered the most critical auxiliary system on a livestock carrier?

Ventilation failure in warm weather can cause mass mortality within hours, so fans and their power supply have top priority. Reference: export state livestock ship requirements, e.g. AMSA Marine Order 43.

Why is stability on a livestock carrier particularly demanding?

Consumables and effluent change throughout the voyage and multi-deck pens raise KG and windage. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, the 2008 Intact Stability Code and the ship's approved stability booklet.

An effluent tank must be entered for inspection. What is the correct approach?

Effluent tanks can contain H2S, ammonia or low oxygen, and H2S deadens the sense of smell. Reference: SOLAS regulation XI-1/7 and IMO enclosed space entry recommendations.

Under which instrument are animal carcasses on board treated?

Carcasses are a category of garbage, and disposal at sea is restricted by location. Reference: MARPOL Annex V.

Which body issues the certificate a livestock ship needs before loading in Australia?

AMSA issues the Australian Certificate for the Carriage of Livestock. Reference: AMSA Marine Order 43.
