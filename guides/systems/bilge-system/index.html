Bilge System on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

The bilge system collects and removes water and oily leakage from the ship's holds, machinery spaces, voids, cofferdams, chain lockers and other dry spaces. It uses a common bilge main with screw-down non-return valves, strainers and power pumps. Oily machinery-space bilge water is normally collected in a bilge holding tank, then processed through approved 15 ppm separating equipment under MARPOL Annex I conditions or landed to a reception facility, and is never pumped directly overboard except to secure the safety of the ship or save life.

What the bilge system is for

Every dry space on a ship collects water: condensation, gland and seal leakage, wash water, rain through hatches, sweat from cargo, and in the worst case flooding from damage. The bilge system gets that water out before it damages cargo, corrodes structure, affects stability through free surface, or floods machinery.

It has two separate jobs, and mixing them up is a common mistake:

  • Safety of the ship. It must be able to dewater any watertight compartment in normal service and, within its capacity, after minor flooding. This side comes from SOLAS Chapter II-1 and classification society rules.
  • Pollution prevention. Machinery-space bilge water is oily. It must be collected, stored and processed under MARPOL Annex I. It may only go overboard through approved 15 ppm equipment, except to save the ship or life.

The pump that can save the ship from flooding must not become the pump that dumps oil over the side on a quiet night watch. Most bilge system rules, valve arrangements and checks exist to keep those two jobs apart.

Main components

Layouts vary by ship type, builder and class, but you will find most of the following on any merchant ship:

Component Purpose
Bilge wells / hat boxes Low collecting points in holds and machinery spaces where water drains to the suction
Strum box (rose box) Perforated strainer at the end of a hold or tank-top suction; stops large debris
Mud box Accessible strainer box in machinery-space bilge lines, with a lid for cleaning
Screw-down non-return (SDNR) valves Isolate each suction and prevent backflow into a compartment
Bilge main and branches Common line linking the suctions to the pumps through a valve chest or manifold
Power bilge pumps Usually reciprocating or self-priming centrifugal; general service or ballast pumps are often arranged to double as bilge pumps
Eductors (ejectors) Driven by fire or ballast water; used for holds, chain lockers, fore peak voids and spaces with no direct pump suction
Bilge holding tank Collects oily machinery-space bilge water for processing
Oily water separator (15 ppm) Separates oil before overboard discharge; has an oil content meter and an automatic stopping device
Sludge tank Receives separated oil and purifier residues
Bilge level alarms / water ingress detectors Warn the watch of rising water, essential in unattended machinery spaces
Sounding pipes Manual check of bilge well levels where fitted

The SDNR valve is the heart of the system. It lets you open one suction at a time. Its non-return function stops water already in the bilge main from flowing back into a dry hold, and stops water spreading from a flooded compartment into an intact one through the piping.

How it works, step by step

A typical machinery-space bilge pumping sequence, done to your ship's SMS and the Chief Engineer's standing orders:

  1. Sound or check the well level and look at what is in it. Clear water, oily water and anything with a sheen or emulsion are handled differently.
  2. Line up the pump suction. Open the bilge main valve to the pump and the SDNR valve for one well only.
  3. Line up the discharge to the bilge holding tank. On most ships this is the only normal destination for engine-room bilges.
  4. Prime and start the pump. Centrifugal bilge pumps need a working priming device (vacuum pump or air ejector). Reciprocating pumps are self-priming.
  5. Watch suction pressure and discharge. A steady vacuum with flow means you are pumping. A rising vacuum with no flow means a choked strainer. A falling vacuum with no flow means you are drawing air.
  6. Stop before the well runs dry. This avoids drawing air and running the pump without liquid.
  7. Close the suction valve, then the main valves. Never leave bilge suctions open.
  8. Record the transfer as your ship requires. Processing through the separator and disposal of residues are Oil Record Book Part I entries under MARPOL Annex I.

Cargo hold bilges on dry cargo ships usually pump overboard through the bilge or ballast system, or via eductors. Before you pump, check the water is free of oil and of cargo residues that MARPOL Annex V does not allow you to discharge. Follow your SMS: some cargoes and areas require the water to be retained.

Routine checks and maintenance

Intervals come from your planned maintenance system and the manufacturer, not from a fixed international figure. The usual routine:

  • Each watch / daily rounds: check machinery-space bilge levels visually and on the alarm panel. Note any unusual rise and trace its source.
  • Bilge alarms: test the float or level switches by physically lifting or flooding them, not just by the panel test button. Confirm the alarm reaches the bridge and the duty engineer where required.
  • Mud boxes and strainers: open and clean them. Check the lid joint, because a leaking lid is a classic air leak.
  • SDNR valves: check they open, close and seat. Spindles on rarely used hold suctions seize if they are not exercised.
  • Bilge pumps: check glands, priming device, relief valve, and suction and discharge gauges.
  • Eductors: do a test run, especially fore peak and chain locker eductors that may only be used in an emergency.
  • Separator and oil content meter: run and maintain them as the manufacturer and the PMS require. Never tamper with or bypass the meter or stopping device.
  • Bulk carriers: test the hold, ballast and dry-space water level detectors required by SOLAS Chapter XII, at the intervals set by your PMS and class.

Before loading dry bulk: clean the hold bilge wells and test the suctions by pumping water out of them. Check the strum box is clear. For fine or grain cargoes, many SMSs require the bilge well covers to be wrapped in hessian (burlap) so the water drains through but the cargo does not.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Pump runs, no suction, vacuum low Air leak at a mud box lid, a flange joint or the pump gland; a second suction valve left open on a dry well; priming device not working
Pump runs, no flow, vacuum high Choked strum box, mud box or strainer; suction valve not actually lifting (seized spindle or valve disc detached)
Water appears in a hold or space that was dry SDNR valve not seating, so it is backflowing from the main; wrong valve opened during another space's pumping
Bilge alarm never activates Float jammed by sludge or rag; switch failed; wiring fault. Test it physically
Constant alarm or rapid level rise Real leak (sea water pipe, stern tube seal, cooler, gland), or float stuck high
Separator alarms or recirculates constantly Emulsified bilges from detergents, chemical cleaners or a high oil content, so the oil content meter reads over 15 ppm
Hold suction blocks during pumping Cargo residue in the well; strum not protected before loading

The most common real-world problem is detergent in the engine-room bilges. Cleaners emulsify oil so the separator cannot break it out. Keep chemicals out of the bilges unless they are approved as compatible with your separator.

Safety and pollution precautions

  • Never pump machinery-space bilges directly overboard in normal operation. MARPOL Annex I allows discharge only through approved 15 ppm equipment, under the conditions in the Annex. There is an exception for discharges needed to secure the safety of the ship or save life at sea.
  • Emergency bilge suction (often from a large sea water pump, as class rules arrange) is for flooding only. If it is used, inform the Master and Chief Engineer, and record the circumstances fully.
  • Lock or control overboard valves as your company requires. Many companies seal or padlock them as part of their environmental policy.
  • Cargo holds, pipe ducts, cofferdams, voids and deep bilge wells or pits can all be enclosed spaces, depending on cargo and ventilation. Follow your SMS enclosed space entry and permit-to-work procedures, including atmosphere testing, before going down to clean a strum box or bilge well.
  • Isolate pumps (lock-out tag-out) before opening a mud box or strainer on a line that can be started remotely.
  • Stability: water sloshing in holds or machinery spaces causes free surface effect. A rising level the watch cannot explain is a safety matter, not just a housekeeping one.

Worked scenario: rising engine-room bilge at night (UMS)

The bilge high-level alarm sounds at 0230. You are the duty engineer.

  1. Acknowledge the alarm and go down. Inform the bridge as your standing orders require.
  2. Find the source before pumping. Check sea water lines, coolers, pump glands, stern tube seal area, fresh water and boiler drains. Then decide whether the water is clean or oily.
  3. If it is a manageable leak: isolate it if you can. Pump the affected well to the bilge holding tank, one suction at a time. Watch the rate of rise against your pumping rate.
  4. If the level keeps rising faster than you can pump: call the 2nd Engineer and Chief Engineer at once. Bring a second bilge pump on line. The Chief Engineer and Master decide whether to use the emergency bilge suction.
  5. Afterwards: make the correct log and Oil Record Book entries, raise a defect or near-miss report, and plan the separator processing for the holding tank.

The examiner wants to hear that you investigate before you pump, escalate early, and keep oil out of the sea.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Why are bilge suction valves screw-down non-return? To isolate each suction and to stop backflow from the bilge main into a dry or intact compartment.
  • Why do you open only one suction at a time? If one well runs dry, the pump draws air through it and loses suction on all the others.
  • Difference between a strum box and a mud box? A strum box is the strainer at the suction end in the well. A mud box is an accessible strainer in the line, usually in the machinery space, that you open and clean.
  • Where does engine-room bilge water go? To the bilge holding tank, then through the 15 ppm separator or to a shore reception facility. Separated oil goes to the sludge tank, all recorded in the Oil Record Book Part I.
  • How do you dewater the chain locker or a space with no pump suction? Usually by an eductor driven by fire or ballast main pressure.
  • What do you check in hold bilges before loading? The wells are clean, suctions are tested and working, strums are clear and protected where the SMS requires, and on bulk carriers the water ingress detectors are tested.
  • Which regulation covers bilge pumping arrangements? SOLAS Chapter II-1, with the details in class rules. The oily water side is MARPOL Annex I.
Key points
  • The bilge system removes water from dry spaces for ship safety and handles oily bilge water for pollution prevention.
  • SDNR valves isolate each suction and prevent backflow into intact compartments.
  • Open one bilge suction at a time, or a dry well will make the pump lose suction.
  • Engine-room bilges go to the holding tank, then through the 15 ppm separator or to a shore reception facility, never directly overboard in normal service.
  • Emergency bilge suction is for flooding only; inform senior officers and record its use fully.
  • Test bilge alarms physically and keep detergents out of the bilges.
  • On bulk carriers, test hold suctions and water ingress detectors before loading.

Frequently asked questions

Can engine room bilge water be pumped directly overboard?

Not in normal operation. MARPOL Annex I requires machinery-space oily bilge water to be discharged only through approved 15 ppm separating equipment with an oil content meter and automatic stopping device, under the conditions in the Annex. Direct discharge is only excused where needed to secure the safety of the ship or save life.

Why does my bilge pump lose suction?

The usual causes are an air leak at a mud box lid, joint or gland, a second suction valve left open on a dry well, or a faulty priming device. A choked strainer gives a different symptom: high vacuum with no flow.

What is the difference between a bilge well, a strum box and a mud box?

The bilge well is the low collecting point where water drains. The strum box is the perforated strainer at the suction end inside the well. The mud box is an accessible strainer box in the bilge line, typically in the machinery space, that you open for cleaning.

How many bilge pumps must a cargo ship have?

SOLAS Chapter II-1 sets minimum bilge pumping arrangements, and passenger ships need more pumps than cargo ships depending on size. Other suitable pumps, such as ballast or general service pumps, can often be counted if properly connected. Exact numbers and capacities depend on ship type and classification society rules, so check your ship's approved drawings.

Why wrap hold bilge wells in hessian before loading bulk cargo?

It lets water drain into the well while stopping fine cargo or grain from entering and blocking the strum box and suction line. Whether it is required, and how to do it, depends on your company SMS and the cargo.

Test yourself

Why are bilge suction valves of the screw-down non-return type?

SDNR valves isolate individual suctions and stop water flowing back from the bilge main into dry or intact spaces. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1 bilge pumping arrangements and class rules.

A bilge pump is running with a high suction vacuum but no discharge. What is the most likely cause?

High vacuum with no flow points to a restriction on the suction side, such as a blocked strainer. Air leaks and dry wells give low vacuum, and on a centrifugal pump a failed primer means it cannot build suction vacuum at all.

In normal operation, where should machinery-space oily bilge water be transferred?

Oily bilge water is stored and processed through approved 15 ppm equipment, with entries in the Oil Record Book Part I. Reference: MARPOL Annex I.

What is the usual way to dewater a chain locker that has no direct pump suction?

Chain lockers and similar forward spaces are commonly drained by eductors, which have no moving parts in the space. The exact arrangement follows the ship's approved drawings and class rules.

Which SOLAS chapter contains water level detector requirements for bulk carrier holds?

SOLAS Chapter XII (bulk carriers) requires hold, ballast and dry-space water level detectors and pumping arrangements for certain forward spaces. Chapter III covers life-saving appliances, Chapter V safety of navigation and Chapter IX the ISM Code.
