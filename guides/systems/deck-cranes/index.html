Deck Cranes on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

Most shipboard deck cranes are electro-hydraulic pedestal cranes. An electric motor drives hydraulic pumps that power separate hoist, luffing and slewing motions, and spring-applied brakes and limit switches keep the load safe. Safe operation depends on the condition of the wires, brakes, limits and slewing bearing, a valid examination and test record, and disciplined lifting procedures under the company SMS.

What deck cranes do and where you find them

Deck cranes let a ship work cargo, stores and spares without relying on shore gear. You will find them on geared bulk carriers and general cargo ships, multipurpose and heavy-lift vessels, some container feeders, and offshore vessels. Smaller provision and hose-handling cranes are fitted on almost every tanker and gas carrier.

Typical shipboard types:

  • Pedestal (jib) cranes: the common geared-bulker type. The jib is luffed by wire or by hydraulic cylinder.
  • Knuckle-boom cranes: compact and common for stores and on offshore vessels.
  • Gantry cranes: travel along rails over the hatches on some specialised ships.
  • Hose-handling and provision cranes: small cranes on tankers and gas carriers.

Some geared bulkers and many multipurpose and heavy-lift ships can operate two cranes in twin (combined) mode to lift heavier units. Twin mode has its own SWL, its own control logic and specific procedures in the maker's manual.

On deck the Chief Officer is usually responsible for cargo gear. Engineers and the ETO maintain the hydraulic and electrical side. Exactly who does what varies with the company SMS.

Main components

Names vary between makers, but a typical electro-hydraulic pedestal crane has the following parts.

  • Pedestal: welded to the ship's structure and part of the load path. It carries the slewing bearing.
  • Slewing bearing (slewing ring): a large roller or ball bearing with a toothed ring gear. It carries the whole crane and load moment. Its fixing bolts are critical items.
  • Crane house and cabin: contain the operator's position, the hydraulic power pack, electrical panels and often the winches.
  • Jib (boom): the main lifting arm. Its heel pins and the luffing arrangement need regular inspection and greasing.
  • Hoisting winch: drum, hydraulic motor, gearbox and brake for the cargo wire.
  • Luffing winch or cylinder: raises and lowers the jib, which changes the working radius.
  • Slewing drives: hydraulic motors with gearboxes and pinions meshing with the ring gear. Each has its own brake.
  • Wire ropes, sheaves and hook block: the cargo (hoist) wire, the luffing wire where fitted, sheaves on the jib head and the hook with its safety latch and swivel.
  • Hydraulic power pack: electric motor, pumps, tank, filters, cooler, relief and counterbalance valves.
  • Safety devices: hoist upper and lower limits, luffing limits, slew limits where fitted, overload or load-moment protection, slack-wire detection on some models, emergency stops and the emergency lowering arrangement.
  • Slip ring: carries power and signals from the fixed pedestal to the rotating crane house.

Every crane must be clearly marked with its SWL. Where the SWL changes with radius or operating mode, a load chart must be displayed in the cabin.

How an electro-hydraulic crane works – step by step

This sequence is typical. Always follow the maker's manual and your SMS checklist.

  1. Power up. Close the crane supply breaker on the switchboard. The space heaters, which keep the motors and panels dry in port and at sea, are normally switched over or off.
  2. Start the power pack. The electric motor drives one or more hydraulic pumps, often variable-displacement axial piston pumps in a closed loop. In cold weather, warm the oil up as the maker directs before applying load.
  3. Release the stowage. Unlash the jib from its cradle (jib rest) and take off any hook lashing.
  4. Operate the joysticks. Joystick movement signals proportional or servo valves, or the pump swash plates. This sets the flow direction and quantity to each hydraulic motor, so the speed is proportional to stick deflection.
  5. Brakes release automatically. Hoist, luffing and slewing brakes are normally spring-applied and hydraulically released. When the motion is commanded, pressure lifts the brake. When the stick returns to neutral or power is lost, the springs set the brake. This is the fail-safe principle.
  6. Load holding. Counterbalance or holding valves stop the load or jib running down under gravity while the brake engages.
  7. Limits and overload protection. As the hook nears the jib head, or the jib reaches its minimum or maximum angle, the limit switch stops that motion. Usually you can still move in the safe direction. Overload protection stops hoisting, or stops luffing out, when the load or load moment is exceeded.
  8. Stow after work. Lower the jib into its cradle, secure the hook, stop the power pack, isolate the supply and switch the heaters back on.

Some ships have all-electric cranes with variable-frequency drives instead of hydraulics. The principles of fail-safe brakes and limits are the same.

Routine checks and maintenance

Maintenance intervals come from the maker and the planned maintenance system. The list below shows typical content. It is not a substitute for your PMS.

Before each use (operator or duty officer)

  • Look over the wires for broken strands, kinks, crushing, birdcaging and corrosion. Check that the wire is spooling correctly on the drum.
  • Check the hook, safety latch and swivel.
  • Check the hydraulic oil level and look for leaks around the power pack and hoses.
  • Test the emergency stop, and the hoist and luffing limits, without load.
  • Confirm the brakes hold, and listen for unusual noise from pumps, gearboxes and the slewing gear.
  • Confirm the SWL marking and load chart are legible, and that the radius and heel are within limits.

Periodic (weekly to annual, per PMS)

  • Grease the slewing bearing and ring gear, jib heel pins, sheaves and wire ropes.
  • Take hydraulic oil samples for analysis. Change filters on the differential-pressure indication or at the PMS interval.
  • Clean the oil cooler and check it is working.
  • Inspect brake linings and check holding.
  • Check slewing bearing bolt tension and take wear or clearance measurements as the maker specifies. Rocking or tilt measurements are common.
  • Clean and inspect slip rings, carbon brushes and electrical panels, and check motor insulation resistance.
  • Inspect gearbox oil levels and condition.
  • Renew wires when they reach discard criteria. Keep the manufacturer's certificate for the new rope and record the renewal in the register. Never leave fewer dead turns on the drum than the maker specifies.

Statutory and class examinations are covered in the regulations section below.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes First actions
Load creeps down when the stick is in neutral Worn or contaminated brake lining, faulty counterbalance valve, internal leakage Land the load at once, stop work, report, investigate brake and valve
Slow or weak hoisting Worn pump, relief valve set low or leaking, clogged filter, low oil level Check level and filter indication, record pressures, compare with the maker's data
Hydraulic oil overheating alarm Fouled cooler, cooler fan or water fault, internal leakage, prolonged heavy duty Stop and allow cooling, clean the cooler, check the oil
Jerky or erratic motion Air or water in the oil, sticking proportional valve, joystick or signal fault Check the oil condition, bleed as per manual, test the control circuit
Crane trips or will not start Slip ring moisture or wear, insulation fault, emergency stop or limit circuit open Check the heaters were on, test insulation, trace the safety chain
Knocking or rocking when slewing Slewing bearing wear, loose bolts, damaged pinion or ring gear teeth Stop, inspect, measure clearance, check bolts
Wire jumping or spooling badly Slack wire, side-loading or off-lead pull causing excessive fleet angle, damaged sheave, kinked wire Stop, inspect sheaves and wire, re-spool under tension

Worked scenario. During grab discharge the duty AB reports that the grab sinks slowly while the crane is stopped over the hatch. Treat this as a brake or holding-valve failure, not a nuisance.

  1. Tell the operator to land the grab on the cargo or the deck.
  2. Stop that crane and inform the Chief Officer and the duty engineer.
  3. Isolate the crane under lock-out tag-out.
  4. Inspect the brake and counterbalance valve.
  5. Return the crane to service only after the defect is repaired and the brake has been tested. Record it, and if the company SMS requires it, raise a non-conformity.

In the meantime, the stevedores can keep working with the other cranes.

Safety precautions

  • Never exceed the SWL for the radius and mode in use. Never bypass overload protection or limit switches. Bypass keys exist for specific maintenance and test situations only, under the maker's procedure and with authorisation.
  • Competent operators only. Shore crane drivers operating ship's cranes should be briefed on that crane's controls and emergency stop.
  • One signaller, agreed signals. The signaller must be clearly identifiable and in sight of the operator, or use radios on a dedicated channel.
  • Nobody under a suspended load. Use tag lines and keep hands off the load while it is being landed.
  • Weather and ship limits. Respect the maker's wind and heel or trim limits. Watch for list developing during cargo work.
  • Check the lifting gear. Slings, shackles and spreaders must be certified, marked and inspected. The crane SWL does not cover defective gear.
  • Maintenance safety. Use permit to work and lock-out tag-out. Wear a harness for work on the jib or at height. Relieve stored hydraulic pressure and secure the jib before opening hydraulic lines.
  • Sea stowage. The jib must be in its cradle, the hook lashed, the house locked against slewing as the maker requires, the power isolated and the heaters on.

Rules, registers and certification

Requirements depend on flag, class and port state, so check yours. The firm points are these.

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, regulation 3-13 sets requirements for the design, construction, installation, testing, examination and maintenance of onboard lifting appliances and anchor-handling winches. It applies from 1 January 2026 to lifting appliances with an SWL of 1 tonne or more, supported by IMO guidelines. Design and construction requirements apply to appliances installed on or after that date. Appliances installed before it are brought within scope for operational testing, examination and maintenance.
  • ILO Convention No. 152 (Occupational Safety and Health in Dock Work) requires lifting appliances to be tested and thoroughly examined and records to be kept. Many port states enforce it through inspections of the ship's gear.
  • The ship's register of lifting appliances (cargo gear register) holds test and examination certificates, wire rope certificates and records of thorough examinations. Port State Control and stevedores regularly ask to see it.
  • In common practice a thorough examination by a competent person takes place at least every 12 months. A proof-load test is carried out at a longer interval, frequently five years, and after any repair or renewal of a load-bearing part. The exact intervals and proof-load margins come from class rules and the flag administration.
  • The ISM Code requires maintenance of equipment under the SMS, including critical equipment, and MLC 2006 (Regulation 4.3) covers occupational safety on board.

Oral exam questions

Typical questions and the points an examiner wants to hear:

  • "Why are crane brakes spring-applied?" Fail-safe principle. If hydraulic pressure or power is lost, the springs set the brakes and the load is held.
  • "What is the function of a counterbalance valve?" It holds the load or jib against gravity and controls lowering, so the load cannot run away.
  • "What would you check before cargo operations?" Wires, hook and latch, oil level and leaks, limit switches, emergency stop, brakes, SWL marking, communications and the gear register.
  • "The load creeps down. What do you do?" Land it, stop, isolate, report, inspect the brake and valve, and do not resume until tested.
  • "What documents relate to cranes?" The register of lifting appliances, test and examination certificates, wire rope certificates and PMS records.
  • "How do you stow a crane for sea?" Jib in the cradle, hook secured, slew locked as required, power isolated, heaters on.
  • "Why keep space heaters on?" To prevent condensation in motors, panels and slip rings, which causes insulation faults and trips.
Key points
  • Most ship deck cranes are electro-hydraulic, with separate hoist, luffing and slewing motions.
  • Brakes are spring-applied and hydraulically released, so loss of power sets the brakes.
  • Never exceed the SWL for the radius and mode in use, and never bypass limits or overload protection.
  • A load creeping down in neutral means landing the load and stopping the crane immediately.
  • Keep the register of lifting appliances, test and examination certificates and wire certificates up to date.
  • SOLAS regulation II-1/3-13 on lifting appliances applies from 1 January 2026, and flag and class set the detail.
  • Stow for sea with the jib cradled, hook secured, power isolated and heaters on.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for deck cranes on a merchant ship?

The Master holds overall responsibility. The Chief Officer normally oversees cargo gear and its register, and engineers and the ETO maintain the hydraulic and electrical systems. The exact split is defined in the company SMS and the planned maintenance system.

How often must ship cranes be load tested?

It depends on the flag administration, class and the applicable rules. Common practice is a thorough examination at least every 12 months and a proof-load test at a longer interval, frequently five years. A proof-load test is also required after repairs or renewals of load-bearing parts. Always check your flag and class requirements and the gear register.

What does SWL mean on a crane?

SWL is the safe working load, the maximum load the crane is certified to lift in a given configuration. Where it varies with radius or mode, such as twin operation, a load chart in the cabin shows the limits. The weight of the grab, spreader or lifting gear counts towards the load.

When should a crane wire be renewed?

Renew it when it reaches the discard criteria given by the maker or the applicable standard. Signs include broken wires, reduction in diameter, corrosion, kinks, crushing or birdcaging. Keep the new rope's certificate in the register and record the renewal.

Can stevedores operate the ship's cranes?

In many ports stevedores operate ship's cranes. The ship remains responsible for providing gear in safe working order with valid certification. Brief the drivers on that crane's controls, limits and emergency stop, and keep a crew member available to respond to faults.

Test yourself

What happens to a typical crane hoist brake if hydraulic pressure is lost?

Crane brakes are normally spring-applied and hydraulically released, which is the fail-safe principle (maker's manual; class rules).

During discharge the grab slowly sinks while the joystick is in neutral. What is the correct first action?

Creep indicates brake or holding-valve failure, so the crane must be stopped and repaired under the SMS (ISM Code, maintenance requirements).

Why are crane space heaters switched on when the crane is idle?

Moisture causes insulation faults and trips, so heaters keep electrical components dry (maker's manual).

Which document records test and examination certificates for the ship's cranes?

Certificates and examination records for lifting appliances are kept in the register (ILO Convention No. 152).

Which of the following must be counted against the crane's SWL?

Everything suspended below the hook, such as a grab, spreader or slings, counts towards the load against the SWL (maker's load chart; class rules).
