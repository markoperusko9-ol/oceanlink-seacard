Oily Water Separator (15 ppm): Operation, Faults and Exam Questions

Quick answer

The oily water separator (OWS) treats machinery space bilge water so that the oil content is no more than 15 ppm before it goes overboard. A 15 ppm bilge alarm monitors the effluent. On ships of 10,000 GT and above it must also stop the overboard discharge automatically when the limit is exceeded. Every operation is recorded in the Oil Record Book Part I, and any bypass or tampering is a serious criminal matter in many port states.

What the OWS is for and when it is required

Every engine room makes oily bilge water. It comes from gland leakage, purifier drains, leaking flanges, tank top washing and condensate. MARPOL Annex I lets you discharge this machinery space bilge water to sea only if it has been treated and only under strict conditions. The oily water separator, together with its 15 ppm bilge alarm, is the equipment that makes this legal.

At chapter level, the requirements are:

  • MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 14 covers the oil filtering equipment itself. Ships of 400 GT and above must carry oil filtering equipment. Ships of 10,000 GT and above must also have an alarm and an arrangement that stops the discharge automatically when the oil content goes above 15 ppm.
  • MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 15 sets the discharge conditions. These are covered in detail in the regulations section below.
  • Resolution MEPC.107(49) gives the type-approval specifications for equipment installed on or after 1 January 2005. This covers the separator, the 15 ppm bilge alarm and the tamper-resistance features.

The OWS is listed in the supplement to the IOPP Certificate. Form A is used for ships other than oil tankers and Form B for oil tankers. Port State Control inspectors commonly examine it.

On oil tankers, cargo pump-room bilges and cargo residues must not go through the machinery space OWS. Discharges from the cargo area fall under a separate regulation with different limits.

Main components

Makers differ in the details, but a typical modern installation has these parts:

Component Function
Bilge wells and bilge water holding tank Collect the oily water. The holding tank gives time for gross oil to settle out before treatment.
Feed pump Usually a positive displacement, low-shear pump such as an eccentric screw (mono) or piston type. It is chosen so that it does not churn the oil into a fine emulsion.
First stage (gravity/coalescer) Plate packs or coalescing media slow the flow. Small oil droplets join into larger ones and rise to the top.
Oil collecting space and oil level probe A capacitive or similar probe senses the oil–water interface. It opens a solenoid or pneumatic valve to drain the separated oil to the waste oil or sludge tank.
Heating coil (on some units) Lowers oil viscosity to help separation. It must stay within maker's limits.
Second stage Coalescing filter elements, absorption filters or membrane or other polishing units take out the remaining fine droplets.
15 ppm bilge alarm (oil content meter) Takes a continuous sample of the effluent. It measures oil content optically, usually by light scattering and/or absorption.
Three-way valve / automatic stopping device When the alarm trips, it diverts the effluent back to the bilge holding tank and stops the overboard discharge.
Sampling cocks, clean water flushing line, overboard valve Used for testing, cleaning the sensor and isolating the discharge. Many companies seal or lock the overboard valve.

Many ships also have a separate oily bilge tank and a sludge tank. The sludge tank is governed by Annex I Regulation 12. Know which tank on your ship takes the separated oil.

How it works, step by step

This is a general sequence. Always follow the maker's manual and your company SMS, because some companies require the Chief Engineer's or Master's permission before every overboard discharge.

  1. Check the conditions.
    • The ship must be proceeding en route.
    • It must not be in the Antarctic area, Arctic waters (Polar Code), or any other area where national rules or your SMS prohibit discharge.
    • The bridge should be informed.
    • The sludge or waste oil tank must have room for the separated oil.
  2. Let the holding tank settle. Skim or transfer the top oil layer to the sludge tank first if your system allows it. Feeding a thick oil layer straight into the OWS overloads it.
  3. Fill and purge. Fill the separator with clean water (sea or fresh, as designed) and vent the air. An air-locked separator separates badly, and air bubbles upset the bilge alarm.
  4. Start the feed pump. Check that the flow is within rated capacity and that suction and discharge pressures are normal.
  5. Watch the oil discharge cycle. The level probe should open the oil drain valve when oil builds up and close it when water reaches the probe again.
  6. Monitor the 15 ppm alarm. Above 15 ppm, the three-way valve sends the effluent back to the bilge tank (and on many units the system shuts down). If the reading stays near the limit, stop and investigate. Do not keep restarting.
  7. Stop correctly. Flush the separator and the bilge alarm sensor with clean water so that oil does not set on the elements and the measuring cell.

  8. Record it. Make the Oil Record Book Part I entry, normally under code D. (Code E is used where the discharge is started automatically.) Include:

    • start and stop times and positions
    • quantity discharged
    • the tank emptied

    The figures must agree with tank soundings.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each operation - Check the oil drain valve and level probe for correct action. - Look for leaks from the feed pump seal and pipework. - Confirm that the three-way valve actually moves. Many engineers test the alarm function by drawing a sample at the cock, as the maker permits.

Periodic (per PMS and maker) - Clean the coalescer plates and the separator body, and drain any settled sediment. - Renew the coalescing or absorption elements. Pressure drop across the filter stage is the usual indicator. - Clean the bilge alarm measuring cell (cuvette). A dirty cell gives false high readings. - Overhaul the feed pump. Worn stators and rotors on screw pumps reduce flow and can increase shearing. - Test the level probe and the solenoid valves.

Bilge alarm calibration and records - MEPC.107(49)-type alarms must record date, time and alarm status. They must keep these records for at least 18 months. - Their accuracy is checked by the manufacturer or an authorised person at intervals required by the guidelines and the flag Administration. Keep the calibration certificate on board. - The alarm is designed and sealed so that it cannot be adjusted by the crew. Never break those seals.

Source control - Much OWS trouble starts in the bilges, so keep them clean. - Use only cleaning chemicals approved as separator-friendly (quick-breaking) by your company. - Keep detergent-laden water, soot water and chemical drains out of the oily bilge system where possible.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Alarm reads high from start-up Air in the sample line, dirty measuring cell, separator not filled with clean water first, sample valve partly closed
Reading creeps up during operation Elements saturated, oil layer not being drained (probe or valve fault), feed rate too high, too much oil in the feed
High reading that will not clear even with good separation Stable emulsion from detergents or chemicals, fine solids (rust, soot) scattering light in the sensor
Oil drain valve never opens Fouled or failed level probe, solenoid failure, no control air or power, strainer choked
Low flow, high suction vacuum Choked suction strainer, worn feed pump, closed or blocked valve
Rising pressure difference across the second stage Clogged filter elements, which are due for change
Water found in the sludge tank Oil drain valve stuck open or probe set wrongly

Worked scenario. You start the OWS and the alarm reads high at once, then falls to a few ppm after a minute.

  • This is typical of air or old oil in the sample line after a period of standby.
  • Flush with clean water and run again before deciding the separator is faulty.
  • If instead the reading climbs steadily over twenty minutes of a run, suspect saturated elements or an oil layer that is not being removed. Stop and inspect.

Safety, compliance and the 'magic pipe' trap

OWS bypassing and false Oil Record Book entries are among the most frequently prosecuted MARPOL Annex I violations. Port state authorities, especially in the United States, have prosecuted shipping companies and individual engineers personally. The cases involve:

  • bypass hoses (the so-called magic pipe)
  • flushing the sensor with fresh water during discharge so that it reads clean
  • false Oil Record Book entries

Crew who blow the whistle have been central to many of these cases.

Practical rules:

  • Never connect any temporary pipe or hose that could bypass the OWS or the bilge alarm.
  • Never dilute the sample or feed clean water to the alarm during discharge to fool it.
  • Never make an ORB entry that does not match what actually happened and what the soundings show. Inspectors compare ORB figures, tank soundings, alarm records and incinerator or shore delivery receipts.
  • If you are told to do any of the above, refuse and report through the SMS (Designated Person Ashore) or the flag state.
  • If the OWS is defective, record it under code F of the ORB and report it to the company. Keep the bilge water on board for shore disposal until repairs are done.
  • Normal personal precautions apply: gloves and eye protection when changing elements, and lock-out tag-out on feed pump and heater circuits.

Oral exam style questions

Q: What are the conditions for discharging machinery space bilge water outside special areas? A: All of these must be met:

  • The ship is 400 GT or above and proceeding en route.
  • The water is processed through approved oil filtering equipment.
  • The oil content of the undiluted effluent does not exceed 15 ppm.
  • On oil tankers, the water does not originate from cargo pump-room bilges and is not mixed with cargo residues.

Q: Why is a positive displacement pump preferred for the feed? A: It causes low shear and does not break oil into fine droplets. Fine droplets are hard to separate and may form an emulsion that the separator cannot handle.

Q: What happens when the 15 ppm alarm trips on a ship over 10,000 GT? A: The automatic stopping device acts. Normally the three-way valve diverts the effluent back to the bilge tank, so nothing above 15 ppm goes overboard.

Q: How long must the bilge alarm keep its records? A: At least 18 months, for equipment to MEPC.107(49).

Q: Where do you record an OWS discharge, and where do you record a breakdown? A: Discharge goes in Oil Record Book Part I under code D. (Code E is used where the discharge is started automatically.) The condition or failure of the oil filtering equipment goes under code F.

Q: Is discharge permitted in the Antarctic area? A: No. Any discharge of oil or oily mixtures from any ship is prohibited there.

Q: Name three causes of a false high reading on the bilge alarm. A: Any three of these:

  • air bubbles in the sample
  • a dirty measuring cell
  • fine solids such as rust or soot
  • emulsified water from detergents
Key points
  • The OWS lets machinery space bilge water go overboard only at 15 ppm oil content or less, undiluted, with the ship en route.
  • Ships of 400 GT and above need oil filtering equipment; 10,000 GT and above also need the 15 ppm alarm with automatic stopping.
  • The feed pump is low-shear positive displacement so it does not emulsify the oil.
  • False high readings usually come from air, a dirty sensor cell or fine solids; flush with clean water before blaming the separator.
  • MEPC.107(49)-type bilge alarms record alarm status and keep the data for at least 18 months.
  • Discharges go in ORB Part I under code D (code E if started automatically); equipment condition and failures go under code F.
  • Any bypass, sensor dilution or false entry can lead to personal criminal prosecution.

Frequently asked questions

What does 15 ppm mean on an oily water separator?

It means 15 parts of oil per million parts of effluent. This is the maximum oil content allowed for discharging treated machinery space bilge water to sea under MARPOL Annex I. The 15 ppm bilge alarm measures the undiluted effluent continuously. It must stop or divert the discharge if the limit is exceeded.

Can I use the OWS when the ship is at anchor or alongside?

No, not for overboard discharge. MARPOL Annex I requires the ship to be proceeding en route when discharging processed bilge water. Many ports and company SMS rules add further restrictions, so keep the water on board and land it ashore if the ship is not proceeding en route.

Why does the 15 ppm alarm keep tripping when the water looks clean?

The sensor measures oil optically, so anything that scatters light can push the reading up. Common culprits are: - air bubbles - a fouled measuring cell - rust or soot particles - emulsions from detergents Flush the sensor with clean water, purge any air and check the bilge water source before suspecting the separator stages.

Who is allowed to calibrate the 15 ppm bilge alarm?

Calibration and accuracy checks are done by the manufacturer or a person authorised by them. This is done at intervals set by the guidelines and the flag Administration. Ship's crew must not adjust or break the seals on a type-approved bilge alarm. Keep the current calibration certificate on board for surveys and PSC.

What should I do if the OWS breaks down at sea?

Stop all overboard discharge and record the defect in the Oil Record Book Part I under code F. Then report it to the Chief Engineer and the company. Retain the bilge water in the holding tank and arrange shore delivery if needed. Never improvise a workaround that sends untreated water overboard.

Test yourself

What is the maximum oil content permitted in processed machinery space bilge water discharged at sea?

MARPOL Annex I Regulation 15 limits undiluted effluent to 15 ppm. The 30 litres per nautical mile figure belongs to the separate tanker cargo-area regime.

Why is the OWS feed pump normally a low-shear positive displacement type?

High-shear pumps emulsify oil, and emulsions defeat gravity and coalescing separation. This is standard design practice reflected in maker's manuals.

On a ship of 10,000 GT and above, what must happen when the bilge alarm detects more than 15 ppm?

MARPOL Annex I Regulation 14 requires an alarm and automatic stopping arrangement on these ships. Dilution is prohibited.

The bilge alarm reads high immediately after start-up and then settles low. What is the most likely cause?

Air bubbles and stale oil in the sample line cause transient false high readings. Saturated elements give a reading that rises steadily, per typical maker's guidance.

Under which Oil Record Book Part I code is an overboard discharge of bilge water through the OWS normally recorded?

Code D covers non-automatic starting of the discharge overboard of machinery space bilge water (code E if started automatically). Code C covers sludge and oil residues, H covers bunkering and F covers the condition of the oil filtering equipment (MARPOL Annex I, Appendix III).
