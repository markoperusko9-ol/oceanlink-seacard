Emergency Fire Pump: How It Works, Testing and Common Faults

Quick answer

The emergency fire pump is an independently driven, fixed pump located outside the main machinery space. It keeps water on the fire main when a fire in any one compartment could put all the main fire pumps out of action. SOLAS Chapter II-2 Regulation 10 sets when it is required and how it must be arranged, and the FSS Code Chapter 12 sets its technical standard. Your job is to know where it is, how to start it alone and in the dark, and how to prove it delivers water to deck.

Why the emergency fire pump exists

On most cargo ships the main fire pumps sit in the engine room. They are usually the fire pumps, the general service pumps or the fire and ballast pumps. That arrangement fails exactly when you need water most, because an engine room fire can kill the pumps, their power supply or the sea suction.

SOLAS Chapter II-2 Regulation 10 deals with this. In cargo ships it requires an alternative means of supplying water if a fire in any one compartment could put all the fire pumps out of action. That alternative is normally a fixed, independently driven emergency fire pump. Passenger ships have their own, stricter arrangement requirements in the same regulation. Some smaller cargo ships may meet the requirement by placing main fire pumps in separate compartments instead. Whether your ship needs a dedicated emergency fire pump depends on her size, type and layout, as approved by the flag Administration and class.

The principle every examiner wants to hear is this: the emergency fire pump, its source of power and its sea connection must not be knocked out by the same fire that disables the main fire pumps.

Main components and typical arrangements

You will meet three common layouts at sea.

Type Drive Typical location
Diesel-driven centrifugal Own dedicated diesel engine Separate pump room, steering flat area or a space aft or forward
Electric centrifugal Motor fed from the emergency switchboard Separate space low in the ship, or a submersible pump in a trunk
Hydraulic or submersible Hydraulic power pack, often diesel or emergency-supplied Pump in a sea trunk, power pack in a separate space

Typical components are:

  • Sea chest and sea suction valve. These are normally dedicated to the emergency pump and located outside the main machinery space. Where SOLAS permits the sea chest to be inside, the suction valve must be remotely operable from the emergency pump compartment and the pipe kept as short as practicable.
  • Suction strainer. It is often a basket type that can be cleaned without opening the hull.
  • Pump. This is almost always centrifugal, single-stage or multi-stage.
  • Priming arrangement. This may be a vacuum or ejector priming device, a self-priming design, or a pump mounted low enough to have a positive suction head.
  • Driver. This is a diesel engine with its own fuel tank, starting system and cooling, or an electric or hydraulic motor.
  • Discharge valve and non-return valve into the fire main.
  • Relief valve. It is fitted where the pump can exceed the safe working pressure of the fire main.
  • Pressure gauge and compound gauge on the discharge and suction.
  • Isolating valve on the fire main. It sits outside the machinery space and separates the engine room section of the main from the deck section.

What SOLAS and the FSS Code require (chapter level)

The detailed technical standard for fixed emergency fire pumps on cargo ships is in FSS Code Chapter 12. In outline:

  • Capacity. The pump must deliver at least a set proportion of the total required fire pump capacity, with an absolute minimum. While delivering that quantity, it must maintain the SOLAS minimum hydrant pressure. That pressure depends on ship type and tonnage.
  • Suction. The total suction head and net positive suction head must let the pump work under all the list, trim, roll and pitch the ship is likely to see. This is generally taken to include normal ballast conditions; only the ballast condition on entering or leaving dry dock is excluded.
  • Diesel drive. The engine must be readily startable from cold. Hand cranking is the usual method, with alternatives permitted where hand starting is impractical. It must have a service fuel tank holding enough fuel to run at full load for a specified period. Further fuel must be held outside the main machinery space.

The arrangement requirements are in SOLAS, not the FSS Code:

  • Location and access (SOLAS II-2 Reg 10). The pump space must be separated from machinery spaces of category A, with access independent of the main machinery space. Ventilation must be arranged so smoke from a machinery space fire cannot enter, and the space needs adequate lighting. Short lengths of suction or discharge piping may pass through the machinery space only if suitably protected.

Check the current FSS Code and SOLAS text and your class rules for the exact capacity, pressure and fuel-endurance figures that apply to your ship. Do not quote numbers in an oral exam unless you have read them in the instrument.

If the pump is electrically driven, it is one of the services the emergency source of electrical power must supply under SOLAS Chapter II-1.

Routine maintenance and testing fall under the onboard maintenance plan required by SOLAS II-2 Regulation 14. IMO guidance on inspection intervals is in MSC.1/Circ.1432.

Starting and running it: step by step

The exact sequence is in your ship's instruction posted at the pump. A typical diesel-driven start goes like this:

  1. Communicate. Inform the bridge and the engine control room. During a test, post a crew member on deck at a hydrant with a radio.
  2. Line up the suction. Open the sea suction valve and check the strainer cover is tight.
  3. Isolate the engine room section. Close the fire main isolating valve outside the machinery space. If you skip this, much of your water runs into a damaged engine room main and deck pressure collapses.
  4. Check the engine. Confirm fuel is open, the fuel level is adequate, and lube oil and coolant levels are correct. Confirm battery voltage or hydraulic accumulator pressure if fitted.
  5. Start the engine using the primary method: electric, hydraulic or hand crank. Keep the alternative method in reserve.
  6. Prime the pump. Engage the priming device or ejector until the suction compound gauge shows vacuum and water appears. Then disengage it if it is clutch-operated.
  7. Open the discharge slowly. Watch discharge pressure build and check the relief valve is not lifting continuously.
  8. Prove water on deck. Open the hydrants agreed for the test, typically the highest and most remote ones. Confirm effective jets and record the pressure.
  9. Monitor. Check engine temperature, oil pressure, gland leakage and vibration.
  10. Shut down. Close the hydrants, then the pump discharge. Stop the drive and close the sea suction. Open the isolating valve again and return all valves to their normal sea-going position (isolating valve normally open), then top up fuel and record the test in the log and PMS.

For an electrically driven pump, the steps are the same. The difference is that you start from the local starter or a remote push-button and confirm the supply is coming from the emergency switchboard.

Routine checks and maintenance

Frequencies come from your planned maintenance system, the maker's manual and the flag-approved onboard maintenance plan. Common practice is:

  • Regular running test. Start the pump, prime it, deliver water to deck and log the pressures. Many companies do this weekly or alongside the fire drill.
  • Starting system. Check battery condition and charger, hydraulic accumulator pressure and the hand-crank gear. Try the secondary start method periodically.
  • Fuel. Keep the service tank topped up after every run. Drain water from the tank bottom and keep the fuel shut-off accessible.
  • Engine. Check lube oil level, coolant, V-belts, air filter and exhaust. Change filters per the maker's schedule.
  • Pump. Clean the suction strainer, check gland packing or the mechanical seal, and look for corrosion on the impeller and casing. Overhaul as per the PMS.
  • Valves. Operate the sea suction, discharge, isolating and relief valves through their full travel. Seized valves are a classic failure.
  • Space. Check that lighting, emergency lighting, ventilation and access are clear and that the space is not used as a store. The operating instructions must be posted and legible.

The emergency fire pump is a regular target for Port State Control. Inspectors often ask the crew to start it themselves and produce water at a hydrant. The outcome depends on whether the crew can do it without the chief engineer talking them through it.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Engine will not start Flat batteries or failed charger, low hydraulic accumulator pressure, fuel valve shut, air in fuel lines, water in fuel, cold engine
Pump runs but no discharge pressure Not primed, sea valve closed, strainer choked, air leak on the suction side, priming device failed
Pressure builds then drops Air drawn in through gland or suction joints, shallow draught causing loss of suction in a seaway, sea chest vent problem
Good pump pressure, poor jets on deck Isolating valve left open, hydrants open elsewhere, leaking or burst fire main
Noise and vibration Cavitation from excessive suction lift or a blocked strainer, worn bearings, misalignment
Engine overheats Cooling water supply from the pump discharge blocked, low coolant, slipping belt
Relief valve lifting continuously Wrong setting, or the discharge throttled with no hydrant open

Worked scenario. You are in ballast with a stern trim and the sea is rough. The pump primes but loses pressure every time the stern lifts. The suction is breaking the surface or drawing air, because the pump is being asked to lift beyond its capability. Report it. Ballast the ship deeper aft if possible, check the suction strainer and joints, and consider this a deficiency to raise with the master and company. The requirement is that the pump works under the list, trim, roll and pitch likely in service, and a normal ballast passage is generally taken to be one of those conditions.

Safety precautions

  • Never leave hydrants or hoses unattended while the pump is running. A charged hose that whips loose can seriously injure someone.
  • Wear hearing protection in the pump space with a diesel running, and keep loose clothing away from belts and couplings.
  • Hand cranking can kick back. Use the correct grip and decompression lever, and follow the maker's instructions.
  • Treat the emergency fire pump space as possibly poorly ventilated. This applies especially if it is low in the ship or reached via a trunk. Follow your enclosed space procedure if the space is classified as one.
  • Do not isolate or dismantle the pump without permission. Taking the pump out of service needs the chief engineer's and master's approval, and is usually done under a permit to work. Have an alternative arrangement in place and record it.
  • Restore the system after work. Leave valves in the normal sea-going position and log any change.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why must the emergency fire pump be outside the machinery space? A: A single fire in the machinery space must not disable both the main fire pumps and the emergency pump. Its power source and sea connection must also be protected from that fire. SOLAS allows limited exceptions, such as a sea chest inside the machinery space with a suction valve remotely operable from the emergency pump compartment, or short protected pipe runs.

Q: What is the first valve you check after starting the emergency fire pump in a real engine room fire? A: The fire main isolating valve outside the machinery space. It must be closed so the water goes to deck and the hydrants feeding boundary cooling, not into a damaged section of main.

Q: How is a centrifugal emergency fire pump primed? A: Usually with a vacuum or ejector priming device. Alternatively, the pump is self-priming or is mounted below the waterline so it has a positive suction head.

Q: Where are the requirements found? A: SOLAS Chapter II-2 Regulation 10 says when fire pumps and the emergency pump are needed and how they must be arranged and located. The FSS Code Chapter 12 gives the technical standard. Maintenance falls under SOLAS II-2 Regulation 14.

Q: The PSCO asks you to start it. What do you do? A: Inform the bridge and ECR, follow the posted instructions, and line up the suction and isolating valve. Start, prime and deliver water to the agreed hydrant, then report the pressure and the time taken.

Key points
  • The emergency fire pump keeps the fire main working when one compartment fire disables all main fire pumps.
  • The pump, its power source and its sea connection must not be disabled by a fire in the space containing the main fire pumps (limited SOLAS exceptions apply to the sea chest and short protected pipe runs).
  • SOLAS II-2 Reg 10 sets the requirement and arrangement; FSS Code Chapter 12 sets the technical standard.
  • Always close the fire main isolating valve outside the engine room before relying on the emergency pump, and reopen it afterwards.
  • Most no-water faults come from priming, suction air leaks, a choked strainer or a closed sea valve.
  • It must work in normal ballast conditions with the ship rolling and pitching, not just in calm loaded conditions.
  • Every crew member on the fire party should be able to start it unaided; PSC inspectors frequently test this.

Frequently asked questions

Is an emergency fire pump required on every merchant ship?

Not necessarily. SOLAS II-2 Regulation 10 requires an alternative means of water supply where a fire in one compartment could put all fire pumps out of action, and that is usually met with an emergency fire pump. The exact requirement depends on ship type, size and the approved layout, so check your ship's fire control plan and class documents.

How often should the emergency fire pump be tested?

The frequency is set by your ship's onboard maintenance plan under SOLAS II-2 Regulation 14, the company PMS and the maker's manual. IMO guidance is in MSC.1/Circ.1432. Many companies run it weekly or at each fire drill, delivering water to deck.

Why does the emergency fire pump lose suction in ballast condition?

At light draught the suction lift increases. In a seaway the sea chest may draw air as the ship rolls and pitches, and a centrifugal pump then loses prime. If it cannot hold suction in normal ballast conditions, treat it as a deficiency and report it.

What is the fire main isolating valve for?

It separates the engine room section of the fire main from the deck section. When the emergency pump is used during an engine room fire, closing it stops water being lost into damaged piping in the machinery space. It is located outside the machinery space so it can be reached safely, and is normally kept open at sea.

Can the emergency fire pump be electrically driven?

Yes. Many ships have an electric emergency fire pump supplied from the emergency switchboard, with power from the emergency generator. In that case the emergency source of power is part of the system, and its own tests matter just as much.

Test yourself

What is the main design principle behind the emergency fire pump arrangement?

The pump, its power source and its sea connection must not be disabled by a fire in the space containing the main fire pumps, subject to limited exceptions. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 10.

During an engine room fire you start the emergency fire pump but deck hydrant pressure is very poor. What should you check first?

If the isolating valve is open, water escapes into the engine room section of the main. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 10 (fire main arrangements).

Which IMO instrument contains the technical standard for fixed emergency fire pumps on cargo ships?

Chapter 12 of the Fire Safety Systems Code covers fixed emergency fire pumps. Reference: FSS Code, Chapter 12.

The pump runs and the compound gauge shows no vacuum, and no water is delivered. What is the most likely cause?

A centrifugal pump cannot lift water without priming, and suction-side air leaks break the prime. Reference: pump operating principles; FSS Code Chapter 12 (suction head and NPSH requirements).

Under which SOLAS regulation must the emergency fire pump be covered by an onboard maintenance plan?

Regulation 14 of Chapter II-2 requires fire protection systems and appliances to be maintained and tested under an onboard maintenance plan. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 14; MSC.1/Circ.1432.
