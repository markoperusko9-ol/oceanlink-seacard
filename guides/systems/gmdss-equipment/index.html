GMDSS Equipment: What Is Fitted, How It Works and How to Keep It Ready

Quick answer

GMDSS equipment is the set of radios, satellite terminals and locating devices that SOLAS Chapter IV requires so a ship in distress can alert shore authorities and nearby ships quickly. The equipment fitted depends on the sea areas the ship trades in (A1 to A4). It is only useful if it is tested on a routine, logged properly and operated by a certificated GMDSS operator.

Purpose of GMDSS and the sea area concept

The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System is built on one idea. A distress alert goes ship-to-shore first. A Rescue Coordination Centre (RCC) then coordinates the response. You do not rely only on another ship hearing you on Channel 16.

GMDSS covers these functions:

  • Distress alerting (ship-to-shore, ship-to-ship, shore-to-ship)
  • SAR coordinating communications
  • On-scene communications
  • Locating signals
  • Promulgation of Maritime Safety Information (MSI)
  • General radiocommunications
  • Bridge-to-bridge communications

The equipment a ship must carry depends on the sea areas it trades in:

Sea area Defined by
A1 Within radiotelephone coverage of at least one VHF coast station with continuous DSC alerting
A2 Within MF coast station DSC coverage, excluding A1
A3 Within coverage of a recognised mobile satellite service, excluding A1 and A2
A4 Everything outside A1, A2 and A3 (mainly polar regions)

The actual ranges of A1 and A2 are declared by each coastal state. They are published in the official lists of radio signals. Do not assume a fixed mileage.

SOLAS Chapter IV applies to all passenger ships and to cargo ships of 300 GT and above on international voyages. The chapter was substantially revised in amendments that entered into force on 1 January 2024. One effect is that A3 is now defined by any recognised mobile satellite service, not by Inmarsat alone. Your flag administration decides exactly what your ship must carry. The Record of Equipment attached to the Safety Radio Certificate lists it.

Main components on a typical deep-sea ship

A ship trading in A1, A2 and A3 will normally have the following.

VHF with DSC - DSC on Channel 70. - Radiotelephony on Ch 16 (distress and calling), Ch 13 (bridge-to-bridge) and Ch 6 (SAR on-scene). - A dedicated DSC watch receiver on Ch 70.

MF/HF with DSC - MF distress DSC is 2187.5 kHz and MF distress voice is 2182 kHz. - HF DSC distress frequencies are 4207.5, 6312, 8414.5, 12577 and 16804.5 kHz. - A ship with HF fitted watches 8414.5 kHz plus at least one other HF DSC frequency suited to time of day and position.

Ship Earth Station (SES) - A satellite terminal of a recognised service (for example Inmarsat C or Fleet Safety, or Iridium on ships that carry it). - It provides a distress alert facility and an Enhanced Group Call (EGC) receiver for MSI beyond NAVTEX range.

NAVTEX receiver - 518 kHz carries international English-language broadcasts. - 490 kHz is used for national broadcasts where provided.

Satellite EPIRB - Transmits on 406 MHz via Cospas-Sarsat, with a 121.5 MHz homing signal. - Newer units also transmit an AIS locating signal. - Float-free with a hydrostatic release unit (HRU).

Search and rescue locating devices - Radar SART (responds to X-band 9 GHz radar) or AIS-SART. - At least one on each side of passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and above. - At least one on cargo ships of 300 to 500 GT.

Portable two-way VHF radiotelephones for survival craft - At least three on passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and above. - At least two on cargo ships of 300 to 500 GT.

Reserve source of energy - Usually a bank of batteries with an automatic charger. - It must power the radio installation for at least 1 hour if the ship's emergency source fully supplies the radios, or 6 hours if it does not.

Position input - GNSS feeds the DSC equipment so alerts carry position and time automatically.

How a distress alert works, step by step

Worked scenario. You are 3rd Officer on watch in sea area A2. A fire in the engine room is out of control, and the Master orders a distress alert.

  1. Confirm the order and the facts. Get the nature of distress, position, persons on board and intentions.
  2. Send a DSC distress alert, normally from the MF/HF or VHF DSC unit.
    • Lift the cover and select the nature of distress (fire/explosion) if time allows.
    • Press and hold the distress button for as long as the set requires, typically a few seconds.
    • An undesignated alert sent from the button alone is still valid. Never waste time in a real emergency.
  3. The set repeats the alert automatically until it is acknowledged. Watch for the acknowledgement from a coast station.
  4. Follow up by voice on the associated frequency. That is Ch 16 for a VHF alert or 2182 kHz for an MF alert.
    • Start with the distress call: MAYDAY, THIS IS, then the ship name, call sign and MMSI.
    • Then give the distress message: MAYDAY, ship name, call sign/MMSI, position, nature of distress, assistance required, persons on board, any other information, OVER.
  5. Use another means if needed. In A3, or if MF/VHF gets no response, send an alert via the satellite terminal.
  6. Log everything in the GMDSS radio log, with UTC times.
  7. If abandoning, take the portable VHFs and SARTs into the survival craft.
    • Activate the SART only when in the craft.
    • Let the EPIRB float free, or activate it manually and take it with you.

Receiving another ship's alert is a common exam trap.

  • On VHF or MF, do not rush to acknowledge by DSC. Listen on the associated voice frequency and allow time for a coast station to acknowledge. Acknowledge by voice if you are in a position to help.
  • On HF, do not acknowledge by DSC. Listen on the associated voice frequency. If no coast station acknowledges or responds within about 5 minutes, inform the RCC or coast station by any appropriate means. Do not send a DSC distress relay to all ships.
  • Inform the Master immediately and log it.

Routine tests and maintenance

Exact routines are set by your company SMS, the equipment manuals and administration guidance. A typical pattern looks like this, and every test is recorded in the GMDSS log.

Daily - Check DSC equipment by internal test without radiating. - Check the battery voltage and charger, and that the GNSS position and time in the DSC sets are current. - Check the printer paper and the NAVTEX and EGC receivers.

Weekly - Make an MF/HF DSC test call to a coast station, where this is permitted. - Do a battery off-charge voltage check.

Monthly - Do the EPIRB and SART self-tests, following the manufacturer's procedure. - Check the expiry dates of batteries and HRUs, and the condition of lanyards and brackets. - Check the portable VHFs and their sealed primary batteries. - Check the security and condition of reserve batteries, terminals and electrolyte where applicable.

Periodic and statutory - The radio installation is surveyed for the Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificate (or the passenger ship certificate), including periodic surveys around each anniversary date. - The satellite EPIRB must be tested at intervals not exceeding 12 months. - The EPIRB must undergo shore-based maintenance at intervals not exceeding five years.

Maintenance arrangements. SOLAS requires availability to be assured by one or more of three methods: - duplication of equipment - shore-based maintenance - at-sea electronic maintenance capability

Ships in A1 and A2 use at least one method. Ships in A3 and A4 use a combination of at least two. Most modern ships combine duplication with shore-based maintenance.

Do not use VHF Ch 70 for test calls. Carry out VHF DSC checks with the internal test facility.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause First check
DSC alert shows wrong or no position GNSS input lost, or manual position not updated NMEA connection and GNSS status; enter position manually if needed
Poor VHF range, "high SWR" alarm Damaged antenna, wet connector, coax fault Visual check aloft (with permit), connector condition
MF/HF will not tune or transmit Antenna tuning unit fault, salt-encrusted insulator, broken whip ATU alarms, insulator cleanliness
Satellite terminal "not logged in" Wrong ocean region, antenna blocked, terminal fault Log-in status, line of sight to satellite, reboot per manual
No NAVTEX messages for days Wrong station selection, antenna or receiver fault Station and message-type settings, antenna
Low reserve battery voltage Charger failure, ageing cells Charger output, specific gravity or cell condition
EPIRB self-test fails Battery expired or depleted, internal fault Expiry label; replace or land for service

Registration after a change of flag. The EPIRB is coded with a country-specific identity, often the MMSI. When a ship changes flag or MMSI, the EPIRB must be re-programmed and re-registered with the new flag's registry. A wrongly coded EPIRB can delay SAR.

Safety precautions and false alerts

Radiation and RF burns - Do not touch MF/HF antennas or the ATU while the set may transmit. - Before working aloft near antennas or radar scanners, isolate the transmitters under the permit to work. - Tag the equipment as part of lock-out tag-out.

Batteries - Lead-acid batteries give off hydrogen when charging. Keep the battery room ventilated and free of ignition sources. - Wear eye protection when handling electrolyte. - EPIRBs, SARTs and portable VHFs use lithium batteries. Do not open, puncture or improperly dispose of them.

Antennas and paint - Never paint over antennas, insulators or EPIRB float-free mechanisms. - Do not stow gear where it would obstruct a float-free release.

False alerts False alerts waste SAR resources. In some jurisdictions they can lead to penalties.

  1. Inadvertent DSC alert. Stop the transmission or repetition and cancel it using the set's cancel function where fitted. Then make a voice cancellation on the associated frequency (Ch 16 or 2182 kHz). Say "All stations" three times, then give ship name, call sign, MMSI and position, and cancel the distress alert sent at the stated UTC time. Log it.
  2. Inadvertent EPIRB activation. Switch it off and contact the nearest RCC or coast station by any means to cancel. Log it and inform the Master and company.
  3. Inadvertent satellite alert. Notify the appropriate RCC via the satellite terminal or another means and cancel. Log it.

Testing EPIRBs and SARTs - Use only the self-test function. - Never test by full activation. - Keep SART tests short to avoid confusing other ships' radars.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners usually go beyond lists and ask what you would actually do.

  • What are the GMDSS sea areas, and what equipment does a ship trading in A3 need? Explain the area definitions. Then go through the VHF and MF/HF DSC, satellite SES with EGC, NAVTEX, EPIRB, SARTs, portable VHFs and the reserve source.
  • You receive a VHF DSC distress alert in A1. What do you do? Inform the Master and listen on Ch 16. Allow the coast station time to acknowledge, then acknowledge by voice if you can assist. Do not acknowledge by DSC unless directed. Log it.
  • Your cadet presses the distress button by mistake. Action? Cancel by DSC where possible and make a voice cancellation on the associated frequency. Log it and inform the Master.
  • How long must the reserve source last? One hour if the emergency source fully supplies the radios, otherwise six hours.
  • How do you know the EPIRB is in date? Check the battery and HRU expiry labels and the annual test record. Check the shore-based maintenance date, which must not exceed five years.
  • What is the difference between a radar SART and an AIS-SART on the display? A radar SART shows as a line of 12 dots outward from its position on an X-band radar. An AIS-SART appears as an AIS target with a SART identity on AIS-capable displays.
  • Who may operate the GMDSS installation? A person holding the appropriate certificate (GOC or ROC) recognised by the flag. One person is designated as primarily responsible for radiocommunications during distress incidents.
Key points
  • SOLAS Chapter IV sets GMDSS carriage based on the sea areas (A1–A4) the ship trades in.
  • VHF DSC distress is Ch 70, MF DSC distress is 2187.5 kHz, and the voice follow-up is on Ch 16 or 2182 kHz.
  • NAVTEX international broadcasts are on 518 kHz; EGC covers MSI beyond NAVTEX range.
  • The satellite EPIRB must be tested at least every 12 months and shore-serviced at least every five years.
  • The reserve source must last 1 hour if the emergency source supplies the radios, otherwise 6 hours.
  • Cancel any false alert immediately by DSC where possible and by voice, and log it.
  • Log every test, distress, urgency and safety communication in the GMDSS radio log with UTC times.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a GOC and an ROC for GMDSS?

The General Operator's Certificate (GOC) covers operation of all GMDSS equipment for sea areas A1 to A4. The Restricted Operator's Certificate (ROC) covers VHF equipment for ships operating only in sea area A1. Most deep-sea officers need the GOC, but the flag administration decides what it accepts and may require an endorsement.

What should I do if I press the DSC distress button by mistake?

Stop the alert and use the set's cancel function if it has one. Then make a voice cancellation on the associated frequency (Ch 16 for VHF, 2182 kHz for MF), giving ship name, call sign, MMSI, position and the time of the false alert. Inform the Master and log it.

How often should the EPIRB and SART be tested on board?

Self-tests are usually done monthly using the manufacturer's procedure, but your company SMS sets the exact routine. Separately, SOLAS requires the satellite EPIRB to be tested at intervals not exceeding 12 months and to receive shore-based maintenance at intervals not exceeding five years. Never test either device by full activation.

Does the EPIRB need changing when the ship changes flag?

Usually yes. The EPIRB's coded identity is tied to the flag's country code, often through the MMSI. It must be re-programmed and registered with the new flag's registry. A wrongly coded or unregistered EPIRB can delay rescue.

Is Inmarsat still the only satellite system for GMDSS?

No. Since the SOLAS Chapter IV amendments that entered into force on 1 January 2024, sea area A3 is defined by the coverage of a recognised mobile satellite service. Iridium has also been recognised by IMO. Which service your ship uses is shown in the Record of Equipment.

Test yourself

How is GMDSS sea area A2 defined?

A2 is the area within MF coast station DSC coverage, excluding A1. Its extent is declared by the coastal state (SOLAS Chapter IV, Regulation 2).

On which VHF channel is a DSC distress alert transmitted?

Channel 70 is reserved for DSC. Ch 16 is used for the voice follow-up, Ch 13 for bridge-to-bridge and Ch 6 for SAR on-scene communications (ITU Radio Regulations; SOLAS Chapter IV).

What does SOLAS require for satellite EPIRB testing and maintenance?

SOLAS Chapter IV, Regulation 15 requires annual testing and shore-based maintenance at intervals not exceeding five years. Monthly on-board checks use the self-test only.

A DSC distress alert is sent by mistake on VHF. What is the correct action?

False alerts must be cancelled immediately by DSC where fitted and by voice on the associated frequency, then logged. This is the standard procedure in IMO guidance on avoiding and cancelling false distress alerts (Res. A.814(19)) and the ITU Radio Regulations.

On what frequency are international English-language NAVTEX broadcasts made?

518 kHz is the international NAVTEX frequency. 490 kHz is used for national broadcasts, 2182 kHz is MF distress voice and 8414.5 kHz is an HF DSC frequency (ITU Radio Regulations).
