AIS (Automatic Identification System) on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

AIS is a VHF transponder that automatically broadcasts your ship's identity, position, course, speed and voyage data to other ships and shore stations, and receives theirs. SOLAS Chapter V requires Class A AIS on most seagoing merchant ships. The officer of the watch must keep the manual data correct and check it every watch, and must remember that AIS supports collision avoidance but never replaces radar and a proper look-out.

What AIS is for and who must carry it

AIS was brought in to improve safety of navigation and maritime domain awareness. It lets ships identify each other by name and call sign. It also gives VTS and coast authorities a live traffic picture and supports search and rescue and security monitoring.

The carriage requirement sits in SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19. In outline, AIS is required on:

  • ships of 300 GT and upwards engaged on international voyages
  • cargo ships of 500 GT and upwards not engaged on international voyages
  • all passenger ships, irrespective of size

These ships carry Class A equipment. Smaller craft and yachts often carry Class B, which transmits less often, at lower power and with lower priority. As a result, some small targets will appear later or less often on your display. Many craft carry no AIS at all.

Domestic and non-convention vessels follow their own administration's rules. Fishing vessels and inland craft may have separate national requirements, so check with your flag or coastal state.

Main components

A typical Class A installation consists of:

  • Transponder unit: a VHF transmitter, two TDMA receivers (one for each AIS channel) and a DSC receiver used for channel management.
  • VHF antenna: a dedicated antenna, ideally clear of the main VHF antennas to limit interference.
  • Internal GNSS receiver: provides timing for slot synchronisation. It also gives a back-up position if the external source fails.
  • External position source: normally the ship's main GNSS receiver. This is the position other ships should be seeing.
  • Heading input: from the gyrocompass. A rate of turn (ROT) input is also connected where available.
  • Minimum Keyboard and Display (MKD): the basic display used to enter data and read alarms. Many ships also show AIS targets on radar/ARPA and ECDIS.
  • Pilot plug: a connection near the conning position so a pilot can plug in a portable pilot unit.
  • Power supply: how the unit is fed, including from the emergency source, depends on the ship's design. Know your own ship's arrangement.

How AIS works, step by step

  1. Data collection. The transponder takes position, COG and SOG from GNSS, heading from the gyro, ROT where fitted, and UTC. Static and voyage data come from what has been programmed or entered.
  2. Slot timing. Each minute is divided into time slots on two VHF channels: AIS 1 (161.975 MHz) and AIS 2 (162.025 MHz). GNSS time keeps every unit synchronised.
  3. Self-organising access. Class A units use SOTDMA. Each unit reserves future slots and announces those reservations, so neighbouring ships avoid transmitting over each other. In very congested areas the system reduces range for distant targets but keeps nearby ships reporting.
  4. Transmission. The unit alternates between the two channels and broadcasts three types of data at different rates:
Data type Contents Reporting
Static MMSI, IMO number, name, call sign, length and beam, ship type, antenna position Every 6 minutes and on request
Dynamic Position with accuracy flag, UTC, COG, SOG, heading, ROT, navigational status Every few seconds underway, faster at higher speed or when altering; every 3 minutes at anchor or moored and not moving faster than 3 knots
Voyage-related Draught, hazardous cargo category, destination, ETA Every 6 minutes, and when changed
  1. Reception and display. Every unit within VHF range decodes these messages. Targets are shown on the MKD, radar and ECDIS. Short safety-related text messages can also be sent and received.

Range is VHF line-of-sight, so antenna height matters. Shore networks and satellite receivers extend the picture for authorities, but you only see what your own receiver hears.

Routine checks and maintenance

Before departure (OOW / navigating officer):

  • Set the navigational status correctly, for example under way using engine, at anchor, moored or restricted in ability to manoeuvre. An incorrect navigational status, such as still showing moored or at anchor at sea, is a common and easily avoided error.
  • Update draught, destination, ETA and hazardous cargo category. For destination, use the port's recognised format, or UN/LOCODE where the company asks for it.

Every watch:

  • Check the MKD for alarms.
  • Confirm the position source is external GNSS and not the internal back-up.
  • Confirm heading matches the gyro and the unit is receiving targets.
  • Cross-check a few AIS targets against radar echoes.

Periodically (navigating officer / ETO):

  • Verify static data against ship's documents: MMSI, IMO number, call sign, dimensions and antenna position. Static data is normally password-protected and changed only by authorised personnel.
  • Inspect the antenna, cable runs and connectors for damage and corrosion.
  • Keep a record of faults in line with the company SMS.

Annually:

  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 18 requires an annual performance test by an approved surveyor or an approved testing or servicing facility. IMO guidance on this test is in MSC.1/Circ.1252. Keep the test report on board, because it is a routine PSC and flag inspection item.
  • Software updates and repairs are done by authorised service technicians, not improvised by ship's staff.

The unit's built-in integrity test raises most alarms on the MKD. Log every failure, inform the master, and follow the SMS for reporting defects. A required AIS that is not working can lead to detention or require flag state dispensation, depending on circumstances.

Class A units on tankers automatically switch to low transmit power (1 W) when navigational status is set to moored and SOG is below 3 knots. This is one more reason to keep navigational status correct.

Safety precautions and good practice

  • AIS is an aid, not a source of truth. Other ships' data may be wrong, delayed or missing, and many vessels do not transmit at all. COLREG Rule 7 requires you to use all available means to determine risk of collision, and radar plotting and visual bearings remain primary.
  • COG/SOG is not heading. A target crabbing in a strong set will show a COG quite different from its heading.
  • Don't negotiate passing arrangements on VHF just because you have a name. Comply with the steering and sailing rules.
  • Switching off: IMO guidance allows the master to switch AIS off where continued operation might compromise the safety or security of the ship. The decision is the master's. Record it in the log book and switch AIS back on as soon as the threat has passed. Current industry piracy guidance (BMP) generally recommends keeping AIS switched on in high-risk areas, transmitting limited data, unless the master judges otherwise. Check current regional guidance. Company SMS and flag requirements may add conditions.
  • Working aloft near antennas: the AIS antenna transmits. Follow the permit-to-work and isolation procedures for working aloft near radio and radar aerials.
  • AIS-SART and AIS AtoN: AIS also carries AIS-SART distress transmissions, shown with a distinctive symbol, and buoy or virtual aids to navigation. Treat a virtual AtoN as charted information you must verify, not as a physical mark.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: Which AIS data does the OOW enter manually? Navigational status and voyage-related data: draught, destination, ETA and hazardous cargo category.

Q: Can you use AIS alone to assess risk of collision? No. AIS supplements radar, ARPA and visual observation. Its data can be wrong, and not all vessels transmit.

Q: Your AIS shows internal GNSS as position source. What does that tell you? The external position feed has failed. Your transmitted position may be less accurate, so investigate, inform the master and log it.

Q: When can AIS be switched off? Only on the master's decision, where transmitting would endanger the ship's safety or security. Log the decision and restore AIS when it is safe.

Q: What regulation requires the annual test? SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 18.

Q: Why might a fishing boat ahead not appear on your AIS display? It may carry no AIS, carry Class B with lower reporting rates, be beyond VHF range, or have its AIS switched off. Your look-out and radar must find it anyway.

Key points
  • SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 requires Class A AIS on most seagoing merchant ships and all passenger ships.
  • AIS broadcasts static, dynamic and voyage-related data on 161.975 MHz and 162.025 MHz using self-organised time slots.
  • The OOW is responsible for correct navigational status, draught, destination and ETA.
  • SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 18 requires an annual AIS test, and the report must be kept on board.
  • Common faults are lost GNSS or gyro input, antenna problems and wrong static data. Log and report all of them.
  • AIS supports collision avoidance but never replaces radar, a proper look-out or the COLREGs.
  • Only the master may switch AIS off, for safety or security reasons, and the decision must be logged.

Frequently asked questions

Which ships must carry AIS?

Under SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19, AIS is required on ships of 300 GT and upwards on international voyages, cargo ships of 500 GT and upwards not on international voyages, and all passenger ships regardless of size. Non-convention and domestic vessels follow their flag or coastal state's own rules.

Can the master switch off AIS in a piracy area?

IMO guidance allows the master to switch AIS off where its operation might compromise the safety or security of the ship, provided the decision is logged and AIS is restored as soon as the threat has passed. However, current industry piracy guidance (BMP) generally recommends keeping AIS switched on, transmitting limited data, unless the master judges otherwise. Check current regional guidance and the company SMS.

Why does another ship's AIS heading differ from its course?

Heading comes from the ship's gyro, while COG comes from GNSS and shows the track over ground. With set, leeway or drift the two can differ considerably. A heading shown as unavailable usually means the target's gyro interface has failed.

Who can change the MMSI or ship's dimensions in the AIS?

Static data is normally password-protected and is set at installation or by authorised service personnel. Ship's officers should verify it against the ship's documents and report errors. The exact procedure depends on the equipment and the company SMS.

Is the AIS annual test the same as the radio survey?

The annual AIS test is a separate SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 18 requirement. It is carried out by an approved surveyor or an approved testing or servicing facility, which issues a test report. Some administrations allow it to be done alongside the radio survey, but you need the AIS test report regardless.

Test yourself

Which of the following is entered manually by the OOW rather than taken from a sensor?

Navigational status and voyage data are manual entries, while SOG, heading and ROT come from connected sensors (IMO Res. A.1106(29)).

On which frequencies does shipborne AIS primarily operate?

AIS 1 and AIS 2 are 161.975 MHz and 162.025 MHz. The other options are DSC/VHF Ch 16, NAVTEX, and X/S-band radar (ITU-R M.1371).

How often must the AIS undergo a performance test by an approved surveyor or facility?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 18 requires an annual test of AIS, with guidance in MSC.1/Circ.1252.

A target's AIS data shows heading as not available but COG and SOG are present. What is the most likely cause?

Heading comes from the gyro, while COG and SOG come from GNSS, so a missing heading points to a gyro interface fault (IMO Res. MSC.74(69), Annex 3).

What is the correct use of AIS in collision avoidance?

COLREG Rule 7 requires use of all available means. AIS supplements, but never replaces, radar and visual observation.
