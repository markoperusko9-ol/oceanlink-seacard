HVAC on Ships: How the Air Conditioning and Ventilation Plant Works

Quick answer

A ship's HVAC plant supplies filtered, temperature- and humidity-controlled air to the accommodation, bridge and control rooms. It uses an air handling unit served by a vapour-compression refrigeration plant for cooling and steam, hot water or electric coils for heating. Watchkeepers need to know how the cycle works, what to check daily, how to read common faults, and how ventilation links to fire safety under SOLAS Chapter II-2.

What the HVAC plant is for

HVAC stands for heating, ventilation and air conditioning. On a merchant ship it does four jobs:

  • Comfort and health. It keeps cabins, mess rooms, the bridge and the engine control room at a liveable temperature and humidity. Fatigued, overheated watchkeepers make mistakes.
  • Fresh air supply. It brings in outside air and removes stale air, cooking fumes and moisture.
  • Equipment protection. Electronics on the bridge, in the ECR and in the radio and switchboard rooms need cool, dry air.
  • Fire and smoke control. In a fire the ventilation must be stopped and closed off so it does not feed the fire or spread smoke.

MLC 2006 (Regulation 3.1 and Standard A3.1) requires accommodation to be adequately ventilated and heated. Most ships must also have air conditioning for crew accommodation, the radio room and any centralised machinery control room. The exception is ships regularly trading where temperate conditions make it unnecessary. Detailed design standards come from the flag administration and class.

Engine room ventilation is usually a separate system of supply and exhaust fans. It provides combustion air and removes heat, but it shares the same fire-stop principles. Provision rooms are handled by the separate provision refrigeration plant.

Main components

Layouts vary by ship size and builder, but most cargo ships have a central unit along these lines.

Air handling unit (AHU) - Fresh air intake with louvres, plus a recirculation inlet. Dampers set the mixing ratio. - Filters, usually washable or replaceable panel or bag filters. - Cooling coil. This is either a direct-expansion (DX) evaporator or a chilled-water coil. - Drip tray and condensate drain. - Heating coil (steam, hot water or electric), sometimes split into pre-heat and re-heat stages. - Humidifier, often steam injection, used in cold climates. - Supply fan, belt- or direct-driven.

Distribution - Supply ducts, with fire dampers where ducts pass through fire divisions. - Terminal units or diffusers in cabins. Some have local reheaters or individual adjustment. - Exhaust fans for toilets, galley, laundry and hospital. Galley exhaust has its own grease filters and duct.

Refrigeration plant (DX type) - Compressor: reciprocating or screw, with capacity control such as unloaders or a slide valve. - Condenser, sea-water cooled or on the central fresh-water cooling system. - Liquid receiver, filter drier and sight glass. - Thermostatic or electronic expansion valve. - Safety cut-outs: HP, LP and oil differential pressure, plus motor protection.

Larger ships and passenger ships often use chilled-water plants instead. A refrigeration unit cools water, which is pumped to coils in several AHUs and fan-coil units.

How it works, step by step

Cooling mode (tropics) 1. Fresh air and recirculated air mix in the AHU. The damper ratio is set per the maker and company SMS. 2. The mixed air passes through the filters. 3. The air crosses the cooling coil. Refrigerant or chilled water absorbs heat. The air is cooled below its dew point, so moisture condenses and drains from the tray. This dehumidifying is as important as the cooling. 4. If the air is now too cold, a re-heat stage brings it up to a comfortable supply temperature. 5. The supply fan pushes the air through the ducts to the spaces. 6. Air returns via corridors or return ducts, or is exhausted. Air from toilets, the galley, the laundry and the hospital goes overboard and is not recirculated.

The refrigeration cycle behind it 1. The compressor draws low-pressure refrigerant vapour from the evaporator and compresses it to high pressure and temperature. 2. In the condenser, cooling water removes heat and the vapour condenses to liquid. 3. The liquid passes through the receiver, filter drier and sight glass. 4. The expansion valve drops the pressure and meters liquid into the evaporator. It is controlled by superheat at the evaporator outlet. 5. In the evaporator the liquid boils, absorbing heat from the air or water, and returns as vapour to the compressor.

Heating mode (cold weather) Cooling is off and the heating coils are active. The humidifier adds moisture, because heated outside air is very dry. Pre-heat stops the coils and filters icing up in sub-zero conditions.

Routine checks and maintenance

Exact intervals come from the maker's manual and the ship's planned maintenance system, not from a general rule.

Daily rounds (engine watch or duty engineer) - Suction and discharge pressures, and oil pressure, against normal values for the current sea temperature and load. - Sight glass: clear liquid, no persistent bubbles, correct moisture indicator colour. - Compressor oil level, and any unusual noise or vibration. - Condenser cooling water inlet and outlet temperatures. - AHU supply air temperature and humidity. - Condensate draining freely. - Fans running smoothly, with no belt squeal. - Oil or refrigerant traces at joints. Oily stains often show a refrigerant leak.

Periodic - Clean or replace filters. Dirty filters are the most common cause of poor cooling. - Clean the drip trays and drains, and disinfect them to discourage bacterial growth, including Legionella. - Inspect and tension fan belts, and grease bearings. - Clean condenser tubes. - Leak test, and replace the filter drier when the indicator or maker's schedule calls for it. - Test fire dampers and remote fan stops as required by the PMS. These items may be examined during surveys and Port State Control inspections. - Clean galley exhaust ducts and grease filters. Grease-laden ducts are a recognised fire risk.

Record all refrigerant top-ups. Under MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 12, ships of 400 gross tonnage and above with rechargeable systems containing ozone-depleting substances must keep an ODS Record Book. Deliberate venting of ODS is prohibited. Some regional rules, such as EU F-gas legislation, add further leak-check and record requirements for other refrigerants. Check what applies to your flag and trading area.

Common faults and what they look like

Symptom Likely causes
Low suction pressure, poor cooling, frost on the coil Dirty filters, iced evaporator, low refrigerant charge, faulty or blocked expansion valve, choked filter drier, fan belt slipping
High discharge pressure, HP trip Fouled condenser, high sea temperature, low cooling water flow, non-condensable gases (air), overcharge
Bubbles in the sight glass Low charge (leak) or a restriction before the sight glass
Compressor short-cycling Thermostat or LP switch differential set too close, low charge, oversized capacity at low load
Oil pressure trip Low oil level, oil diluted by liquid refrigerant, worn oil pump
Compressor knocking, frosted suction line Liquid floodback: expansion valve stuck open or evaporator airflow too low
Wet ducts, water on cabin deckheads Blocked condensate drain or overflowing drip tray
Musty smell Stagnant water in the drip tray or wet filters
Cabins too dry in winter Humidifier fault or steam supply shut

Worked scenario: HP trip in the Gulf in summer The compressor keeps tripping on high pressure in the afternoon.

  1. Check the cooling water inlet temperature and flow across the condenser. Is the strainer choked? Is the pump delivering? Are the valves fully open?
  2. Stop the plant and keep cooling water running until temperatures equalise. Then compare condenser pressure with the saturation pressure for the condenser or cooling-water temperature. A pressure noticeably higher than saturation indicates non-condensables. Note that high subcooling while running can also mean overcharge.
  3. Look at the condenser history. When were the tubes last cleaned?
  4. If you suspect air, purging must follow the maker's and company procedure, with refrigerant recovered, not vented.
  5. Do not raise the HP cut-out setting to stop the trips. It is a safety device.
  6. Log the findings and inform the second engineer or chief engineer.

Safety precautions

  • Fire. SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires that power ventilation can be stopped, and main inlets and outlets closed, from outside the spaces served. It also covers fire dampers in ducts passing through fire divisions, and galley exhaust duct arrangements. Know where your fan stops and damper controls are. Before a fixed CO2 release, ventilation to the protected space must be stopped and closed.
  • Smoke. In an accommodation fire, the recirculating AHU can spread smoke throughout the house. Stopping it early is part of most fire plans.
  • Refrigerant. Common halocarbon refrigerant vapours are heavier than air and can displace oxygen in a compartment or low space. Liquid causes frostbite, so wear gloves and goggles. Many refrigerants give off toxic decomposition products when exposed to flame or hot surfaces.
  • Hot work. Never weld or braze on a charged refrigerant line. Recover the refrigerant and ventilate first, under a permit as per the SMS.
  • Isolation. Fans and compressors restart automatically on thermostat demand. Isolate electrically and tag out before work on belts, coils or the compressor.
  • Pressure. Use proper gauges and recovery equipment. Do not open the system without confirming it is depressurised.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: Why does the cooling coil remove humidity as well as heat? Air passing over the coil is cooled below its dew point, so water vapour condenses and is drained away.

Q: What is superheat and why does it matter? Superheat is the temperature of the vapour at the evaporator outlet above its saturation temperature. The expansion valve controls it. Too little superheat risks liquid reaching the compressor. Too much means the evaporator is starved and capacity is lost.

Q: How would you detect air in the system? Discharge pressure will be higher than the saturation pressure for the actual condensing temperature, with the plant stopped and temperatures equalised.

Q: Why is galley and toilet air not recirculated? It carries odours, moisture, grease and contaminants. These spaces are exhausted directly.

Q: What happens to ventilation when there is a fire in the accommodation? Fans are stopped from outside the space, fire dampers and closures are shut, and the fire plan is followed.

Q: What records apply to refrigerant? On ships of 400 gross tonnage and above with rechargeable systems containing ozone-depleting substances, the ODS Record Book under MARPOL Annex VI applies. Company SMS and any regional rules may require more.

Key points
  • Ship HVAC filters, cools, dehumidifies, heats and humidifies air for accommodation and control spaces.
  • Most cargo ships use an AHU with a DX or chilled-water coil served by a vapour-compression plant.
  • Dirty filters, fouled condensers and low refrigerant charge cause most cooling complaints.
  • Never raise an HP or LP cut-out setting to cure repeated trips; find the cause.
  • Fan stops and fire dampers are SOLAS Chapter II-2 fire-safety items and must be tested per the PMS.
  • Refrigerant must be recovered, not vented; ships of 400 GT and above with rechargeable ODS systems need an ODS Record Book under MARPOL Annex VI.
  • Clean drip trays and drains to prevent water damage, odours and bacterial growth.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the ship's AC stop cooling properly in very hot sea areas?

High sea-water temperature reduces the condenser's ability to reject heat, so discharge pressure rises and capacity falls. A fouled condenser or choked strainer makes this much worse and often leads to HP trips. Clean condensers and good cooling water flow before entering hot areas help a lot.

What refrigerant is used in ship air conditioning?

It depends on the build date and maker. Older ships may still have HCFC systems such as R22, while newer installations use HFCs or alternatives. Under MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 12, new installations containing ozone-depleting substances are prohibited; for HCFCs such as R22 the prohibition applies to installations from 1 January 2020. Always check the plant nameplate and maker's manual before topping up.

Who is responsible for the AC plant on board?

This varies by company SMS. Commonly a designated engineer, often the third or fourth engineer, looks after it under the second engineer's direction, with the ETO handling controls and electrics. Daily checks are usually part of engine room rounds.

Why is water dripping from the vents in my cabin?

Usually the condensate drain from the AHU drip tray or a local unit is blocked, so water overflows into the ducts. It can also happen if a coil is iced and then thaws. Report it so the drain can be cleared before the water causes damage or mould.

Do fire dampers in the accommodation need testing?

Yes. SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires fire dampers in ducts passing through fire divisions, and they must work when needed. Test intervals are set by the planned maintenance system, maker and flag or class requirements. Dampers and their markings may be examined during surveys and Port State Control inspections.

Test yourself

Why is moisture removed from the air as it passes over the AHU cooling coil?

Dehumidification occurs because the coil surface is below the air's dew point; condensate drains from the tray (basic refrigeration and air-conditioning principle).

An AC compressor repeatedly trips on high discharge pressure in tropical waters. What is the most likely cause to check first?

Poor heat rejection at the condenser raises condensing pressure; low charge, dirty filters and a closed expansion valve tend to give low pressures. Refer to the maker's fault-finding guide.

Under MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 12, what is required for ships of 400 GT and above with rechargeable systems containing ozone-depleting substances?

MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 12 requires an ODS Record Book on ships of 400 GT and above where such systems are fitted; deliberate emissions are prohibited.

A frosted suction line and a knocking compressor most likely indicate:

Liquid returning to the compressor (low superheat) frosts the suction line and causes knocking; low charge gives high superheat instead.

In an accommodation fire, what should happen to the HVAC system?

SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires that ventilation can be stopped and openings closed from outside the spaces served to limit fire and smoke spread.
