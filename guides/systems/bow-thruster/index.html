Bow Thruster: Components, Operation, Maintenance and Common Faults

Quick answer

A bow thruster is a propeller in a transverse tunnel through the forward hull that pushes the bow to port or starboard during berthing, unberthing and slow manoeuvring, and it works best at very low speed. Most large merchant ships use an electric motor driving a controllable pitch propeller through a right-angle gearbox. It is normally started at zero pitch once adequate generator capacity is confirmed, often through a power management system start request.

What a bow thruster does and when it works

The bow thruster gives transverse thrust at the bow, independent of the main engine and rudder. It is used to:

  • Breast the bow on or off a berth
  • Turn the ship in a confined basin
  • Hold the bow against wind while tugs are made fast or let go
  • Reduce the number of tugs needed, depending on port rules and the pilot

It is a low-speed tool. As headway builds, water flowing past the tunnel mouths deflects the thruster jet and transverse force falls away sharply. At normal manoeuvring speeds it can be almost useless. Treat the figures on your ship's pilot card and manufacturer's data as the reference, not a general rule of thumb.

Immersion matters too. In light ballast the tunnel may sit close to the surface. The propeller can then draw air, which shows up as noise, vibration and poor thrust. Many ships also have a stern thruster, and the same principles apply to it.

Main components

Component Function
Tunnel Transverse duct through the bow below the waterline. Usually fitted with protective grids or bars at each end.
Propeller Controllable pitch (most common on large merchant ships) or fixed pitch with a variable-speed or reversing drive.
Right-angle gearbox Bevel gear in the hub inside the tunnel. Turns the drive from the vertical motor shaft to the horizontal propeller shaft.
Drive motor Usually electric. On larger ships it is often high voltage. Some ships use hydraulic or diesel drive.
Hydraulic power unit Servo oil pump(s), valves and filters that move the CPP blades. Also lubricates the hub on many designs.
Header (gravity) tank Mounted above the waterline. Keeps oil pressure in the hub slightly above outside sea pressure, so any seal leak is oil out rather than water in.
Shaft seals Usually lip-type seals on the propeller shaft. They keep seawater out and oil in.
Starter and controls Star-delta, autotransformer, soft starter or drive. Controls are on the bridge and bridge wings, with local control in the thruster room.
Feedback and indication Pitch feedback, load (ammeter or kW), running and alarm lamps, emergency stop.
Motor protection Overload and short-circuit protection, winding temperature sensors on many motors, space heaters against condensation.

Arrangements vary a lot between makers. Always work from the ship's own manual and drawings.

How it works: starting and using a CPP electric thruster, step by step

A typical sequence for a CPP thruster with an electric motor is below. Your ship's procedure takes precedence.

  1. Engine room prepared. Thruster room checked, header tank level normal, space heater on until start, no alarms on the panel.
  2. Hydraulic pump started, usually from the bridge panel. Pitch is confirmed at zero. Many systems block a motor start unless pitch is at zero and servo oil pressure is available.
  3. Start request to the power management system. The thruster is a heavy consumer. The PMS checks spare capacity and may start and connect another generator before it releases the start. If you lack generators on the board, it waits or refuses.
  4. Motor started at zero pitch. With no propeller load the motor runs up quickly, so the high starting current is brief and the load on the generators is minimised.
  5. Control station selected. Command is taken at the station in use, for example the wing for berthing, and the transfer is confirmed.
  6. Pitch applied progressively. Load is watched on the ammeter or kW indicator. Many systems have load limiting that slows pitch increase when the switchboard is near its limit.
  7. After use: pitch to zero, stop the motor, stop the hydraulic pump, and let the PMS shed the extra generator. The engine room is informed and the space heater comes back on.

Direction convention. The lever is normally moved in the direction you want the bow to go. Confirm this on joining. A thruster that responds the opposite way to the lever is a serious defect. Test it before you are inside the harbour.

Routine checks and maintenance

Planned maintenance under the ship's SMS is the authority. Typical items:

Pre-arrival / pre-departure (deck and engine together) - Start and run with the engine room in attendance. Check response from every control station, including the bridge wings. - Confirm the direction of thrust matches the lever and the pitch indicator agrees with local indication. - Test the emergency stop where the procedure permits. - Confirm generators are available to support the load.

Regular engine room rounds - Header tank level. A rising level or milky oil suggests water ingress. A falling level with no visible leak in the inboard system suggests oil going out past the seals. - Hydraulic unit: pressures, filters, leaks, oil temperature. - Motor space heater working, thruster room dry and ventilated. - Oil samples for analysis at the interval in the PMS. Watch for water and wear metals.

Periodic - Insulation resistance of the motor. On HV motors this needs HV-qualified personnel, isolation, earthing and a permit to work. - Grease the motor bearings as specified, without over-greasing. - Function-test alarms and trips.

Drydock - Inspect the blades, hub, seals, tunnel, grids and anodes, and renew seals if required. - Check for fishing net or rope around the hub.

Some designs allow parts to be withdrawn afloat, but most underwater work is done in dock or by divers.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Start refused Pitch not at zero, no servo oil pressure, PMS has insufficient generator capacity, emergency stop latched, control station not selected, motor trip not reset
Motor trips during use Pitch applied too fast, overload from a fouled propeller, high winding temperature, switchboard overload protection, earth fault
Pitch not responding or hunting Hydraulic pump or valve fault, contaminated oil, blocked filter, faulty feedback transmitter, control signal fault
Thrust in wrong direction or weak Feedback or set-point wrongly calibrated, air drawn in at shallow draught, damaged blades, grids blocked
Noise and vibration Cavitation or air ingestion, debris or rope in the tunnel, damaged blade, bearing or gear wear
Header tank level rising, milky oil Seawater ingress past shaft seals
Header tank level falling Oil leaking to sea past seals, or an external leak in the system
Low insulation resistance Moisture from the space heater being off, flooding or condensation in the thruster room

Worked scenario. You are on approach to the berth. The bow thruster trips at about half pitch to starboard and the PMS shows high load.

  1. Tell the master and pilot immediately. They must plan on tugs, rudder and engine only.
  2. In the engine room, check what tripped: thruster protection or the generator breaker.
  3. Restore generators first if any have dropped off.
  4. Find the cause before restarting. A blackout risk outweighs one more push.
  5. On a restart, apply pitch gradually.
  6. Log the event and raise a defect report.

Safety precautions

  • Persons and craft near the bow. Do not run the thruster when mooring boats, divers, a pilot boat or line handlers' craft are near the tunnel mouths. The jet and suction can capsize small boats and kill swimmers. Many SMS checklists require the bridge to confirm the area is clear.
  • Diving and tunnel work. Before divers or dock workers go near the tunnel, isolate and lock out the motor breaker and the hydraulic pump. Disable the remote controls, tag every control station and use a permit to work.
  • Electrical. HV thruster motors and switchgear need HV procedures and trained personnel.
  • Power and blackout risk. Never bypass PMS load-limiting or start interlocks to get the thruster going. A blackout during berthing is far worse than losing the thruster.
  • Thruster room. The room is often a forward compartment low in the ship. Check whether your SMS classes it as an enclosed space or needs ventilation before entry. Check the bilges for flooding.
  • Pollution. Report oil sheen near the tunnel. Some jurisdictions, notably US waters, have rules on lubricants at oil-to-sea interfaces such as thruster seals. Follow company and port requirements.

Regulatory points

No IMO convention requires merchant ships to carry a bow thruster. Fitting one is an owner's decision, approved by class. Where one is fitted:

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 covers the electrical installation, including generating capacity, protection and HV arrangements. Class rules add detail.
  • IMO Resolution A.601(15) (recommendatory) covers the provision and display of manoeuvring information. The pilot card includes thruster particulars so the pilot can plan with them.
  • The ISM Code requires the equipment to be maintained under the SMS. Its maintenance element includes testing standby or critical equipment the company has identified.
  • MARPOL Annex I prohibits the discharge of oil, which is relevant to seal leakage.

Port-specific tug and thruster rules come from local port authorities, not IMO.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Why is a CPP thruster motor started at zero pitch? To minimise the starting load and shorten the high starting current, so the generators and switchboard do not trip.
  • What is the purpose of the header tank? To keep hub oil pressure above sea pressure, so seal leakage is outward and water ingress can be detected.
  • What tells you seawater is entering the hub? The header tank level rises, the oil looks milky, and the oil analysis shows water.
  • Why might the PMS refuse a start? Because there is not enough spare generating capacity. The PMS starts another generator first or blocks the start.
  • Why is the thruster less effective at speed? Flow past the hull deflects the jet and reduces the pressure difference across the bow.
  • What isolations do you need before divers work near the tunnel? Motor breaker and hydraulic pump locked out, every control station tagged, and a permit issued and coordinated with the bridge.
  • Why keep the space heater on when stopped? To prevent condensation in the windings and so protect insulation resistance.
Key points
  • A bow thruster gives transverse thrust at the bow and is effective mainly at very low speed.
  • Most large merchant ships use an electric motor with a CPP, started at zero pitch with generator capacity confirmed, often via the PMS.
  • The header tank keeps hub oil pressure above sea pressure. A rising level suggests water ingress, a falling level suggests oil loss.
  • Test the thruster from every control station before arrival, including direction against the lever.
  • Never bypass load-limiting or start interlocks, because a blackout while berthing is worse than losing the thruster.
  • Keep the thruster stopped when boats or divers are near the tunnel, and lock out fully before underwater work.
  • No IMO convention requires a bow thruster, but its electrical installation and maintenance fall under SOLAS II-1, class rules and the ISM Code.

Frequently asked questions

Why won't the bow thruster start from the bridge?

The usual causes are pitch not at zero, no hydraulic or servo pressure, insufficient generator capacity in the PMS, a latched emergency stop or a trip not yet reset. Check the alarm panel and ask the engine room which interlock is active. Do not force a start by bypassing interlocks.

At what speed does a bow thruster stop being effective?

Effectiveness falls sharply as headway increases, because water flow past the hull deflects the jet. The exact figure depends on the hull and thruster design. Use your ship's pilot card and manufacturer's data rather than a general number.

Is a bow thruster mandatory under SOLAS?

No. No IMO convention requires merchant ships to carry one. Where fitted, it must meet class rules and the SOLAS Chapter II-1 electrical requirements, and be maintained under the ship's SMS.

What does a rising level in the bow thruster header tank mean?

It usually means seawater is getting past the shaft seals into the hub. Take an oil sample, check for milky oil and inform the chief engineer. The seals may need attention at the next opportunity, often in drydock.

Who operates the bow thruster during berthing?

It is normally operated from the bridge or bridge wing by the master, or by the pilot or an officer under the master's direction, according to the master-pilot exchange and company procedures. The engine room keeps the power supply ready and monitors the plant.

Test yourself

Why is a CPP bow thruster motor normally started with the pitch at zero?

Zero pitch means the motor starts without propeller load. It runs up to speed quickly, so the high starting current lasts only briefly and the risk of tripping generators or the switchboard is reduced. This follows manufacturer design and the ship's start interlocks.

What is the main purpose of the bow thruster header (gravity) tank?

The elevated tank gives a static head, so any leakage at the seals is oil out rather than water in, and level changes reveal seal problems. This follows standard manufacturer design.

A ship approaches the berth at moderate headway and the bow thruster has little effect. What is the most likely reason?

Thruster effectiveness falls sharply with headway because of the water flow past the tunnel. IMO Resolution A.601(15) recommends that manoeuvring information, including thruster particulars, is provided on the pilot card.

Before divers inspect the bow thruster tunnel, what isolation is required?

Positive isolation of every energy source, with tags and a permit, is needed before anyone goes near the tunnel. Lever positions and control selection do not protect the diver. This falls under the ISM Code and the company SMS.

Which convention requires merchant ships to be fitted with a bow thruster?

No IMO instrument requires a bow thruster. Where one is fitted, SOLAS Chapter II-1 electrical requirements and class rules apply to its installation.
