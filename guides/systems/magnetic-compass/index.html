Magnetic Compass on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

The magnetic compass is the ship's power-independent heading reference, required under SOLAS Chapter V. It reads magnetic north corrupted by the ship's own magnetism (deviation), so the watchkeeper must determine compass error once a watch and apply variation and deviation correctly. Its reliability depends on correct adjustment, a current deviation card, and keeping magnetic material away from the binnacle.

Why the magnetic compass still matters

Your gyro, GNSS and ECDIS all depend on electrical power. The magnetic compass does not. When you have a blackout, a gyro failure or a fire in the bridge equipment room, the magnetic compass is what you steer by.

SOLAS Chapter V requires every ship, regardless of size, to carry a properly adjusted standard magnetic compass, or other power-independent means, to determine heading and display it at the main steering position. It also requires a pelorus or bearing device independent of power, and a means of correcting headings and bearings to true at all times. Ships of 150 GT and upwards, and passenger ships of any size, must also carry a spare magnetic compass interchangeable with the standard one, or an accepted alternative.

On most merchant ships you will find:

  • A standard compass, usually on the monkey island on the centreline, well away from steel and electrical gear.
  • A steering compass, or a transmitting magnetic compass (TMC), which shows the standard compass reading electronically or optically at the steering position. Many TMCs also feed heading to the autopilot or a heading monitor.
  • A spare bowl stowed in its box. Keep it away from magnets and loudspeakers.

Main components

The bowl and card

  • Compass card: carries the graduations, 000 to 359. Under it is a system of parallel magnetic needles or a ring magnet.
  • Float: a sealed chamber under the card. It makes the card nearly weightless in the liquid, which reduces friction and wear on the pivot.
  • Pivot and jewel bearing: the card sits on a hard pivot in a jewelled cap. A worn pivot makes the card sluggish.
  • Damping liquid: fills the bowl and damps card oscillation. Modern bowls commonly use a light oil. Older designs used an alcohol and distilled water mix. Always use the maker's specified fluid.
  • Expansion chamber or diaphragm: takes up liquid expansion and contraction with temperature, so the bowl neither bursts nor draws in a bubble.
  • Lubber line: marks the ship's head. It must be exactly aligned with the fore-and-aft line.
  • Gimbals: keep the bowl level as the ship rolls and pitches.

The binnacle and correctors

  • Binnacle: the non-magnetic stand that holds the bowl, lighting and correctors.
  • Fore-and-aft and athwartships corrector magnets: horizontal permanent magnets inside the binnacle. They counter the deviation caused by the ship's permanent magnetism (the permanent parts of coefficients B and C).
  • Quadrantal correctors: soft iron spheres or plates either side of the bowl. They correct quadrantal deviation from induced magnetism in horizontal soft iron (coefficient D).
  • Flinders bar: a vertical soft iron bar in a tube on the binnacle, usually forward or aft. It corrects deviation from magnetism induced in the ship's vertical soft iron, which changes with magnetic latitude.
  • Heeling error magnet: a vertical magnet in a bucket directly under the bowl. Its height is adjusted to reduce heeling error.
  • Azimuth circle or azimuth mirror: fits on top of the bowl for taking bearings of terrestrial objects and celestial bodies.
  • Lighting: card illumination, usually with a dimmer.

Do not move, add or remove any corrector without the master's authority. The positions were set by the adjuster and recorded with the deviation card.

How it works and where the errors come from

Step by step

  1. The card magnets align with the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field and point to magnetic north.
  2. The ship turns around the card, which stays steady. The lubber line shows the ship's head against the card.
  3. The liquid and float damp swinging, and the gimbals keep the card level in a seaway.
  4. The ship's own steel adds its own magnetic field, so the card actually settles on compass north, not magnetic north.

The errors you must apply

  • Variation: the angle between true and magnetic north. It depends on position and changes slowly over the years. Take it from the chart or ECDIS and update it for the year.
  • Deviation: the angle between magnetic and compass north. It is caused by the ship's magnetism and changes with the ship's heading. Take it from the deviation card.
  • Compass error: the combined effect of variation and deviation, the angle between true and compass north.

Convert from compass to true by applying deviation and then variation. Easterly errors are added and westerly errors are subtracted. The old memory aid is error East, compass least; error West, compass best.

Other effects to know

  • Heeling error: when the ship heels, the vertical magnetic components no longer act straight through the card. This causes deviation that can make the card swing as she rolls. It is worst on northerly and southerly headings.
  • Gaussin error: after a long time on one heading, or after hammering or welding, the hull picks up temporary magnetism. This gives deviation not shown on the card, and it fades over hours or days.
  • High magnetic latitudes: the horizontal field is weak, so the compass becomes sluggish and less reliable.

Worked scenario: compass error and deviation

You are on a steady course and want to check the standard compass.

  • Gyro heading: 090°
  • Gyro error from your last azimuth: 1° High. True heading is therefore 089°T.
  • Standard compass heading: 095°C
  • Variation from the chart, updated: 3°W

Step 1: Find the compass error. True is 089° and compass is 095°. The compass reads higher than true, so the error is westerly. Compass error = 6°W.

Step 2: Find the deviation. Deviation is compass error minus variation. 6°W minus 3°W gives 3°W.

Step 3: Compare with the deviation card. If the card shows about 3°W for ship's head 095°C, the compass is behaving normally. If it differs significantly, look for a cause. Check for new equipment near the binnacle, steel cargo, recent hot work, a disturbed corrector, or a long spell on one heading. Then report to the master.

Step 4: Record it. Enter the compass error in the compass error book or log, as your company SMS requires.

You can also find true bearings by azimuth or amplitude of a celestial body, or by a charted transit. Comparing the compass bearing or heading directly against a true bearing from an azimuth, amplitude or charted transit is the cleanest method, because it does not depend on the gyro error being correct.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch

  • The STCW Code watchkeeping provisions require gyro and magnetic compass errors to be determined once a watch and, when possible, after any major alteration of course. Standard and gyro compasses are to be compared frequently.
  • Compare the steering compass or TMC with the standard compass, and record the comparison.
  • Check that the card lighting works and is dimmable.

Periodically (follow the PMS and SMS)

  • Inspect the bowl for bubbles, cloudiness, discolouration or leaks.
  • Check that the card swings freely. Deflect it with a small magnet, remove the magnet, and watch it return smoothly to the same reading.
  • Check the gimbals for free movement and the binnacle for corrosion and secure fastenings.
  • Clean the glass and the azimuth mirror, and check the azimuth circle turns freely.
  • Confirm the spare bowl is intact and correctly stowed.
  • Check the TMC sensor and display if fitted.

Adjustment and the deviation card

Adjustment is normally done by a qualified compass adjuster swinging the ship through all headings. Afterwards a new deviation card is issued and the corrector positions are recorded. How often adjustment is required, and who may do it, varies by flag state and company SMS. Check your flag's requirements rather than assuming a fixed interval.

Re-adjustment is commonly needed:

  • after structural repairs or major hot work near the compass;
  • after installing electrical or magnetic equipment nearby;
  • when observed deviations no longer match the card;
  • after carrying magnetic cargo;
  • after a large change in magnetic latitude, if the Flinders bar setting is no longer effective.

Never try to open a sealed bowl on board unless the maker's manual allows it.

Safety precautions

  • Keep magnetic items away from the binnacle. This includes tools, mobile phones, radios, loudspeakers, steel chairs and handheld lamps. Check the safe distances marked on bridge equipment.
  • Control hot work and modifications near the compass. Treat any welding or new installation on the monkey island or wheelhouse as a reason to check deviation afterwards.
  • Do not tamper with correctors. Unauthorised changes invalidate the deviation card.
  • Watch for cargo effects. Steel coils, scrap and similar cargo can change deviation, so check the compass error after loading.
  • Take care on the monkey island. Use the handrails, take care in bad weather and at night, and tell the bridge before going up.
  • Know your failover. Every officer should know how to steer by the standard compass or TMC when the gyro fails. This includes reading the compass from the steering position and applying compass error to courses.
  • Use the right fluid when topping up. Some damping fluids are flammable or irritant, so follow the maker's handling guidance.

Oral exam questions examiners actually ask

Q: Why carry a magnetic compass when we have two gyros? It is independent of electrical power and is required by SOLAS Chapter V. It is your heading reference when the gyro or the power fails.

Q: What is the difference between variation and deviation? Variation is caused by the Earth's field and changes with position. Deviation is caused by the ship's own magnetism and changes with heading.

Q: What does the Flinders bar correct, and why does it need attention on long voyages? It corrects deviation from magnetism induced in the ship's vertical soft iron. That induced magnetism changes with magnetic latitude, so a correction set in one latitude may not hold in another.

Q: Name the correctors and what each one corrects. - Fore-and-aft and athwartships magnets correct permanent magnetism. - The spheres correct quadrantal deviation. - The Flinders bar corrects induced vertical soft iron. - The heeling magnet corrects heeling error.

Q: How would you check the compass error at sea? By azimuth or amplitude of a celestial body, by a transit, or by comparison with a gyro of known error. Record the result and compare it with the deviation card.

Q: You notice a bubble in the bowl. What do you do? Report it, then top up with the specified fluid if the maker permits, or exchange for the spare bowl. Afterwards, recheck the compass error.

Q: What is Gaussin error? Temporary deviation from magnetism the hull picks up after a long time on one heading, or after hammering or welding. It decays with time on a new heading.

Key points
  • The magnetic compass is the power-independent heading reference SOLAS Chapter V requires on every ship.
  • Ships of 150 GT and upwards, and all passenger ships, need an interchangeable spare compass or accepted alternative.
  • Deviation is caused by the ship's magnetism and changes with heading. Variation changes with position.
  • Determine compass error once a watch and, when possible, after major alterations of course.
  • Correctors (magnets, spheres, Flinders bar, heeling magnet) must not be moved without authority.
  • Bubbles, sluggish cards and unexplained deviation are the faults you will meet most often.
  • How often adjustment is required, and who may adjust, depends on flag state and company SMS.

Frequently asked questions

How often should the magnetic compass error be checked?

The STCW Code watchkeeping provisions require compass error to be determined once a watch and, when possible, after any major alteration of course. Standard and gyro compasses should also be compared frequently. Your company SMS may add further requirements.

How often must a ship's magnetic compass be adjusted?

There is no single international interval in SOLAS. It requires the compass to be properly adjusted, and the flag state and company SMS set the detail. Re-adjustment is also needed when observed deviations no longer agree with the deviation card, or after structural or equipment changes near the compass.

What causes a bubble in the compass bowl?

Usually a slow loss of fluid through a seal, or contraction of the liquid in cold conditions beyond what the expansion chamber can take up. Top up with the maker's specified fluid through the filling plug if permitted, or exchange the bowl. Then recheck the compass error.

What is the difference between the standard compass and the steering compass?

The standard compass is mounted in the best magnetic position, usually on the monkey island, and is the reference compass. The steering compass, or a transmitting magnetic compass display, shows heading at the steering position. It should be compared regularly with the standard compass.

Why does the magnetic compass become unreliable in high latitudes?

Near the magnetic poles the horizontal component of the Earth's field is weak. The directive force on the card is small, so the card becomes sluggish, and the ship's own magnetism has a proportionally larger effect.

Test yourself

What does the Flinders bar correct?

The Flinders bar is vertical soft iron that offsets induced magnetism in the ship's vertical soft iron, which varies with magnetic latitude. Reference: standard compass adjustment theory (SOLAS Ch V Reg 19 requires the compass to be properly adjusted).

Compass error is 6°W and variation is 2°E. What is the deviation?

Deviation is compass error minus variation: 6°W minus 2°E gives 8°W.

You notice a bubble in the standard compass bowl. What is the correct action?

Only the maker's specified fluid should be used, and a bubble can obscure or drag the card. Reference: compass maker's manual / company PMS.

What do the soft iron spheres on the binnacle correct?

The quadrantal spheres counter deviation from induced magnetism in horizontal soft iron, which peaks on intercardinal headings. Reference: standard compass adjustment theory (SOLAS Ch V Reg 19 requires the compass to be properly adjusted).

According to the STCW Code watchkeeping provisions, when should compass error be determined?

The OOW is to determine gyro and magnetic compass errors once a watch and, when possible, after major course alterations. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2.
