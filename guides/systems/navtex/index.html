NAVTEX: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

NAVTEX is the automated MF service that delivers maritime safety information (navigational warnings, weather warnings, forecasts and search and rescue information) to ships in coastal waters, mainly on 518 kHz in English. SOLAS Chapter IV requires ships to be able to receive maritime safety information throughout their voyage, and where the international NAVTEX service is provided this is done with a NAVTEX receiver. The OOW has to select the right stations and message types, read and act on what prints, and know what the four-character message identifier means.

What NAVTEX is and why it matters

NAVTEX is an international direct-printing service for broadcasting maritime safety information (MSI) to ships. It forms part of the GMDSS and of the World-Wide Navigational Warning Service run jointly by IMO and IHO, with WMO responsible for the meteorological content.

On the bridge it is the main source of coastal navigational warnings, such as:

  • an unlit wreck
  • a buoy off station
  • a drilling rig moving into a TSS
  • a light that has failed
  • a gale warning
  • a vessel overdue or a person in the water

The receiver runs unattended and prints or displays messages as they arrive. Coverage is coastal. The practical range of an MF NAVTEX station is a few hundred nautical miles and varies with propagation, so it does not reach the open ocean. Outside NAVTEX coverage, MSI is received by Enhanced Group Call (EGC) through a recognised mobile satellite service, for example SafetyNET.

If the receiver is set up wrongly, you can sail straight into a hazard that has been broadcast for days. That is why examiners keep asking about it.

Frequencies, stations and the message identifier

Frequencies

Frequency Use
518 kHz International NAVTEX, in English. This is the core service.
490 kHz National NAVTEX, often in a local language
4209.5 kHz HF NAVTEX, used in some areas (notably tropical) where MF reception is poor

Time-sharing. Many stations share 518 kHz. Interference is avoided by geographic spacing and by time-sharing. Each station gets a letter (A to X) and transmits in a 10-minute slot every four hours. Vital warnings may also go out at the first available opportunity outside the routine schedule.

The message identifier (B1 B2 B3 B4). Every message begins with ZCZC, followed by four characters:

  • B1: transmitter identity. A letter identifying the station within its NAVAREA.
  • B2: subject indicator. The type of message.
  • B3 B4: serial number. Runs 01 to 99. The special number 00 means the message is always printed, even if an identical one was received earlier.

Common subject indicators (B2)

B2 Message type Can it be deselected?
A Navigational warnings No
B Meteorological warnings No
C Ice reports Yes
D SAR information and piracy/armed attack warnings No
E Meteorological forecasts Yes
L Navigational warnings additional to A No
J GNSS messages Yes
Z No messages on hand Yes

A, B, D and L cannot be rejected by the receiver. The others can be switched off, but you should only do so with a good reason. Forecasts (E) are useful to most ships, so think carefully before turning them off.

Station letters, schedules and coverage areas are published in the national and Admiralty lists of radio signals and in IMO's NAVTEX publications. Always use the current edition, because stations do change.

Main components and how it works, step by step

Components

  • Antenna. Usually a short whip, often an active antenna with a pre-amplifier, mounted clear of the funnel and other aerials.
  • Antenna cable and any lightning/surge protection.
  • Receiver unit. Contains one or more dedicated receivers. Modern sets often receive 518 kHz and 490 kHz at the same time.
  • Display and/or printer. Older sets print on paper. Modern sets have a screen, message storage and sometimes an output to INS or ECDIS.
  • Power supply. Normally arranged with the other GMDSS equipment so it keeps running when main power fails. Check your ship's GMDSS supply arrangement.
  • Alarm. Sounds for SAR (D) messages and, depending on the model, for other priority traffic.

How a message reaches you

  1. The coordinator (NAVAREA, sub-area or national) issues a warning and passes it to the NAVTEX station.
  2. The station transmits in its time slot using narrow-band direct printing in forward error correction (FEC) mode. This is one-way: the ship never acknowledges.
  3. The receiver decodes the message and reads the identifier.
  4. It checks the B1 and B2 settings. Messages from a station that has not been selected are discarded. Messages from a selected station are discarded only if their subject has been deselected. Subjects A, B, D and L can never be deselected.
  5. It checks the identifier against its memory. If the message was already received correctly, it is not printed again. This is why one warning repeated every four hours only appears once.
  6. If the message is new, or garbled the first time, it is printed or displayed and the identifier is stored. Stored identifiers are cleared automatically after a set period, typically about three days.
  7. A D message triggers the alarm.

Bridge routine: setting up and using NAVTEX

At passage planning

  • Identify the NAVTEX stations covering the route, including their B1 letters and schedules.
  • Note the change-over points in the passage plan, for example: "Select station X from abeam point Y."
  • Note where NAVTEX coverage ends and EGC takes over.

Before departure / on entering a new area

  • Select the stations along the route. Selecting only your current station means you will miss warnings for waters ahead.
  • Deselecting unwanted distant stations is acceptable. It cuts clutter and stops irrelevant warnings from distant stations heard at night.
  • Check the date and time on the receiver.

On watch

  • Read every new message.
  • Plot navigational warnings that affect the route on the chart or ECDIS.
  • Brief the Master on anything significant.
  • Respond as required to SAR (D) messages and record your actions.
  • Keep and file messages, and record cancellations, as your company SMS requires. Most companies want in-force warnings kept in a file or folder on the bridge.

Worked scenario. You are the 3rd Officer on a bulk carrier heading towards a coastal TSS. Two days ago the receiver was reset after a power interruption. Since then it has reprinted a batch of old messages, and the C/O asks why. A reset can clear the stored identifiers, so warnings already received are treated as new and print again.

In this case the reprinting is harmless. The real risk is a different one: check that your station selection survived the reset. Some receivers fall back to a default setting. If the station covering the TSS approaches is no longer selected, you will miss the rig move notice published for it.

Routine checks and maintenance

Actual intervals come from the maker's manual, your company SMS and the flag administration's GMDSS requirements. Typical checks are:

  • Daily: Receiver on and displaying recent traffic. Station and subject selection correct for the area. Paper stock and printing quality (paper sets). Clock correct.
  • Self-test: Run the built-in test as required and record the result in the GMDSS radio log or as the SMS specifies.
  • Antenna: Inspect for physical damage, loose mountings, corroded connectors and broken or chafed cables. Do this at the intervals set by the PMS.
  • Power: Confirm the receiver stays operational on its alternative/emergency supply as arranged on your ship, as part of the GMDSS power checks.
  • Records: Keep printed or saved messages in line with the SMS. If equipment is defective, record it and report it according to company and flag requirements.

Radio surveys and shore-based maintenance arrangements cover the wider GMDSS installation. The NAVTEX receiver is inspected as part of the Cargo Ship Safety Radio survey, or its equivalent.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes First actions
No messages at all for many hours in a covered area Station deselected, receiver off or frozen, antenna or cable fault, power fault Check the selection against the station list. Check the schedule, since the station only sends every four hours. Inspect the antenna and connections. Run the self-test.
Garbled text, many error characters Weak signal, interference from onboard equipment, night-time skywave interference from distant stations on the same frequency, damaged antenna Check the antenna and earthing. Note whether the problem is worse at night. Compare with the next scheduled broadcast.
Old messages reprinting Identifier memory cleared by a reset or power loss Check that the settings are intact. The reprints are not dangerous in themselves.
Messages from far-off stations, local ones missing Wrong B1 selection, possibly night propagation Reselect stations for the area and passage ahead.
Nothing printed but the display shows messages Printer fault or paper out Restore the paper. Read the stored messages on screen meanwhile.
Wrong date/time on messages Clock not set or GNSS input lost Correct the clock and check the interface.

If the receiver is out of service, tell the Master. Use alternative sources of MSI, such as EGC, coast radio stations and local VTS. Record the defect, and arrange repair according to the ship's GMDSS maintenance arrangements.

Regulations in brief

  • SOLAS Chapter IV (Radiocommunications) requires ships to be able to receive maritime safety information throughout their voyage. In areas where the international NAVTEX service is provided, this is done with a NAVTEX receiver. The same chapter covers the continuous watch for MSI, GMDSS power supplies and maintenance arrangements.
  • SOLAS Chapter V deals with the provision of navigational warnings by governments and with passage planning, which in practice includes using MSI.
  • IMO performance standards for NAVTEX receivers (Resolution MSC.148(77)) set how the receiver must behave. This covers message rejection rules, identifier storage and alarms.
  • The IMO NAVTEX Manual and the Joint IMO/IHO/WMO Manual on MSI describe how the service is run.

Logbook entries, radio log format and who on board is designated for GMDSS duties all depend on the flag state and company SMS.

Oral exam: questions examiners actually ask

  • What is NAVTEX and what frequency is the international service on? It is an MF direct-printing MSI service, on 518 kHz in English. Also mention 490 kHz national and 4209.5 kHz HF.
  • Explain B1 B2 B3 B4. B1 is the station, B2 the subject, and B3 B4 the serial number. Be ready to give A, B, D, E and L from memory.
  • Which messages can't you reject, and why? A, B, D and L. These are navigational warnings, met warnings and SAR/piracy information, which are essential to safety.
  • What does serial 00 mean? The message is always printed and is not suppressed as a repeat.
  • Why doesn't the same warning print every four hours? The receiver stores the identifiers of messages received correctly and suppresses repeats.
  • How do you choose which stations to select? From the passage plan, covering the current area and the areas ahead, using the current list of radio signals.
  • What do you do if NAVTEX fails at sea? Inform the Master. Use EGC and other MSI sources. Log the defect and report it per the SMS.
  • Where does NAVTEX coverage stop, and what replaces it? It covers coastal waters only. Beyond that, MSI comes by EGC through a recognised mobile satellite service.

Examiners like candidates who connect the equipment to the passage plan, not just recite codes.

Key points
  • NAVTEX broadcasts coastal maritime safety information; the international service is on 518 kHz in English.
  • The message identifier B1 B2 B3 B4 gives the station, subject and serial number.
  • Subject indicators A, B, D and L cannot be deselected; serial 00 always prints.
  • Stations share frequencies by time-slot, each transmitting for 10 minutes every four hours.
  • Select stations from the passage plan, covering the areas ahead as well as your current area.
  • Beyond NAVTEX range, MSI is received via EGC through a recognised mobile satellite service.
  • SOLAS Chapter IV requires ships to receive MSI throughout the voyage; logging and checks follow flag and SMS requirements.

Frequently asked questions

What is the range of NAVTEX?

NAVTEX is a coastal service. An MF station's practical range is a few hundred nautical miles, depending on transmitter power and propagation. Published station data in the lists of radio signals show each station's coverage area. Beyond that, rely on EGC.

Can I switch off weather forecasts on NAVTEX?

Subject E (meteorological forecasts) can be deselected on the receiver. Most ships keep it on because it is useful. Meteorological warnings (B), navigational warnings (A and L) and SAR information (D) cannot be rejected.

Why did my NAVTEX print old messages again?

After a power loss or reset, the receiver may lose its stored list of message identifiers, so it treats repeated broadcasts as new. The reprints are harmless. Check that your station and subject selections were not reset to defaults.

What is the difference between 518 kHz and 490 kHz NAVTEX?

518 kHz carries the international service in English and is the one required for SOLAS ships. 490 kHz is used for national NAVTEX broadcasts, often in a local language. Many modern receivers can monitor both at once.

Is NAVTEX required in every sea area?

SOLAS Chapter IV requires ships to be able to receive maritime safety information throughout their voyage. In areas where the international NAVTEX service is provided, this is done with a NAVTEX receiver. Where there is no NAVTEX coverage, MSI is normally received via EGC from a recognised mobile satellite service (and, in some areas, HF MSI broadcasts). Check your ship's radio equipment certificate and flag requirements for the specific arrangement.

Test yourself

On which frequency is the international NAVTEX service broadcast in English?

International NAVTEX is on 518 kHz and 490 kHz is national NAVTEX. 2182 kHz is the MF radiotelephony distress frequency and 156.525 MHz (VHF Ch 70) is the VHF DSC frequency (IMO NAVTEX Manual).

Which subject indicator may be deselected on a NAVTEX receiver?

A, B, D and L cannot be rejected; E may be deselected (IMO Resolution MSC.148(77)).

A NAVTEX message carries serial number 00. What does this mean?

Serial 00 marks a message that must always be printed regardless of earlier reception (IMO NAVTEX Manual).

In the identifier B1 B2 B3 B4, what does B1 represent?

B1 identifies the transmitter; B2 is the subject and B3 B4 the serial number (IMO NAVTEX Manual).

A ship is on an ocean passage well beyond NAVTEX coverage. How is maritime safety information received?

Outside NAVTEX coverage, MSI is broadcast via EGC, e.g. SafetyNET (SOLAS Chapter IV; Joint IMO/IHO/WMO Manual on MSI).
