Gyrocompass on Merchant Ships: How It Works, Errors, Checks and Faults

Quick answer

A gyrocompass uses a fast-spinning rotor, controlled by gravity and damped, to settle on the true meridian. It feeds heading to the autopilot, radar/ARPA, ECDIS, AIS and VDR. The OOW must find its error by an external reference at least once a watch, and the gyro must never be switched off casually because it takes hours to settle again.

What the gyrocompass does and why it matters

The gyrocompass gives a true heading that is not affected by the ship's magnetism or by local magnetic anomalies. On a modern bridge it is much more than a steering aid. It is the heading source for most of the navigation suite:

  • Autopilot / track control: holds the heading or track.
  • Radar and ARPA: gives a north-up, stabilised picture and true vectors.
  • ECDIS: provides the heading line and heading data for radar overlay.
  • AIS: transmits heading to other ships.
  • VDR: records heading.
  • Repeaters: the steering repeater, bridge-wing bearing repeaters and, where fitted, the repeater at the emergency steering position.

When the gyro goes wrong, it can take several systems with it. A drifting gyro can make ARPA report a wrong aspect and wrong CPA, and AIS will broadcast a false heading to every ship nearby. That is why examiners treat the gyro as a core watchkeeping topic, not as one for the ETO alone.

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires ships of 500 GT and upwards to carry a gyrocompass, or other non-magnetic means of determining heading, that can transmit heading to the radar plotting aid, AIS and other equipment. It also requires a heading repeater, or other means, to show heading at the emergency steering position if one is provided, and a bearing repeater, or other means, to take bearings over an arc of 360° (ships under 1,600 GT only as far as possible). Exact fit and arrangements are checked against the ship's Record of Equipment and flag state requirements.

Main components

Designs vary by maker (Sperry, Anschütz, Yokogawa, Tokyo Keiki and others), but a conventional mechanical gyrocompass has these parts:

Component Function
Sensitive element (gyro rotor or gyrosphere) Spins at high speed and provides gyroscopic inertia. In some designs it floats in a supporting liquid inside a sphere.
Control element Gravity control, either a pendulous weight or a liquid ballistic, which makes the gyro north-seeking.
Damping arrangement Reduces the oscillation about the meridian so the gyro settles.
Follow-up (phantom) system A servo that follows the sensitive element without loading it and drives the compass card and transmitter.
Transmission system Sends heading to repeaters and equipment. Older ships use step-by-step or synchro outputs; newer ones use digital serial data.
Power supply / inverter Provides the specific voltage and frequency the rotor and electronics need. It is normally backed by the emergency source and often by a battery or UPS.
Control and alarm unit Takes speed and latitude inputs and gives power-fail, system-fail and follow-up alarms.
Repeaters Steering, bearing and remote repeaters.

Many modern ships have fibre-optic gyrocompasses (FOG); ring-laser types also exist but are less common. These have no spinning rotor. They measure Earth's rotation optically (the Sagnac effect) and settle much faster. The principles of error checking described below still apply.

How it works, step by step

  1. Gyroscopic inertia. A free, fast-spinning rotor keeps its spin axis fixed in space, relative to the stars.
  2. Precession. If a torque is applied to the spin axis, the axis moves at 90° to the applied force, in the direction of spin.
  3. Apparent motion. Because the Earth rotates under it, a free gyro appears to drift in azimuth and to tilt. The amount depends on latitude. A free gyro is therefore not a compass.
  4. Control. Gravity control is added. When the axis tilts, the control produces a torque, and the resulting precession drives the north end towards the meridian. The gyro now seeks north, but it swings past the meridian and oscillates about it in an ellipse.
  5. Damping. Damping, either in tilt or in azimuth depending on the design, shrinks that ellipse into a converging spiral. The spin axis then settles on, or very close to, the meridian.
  6. Settling. A conventional gyro takes several hours to settle from cold. The exact time depends on the make and the latitude, so check the manual. This is why the gyro is started well before sailing and not stopped during short port stays without the Master's agreement.

The undamped period of oscillation is designed to be about 84 minutes (the Schuler period). Tuning to this period largely cancels the error that would otherwise arise when the ship changes speed or course.

Gyro errors you must know

  • Speed and course error (steaming error). The ship's north-south speed combines with the Earth's rotation, and the gyro settles at right angles to the resultant motion, not to the true meridian. On northerly courses the gyro reads high (westerly error). On southerly courses it reads low (easterly error). The error is greatest on N/S headings, zero on E/W headings, and grows with speed and latitude. It is corrected using speed and latitude inputs, either set manually or fed automatically from the log and GNSS.
  • Latitude (damping) error. Compasses damped in tilt settle slightly off the meridian by an amount that varies with latitude. It is removed by a latitude setting or input.
  • Ballistic deflection error. This is a temporary error after large changes of speed or course. It is largely eliminated by Schuler tuning, but expect some heading settling after big alterations.
  • Rolling (quadrantal/intercardinal) error. Caused by rolling on intercardinal headings. Designers minimise it, for example by using a twin-gyro sensitive element.
  • Mechanical and alignment errors. These include a lubber line misaligned with the fore-and-aft line, repeaters out of step with the master compass, and transmission faults.

Worked example: speed error. The approximate error in degrees is speed × cos(course) ÷ (15.7 × cos(latitude)). For 20 knots on course 000° at latitude 30°: 20 × 1 ÷ (15.7 × 0.866) ≈ 1.5° high. If someone leaves the speed input at zero, the uncorrected error is of this order. This is why the speed and latitude settings belong on your watch handover checklist.

Routine checks and maintenance

Every watch (OOW):

  • Find the gyro error by an external reference: an azimuth or amplitude, a transit, leading lights, or a known heading alongside. STCW requires the compass errors to be determined at least once a watch and, when possible, after any major course alteration.
  • Compare the gyro with the magnetic compass and log both.
  • Check that all repeaters agree with the master compass. Check bridge-wing repeaters before you rely on them for bearings.
  • Confirm that the speed and latitude inputs (or their sources) are correct.
  • Confirm that the heading shown on radar, ECDIS and the autopilot agrees with the gyro.

Worked example: gyro error from an azimuth. The computed true azimuth is 245.5° and the observed gyro bearing is 246.5°. The gyro reads more than true, so the error is 1° high. A gyro course of 090° is therefore 089° true. Memory aid: gyro best, error west (high). Record the error in the compass error book or logbook according to company procedure.

Periodic (ETO / engineers / bridge team, per maker and PMS):

  • Test the alarms: power failure, system failure, follow-up.
  • Check the operation of the ventilation fan and the operating temperature.
  • On liquid-supported types, check the gyrosphere height and current against the maker's limits.
  • Replace the supporting liquid and service or replace the sensitive element at the maker's intervals. This is often done by an authorised service engineer.
  • Check the changeover to emergency power and the UPS or battery back-up.

Intervals and limits come from the manufacturer's manual and the ship's planned maintenance system. Do not guess them.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause / first action
Gyro error growing steadily over several watches Sensitive element deteriorating, wrong latitude input, or a supporting-liquid problem. Monitor, compare with the magnetic compass, and report to the Master and ETO.
Error fits the speed-error pattern (high on northerly, low on southerly courses) Speed or latitude input wrong or lost. Check the log or GNSS feed and any manual setting.
Repeater disagrees with the master compass Repeater out of step after a power interruption, or a transmission fault. Re-align the repeater to the master compass.
Heading hunting or oscillating Follow-up system fault. Check the alarms and switch the autopilot to the alternative heading source if available.
Power-fail or system alarm Supply or inverter fault. Check whether the gyro has gone to back-up power, and expect a reduced running time on battery.
Heading mismatch alarm on autopilot or ECDIS Gyro and second heading source (magnetic/THD or second gyro) disagree. Find out which is wrong before acting.

If the gyro fails at sea: go to hand steering on the magnetic compass, which must have a known deviation. Inform the Master. Remember that ARPA vectors, ECDIS radar overlay and AIS heading are now unreliable or lost. Increase the look-out and use more visual bearings. If two gyros are fitted, change over and check that every consumer has actually switched.

Safety precautions

  • Do not switch the gyro off without the Master's permission and the ETO's knowledge. A conventional gyro needs hours to settle again before it can be trusted.
  • Before maintenance, isolate the supply under the ship's permit-to-work and lock-out tag-out procedures. Some units hold high voltages or stored charge.
  • Treat supporting fluids and other chemicals according to their safety data sheets. Some older designs contain mercury, which must be handled and disposed of as hazardous waste.
  • Never open or move a running gyro. Rotor speed is high and the bearings are delicate.
  • After a blackout, check every repeater and every heading consumer, not only the master compass.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires the emergency source of electrical power to supply the navigational aids required by Chapter V. Know how your gyro is fed and how long its back-up lasts.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

  • Why is a free gyro not a compass? Earth's rotation causes apparent drift and tilt. Control and damping are needed to make it settle on the meridian.
  • Explain precession and how it makes the gyro north-seeking. Gravity control turns tilt into a torque. The resulting precession moves the north end towards the meridian.
  • What is speed error and how is it corrected? Describe the direction (high on northerly, low on southerly courses), when it is maximum and zero, and how it is corrected with speed and latitude inputs.
  • Why about 84 minutes? Schuler tuning minimises ballistic deflection error.
  • How do you find gyro error at sea? Azimuth or amplitude, transits, leading lines. Explain how the error is named and applied.
  • Your gyro fails in a TSS at night. Actions? Hand steer on the magnetic compass, inform the Master, recognise the degraded ARPA/ECDIS/AIS picture, and rely on visual bearings and look-out.
  • What is a FOG and how does it differ? No rotor, measures rotation optically, settles faster, still needs error checks.
Key points
  • A gyrocompass gives true heading using gyroscopic inertia, precession, gravity control and damping.
  • It feeds the autopilot, radar/ARPA, ECDIS, AIS and VDR, so a gyro fault degrades the whole bridge.
  • Speed error is greatest on N/S courses and zero on E/W, reading high on northerly courses; check speed and latitude inputs.
  • Find the gyro error by an external reference at least once a watch and after major alterations of course.
  • A conventional gyro takes several hours to settle, so never switch it off casually.
  • After any power interruption, check every repeater and heading consumer against the master compass.
  • If the gyro fails, steer by the magnetic compass, inform the Master and treat ARPA/AIS heading data as unreliable.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a ship's gyrocompass take to settle?

A conventional mechanical gyro typically takes several hours from start-up. The exact time depends on the make and the latitude, so follow the manufacturer's manual. Fibre-optic types settle much faster. This is why the gyro is started well before departure.

How do you calculate gyro error?

Compare a true bearing with the bearing observed on the gyro. The true bearing can come from an azimuth, an amplitude, a transit or leading lights. If the gyro reads more than true, the error is high; if it reads less, the error is low. Apply the error to every gyro course and bearing until it is checked again.

Why does the gyrocompass have a speed error?

The gyro settles at right angles to the combined motion of the Earth's rotation and the ship's north-south speed, not exactly on the meridian. The error is largest on north or south headings and increases with speed and latitude. It is corrected by speed and latitude inputs, set manually or fed from the log and GNSS.

What should the OOW do if the gyrocompass fails?

Change to hand steering on the magnetic compass and inform the Master. Change over to the second gyro if one is fitted. Remember that ARPA true vectors, ECDIS radar overlay and AIS heading are now unreliable, so increase the look-out and use visual bearings. Log the failure and report it according to the company SMS.

Is a gyrocompass compulsory on all ships?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires a gyrocompass, or other non-magnetic means of determining and transmitting heading, on ships of 500 GT and upwards. Smaller and non-SOLAS vessels follow their flag state's rules. Check the ship's Record of Equipment for what is actually required on board.

Test yourself

A ship steams north at speed. With no speed correction applied, how will the gyrocompass read?

On northerly courses the gyro settles west of the meridian and reads high; the error is greatest on N/S courses and zero on E/W. Reference: standard gyrocompass theory; correction via speed and latitude inputs.

What makes a free gyroscope become north-seeking?

Gravity control turns tilt into a torque, and the resulting precession moves the spin axis towards the meridian; damping then settles it. The gyro is non-magnetic.

How often does STCW require the OOW to determine compass errors at sea?

The watchkeeping principles require compass errors to be determined at least once a watch and, when possible, after any major course alteration. Reference: STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, Part 4-1.

A computed true azimuth is 120.0° and the gyro bearing is 119.0°. What is the gyro error?

The gyro reads less than true, so the error is 1° low and must be added to gyro readings to obtain true.

Why is a conventional gyrocompass tuned to an undamped period of about 84 minutes?

Schuler tuning makes the gyro largely immune to ballistic deflection when the ship changes speed or course. Latitude error is handled separately by the latitude setting.
