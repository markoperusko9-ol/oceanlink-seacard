EPIRB: The Ship's Satellite Distress Beacon Explained

Quick answer

An EPIRB (Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacon) is a float-free satellite beacon. If the ship sinks it releases and activates on its own, then sends a 406 MHz distress alert with the ship's identity and position through the COSPAS-SARSAT system to a rescue coordination centre. Crew carry out monthly visual checks and self-tests. SOLAS Chapter IV requires an annual test and periodic shore-based maintenance, and the beacon's registration must be kept up to date.

What an EPIRB does and why every ship carries one

The EPIRB is the last-resort alerting device of the GMDSS. It is built to send a distress alert even when nobody on board can. If the ship goes down so fast that no DSC call, satellite alert or Mayday is made, the EPIRB floats free, switches itself on and tells the shore that a ship is in distress, which ship it is and where it is.

SOLAS Chapter IV (Radiocommunications) requires ships covered by the GMDSS to carry a satellite EPIRB. In practice that means passenger ships and cargo ships of 300 GT and above engaged on international voyages. The regulation requires the beacon to:

  • float free and activate automatically if the ship sinks
  • be capable of manual activation
  • be installed in a readily accessible position
  • be capable of being manually released and carried by one person into a survival craft

Modern ship EPIRBs work on 406 MHz through the COSPAS-SARSAT satellite system. Inmarsat-E (L-band) EPIRBs were withdrawn from service years ago. If you find a reference to them in an old manual, treat it as history only.

Do not confuse the EPIRB with the SART. The EPIRB alerts the shore through satellites. The SART (radar or AIS type) helps a nearby ship or aircraft home in on the survival craft. They do different jobs, and both are needed.

Main components

A typical float-free 406 MHz installation consists of:

  • Beacon unit – a buoyant, watertight housing that contains:
    • the 406 MHz transmitter
    • a 121.5 MHz homing transmitter for SAR aircraft and vessels
    • on most modern units, an internal GNSS receiver that encodes the position in the distress message
  • AIS locating signal – IMO's revised performance standards for float-free 406 MHz EPIRBs added this function for new installations from mid-2022. Older units may have only 121.5 MHz homing.
  • Flashing light (low-duty-cycle strobe) – a flashing light that helps visual location at night.
  • Battery pack – lithium. It is sized to keep the beacon transmitting for at least 48 hours, and it has an expiry date marked on the unit.
  • Water (sea) switch / contacts – these activate the beacon automatically once it is in the water and out of its bracket.
  • Float-free bracket or housing – usually fitted on the monkey island or a bridge wing with a clear path upwards.
  • Hydrostatic release unit (HRU) – a pressure-operated release. It frees the beacon when the ship sinks to a depth of a few metres.
  • Lanyard – used to tether the beacon to a liferaft or survivor. It is not meant to be tied to the ship.
  • Self-test switch and ARM/ON/OFF controls – their layout varies by manufacturer.
  • Labels – the beacon's 15-character Hex ID (Unique Identifier), the ship's name, call sign and/or MMSI, the battery expiry date and the HRU expiry date.

The Hex ID is the key item. The rescue centre uses it to look up the ship's registration details: owner, emergency contacts and vessel description.

How it works, step by step

Scenario: rapid foundering at night, no time to send an alert.

  1. Ship sinks. As the housing goes under, water pressure acts on the HRU. At a depth of a few metres (within about 4 m for typical units) the HRU cuts or releases the retaining strap.
  2. Float-free. The housing opens or the spring pushes the beacon clear, and the beacon floats to the surface.
  3. Automatic activation. Once the beacon is out of the bracket and the water contacts are wet, it switches on. The strobe starts flashing.
  4. 406 MHz burst. The beacon transmits short digital bursts. Each burst carries its unique identity and, if the internal GNSS has a fix, the encoded position.
  5. Satellite relay. The COSPAS-SARSAT satellites pick up the signal. These are in low-earth, medium-earth and geostationary orbits. The satellites relay the signal to a ground station (LUT). Without encoded GNSS position, the system can still calculate a position from the signal itself, although this is slower and less precise.
  6. Mission control and RCC. The ground station passes the data to a mission control centre. That centre forwards the alert to the responsible RCC. The RCC checks the registration database, tries to contact the ship and its emergency contacts, and launches SAR.
  7. Final location. SAR units home on the 121.5 MHz signal and, where fitted, the AIS locator. At close range they use the strobe light. In parallel, the SART guides them to the actual survival craft.

Manual use: if you are abandoning ship and have time, release the beacon from its bracket, take it to the survival craft and activate it manually as per the instructions on the label. Tether it to the raft with its lanyard. A beacon tied to the sinking ship goes down with her.

Routine checks and maintenance

Who does the onboard checks varies by company SMS. Usually the GMDSS operator or the officer responsible for radio equipment, often the 2nd or 3rd Officer, does them and records them in the GMDSS radio log. Always follow the manufacturer's manual. The self-test method differs between makes.

Interval What is done Notes
Monthly (onboard) Visual check of the beacon and bracket; self-test; check expiry dates Typical company or manufacturer practice. Record it in the radio log
Annual Test of the satellite EPIRB as required under SOLAS Chapter IV Done by a qualified surveyor or an approved testing or servicing facility. Usually tied to the radio survey window. IMO guidance is in MSC/Circ.1039
Shore-based maintenance Full servicing ashore at the interval the flag accepts (not exceeding five years) IMO guidance is in MSC/Circ.1040. The battery is usually renewed at the same time
As marked HRU replacement Disposable HRUs carry a service-life date, commonly two years. Check the label
As marked Battery replacement Done by an approved service agent only

Monthly visual check: what to look at

  • The housing is intact, with no cracks and no paint or marine growth on the beacon.
  • The beacon is seated correctly, and the release strap and HRU are fitted the right way round.
  • The lanyard is neatly stowed and not made fast to the ship's structure.
  • The battery and HRU expiry dates are in date.
  • The Hex ID, ship name and call sign or MMSI are legible and correct.
  • The float-free path is clear of obstructions such as aerials, awnings or stores lashed nearby.
  • Operating instructions are visible.

Self-test: use only the dedicated self-test function. In self-test mode the beacon sends a special test frame that the satellite system recognises as a test, not an alert. The beacon then shows a pass or fail through a light or buzzer. Each self-test draws on the battery, so do not test more often than the manufacturer recommends. Some units have a separate GNSS self-test that uses even more battery and is restricted to a set number of uses.

Registration: the paperwork that saves lives

An EPIRB that is not registered, or that is registered to the ship's previous identity, still sends an alert. However, the RCC loses valuable time working out who you are.

  • Every 406 MHz beacon must be registered with the authority responsible for its country code. Normally this is the flag administration or its designated registry.
  • The registration links the Hex ID to the ship's name, call sign, MMSI, owner and emergency contacts.
  • On change of flag, name, MMSI or ownership, the registration must be updated. Many administrations also require the beacon to be reprogrammed with the new country code or MMSI by an approved agent. The exact requirement depends on the flag, so check yours.
  • When a beacon is replaced, the old one's registration should be cancelled.
  • Keep the registration certificate or confirmation on board. Port State Control and radio surveyors commonly ask to see it.

Common faults, safety precautions and false alerts

Typical faults and symptoms

Symptom Likely cause
Self-test fail indication Low or expired battery, internal fault, water ingress
No GNSS fix on GNSS self-test Poor sky view during the test, receiver fault, battery low
Beacon will not lift out of the bracket by hand Strap over-tight, HRU fitted wrongly, corrosion or paint on the housing
Condensation or moisture inside the beacon Seal failure. Remove from service and get it to a service agent
Unexpected activation in heavy weather Beacon washed out of its housing or bracket, or the water switch made by spray because the unit was poorly seated
Label shows a different ship or MMSI Beacon not reprogrammed after change of identity or transfer from another ship

Precautions

  • Never open the beacon or tamper with the lithium battery. Leave it to approved service agents.
  • Do not paint the beacon, its housing or the HRU.
  • Never secure the lanyard to the ship.
  • During drills, explain manual release and activation, but do not actually activate the beacon.
  • When handling the beacon in port or during maintenance, take care that it does not get wet while out of the bracket.

Inadvertent activation

False alerts tie up SAR resources and can lead to enquiries from the flag or the RCC. If the beacon goes off by accident:

  1. Stop the transmission as per the manufacturer's instructions and your company procedure. Some authorities prefer the beacon to be left on until the RCC has been contacted, so follow your flag's or company's guidance.
  2. Contact the nearest RCC or coast station without delay to cancel the alert. Give the ship's name, call sign, MMSI, the beacon Hex ID, the position and the time of activation.
  3. Inform the Master and the company. Record everything in the GMDSS log.
  4. Find out why it happened. Re-seat or repair the unit, or replace it, before relying on it again.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

Q: What happens to the EPIRB if the ship sinks? The HRU releases it at a depth of a few metres. It floats free, activates automatically on contact with water and transmits on 406 MHz to COSPAS-SARSAT.

Q: What is the 121.5 MHz signal for? It is for homing by SAR units. It is not for satellite alerting.

Q: How long must it transmit? At least 48 hours on its battery.

Q: What is the Hex ID and why does it matter? It is the beacon's unique identifier. The RCC uses it to look up the registration and identify the ship and its emergency contacts.

Q: What do you check monthly? - Housing, bracket, HRU and lanyard - Battery and HRU expiry dates - Labels and identity - Clear float-free path - Self-test as per the manufacturer - An entry in the radio log

Q: What testing does SOLAS require? An annual test of the satellite EPIRB, and shore-based maintenance at intervals accepted by the administration, not exceeding five years.

Q: The EPIRB has gone off in your watch by accident. Action? Stop the transmission as per procedure, cancel with the nearest RCC or coast station giving the ship's details and Hex ID, inform the Master, and log it.

Q: What is the difference between an EPIRB and a SART? The EPIRB alerts the shore through satellites. The SART allows a nearby searching unit to locate the survival craft by radar or AIS.

Key points
  • The EPIRB is a float-free 406 MHz beacon that alerts rescue centres through COSPAS-SARSAT when nobody on board can.
  • The HRU releases it within a few metres of depth, and it activates automatically when it is in the water and out of its bracket.
  • The 406 MHz signal carries identity and GNSS position; 121.5 MHz, and AIS on newer units, are used for final homing.
  • Monthly onboard checks cover the self-test, expiry dates, labels, lanyard and a clear float-free path.
  • SOLAS Chapter IV requires an annual test and shore-based maintenance at intervals not exceeding five years.
  • Registration must match the ship's current flag, name and MMSI; reprogramming rules vary by flag.
  • Cancel any false alert at once with the nearest RCC or coast station and log it.

Frequently asked questions

How often should the EPIRB be tested on board?

Most companies and manufacturers call for a monthly visual check and self-test, recorded in the GMDSS radio log. In addition, SOLAS requires an annual test by a qualified person or approved facility. Do not self-test more often than the manufacturer recommends, because each test uses battery capacity.

What is the difference between an EPIRB and a SART?

The EPIRB sends a distress alert with identity and position to rescue centres through satellites. A SART, either radar or AIS type, is used at the scene so that ships and aircraft can locate the survival craft. Ships need both, and they are not interchangeable.

What should I do if the EPIRB is activated by mistake?

Stop the transmission following the manufacturer's instructions and company procedure, then contact the nearest RCC or coast station immediately to cancel the alert. Give the ship's name, call sign, MMSI, the beacon Hex ID and the position, inform the Master, and record it in the log.

Does the EPIRB need to be re-registered when the ship changes flag?

Yes. The registration must reflect the ship's current identity and owner. Many administrations also require the beacon to be reprogrammed with the new country code or MMSI by an approved service agent. The exact rule depends on the new flag.

Can I replace the EPIRB battery myself?

No. The battery is a sealed lithium pack and must be replaced by an approved service agent, usually as part of shore-based maintenance. Opening the beacon on board can break its watertight seal and invalidate its approval.

Test yourself

On which frequency does a modern ship's EPIRB send its satellite distress alert?

Satellite alerting through COSPAS-SARSAT is on 406 MHz; 121.5 MHz is only for homing. Reference: SOLAS Chapter IV.

What should the EPIRB lanyard be used for?

A lanyard made fast to the ship would drag the beacon down with her; it is meant for tethering to survival craft. Reference: manufacturer's operating instructions.

How long must a 406 MHz EPIRB be able to keep transmitting on its battery?

IMO performance standards require a battery capacity for at least 48 hours of operation. Reference: IMO Resolution MSC.471(101).

What is the main purpose of the EPIRB's Hex ID?

The unique identifier links the alert to the registration database entry for the ship and its emergency contacts. Reference: SOLAS Chapter IV (registration of GMDSS identities).

Which statement about periodic EPIRB testing under SOLAS is correct?

SOLAS Chapter IV requires annual testing and periodic shore-based maintenance; IMO guidance is in MSC/Circ.1039 and MSC/Circ.1040.
