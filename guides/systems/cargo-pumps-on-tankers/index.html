Cargo pumps on tankers: a practical guide for officers, pumpmen and cadets

Quick answer

Most tanker cargo pumps are centrifugal. They are either in a pump room driven through the bulkhead, deepwell pumps with a motor on deck, or submerged hydraulic or electric pumps inside the tank. Stripping pumps, eductors or a vacuum stripping system recover the last of the cargo. You need to understand suction conditions, cavitation, seals and the pump-room safety arrangements to run a discharge safely and to pass your oral.

What cargo pumps do and the main types

Cargo pumps discharge cargo to shore and transfer it between tanks. On many ships they also drive crude oil washing and tank cleaning, and sometimes ballast. The pump arrangement depends on the ship type and on the builder's and owner's choices. Always learn the system on your own ship from the operating manual and the cargo piping diagram.

Common arrangements:

  • Pump-room centrifugal pumps. These are typical on crude carriers and large product tankers. The pumps sit at the bottom of a pump room aft of the cargo tanks. They are driven through a gastight bulkhead seal by a steam turbine or electric motor in the engine room.
  • Deepwell pumps. These are common on product and some gas tankers. The motor (electric or hydraulic) sits on deck, and a long shaft runs down to an impeller near the tank bottom. The line shaft bearings are usually lubricated by the cargo itself.
  • Submerged hydraulic pumps. These are widely used on chemical and product tankers, often one pump per tank. A hydraulic motor at the bottom of the tank drives the impeller. High-pressure hydraulic oil comes from a power pack, and a cofferdam separates the cargo from the hydraulic oil.
  • Submerged electric pumps. These are standard on LNG carriers and are also fitted on some LPG carriers (many LPG carriers use deepwell pumps instead). The motor and pump sit together in the tank and are cooled and lubricated by the cargo.
  • Stripping equipment. This means positive displacement pumps (reciprocating or screw), eductors driven by cargo or water, or an automatic vacuum stripping system on the main pumps.

The basic split matters for your oral. Centrifugal pumps give high volume and are tolerant of throttling, but they are not self-priming and suffer when suction is poor. Positive displacement pumps self-prime and strip well, but they must never be run against a closed discharge.

Main components

Centrifugal pump (pump-room type):

  • Casing (volute) and impeller, often with an inducer on the suction side to help with low suction head.
  • Shaft with mechanical seal or gland packing.
  • Bearings with temperature sensors.
  • Bulkhead shaft seal (gastight) where the drive shaft passes from the engine room into the pump room.
  • Suction strainer.
  • Suction and discharge valves.
  • Pressure gauges on suction and discharge.
  • A minimum-flow or recirculation line where fitted.

Drive (turbine type):

  • Steam turbine with governor and overspeed trip.
  • Lubricating oil system.
  • Condenser or exhaust arrangement.

Vacuum stripping (automatic unloading) system:

  • A separator tank on the pump suction.
  • Vacuum pumps that draw off vapour and gas.
  • A level-controlled discharge valve that throttles the pump as suction weakens. This keeps the pump primed while the tank is drained down.

Submerged hydraulic pump:

  • Pump head with hydraulic motor and impeller in the tank sump.
  • Pipe stack carrying the cargo discharge, hydraulic supply and return, and the cofferdam.
  • Cofferdam purge connection.
  • Deck control valve.
  • Hydraulic power pack with filters, cooler and its own alarms.

Monitoring and controls:

  • Cargo control room console with pressure readouts and pump speed or hydraulic pressure.
  • Temperature alarms.
  • Emergency stops in locations defined by the ship's design.
  • Tank level gauges.
  • Inert gas system readouts, since IG supply must keep pace with the discharge rate.

How a discharge runs, step by step

This is a generic sequence for a centrifugal cargo pump discharge. Always follow your ship's cargo plan, operating manual and SMS. The chief officer's discharge plan and the agreed ship/shore information take precedence.

  1. Pre-checks. Do these before starting:
    • Line-up checked by two people against the plan.
    • Pump-room ventilation running and atmosphere checked if entry is needed.
    • Bilge alarms and temperature alarms tested.
    • Inert gas plant ready, where fitted and required.
    • Shore confirms it is ready and has given its maximum pressure and rate.
  2. Prime the pump. A centrifugal pump needs liquid at the impeller. Open the tank suction so that cargo floods the pump, and vent air if the design requires it.
  3. Start against a closed or nearly closed discharge. This is normal for centrifugals, because power demand is lowest at zero flow. Do not run it like this for long, because the trapped liquid heats up quickly.
  4. Open the discharge slowly and bring the pump up to speed. Start at a reduced rate until shore confirms it is receiving cargo and there are no leaks.
  5. Increase to the agreed rate. Watch the following:
    • Discharge pressure against the shore and manifold limits.
    • Suction pressure.
    • Bearing and casing temperatures.
    • Tank pressure. IG supply must keep the tanks positive and must never let them go under vacuum.
  6. Monitor throughout. Hold regular pump-room rounds where the SMS permits entry. Check for gland or seal leaks, vibration, unusual noise and bilge levels. Log pressures, rates and tank levels.
  7. Draining down. As tank levels fall, suction head drops and cavitation risk rises.
    • Trim the ship by the stern for draining, as the plan requires.
    • Reduce the rate.
    • Bring in the vacuum stripping system, or change over to the stripping pump or eductor.
  8. Stripping and line draining. Strip the tanks, then drain the lines and pump to shore through the small-diameter line where fitted.
  9. Shut down. Reduce speed, close the discharge, then stop. On a turbine, follow the shutdown and drain procedure. Secure valves as the plan requires.

Worked scenario. Near the end of discharging a crude tank, the discharge pressure starts swinging, the pump note becomes rough and the rate falls. This is classic cavitation from falling suction head. Do not simply open up the turbine. Reduce the pump speed and check that the stripping system is controlling. Confirm the trim, then change over to stripping if the tank is nearly empty. Running on in cavitation damages the impeller and seals.

Routine checks and maintenance

Intervals come from the maker's manual and the ship's planned maintenance system, not from a universal rule.

Before and during each operation:

  • Lube oil levels and pressures.
  • Seal and gland condition.
  • Temperature sensor and alarm function.
  • Emergency stop function, tested as your SMS requires.

Pump room:

  • Bilges kept dry and bilge alarms tested.
  • Gas detection sampling points clear.
  • Ventilation fans and dampers working.
  • No rags or oily debris.
  • Bulkhead seal checked for leakage and lubrication.

Strainers: Clean them after cargoes that leave sediment, and whenever suction pressure suggests a restriction.

Submerged hydraulic pumps:

  • Purge the cofferdams at the interval the maker and SMS specify, and record what comes out. Cargo in the purge suggests cargo seal wear. Hydraulic oil suggests hydraulic seal wear.
  • Monitor hydraulic oil condition and filters.
  • Watch for hydraulic oil contamination of the cargo, which is a commercial problem on chemical and clean product cargoes.

Turbine drives:

  • Test the overspeed trip as the maker requires.
  • Check governor operation.
  • Make sure steam lines are properly drained and warmed before starting.

Condition monitoring:

  • Vibration readings.
  • Comparison of performance against the shop test curve. Rate at a given speed and pressure that falls over time points to impeller or wear-ring wear.

Safety precautions and the regulatory framework

The cargo pump room is one of the most hazardous spaces on a tanker. It sits low in the ship, it is next to cargo and it is full of potential leak sources. Fatalities have occurred there from gas, oxygen deficiency and fire.

Pump-room safety:

  • Treat the pump room according to your SMS. Many companies treat it as an enclosed space or apply specific entry controls.
  • Before entry, check:
    • Ventilation is running.
    • Atmosphere is tested.
    • Communication is arranged.
    • Escape sets or EEBDs are available as required.
    • Someone on deck knows who is inside.
  • Never let the pump room become a store. Oily rags and debris feed fires.

Regulatory framework (at chapter level, and only where it applies to your ship type and build date):

  • SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4 covers the protection of cargo pump rooms on tankers. It includes:
    • Temperature sensing for bulkhead shaft glands, bearings and pump casings.
    • Continuous monitoring of hydrocarbon gas concentration.
    • Bilge high-level alarms.
    • Lighting interlocked with ventilation.

Applicability depends on the ship's construction date. - SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 10 requires fixed fire-extinguishing arrangements in cargo pump rooms. The FSS Code gives the technical standards for fixed gas, foam and water-spray systems. - MARPOL Annex I covers oil tanker pumping, piping and discharge arrangements and crude oil washing. - MARPOL Annex II, Regulation 12 sets maximum stripping residue quantities for noxious liquid substance tankers. For ships built on or after 1 January 2007, the limit is 75 litres for Category X, Y and Z products. These limits are verified by a stripping test. - The IBC Code and IGC Code contain cargo-specific pumping and equipment requirements for chemical and gas carriers.

Operational safety:

  • Stay within manifold and shore pressure limits.
  • Agree an emergency stop procedure with the terminal.
  • Never close a valve against a running positive displacement pump.
  • Avoid closing valves fast against flow, because of pressure surge.
  • Keep inert gas supply matched to the discharge rate, where IG is required.

Oral exam style questions

Practise answering these out loud, in your own words.

  • Why do you start a centrifugal pump with the discharge closed but a positive displacement pump with it open? At zero flow a centrifugal draws minimum power, and pressure is limited by its curve. A PD pump keeps displacing liquid, so pressure rises until something fails or the relief valve lifts.
  • What is NPSH and why does it matter on a tanker? Net positive suction head is the margin between suction pressure and the cargo's vapour pressure. If the available NPSH falls below what the pump requires, the cargo flashes to vapour at the impeller and the pump cavitates. Low tank levels and volatile or warm cargoes reduce the margin.
  • How would you recognise and deal with cavitation during draining? Answer from the symptoms and actions in the sections above.
  • What safety features protect a cargo pump room? Expect to list:
    • Temperature sensors and alarms.
    • Gas monitoring.
    • Bilge alarms.
    • Ventilation and lighting interlock.
    • Fixed fire-fighting.
    • The gastight bulkhead seal.
    • Emergency stops.
  • What does a cofferdam purge tell you on a submerged pump? Whether the cargo seal or the hydraulic seal is leaking, and how the leakage trend is developing.
  • Why is trim by the stern used when stripping? To run cargo towards the aft suctions or wells, so the pump keeps its suction as long as possible.
Key points
  • Most tanker cargo pumps are centrifugal: high volume, but not self-priming and vulnerable to cavitation when suction head is low.
  • Pump arrangements include pump-room pumps driven through a bulkhead seal, deepwell pumps, and submerged hydraulic or electric pumps.
  • Start centrifugals against a closed or nearly closed discharge; never close the discharge of a running positive displacement pump.
  • Cavitation shows as noise, fluctuating pressure and falling rate. Reduce the rate and strip rather than pushing more power.
  • Purge cofferdams on submerged hydraulic pumps as the maker and SMS require, and log the results to track seal wear.
  • SOLAS II-2 Regulations 4 and 10 cover cargo pump-room protection and fire-extinguishing; MARPOL Annex II limits stripping residues on chemical tankers.
  • Pump-room entry, maintenance intervals and test frequencies follow your company SMS and the maker's manual.

Frequently asked questions

What type of cargo pump is used on oil tankers?

Mostly centrifugal pumps. Crude carriers usually have them in a pump room, driven by steam turbines or electric motors. Product and chemical tankers often use deepwell or submerged hydraulic pumps in each tank. Stripping is done by positive displacement pumps, eductors or a vacuum stripping system.

Why does a cargo pump cavitate near the end of discharge?

As the tank level falls, the suction head at the pump drops. Eventually the pressure at the impeller eye falls below the cargo's vapour pressure. Vapour bubbles form and collapse, causing noise, vibration, pressure swings and impeller damage. Reducing the rate, trimming by the stern and changing over to stripping are the usual remedies.

What is the purpose of the cofferdam on a Framo-type submerged pump?

The cofferdam separates the cargo from the hydraulic oil that drives the pump motor. Purging it shows whether cargo or hydraulic oil is leaking past the seals. The maker and the company SMS set how often you purge and what leakage is acceptable.

Is a cargo pump room an enclosed space?

Cargo pump rooms carry enclosed-space type hazards: hydrocarbon gas, possible oxygen deficiency and toxic cargo vapours. Companies apply specific entry controls, including ventilation before entry, atmosphere testing and communications. Follow your own SMS, as the exact procedure differs between companies.

What is the stripping residue limit for chemical tankers?

Under MARPOL Annex II, Regulation 12, ships built on or after 1 January 2007 must strip Category X, Y and Z tanks to no more than 75 litres of residue. Older ships have different limits depending on their build date. Performance is confirmed by a stripping test recorded in the ship's documents.

Test yourself

How should a centrifugal cargo pump normally be started?

Centrifugal pumps draw minimum power at zero flow and must have liquid at the impeller, so they are primed and started against a closed or nearly closed discharge. This is standard pump practice reflected in maker operating manuals.

During draining, discharge pressure fluctuates, the pump sounds like gravel and the rate drops. What is the best first action?

These are signs of cavitation from falling suction head. Reducing the rate and changing over to stripping protects the pump, while adding power makes the damage worse.

Which feature is required by SOLAS Chapter II-2 for cargo pump-room protection on tankers to which it applies?

SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4 requires temperature sensing for glands, bearings and casings, alongside gas monitoring, bilge alarms and a lighting/ventilation interlock. Regulation 10 allows several types of fixed fire-extinguishing system, not just CO2.

A cofferdam purge on a submerged hydraulic cargo pump produces hydraulic oil. What does this most likely indicate?

The cofferdam lies between the cargo and the hydraulic system, so hydraulic oil in the purge points to the hydraulic seal. Cargo in the purge would point to the cargo seal, as described in maker manuals.

Why must the discharge of a running positive displacement stripping pump never be closed?

A positive displacement pump delivers a fixed volume per stroke or revolution, so pressure climbs against a closed valve until the relief valve lifts or something fails. This is basic pump theory reflected in maker manuals.
