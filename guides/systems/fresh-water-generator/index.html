Fresh Water Generator on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

Most merchant ships make their own fresh water in a low-pressure evaporator. It boils seawater under vacuum using waste heat from the main engine jacket cooling water, then condenses the vapour into distillate. This guide covers how the plant works, how to start and stop it, routine checks, common faults and the questions examiners ask.

What the fresh water generator does and why it runs under vacuum

The fresh water generator (FWG), also called the evaporator, produces distilled water from seawater. That water feeds the domestic system, boiler feed and engine cooling make-up. On most motor ships it is a low-pressure evaporator: seawater boils inside a shell held at a deep vacuum, so it boils well below 100°C.

There are three reasons for running under vacuum:

  • Free heat. Main engine jacket cooling water carries a lot of heat that would otherwise go to the central or seawater cooler. At low pressure that heat is hot enough to boil seawater, so the plant costs little to run apart from pump power.
  • Less scale. Hard scale, especially calcium sulphate, forms much more readily at high temperatures. A low boiling temperature keeps scaling down.
  • Simpler construction. There is no need for a pressurised steam-heated vessel when waste heat will do.

There is one consequence that cadets often miss. Because the water boils at a low temperature, the distillate is not sterilised by the process. That is why drinking water needs separate treatment downstream.

Some ships, especially passenger ships and vessels with little waste heat, use reverse osmosis (RO) plants instead of, or as well as, evaporators. RO pushes seawater through membranes at high pressure. It is a different technology with its own maintenance regime. This guide concentrates on the vacuum evaporator.

Main components

Designs vary between makers, with plate-type and shell-and-tube versions. The building blocks are much the same:

Component Function
Evaporator section (heater) Plates or tubes heated by jacket water; seawater feed boils on the other side
Separator / demister Mesh or vane element that strips carried-over brine droplets from the vapour
Condenser Plates or tubes cooled by seawater; vapour condenses into distillate
Combined brine/air ejector Driven by seawater from the ejector pump; creates the vacuum and removes brine and non-condensable gases
Ejector pump Supplies driving seawater to the ejector; condenser cooling and feed water are often taken from the same supply
Feed water line with orifice/flow control Meters seawater into the evaporator at the correct rate
Chemical dosing unit Injects scale inhibitor into the feed
Distillate pump Draws condensed water from the condenser and delivers it onward
Salinometer with solenoid dump valve Measures distillate salinity and diverts off-spec water away from the tanks
Jacket water inlet/outlet and bypass valves Control how much engine heat goes through the heater
Vacuum gauge, thermometers, flowmeter Let you monitor the process

Downstream, drinking water usually passes through a rehardening/mineralising filter and a sterilisation unit (UV, silver ion or chlorination, depending on the ship) before it reaches the potable tanks.

How it works and how to start and stop it

The process in one line: jacket water heats the seawater feed, the feed boils under vacuum, the vapour passes the demister and condenses, the distillate pump sends it through the salinometer to the tanks, and the ejector removes brine and air overboard.

Typical start-up. Always follow the maker's manual and your company SMS, because sequences differ.

  1. Confirm the main engine is running at sea with steady load and jacket water at normal temperature. Confirm the ship is outside the area where your SMS forbids making water (see below).
  2. Open the seawater suction and overboard valves for the ejector pump. Check the vacuum breaker or air-vent valve is shut.
  3. Start the ejector pump and check its discharge pressure against the maker's figure.
  4. Wait for the vacuum to build to the maker's stated value. On many plants this is around 90%, but go by your gauge and manual.
  5. Open the feed water valve. On many units feed starts automatically with the ejector pump. Start chemical dosing.
  6. Open the jacket water inlet and outlet valves slowly, then gradually close the bypass. Watch the main engine jacket water temperature so the engine control valve can follow.
  7. Boiling starts. You will see the vacuum drop slightly and the shell temperature rise.
  8. Once water shows in the condenser sight glass, switch on the salinometer and start the distillate pump. Distillate is dumped or recirculated until the salinity is below the alarm set point, then it goes to the tank.
  9. Adjust the heating to get stable output. Log vacuum, temperatures, salinity and the flowmeter reading.

Stopping. Remove the heat first:

  1. Open the jacket water bypass and close the inlet and outlet.
  2. Stop the distillate pump and switch off the salinometer.
  3. Let the shell cool, then stop the ejector pump and dosing.
  4. Open the vacuum breaker and shut the seawater valves.

Stopping the ejector pump while heat is still on lets the shell lose vacuum while it is hot. Expect scaling and possibly a salty batch next start.

Where not to run it. Coastal and harbour water can carry sewage, oil and silt, and the low boiling temperature will not reliably kill bacteria. Companies therefore set rules in the SMS on how far off the coast the FWG may be used, and some port authorities have their own restrictions. Follow your SMS, not a figure you heard on another ship.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch or daily round:

  • Vacuum, shell temperature, jacket water in/out temperatures and ejector pump pressure, compared against the logbook trend.
  • Salinometer reading and dump valve operation.
  • Distillate output on the flowmeter. A falling trend at the same engine load usually means fouling or a vacuum problem.
  • Dosing tank level, and the scale inhibitor mixed to the maker's ratio.
  • Leaks at plate packs, gaskets and valve glands.

Periodic, per the planned maintenance system:

  • Salinometer test. Check the alarm and dump function using the test facility or a test solution, as the maker describes. Clean the cell electrodes.
  • Chemical cleaning of heating and condenser surfaces when output falls or the PMS calls for it. Use only the cleaning agent and concentration the maker approves. Rinse thoroughly before putting the plant back on line.
  • Opening up plate packs or tube stacks. Inspect for scale, fouling, corrosion, damaged gaskets and a holed or collapsed demister.
  • Ejector nozzle and diffuser. Inspect for erosion. A worn nozzle is a classic cause of poor vacuum.
  • Downstream treatment. Replace UV lamps, mineraliser media and filters on schedule. Check chlorine or silver levels where fitted.
  • Potable water tanks. Cleaning, coating inspection and water sampling as your SMS requires.

Common faults and what they tell you

Symptom Likely causes
Poor or falling vacuum Low ejector pump pressure (worn impeller, choked suction strainer); eroded ejector nozzle; air leak at gaskets, sight glass or vacuum breaker; high seawater temperature; too much heat for the condenser to handle
High salinity Overfeeding or too much heat causing priming and carry-over; damaged or misplaced demister; leaking condenser plate or tube letting seawater into the distillate; dirty or faulty salinometer cell giving a false reading
Low output Scaled heating surfaces; low jacket water temperature or flow (low engine load, bypass partly open); fouled condenser; incorrect feed rate
Distillate pump losing suction Pump started too early; air leak on suction side; condenser level too low; worn seal
Main engine jacket water temperature swings Jacket water valves opened or closed too quickly; engine temperature controller not keeping up

Worked scenario. On the 8-12 watch you notice salinity creeping up over two days while vacuum and output are normal. Check the salinometer cell first, since a dirty cell can read high. If the reading is genuine, reduce the heat in case you are overdriving the plant. If salinity stays high at a reduced boiling rate with a steady vacuum, suspect a condenser leak. Seawater at higher pressure than the shell is entering the distillate side. Report it to the 2nd Engineer, keep the distillate dumping, and plan a pressure test of the condenser.

Safety and drinking water hygiene

  • Never bypass or disable the salinometer alarm or dump valve to keep water going to the tank. Salty distillate in boiler feed or jacket water causes corrosion and scale. Salty or contaminated drinking water is a health issue.
  • Chemicals. Scale inhibitors and acid cleaners need the correct PPE and handling per their safety data sheets. Cleaning is normally done under a permit or risk assessment per the SMS.
  • Hot surfaces and isolation. Isolate jacket water and seawater properly, and depressurise before opening up. Lock-out tag-out applies to the pumps.
  • Engine first. Do not let FWG operation upset the main engine. Watch jacket water temperature when bringing the plant on and off line, and take it off line if the engine load drops sharply.
  • Potable water. Under the Maritime Labour Convention, seafarers must have drinking water of suitable quality. The convention also requires frequent documented inspections of drinking water supplies under the master's authority. Treatment, sampling, tank cleaning and record-keeping details come from the flag state and company SMS, so follow those.

Oral exam style questions

Why does the FWG operate under vacuum? To lower the boiling point so that low-grade jacket water heat can be used, and to reduce scale formation at the heating surfaces.

Is distillate from the evaporator safe to drink straight away? No. The low boiling temperature does not reliably sterilise it, and it lacks minerals. It must be rehardened and sterilised before going to potable tanks.

Vacuum has fallen. Talk me through your fault-finding. Check ejector pump discharge pressure and the suction strainer. Then check seawater temperature. Then look for air leaks at the vacuum breaker, gaskets and sight glasses. Finally inspect the ejector nozzle for erosion.

What happens if you feed too much heat? Violent boiling and priming carry brine past the demister, so salinity rises. The condenser may also be overloaded, causing loss of vacuum.

Why stop the heating before the ejector pump? So the shell does not lose vacuum and run hot and dry. That would cause heavy scaling and possibly damage.

Why is scale inhibitor dosed? To keep scale-forming salts in suspension so they leave with the brine rather than depositing on heating surfaces. This preserves heat transfer and output.

Key points
  • The FWG boils seawater under vacuum using main engine jacket water heat, then condenses the vapour into distillate.
  • Vacuum lowers the boiling temperature, which lets waste heat be used and reduces scale.
  • Distillate is not sterilised by low-temperature boiling; drinking water must be rehardened and sterilised.
  • The salinometer and dump valve protect tanks, boilers and engines from salty distillate; never bypass them.
  • Start with vacuum before heat; stop with heat removal before stopping the ejector pump.
  • Where you may make water near coasts and ports is set by company SMS and local rules, not by one universal figure.
  • Poor vacuum, high salinity and low output each point to a distinct set of causes.

Frequently asked questions

What vacuum should a fresh water generator run at?

Use the figure in the maker's manual for your plant. Many low-pressure evaporators run at roughly 90% vacuum, but this varies with design and seawater temperature. Watch the trend in the logbook rather than chasing a single number.

Why can't the fresh water generator be used near the coast or in port?

Coastal and harbour water may contain sewage, oil and silt. The low boiling temperature will not reliably kill bacteria, and oil can foul the plant. The exact distance or area is set by the company SMS and sometimes by port rules.

What is the salinometer alarm set point on a fresh water generator?

It is set by the maker and the company, typically somewhere in the low single-digit to about 10 ppm range depending on the plant and the intended use of the distillate. Check the plant manual and the SMS for your ship. Whatever the setting, test the alarm and dump function regularly.

Why is FWG output low at reduced engine load?

The heat source is the jacket cooling water. At low load there is less heat available, and the jacket water temperature and flow through the heater may fall. Scaled heating plates make it worse, so compare output against the trend at similar loads.

What is the difference between a fresh water generator and a reverse osmosis plant?

A fresh water generator distils seawater by boiling it under vacuum with waste heat. A reverse osmosis plant forces seawater through semi-permeable membranes at high pressure using electrically driven pumps. Each has different maintenance, and both need post-treatment for drinking water.

Test yourself

What is the main reason a ship's evaporator operates under vacuum?

Reduced pressure lowers the boiling point, allowing low-grade jacket water heat to boil seawater and reducing scale. Reference: maker's manual / STCW Code, Table A-III/1 knowledge of auxiliary machinery.

Salinity rises while vacuum and output are steady, even after reducing heat. What is the most likely cause?

Persistent high salinity with stable vacuum and reduced heat points to seawater leaking into the distillate side through the condenser. Reference: plant maker's fault-finding guidance.

When shutting down the fresh water generator, what should be done first?

Remove the heat first so the shell does not lose vacuum while hot, which would cause scaling and possible damage. Reference: maker's operating manual / company SMS.

Why must evaporator distillate be treated before going to drinking water tanks?

Low-temperature distillation neither reliably kills bacteria nor leaves minerals, so rehardening and sterilisation are needed. Drinking water quality is a requirement of MLC 2006, Regulation 3.2.

Which of these is a typical cause of poor vacuum in the evaporator?

The ejector creates the vacuum, so low driving water pressure from a choked strainer or worn pump directly reduces vacuum. Reference: maker's fault-finding guidance.
