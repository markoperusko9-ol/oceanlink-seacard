Watertight Doors on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

Watertight doors are closures in watertight bulkheads that let crew pass through while preserving the ship's subdivision. If one is open or leaking when the hull is breached, flooding can spread into the next compartment and sink the ship. This guide covers how hinged and power-operated sliding doors work, what to check, the faults you will meet, how to stay safe around them, and the exam questions that come up.

What a watertight door is for

A ship's damage stability depends on subdivision. Watertight bulkheads split the hull into compartments so that a breach floods only a limited volume. Every opening in those bulkheads is a weak point. The watertight door exists to close that weak point quickly and reliably.

Keep two terms apart, because examiners will test you on them:

  • Watertight means the closure keeps water out under the head of water likely to occur in intact and damaged conditions, in any direction.
  • Weathertight means water will not get through in any sea condition. This covers spray and green seas on deck, not a sustained head of water.

Watertight doors are also not the same as fire doors. Some doors serve both purposes, but they are built and approved against different requirements.

The golden rule is simple. A watertight door does its job only when it is closed. A door that is open, wedged or obstructed gives the ship no protection at all.

Types and main components

You will meet two broad types on merchant ships.

Hinged watertight doors - A steel door leaf with a rubber gasket that seats against a knife edge on the frame. - Several dogs (cleats) around the edge pull the leaf onto the gasket. On some doors a single lever works all the dogs together. - Common in steering gear flats, stores and wherever a door is kept closed at sea and opened only occasionally for access.

Power-operated sliding watertight doors - The door leaf slides horizontally or vertically in a frame. It seals by metal-to-metal contact with wedges, or by a seal profile, depending on the maker. - They are driven by a hydraulic cylinder or, on some makes, an electric drive. - A hydraulic power unit has a pump, a reservoir and an accumulator charged with nitrogen. The accumulator stores enough energy to work the door when the power supply is lost. - Control handles are fitted on both sides of the door. - A hand pump at the door allows local manual operation. On passenger ships there is also remote hand operation from above the bulkhead deck. - Limit switches detect the open and closed positions and feed the indicators. - Local warning devices give an audible alarm and, where required, a flashing light. - A bridge control panel shows every door's position and allows central closing.

The exact layout varies by maker and by ship. Read the manufacturer's manual for the doors on your own vessel during familiarisation.

How a power-operated sliding door works, step by step

A typical electro-hydraulic sliding door on a passenger ship works like this:

  1. Normal local operation. You move the control handle on your side to OPEN. A valve sends hydraulic oil to the cylinder and the door slides open.
  2. Passing through. The handles on both sides are arranged so that you can hold them in the open position while passing through without accidentally setting the power closing mechanism in operation.
  3. Closing. You release the handle or move it to CLOSE. The door closes under power and the closed limit switch confirms the position. The local and bridge indicators then show closed.
  4. Bridge central closing. On passenger ships the bridge panel usually has a "local control" mode and a "doors closed" mode. In "doors closed" mode all doors close together.
    • Someone may still open a door locally to get through.
    • Once the local handle is released, that door closes again automatically.
  5. Warning. Before and during a remotely ordered closure, the local audible alarm sounds so that anyone nearby gets clear.
  6. Loss of power. The accumulator, and the emergency power supply, still allow the door to be operated a limited number of times.
  7. Total failure. The door can be closed using the hand pump at the door. On passenger ships it can also be closed from the remote hand position above the bulkhead deck.

For passenger ships, SOLAS Chapter II-1 sets detailed performance requirements for these doors. They cover:

  • closing times
  • operation with the ship listed
  • the timing of the audible alarm
  • the power supply from the emergency source
  • stored-energy capacity

Cargo ship requirements are less prescriptive. The details also depend on when the ship was built. Your ship's approved documentation and class records show which requirements apply to your vessel.

Routine checks and maintenance

Watertight doors belong in the planned maintenance system. Under ISM Code section 10, companies typically identify them as equipment whose sudden failure could cause a hazardous situation. Intervals and test methods come from the maker, class and the company SMS. The jobs themselves are much the same on every ship.

Hinged doors - Inspect the gasket. Look for paint, cuts, hardening, missing sections and permanent set, which shows as a deep groove pressed in by the knife edge. - Never paint the gasket or the knife edge. - Grease the dogs and hinges, and check that they move freely. - Adjust the dogs so the gasket compresses evenly all round. - Do a chalk test. Chalk the knife edge, close and dog the door, then open it. A continuous chalk line on the gasket shows even contact. A break in the line shows a gap. - Check that the frame is straight and the knife edge is undamaged.

Sliding doors - Clean the tracks and sill. Rust scale, rags, wire and grit can stop full closure. - Check the hydraulic oil level and look for leaks at the cylinder, hoses and fittings. - Check accumulator pressure against the maker's figure. Recharge the nitrogen only with the correct equipment and procedure. - Test every mode of operation: - locally from both sides - from the bridge - by the local hand pump - by remote hand operation where fitted - Confirm that the alarm and lights work, and that the bridge indication matches the actual door position. - Time the closure and compare it with the maker's and the regulatory figures for your ship type.

Records and drills. SOLAS requires passenger ships to operate their watertight doors frequently and to hold regular drills for them, and these must be logged. Cargo ships follow their SMS and flag requirements. On any ship, record tests, defects and repairs. After a significant repair, class may require a hose test or another tightness test.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Sliding door slow to close or stalls Low accumulator pressure, air in the hydraulics, low oil, a sticking valve, debris in the track
Door closes but leaks on test Damaged or painted gasket, worn wedges, distorted frame, dogs out of adjustment
Bridge shows "open" when the door is shut, or the reverse Limit switch misaligned or damaged, wiring fault, fault in the indicator panel
No alarm on remote closure Failed sounder, a fuse or wiring fault, alarm isolated and not restored
Door drifts open Internal leakage in the valve or cylinder, a faulty check valve
Hand pump does nothing Low oil, air locked, selector valve in the wrong position, worn pump seals
Hinged door dogs seized Lack of greasing, corrosion, paint over the moving parts

Worked scenario. During a bridge closing test, the panel shows one door in the tunnel still open. You go down and find the door stopped about 100 mm short. A temporary welding cable has been run through the doorway.

  • Immediate action: remove the cable and close the door.
  • Confirm: check the indication on the bridge.
  • Report: tell the OOW and the chief engineer.
  • Follow up: run the cable another way, through an approved route or a temporary arrangement accepted under the permit to work.
  • Check for damage: the door has been closing onto an obstruction, so inspect the leaf edge and the seal.

The lesson is that an obstructed door gives you no subdivision at all, however good the hydraulics are.

Safety precautions around watertight doors

Power-operated sliding doors close with great force. They have killed and injured seafarers. Treat them as machinery.

  • Never try to pass through a door that is closing, or when the alarm is sounding.
  • Use the handles as designed. Hold the door open, pass through, then let it close behind you.
  • Do not carry long loads through a door that could be closed remotely. Agree with the bridge first, or set the door to local control under a permit.
  • Never run hoses, cables, ventilation trunks or anything else through a watertight doorway. If an operation genuinely needs it, use a formal permit with the master's approval and a person standing by to clear it.
  • Never wedge, tie back or isolate a door without proper authority and a record of it.
  • Before working on the hydraulics or electrics, isolate and lock out the system and relieve the accumulator pressure. Stored hydraulic energy can move the door even with the power off.
  • Keep notices such as "to be kept closed at sea" clean, legible and obeyed.
  • When you join a ship, find out where every watertight door is, how to work it by hand and where the remote hand operating positions are. This is part of your familiarisation.

Regulatory background

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part B-2 deals with subdivision and watertight and weathertight integrity.
  • It covers openings in watertight bulkheads, separately for passenger ships and for cargo ships.
  • It also covers the construction and initial testing of watertight doors.
  • For passenger ships it covers the periodic operation and inspection of doors and the control of water ingress, including which doors may be open at sea and when.
  • Passenger ship requirements are much more detailed than those for cargo ships. Ro-ro passenger ships have additional provisions.
  • Weathertight doors on exposed decks and superstructures fall mainly under the Load Line Convention as well as SOLAS.
  • Older ships may have been built to earlier editions of SOLAS. Exact requirements, and any flag interpretations, can therefore differ from ship to ship.
  • If you are unsure, the ship's approved plans, class records and the SMS are the authority for your vessel.

Oral exam: questions you should be ready for

Q: What is the difference between watertight and weathertight? Watertight means the closure resists water under the head of water likely to occur in intact and damaged conditions, in any direction. Weathertight means water will not get through in any sea condition, which is protection from the weather rather than a sustained head of water.

Q: How would you close a power-operated sliding door if all power had failed? First use the stored energy in the accumulator, if it is available. Then close it with the local hand pump. On a passenger ship you can also close it from the remote hand position above the bulkhead deck.

Q: Why must you not paint a gasket? Paint hardens the rubber and stops it seating evenly on the knife edge, so the door leaks.

Q: How do you check that a hinged door seals properly? Inspect the gasket and dogs, then do a chalk test on the knife edge. A hose test may be needed after repairs, as class requires.

Q: The bridge panel shows a door open at sea. What do you do? - Treat it as real until it is proved otherwise. - Send someone to check, and close the door if it is not in authorised use. - If it is shut and the indication is false, log it, report it and raise a defect. - Do not assume it is only a sensor fault.

Q: Before working on a door's hydraulic system, what precautions do you take? - Get a permit to work. - Isolate and lock out the power. - Relieve the accumulator pressure. - Inform the bridge so the door is not operated remotely. - Consider whether leaving the door unavailable affects the ship's subdivision while you work.

Key points
  • Watertight doors protect subdivision and damage stability, but only when they are closed.
  • Watertight and weathertight are different standards; examiners test the distinction.
  • Sliding doors work locally, from the bridge and by hand pump, plus remote hand operation on passenger ships.
  • Never paint gaskets or knife edges; chalk-test hinged doors to check even contact.
  • Nothing may run through a watertight doorway; an obstructed door gives no protection.
  • Never pass through a closing door, and relieve accumulator pressure before maintenance.
  • Passenger ship requirements in SOLAS Chapter II-1 are far more detailed than those for cargo ships.

Frequently asked questions

Can watertight doors be left open at sea?

As a rule they are kept closed at sea. Some may be opened briefly for passage, or for specific authorised work, and must be closed straight afterwards. Passenger ships have detailed SOLAS conditions on this. On any ship, follow the master's orders, the SMS and the door notices.

How often are watertight doors tested?

On passenger ships SOLAS requires frequent operation of watertight doors and regular drills, and these must be logged. On cargo ships the intervals come from the company SMS, the maker's manual, class and the flag. Check your planned maintenance system for the exact routine on your ship.

What is the accumulator on a watertight door for?

It stores hydraulic energy under nitrogen pressure. This lets the door be operated a limited number of times when the electrical supply fails. Low accumulator pressure is a common cause of slow or stalled closing.

Why does the alarm sound before a watertight door closes?

It warns anyone nearby that a remotely ordered closure is about to start or is in progress. A sliding door closes with enough force to kill. Never ignore the alarm or try to beat the door.

Are watertight doors and fire doors the same thing?

No. Watertight doors maintain subdivision against flooding. Fire doors maintain fire integrity between spaces. Some doors are approved for both, but you should not assume that a door does both jobs.

Test yourself

During familiarisation you are asked why a hinged watertight door gasket must never be painted. What is the correct reason?

A painted gasket loses its elasticity and does not seat evenly on the knife edge, so the door leaks. This is general maintenance practice supporting SOLAS Chapter II-1 watertight integrity.

All power has failed and a power-operated sliding watertight door is open. What is the correct way to close it?

Sliding watertight doors have stored energy and manual hydraulic means of closing. Passenger ships also have remote hand operation above the bulkhead deck under SOLAS Chapter II-1.

What does 'weathertight' mean?

Weathertight protects against the weather and seas, not a sustained head of water in intact or damaged conditions. See SOLAS Chapter II-1 and the Load Line Convention.

The bridge panel shows a tunnel door open at sea although no work is planned there. What should the OOW do first?

An open indication must be assumed real until proved otherwise, because subdivision depends on the door being closed. Follow the SMS and the master's standing orders.

Before working on a sliding door's hydraulic system, which precaution is essential besides electrical isolation?

The accumulator stores enough energy to move the door with the power off. It must be depressurised under the permit to work and lock-out procedures, as the ISM Code requires the SMS to provide.
