Radar and ARPA on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

Marine radar transmits short microwave pulses and times their echoes to show the range and bearing of ships, land and buoys. ARPA, called target tracking (TT) on newer sets, automatically tracks selected echoes and calculates their course, speed, CPA and TCPA. To use them well you need to understand the inputs they depend on (gyro, log, GNSS), the routine checks, the typical faults, and the COLREG rules that govern radar use.

What radar and ARPA are for

Radar is the main collision-avoidance and navigation sensor that works in darkness, rain and fog. It gives you a measured picture. Unlike AIS, it does not depend on the other vessel transmitting anything.

ARPA (Automatic Radar Plotting Aid) does the plotting you were taught to do by hand. It tracks echoes and shows their true or relative motion, closest point of approach (CPA) and time to CPA (TCPA). On radars built to the current IMO performance standards, the functions are called target tracking (TT), but most people on board still say ARPA.

You use radar for:

  • Collision avoidance: early detection, systematic observation and assessing risk of collision. COLREG Rules 6, 7 and 19 all refer to radar.
  • Position fixing and monitoring: ranges and bearings of fixed objects, parallel indexing and cross-checking GNSS.
  • Search and rescue: detecting a radar SART, which only responds to a 9 GHz (X-band) radar.
  • Weather awareness: squalls and heavy rain show on the screen as clutter.

Main components and the X-band / S-band difference

Component Function
Transceiver (magnetron or solid-state) Generates the transmitted pulses and receives the returning echoes
Modulator Supplies the high-voltage pulses that fire the magnetron
Duplexer / TR switch Lets one antenna transmit and receive, and protects the receiver from the transmitted pulse
Waveguide or coaxial feed Carries the RF energy between the transceiver and the antenna
Antenna (scanner) and drive motor Forms a narrow horizontal beam and rotates it through 360°
Receiver (mixer, local oscillator, IF amplifier) Amplifies the weak echoes and turns them into video
Processor and display Builds the picture and runs tracking, AIS overlay and alarms
Sensor inputs Gyro heading, log speed through water, GNSS position/COG/SOG, AIS

X-band versus S-band

X-band (about 9 GHz, ~3 cm) S-band (about 3 GHz, ~10 cm)
Bearing and range discrimination Better Poorer
Performance in rain and sea clutter Worse, more attenuated Better
Detects SARTs and most racons Yes SARTs no
Typical use Coastal work, pilotage, small targets Open sea, heavy weather, long-range detection

Many ships with two radars run one of each. Many sets use magnetrons, which wear out with transmit hours. Newer solid-state transceivers transmit at much lower peak power and use pulse compression instead.

How it works, step by step

  1. Transmit: the transceiver sends a very short, powerful pulse along the antenna's beam.
  2. Listen: between pulses the receiver listens. The time taken for an echo to come back gives the range, because radio waves travel at a known, constant speed. The antenna's direction at that moment gives the bearing.
  3. Rotate: the antenna sweeps round and builds up a full picture each revolution.
  4. Process: gain, sea clutter (STC), rain clutter (FTC) and interference rejection clean up the video. Short ranges use short pulses and a higher repetition rate for good discrimination. Long ranges use longer pulses for more energy on target.
  5. Stabilise: gyro heading lets the set show north-up or course-up instead of an unstabilised head-up picture.
  6. Track (ARPA/TT): you acquire a target, manually or by an auto-acquisition zone. The processor follows its echo scan by scan and calculates its course, speed, CPA and TCPA. It shows the result as a vector plus a past-position history.

Sea versus ground stabilisation. ARPA works out a target's true motion from its relative motion plus your own motion.

  • If your own motion comes from the log through the water (sea-stabilised), true vectors show each target's heading through the water. That tells you its aspect, which is what you need to judge an encounter under the Steering and Sailing Rules.
  • If your own motion comes from GNSS over the ground (ground-stabilised), a strong tidal set distorts aspect. A ship stemming the tide can look as if it is crossing.

Ground stabilisation is useful for navigation. For collision assessment, know which mode you are in. Relative vectors and CPA/TCPA are not affected by the stabilisation choice, but they are only as good as the tracking itself.

Worked scenario: using ARPA properly in restricted visibility

You are OOW, visibility 1 nm, 12-mile range, sea-stabilised true vectors. An echo is acquired fine on the starboard bow at 9 nm, and ARPA shows CPA 0.4 nm.

  • Do not act on the first vector. A newly acquired target needs time before its data settles. Watch it over several minutes and check the history dots are steady. COLREG Rule 7 warns against assumptions based on scanty radar information.
  • Cross-check. Is the bearing changing appreciably? Does the AIS data roughly agree? Is the target's aspect consistent with the vector?
  • Decide early. Rule 19 applies, not Rules 11 to 18, because the vessels are not in sight of one another. Avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam (other than when overtaking). A substantial alteration to starboard is the usual answer here, made early and large enough to show clearly on the other ship's radar.
  • Use trial manoeuvre. Test, say, 40° to starboard before you commit. Allow for your own ship's turning time.
  • Monitor. After the alteration your own vectors and the target's tracking take time to settle. Keep checking the CPA until the target is past and clear.

If the target alters at the same time, its vector will lag behind reality. This is exactly when a hand-plotted bearing check catches what ARPA has not yet shown.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch and before use (OOW):

  • Confirm the gyro heading on the radar matches the master gyro and that speed input and mode (log/GNSS, sea/ground) are as intended.
  • Check the heading line. Take a visual bearing of a conspicuous object right ahead and compare it with the radar bearing. Errors here corrupt every bearing on the screen.
  • Check range accuracy against a charted fixed object, and that VRM/EBL readings make sense.
  • Run the performance monitor if fitted, and compare the result with the reference value in the manual or log.
  • Set and record appropriate CPA/TCPA alarm limits and guard zones. Check that AIS overlay and chart overlays are correctly aligned.

Periodic (navigating officers / ETO, per the PMS):

  • Clean the antenna radiating face. Do not paint it.
  • Inspect the waveguide and flange joints for moisture and corrosion, and check the scanner motor and gearbox.
  • Record magnetron running hours and compare with the maker's figures. Keep a spare where the PMS requires it.
  • Check cable glands and earthing, and that the cooling fans and filters in the transceiver and display are clear.
  • Carry out an annual or survey-period test by an approved service engineer where the flag or class requires it. Requirements differ by administration and company SMS.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause
Picture present but echoes weak, short detection range Ageing magnetron, poor tuning, water in waveguide, dirty or iced antenna, wrong gain/clutter settings
No video at all, sweep present Magnetron or modulator failure, receiver failure, set left on standby
Picture smeared or rotating Loss of gyro input, so stabilisation is lost
All bearings out by the same amount Heading line misalignment or wrong gyro error applied
Ranges consistently wrong Range calibration error (needs a service engineer)
ARPA vectors unreliable for all targets Wrong or failed log/GNSS input, wrong stabilisation mode
Tracked target suddenly jumps or swaps Target swap with a nearby echo, lost track in clutter
Spiral or dotted interference lines Another radar on the same band; switch interference rejection on
Sector with no echoes Shadow or blind sector from masts, funnel or cargo

False echoes to recognise:

  • Indirect echoes reflected off your own funnel or masts
  • Multiple echoes from a large close target abeam
  • Side-lobe echoes forming an arc at the same range
  • Second-trace echoes from very distant targets

A known blind-sector diagram should be posted at the radar.

Safety precautions

  • RF radiation: do not stand close to a transmitting antenna or look into an open waveguide. The maker states the hazard distance, and it is usually marked near the scanner.
  • Working aloft: before anyone goes up to the scanner, switch the radar off, isolate it at the breaker and tag it out (and the scanner safety switch where fitted), under a permit. A rotating scanner can cause serious injury.
  • High voltage: the modulator and power supplies carry lethal voltages that can remain after switch-off. Only competent persons open the transceiver, following the manual.
  • Hazardous components: follow maker warning labels. Some older microwave components contain materials that must not be broken or handled carelessly.
  • Over-reliance: radar does not replace a visual and aural look-out (Rule 5). ARPA data is no better than its inputs. Small craft, wooden boats and ice can be missed in clutter.

Carriage rules and oral exam questions

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 sets the carriage requirements in tiers by gross tonnage. At some tiers an item is added, and at others an item is replaced by a more capable one:

  • 300 GT and upwards, and all passenger ships: a 9 GHz radar and an electronic plotting aid.
  • 500 GT and upwards: an automatic tracking aid (replacing the electronic plotting aid).
  • 3,000 GT and upwards: a second radar (3 GHz, or where appropriate a second 9 GHz set) and a second automatic tracking aid.
  • 10,000 GT and upwards: an automatic radar plotting aid (in place of the second automatic tracking aid).

Check the exact requirement for your ship against the regulation and your flag's interpretation. Equipment must meet IMO performance standards. For radars installed on or after 1 July 2008 these are Resolution MSC.192(79). STCW requires radar/ARPA competence for deck officers, and course structure varies by administration.

Typical oral questions:

  • Why use sea stabilisation for collision avoidance? Because it shows the target's heading through the water, so you judge aspect correctly.
  • How do you check heading line accuracy? Compare the visual bearing of a conspicuous object nearly right ahead with its radar bearing.
  • What does a SART look like on radar? A line of 12 dots radiating outwards from the SART's position along its bearing. As you close it, the dots become arcs and then concentric circles. It shows on X-band only.
  • Why might ARPA give a wrong CPA immediately after a target alters? Tracking smooths data over time, so vectors lag a manoeuvre.
  • What does Rule 7 say about radar? Use it properly, including long-range scanning and radar plotting, and do not assume from scanty information.
  • What happens to your picture if the gyro fails? Stabilisation is lost and an alarm is raised. The display typically reverts to head-up, or must be switched to it, and true vectors and ARPA data become unreliable.
Key points
  • Radar measures range by echo timing and bearing by antenna direction; ARPA/TT automatically tracks targets and calculates CPA and TCPA.
  • X-band (9 GHz) gives better discrimination and detects SARTs; S-band (3 GHz) performs better in rain and sea clutter.
  • Use sea-stabilised true vectors to judge a target's aspect; ground stabilisation can mislead in strong tidal streams.
  • ARPA output depends entirely on gyro, log and GNSS inputs, so verify them every watch.
  • Check the heading line, range accuracy and performance monitor routinely, and know the blind sectors.
  • Isolate and tag out the radar before working at the scanner, and respect the RF hazard distance.
  • SOLAS V/19 carriage requirements rise in tiers by gross tonnage; check your flag's interpretation.

Frequently asked questions

Is ARPA the same as target tracking (TT)?

Essentially yes. Radars built to the newer IMO performance standards describe automatic tracking as target tracking (TT), while SOLAS carriage wording and older equipment use the term ARPA. The principle is the same: the set tracks echoes and calculates course, speed, CPA and TCPA.

Should I use true or relative vectors?

Both have a place. Relative vectors show at a glance whether a target is heading towards you. True vectors (sea-stabilised) show its aspect and actual movement. Good practice is to switch between them and always check the CPA/TCPA readout.

Can AIS replace ARPA for collision avoidance?

No. AIS only shows vessels that transmit, and their data may be wrong or delayed. Radar detects any reflecting object. Use AIS as a supplement and cross-check it against radar and visual observation.

Why does a SART show only on one of our two radars?

A radar SART responds only to 9 GHz (X-band) radar pulses. It will not show on an S-band set. During a search, use the X-band radar with suitable range and settings.

How often must the radar be serviced by a technician?

It depends on the flag state, class, company SMS and the maker's recommendations. Some administrations require periodic testing by approved service engineers in connection with safety equipment surveys. Check your ship's PMS and flag requirements rather than assuming a fixed interval.

Test yourself

In a strong tidal stream, which stabilisation gives the most reliable indication of another vessel's aspect for applying the COLREGs?

Sea stabilisation uses speed through the water, so true vectors show the target's heading through the water and hence its aspect. Ground stabilisation includes the set and can distort apparent aspect.

Which radar band will display a radar SART?

A radar SART responds to 9 GHz (X-band) radar. AIS-SARTs are a separate device, and S-band radar will not trigger a radar SART.

Every radar bearing is out by the same 3°, while ranges are correct. What is the most likely cause?

A constant bearing error on all targets points to heading line alignment or the heading input. Magnetron ageing reduces detection range, not bearing accuracy.

In restricted visibility, a target detected by radar alone is forward of the beam and a close-quarters situation is developing (not overtaking). Which action does COLREG Rule 19 say should be avoided, so far as possible?

Rule 19(d) says to avoid, so far as possible, altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam other than one being overtaken. Rule 19 applies because the vessels are not in sight of one another.
