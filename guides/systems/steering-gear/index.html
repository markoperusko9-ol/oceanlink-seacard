Steering Gear on Merchant Ships: How It Works, Checks and Faults

Quick answer

Steering gear turns the rudder on command from the bridge. On most merchant ships it is electro-hydraulic: an electric motor drives a hydraulic pump, the pump drives rams or a rotary vane, and a feedback unit stops the rudder at the ordered angle. SOLAS sets performance limits, requires main and auxiliary steering gear (or a main gear with two or more identical power units), alarms, a pre-departure test, and an emergency steering drill at least every three months.

What the steering gear does and why SOLAS treats it seriously

The steering gear converts a helm order (wheel, tiller, autopilot or NFU lever) into rudder movement with enough torque to hold the rudder against water pressure at full speed. Lose it in a narrow channel or a traffic separation scheme and you have a collision or grounding within minutes. For this reason it is one of the most heavily regulated machinery items on board.

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 29 sets the design and performance requirements. Regulation 30 adds requirements for electric and electro-hydraulic gear. Chapter V, Regulation 26 covers testing and drills. In outline:

  • Main steering gear must move the rudder from 35° on one side to 35° on the other at deepest seagoing draught and maximum ahead service speed. Under the same conditions it must move the rudder from 35° on either side to 30° on the other side in not more than 28 seconds.
  • Auxiliary steering gear must move the rudder from 15° on one side to 15° on the other in not more than 60 seconds. The test condition is deepest seagoing draught at half maximum ahead service speed or 7 knots, whichever is greater.
  • Main and auxiliary gear must be arranged so that failure of one does not disable the other. The regulation allows exceptions where the main gear has two or more identical power units and meets the single-failure provisions.
  • Tankers, chemical tankers and gas carriers of 10,000 GT and upwards have stricter single-failure rules. Steering capability must be regained within 45 seconds after a single failure in the piping system or a power unit.

The exact application depends on the ship's type, size and build date. Check your ship's class documentation rather than assuming the newest rule applies.

Main components

A typical electro-hydraulic installation has these parts:

Component Function
Control system (bridge) Follow-up wheel, non-follow-up (NFU) tiller or buttons, and autopilot. Sends the ordered angle to the steering gear.
Control amplifier / control unit Compares ordered angle with actual angle and commands the pump or valves.
Power units (usually two) Electric motor plus hydraulic pump, each with its own starter.
Pump type Variable delivery (radial or axial piston, stroke varied by a control lever) or fixed delivery with solenoid-operated directional valves.
Actuator Ram type (two-ram or four-ram, acting on the tiller through a Rapson slide) or rotary vane (vanes on a rotor keyed to the rudder stock).
Feedback unit Transmitter on the rudder stock or tiller. Reports actual angle to the control unit and the rudder angle indicators. On older gear this is the mechanical hunting gear.
Valve block Isolating valves, bypass valves, and relief (shock) valves that protect against wave impact on the rudder.
Hydraulic tanks Service tank with low-level alarm. SOLAS requires a fixed storage tank able to recharge at least one power actuating system.
Rudder carrier Takes the weight of the rudder and stock.
Rudder stops Limit switches cut the order at maximum helm. Mechanical stops sit slightly beyond them.
Local control and communications Trick wheel or manual solenoid buttons, sound-powered telephone or equivalent, gyro repeater, rudder angle indicator.

How it works, step by step

Take a fixed-delivery pump with solenoid valves and a four-ram gear. The helmsman orders 10° starboard.

  1. The follow-up wheel sends a 10° starboard signal to the control unit in the steering gear room.
  2. The control unit compares this with the feedback signal, which currently reads midships. It finds an error and energises the solenoid for starboard.
  3. The directional valve shifts. Oil from the running pump goes to one pair of diagonally opposite cylinders, and the other pair returns oil to the tank.
  4. The rams push the tiller through the Rapson slide and the rudder stock turns.
  5. The feedback unit reports the angle increasing. At 10° the error is zero, the solenoid de-energises, the valve centres, and the oil is locked in the cylinders. The rudder holds at 10°.
  6. The rudder angle indicators on the bridge and in the steering room show 10°.

With a variable-delivery pump, the pump runs constantly in one direction. The control system strokes the pump off-centre to deliver oil one way or the other. Feedback brings the stroke back to neutral when actual angle matches ordered angle.

A useful point: on a ram gear with a Rapson slide, the effective lever arm grows as the rudder angle increases. Torque therefore rises with helm angle, which is where it is needed most.

Routine checks, pre-departure test and maintenance

Pre-departure test (SOLAS V/26). Within 12 hours before departure, the steering gear must be checked and tested by the ship's crew. The test covers, as applicable:

  • main and auxiliary steering gear
  • remote control systems and each bridge steering position
  • the emergency power supply
  • rudder angle indicators against the actual rudder position
  • alarms for remote control system power failure and for power unit failure
  • automatic isolating arrangements and other automatic equipment

The checks also include:

  • full movement of the rudder according to the required capabilities
  • a visual inspection of the steering gear and its linkage
  • the communication system between bridge and steering gear compartment

Ships on short regular voyages may have different test frequency arrangements approved by their Administration. Follow what your flag and SMS state.

Watchkeeping rounds typically cover:

  • oil level in the service tank
  • leaks at ram glands, pipe joints and the valve block
  • unusual noise or vibration
  • motor temperature
  • rudder carrier condition and lubrication
  • compartment clean, dry and clear of stores
  • block diagram and changeover instructions posted and legible

Planned maintenance follows the maker's manual and your company PMS. Common tasks include:

  • hydraulic oil analysis and filter changes
  • greasing the Rapson slide, crosshead and rudder carrier
  • checking rudder wear-down, jumping clearance and bearing clearances
  • testing alarms
  • checking relief valve settings
  • inspecting flexible hoses

Do not invent intervals. Use the PMS.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Noisy, jerky or spongy rudder movement Air in the hydraulic system (low oil level, leaking suction side, poor venting after maintenance)
Rudder hunting (oscillating about the ordered angle) Feedback unit loose or faulty, control amplifier deadband or gain wrongly set, air in system
Rudder slowly drifts off ordered angle Internal leakage past ram seals or vanes, passing relief or bypass valve, worn directional valve
Rudder slow, fails timing test Worn pump, wrong pump running alone, bypass valve partly open, low oil level
Rudder moves one way only Solenoid coil failure, stuck spool, control card fault
No response from bridge but local control works Control system power failure, wiring or signal fault, wrong selector position
Overheating oil Prolonged heavy steering in weather, passing relief valve, cooler fault
Indicator disagrees with actual rudder Feedback or indicator transmitter misaligned

Electrical protection is specific to steering gear. SOLAS requires power unit circuits to have short-circuit protection with an overload alarm, rather than an overload trip that would stop the motor. Expect alarms for motor overload, phase failure and low hydraulic level on the bridge and in the machinery control position. Power units must restart automatically when power is restored after a failure.

Emergency steering and safety precautions

Emergency steering drills must be held at least once every three months (SOLAS V/26). The drill includes:

  • direct control from within the steering gear compartment
  • the communication procedure with the bridge
  • operation of alternative power supplies, where applicable

The date and details go in the log book. Simple operating instructions and a block diagram of the changeover procedure must be permanently displayed on the bridge and in the steering gear compartment.

Typical changeover to local control (follow your own ship's posted procedure):

  1. The bridge orders emergency steering and the engine room is informed.
  2. The officer goes to the steering gear room and establishes communication by sound-powered phone or the designated system.
  3. Switch the control selector to local, or isolate the remote control as per the diagram.
  4. Steer by the trick wheel or by pressing the manual solenoid buttons, watching the rudder angle indicator and gyro repeater.
  5. Repeat each helm order back and report when it is applied.

Working on the gear:

  • Use a permit to work and lock-out tag-out on both power units and the control supply.
  • Inform the bridge before stopping any pump, even alongside.
  • The rudder can be moved by sea, swell or propeller wash. Close the isolating valves or use the locking arrangement before working near the tiller or rams.
  • Never stand in the swept path of the tiller.
  • After opening the hydraulic circuit, vent air thoroughly, top up the oil, and run full helm both ways before declaring the gear ready.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners like steering gear because it tests regulation, hydraulics and seamanship together. Practise answering these aloud:

  • State the SOLAS performance requirements for main and auxiliary steering gear. Give 35°–30° in 28 seconds for main gear, and 15°–15° in 60 seconds for auxiliary gear, with their speed and draught conditions.
  • What tests are done before departure, and within what time? Within 12 hours. List the items from SOLAS V/26.
  • How often are emergency steering drills held and what do they include? At least every three months: local control, communications, alternative power.
  • Why are mechanical rudder stops set beyond the limit switches? The switches stop the order in normal use. The mechanical stops are the last physical protection if the switches fail.
  • Explain the purpose of the relief (shock) valves. They protect the system from pressure surges when a sea strikes the rudder.
  • What does air in the system do, and how do you remove it? It causes noisy, jerky, spongy steering. Find and stop the source, top up, then vent at the bleed points while running the gear.
  • Your rudder hunts at sea. What do you check? Feedback linkage, control settings, oil level and air, then autopilot settings.
  • Why overload alarm and not trip? Losing steering is more dangerous than overheating a motor.
Key points
  • Most merchant ships use electro-hydraulic steering gear with ram or rotary vane actuators and two power units.
  • Main gear must go from 35° one side to 30° the other in not more than 28 seconds at full speed and deepest draught.
  • Auxiliary gear must go from 15° to 15° in not more than 60 seconds at half speed or 7 knots, whichever is greater.
  • Steering gear must be tested within 12 hours before departure, including alarms, indicators and communications.
  • Emergency steering drills are required at least once every three months and must be logged.
  • Air in the system, feedback faults and internal leakage cause most jerky, hunting or drifting rudder complaints.
  • Isolate both pumps and secure the rudder before working in the tiller area; the sea can move it.

Frequently asked questions

How often must emergency steering drills be carried out?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26 requires emergency steering drills at least once every three months. They must include direct control from the steering gear compartment, the communication procedure with the bridge and, where applicable, operation of alternative power supplies. Record the drill in the log book.

What is the difference between follow-up and non-follow-up steering?

In follow-up mode, you set a rudder angle on the wheel and the gear moves the rudder to that angle and stops, using feedback from the rudder stock. In non-follow-up mode, the rudder moves only while you hold the tiller or button, and stops where it is when you release. NFU is commonly used as a backup if the follow-up system fails.

Why are steering gear motors fitted with an overload alarm instead of an overload trip?

SOLAS requires short-circuit protection with an overload alarm, so the motor keeps running when overloaded. Losing steering at a critical moment is more dangerous than overheating a motor. The alarm warns the watchkeepers so they can take action.

What are the signs of air in the steering gear hydraulic system?

Typical signs are noisy pumps, jerky or spongy rudder movement, hunting, and slower response. Air usually enters through a low oil level, a leaking suction joint or poor venting after maintenance. Fix the source, top up and vent at the bleed points while running the gear.

Who carries out the pre-departure steering gear test?

SOLAS requires the test to be carried out by the ship's crew within 12 hours before departure. In practice the duty engineer usually works in the steering gear room and the duty deck officer on the bridge, as set out in the company SMS. The results are recorded in the log book.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS, what must the main steering gear achieve at deepest seagoing draught and maximum ahead service speed?

The main gear limit is 35° to 30° within 28 seconds. The 15°–15° in 60 seconds figure applies to auxiliary gear (SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 29).

How often must emergency steering drills be held?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26 requires emergency steering drills at least once every three months.

The rudder moves noisily and jerkily and feels spongy. What is the most likely cause?

Entrained air compresses, giving spongy, jerky and noisy operation. It is usually due to low oil level, suction leaks or poor venting.

Within what period before departure must the steering gear be checked and tested?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26 requires the test within 12 hours before departure.

Why are the mechanical rudder stops set slightly beyond the electrical limit switches?

The limit switches stop the helm order before the rudder reaches the mechanical stops. The stops are the last physical protection for the rudder and actuator (classification society rules; see the maker's manual).
