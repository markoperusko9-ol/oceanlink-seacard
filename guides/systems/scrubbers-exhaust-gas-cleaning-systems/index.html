Scrubbers (Exhaust Gas Cleaning Systems): A Practical Guide for Ship's Crew

Quick answer

A scrubber, or exhaust gas cleaning system (EGCS), washes engine and boiler exhaust with seawater or an alkaline solution. This removes sulphur oxides so the ship can burn high-sulphur fuel and still meet the MARPOL Annex VI sulphur limits. To keep the ship compliant, crew must keep it running within its approved parameters, keep the washwater and emission monitoring working, keep the records, and report failures as the ship's procedures require.

What a scrubber does and why ships fit one

MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 14 limits the sulphur content of fuel oil. The global limit has been 0.50% m/m since 1 January 2020, and the limit inside Emission Control Areas is 0.10% m/m. Regulation 4 lets the flag Administration accept an alternative that is at least as effective. An approved exhaust gas cleaning system is the most common example.

The idea is simple. Burn cheaper high-sulphur fuel oil (HSFO), then wash the SOx out of the exhaust before it leaves the funnel. The result in the exhaust must be equivalent to burning compliant fuel.

Two practical points every watchkeeper should understand:

  • The scrubber is your compliance. If it stops working while you are burning HSFO, the ship is out of compliance until you either restore it or change over to compliant fuel.
  • The carriage ban. Since 1 March 2020, a ship may not carry non-compliant fuel for combustion unless it has an approved equivalent fitted. Without a working EGCS arrangement, HSFO on board becomes a problem in itself.

A scrubber may serve the main engine only. It may also serve auxiliary engines and boilers, either as separate units or as one multi-inlet tower.

Open loop, closed loop and hybrid

Type Scrubbing medium Washwater fate Notes
Open loop Seawater, used once Monitored (and treated or diluted where the design provides), then discharged overboard Relies on the natural alkalinity of seawater. Works less well in brackish or fresh water.
Closed loop Fresh water dosed with caustic soda (NaOH), recirculated A small bleed-off is treated. It is then discharged or held on board, and sludge is separated out Can run in low-alkalinity water. It needs caustic stowage and handling.
Hybrid Either mode As above, depending on mode Lets the ship switch to closed loop where open-loop discharge is restricted

Many ports and coastal states restrict or ban open-loop washwater discharge in their waters. These rules are local and they change. Always check the latest local requirements and agent information before arrival. Where discharge is not allowed, you will need to run in closed loop or zero-discharge mode, or change over to compliant fuel.

Main components

Layouts differ by maker, but most systems have:

  • Scrubber tower (reactor). Usually built in the funnel casing, either in-line or U-type. It contains spray nozzles or packed sections, and a quench zone at the inlet that cools the hot exhaust.
  • Demister / droplet separator. Stops washwater being carried up the funnel.
  • Bypass damper (where fitted). Lets exhaust go round the tower. Its position must be recorded.
  • Washwater (scrubbing) pumps and seawater supply. Often large, often frequency-controlled, often with standby units.
  • Closed-loop extras. A process or circulation tank, NaOH storage and dosing pumps, a coolant heat exchanger, a bleed-off holding tank, a washwater treatment unit (hydrocyclones, separator or similar) and a sludge tank.
  • Washwater monitoring. Continuous sensors for pH, PAH (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), turbidity and temperature, at inlet and discharge.
  • Emission monitoring (CEMS). A sample probe, heated sample line, gas conditioning and an analyser measuring SO2 and CO2. Calibration gas bottles are kept for checks.
  • Control system and data logger. Records the parameters needed for compliance.
  • Overboard discharge line. Washwater can be acidic and aggressive to steel, so this is a known corrosion point.

Wetted parts are usually made of special alloys or GRP for the same reason.

How it works, step by step

Chemistry in one line: SO2 dissolves in the washwater and is neutralised by alkalinity. In open loop this is the natural bicarbonate in seawater. In closed loop it is caustic soda. The product ends up mainly as sulphates in solution.

Typical sequence (always follow your maker's manual and the company SMS):

  1. Before start. Check the overboard and suction valves are lined up. Check the tanks: caustic and bleed-off levels in closed loop, and sludge tank space. Confirm the monitoring sensors and CEMS are healthy and the alarms are clear.
  2. Start water first. Start the washwater pumps and establish flow through the quench and spray sections before any hot gas goes into the tower. Running the tower dry with hot exhaust can damage internals and GRP.
  3. Admit exhaust. Close the bypass, or open the inlet damper, according to the design. The tower now carries the exhaust.
  4. Control loop takes over. Water flow, and caustic dosing in closed loop, follow engine load and the measured SO2/CO2 ratio. The ratio method is used because CO2 tracks fuel burnt. A ratio of 4.3 ppm/%v corresponds to 0.10% sulphur fuel, and 21.7 corresponds to 0.50%.
  5. Washwater out. Washwater is monitored and treated as required, then discharged. In closed loop, the bleed-off is cleaned and sludge goes to the sludge tank. EGCS residues must be landed ashore, not discharged at sea or incinerated.
  6. Changeover or stop. Before entering an area where the scrubber cannot run, or before stopping it, change over to compliant fuel in good time. Follow the fuel changeover procedure and record times and positions as required. Only then shut the system down.

Worked scenario. You are 6 hours from a port that bans open-loop discharge. Your hybrid system is running open loop on HSFO. Your options are to switch to closed loop before the limit, if you have caustic and holding capacity, or to change over to compliant fuel. Check bleed-off tank space against the expected port stay. Log the mode change with time and position.

Routine checks and maintenance

Maintenance is planned under the ship's planned maintenance system. That system is the ISM Code requirement in practice. Typical items include:

  • Each watch / daily
    • Washwater pump pressures and flows.
    • Differential pressure across the tower and demister.
    • Exhaust back pressure.
    • pH, PAH and turbidity readings, including whether the readings are plausible.
    • SO2/CO2 ratio trend.
    • Caustic and bleed-off tank levels.
    • Leaks around the tower, discharge pipe and caustic lines.
  • CEMS
    • Calibration and zero/span checks as the manual requires.
    • Calibration gas stock and expiry.
    • Sample line heating and filters.
    • Condensate drains.
  • Washwater sensors
    • Cleaning and calibration. PAH and turbidity probes foul quickly in harbour water.
  • Tower internals, during engine stops and only under enclosed space entry procedures
    • Nozzle condition and blockages.
    • Demister fouling.
    • Corrosion and soot deposits.
    • Damper condition and seal tightness.
  • Overboard discharge pipe and shell connection
    • Inspect for thinning and corrosion. Failures here have caused water ingress on some ships.
  • Records
    • Keep the EGC record book or electronic log complete: operating modes, bypass positions, maintenance and calibration.

The key documents usually include:

  • Approval documents. The EGC Technical Manual and, under Scheme A, the SOx Emission Compliance Certificate.
  • Onboard Monitoring Manual.
  • SOx Emission Compliance Plan, which sets out how the ship shows compliance.

The exact document set depends on the approval scheme and the flag.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms First actions
Washwater pump trip or low flow Low flow or pressure alarm, rising tower outlet temperature, SO2/CO2 ratio climbs Start the standby pump. Check the suction strainer and sea chest. Follow the alarm response in the SMS.
Nozzle blockage Uneven spray, poor SOx removal at normal flow, hot spots Check strainers. Inspect nozzles at the next stop.
Demister fouling High differential pressure, water carry-over, wet deposits on deck Wash if a wash facility is fitted. Inspect at stop.
High exhaust back pressure Higher exhaust temperatures, turbocharger behaviour changes, possible load limits Check dampers, tower dP and soot build-up. Inform the Chief Engineer.
CEMS fault or drift Erratic or frozen readings, calibration failure, sample line alarm Check heating, filters and condensate. Recalibrate. Treat it as a monitoring failure and act on it.
Low washwater pH at discharge pH alarm Check water flow against load and caustic dosing. In open loop, suspect low-alkalinity water.
PAH or turbidity alarm High readings Clean or check the sensor first, then check the washwater treatment unit and separator.
Caustic dosing failure (closed loop) pH falls, SO2/CO2 ratio rises Check the dosing pump, tank level and line freezing or crystallisation.
Bleed-off or sludge tank high High level alarm Plan discharge where it is allowed, or landing ashore. Limit closed-loop operation if necessary.

A white plume above the funnel is normally water vapour and is not, on its own, a fault.

If the EGCS or one of its monitors fails, follow the SOx Emission Compliance Plan and IMO guidance. That means recording the event, attempting repair, reporting to the flag and relevant port State as the guidance and your company require, and changing over to compliant fuel if compliance cannot be restored. Reporting time limits and details are set in the guidance and your plan. Know where they are written before you need them.

Safety precautions

  • Caustic soda (NaOH) is highly corrosive to skin and eyes.
    • Wear full chemical PPE: face shield, goggles, gloves and apron or suit.
    • Keep eyewash and showers working.
    • Have the safety data sheet at the bunkering or transfer point.
    • Never mix NaOH with acids, and watch for heat when diluting.
  • Tower entry is enclosed space entry.
    • Use a permit, isolation, ventilation and atmosphere testing for oxygen and toxic gases, including residual SO2.
    • Keep a standby person and have a rescue plan ready.
    • Confirm that exhaust from other connected engines or boilers cannot reach the tower.
  • Isolation and lock-out
    • Apply to washwater pumps, dosing pumps, dampers and the engine concerned before any work inside.
  • Hot surfaces and hot gas.
    • Expect hot exhaust ducting. Never open inspection doors while the system is live.
  • Acidic washwater.
    • Leaking discharge lines and drains can burn skin and corrode structure. Report leaks immediately.
  • Hull integrity.
    • Treat any leak at the overboard discharge or shell connection seriously. It is a potential flooding route.
  • Hot work near GRP piping or the tower needs a hot work permit and protection of non-metallic parts.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why is the SO2/CO2 ratio used rather than SO2 alone? A: CO2 is proportional to the fuel burnt. The ratio removes the effects of dilution and engine load, and it relates directly to an equivalent fuel sulphur content.

Q: Why must washwater flow be established before exhaust enters the tower? A: To protect the internals and any non-metallic parts from hot dry gas. The quench section needs water to cool the exhaust.

Q: Why might an open-loop scrubber perform poorly in a river or the Baltic? A: Open loop relies on seawater alkalinity to neutralise SO2. Brackish or fresh water has much less, so pH falls and removal drops.

Q: Your CEMS fails mid-passage while burning HSFO. What do you do? A: Follow the SOx Emission Compliance Plan. Use the backup evidence it allows, attempt repair, record everything and report as required. Be ready to change over to compliant fuel if compliance cannot be shown or restored.

Q: What happens to the sludge from a closed-loop system? A: It is held on board and landed to a port reception facility. It is not discharged at sea or incinerated.

Q: What records would a PSC inspector expect? A: Approval documents and the technical manual, the monitoring manual, the compliance plan, continuous monitoring data, the record book or log of operation, maintenance and calibration, bypass and changeover records, and fuel documentation.

Key points
  • A scrubber is an approved equivalent under MARPOL Annex VI Reg 4 that lets the ship burn high-sulphur fuel while meeting Reg 14 limits.
  • Open loop uses seawater alkalinity, closed loop uses caustic soda, and hybrid can do both.
  • Compliance is shown by the SO2/CO2 ratio: 4.3 equates to 0.10% sulphur and 21.7 to 0.50%.
  • Washwater pH, PAH and turbidity are monitored continuously, and residues must be landed ashore.
  • Many ports restrict open-loop discharge, so check local rules before arrival.
  • If the scrubber fails, follow the SOx Emission Compliance Plan: record, repair, report, and change to compliant fuel if needed.
  • Treat caustic soda handling and scrubber tower entry as high-risk jobs with full PPE and permits.

Frequently asked questions

Can a ship with a scrubber burn high-sulphur fuel everywhere?

No. The scrubber must be working and approved, and local rules may ban open-loop washwater discharge in some ports and coastal waters. Where the scrubber cannot be used, the ship must burn compliant fuel. Always check current local requirements before arrival.

What is the difference between open-loop and closed-loop scrubbers?

Open loop uses seawater once and discharges it overboard after monitoring, relying on the natural alkalinity of seawater. Closed loop recirculates fresh water dosed with caustic soda and discharges only a small treated bleed-off, or holds it on board. Hybrids can switch between the two.

What should the engineer do if the scrubber trips while burning HSFO?

Follow the alarm response and the SOx Emission Compliance Plan. Try to restore the system, record the times and details, and report to the flag and port State as required. If compliance cannot be restored, change over to compliant fuel.

Is a white plume from the funnel a sign the scrubber is faulty?

Not normally. Scrubbed exhaust is saturated with water vapour, which condenses into a white plume. Watch instead for droplets falling on deck, which can indicate demister problems or carry-over.

Who is responsible for the scrubber on board?

The engine department normally operates and maintains it under the Chief Engineer, as set out in the company SMS. The bridge often logs positions and times for mode or fuel changeovers. Exact responsibilities vary by company.

Test yourself

Which SO2 (ppm) / CO2 (%v) ratio corresponds to 0.10% sulphur fuel?

4.3 equates to 0.10% sulphur and 21.7 to 0.50%. Reference: IMO 2021 Guidelines for Exhaust Gas Cleaning Systems (MEPC.340(77)).

Why does an open-loop scrubber lose efficiency in brackish water?

Open loop relies on the bicarbonate alkalinity of seawater to neutralise SO2, and brackish water has much less of it. Reference: EGCS principles under MEPC.340(77).

How must sludge from a closed-loop scrubber be disposed of?

EGCS residues are delivered ashore and must not be discharged at sea or incinerated. Reference: MARPOL Annex VI and MEPC.340(77).

During start-up, what should be established before exhaust is admitted to the tower?

Hot dry exhaust can damage the tower internals and non-metallic parts, so water flow comes first. Reference: the EGCS maker's operating manual.

Since 1 March 2020, which ships may carry non-compliant fuel for combustion?

The carriage ban allows non-compliant fuel for combustion only where an approved equivalent is fitted. Reference: MARPOL Annex VI Reg 14 as amended by MEPC.305(73).
