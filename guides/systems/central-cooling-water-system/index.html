Central Cooling Water System on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

In a central cooling system, sea water normally passes only through the central cooler(s). Every other heat exchanger in the engine room is cooled by treated fresh water in a low-temperature (LT) circuit, which also takes heat from a separate high-temperature (HT) jacket water circuit. The design cuts sea water corrosion and fouling to a few components. The engineer's job is to keep temperatures, pressures, water chemistry and expansion tank levels within the maker's limits.

What a central cooling system is and why ships use it

Older ships ran sea water through every cooler in the engine room. These included lube oil coolers, air coolers, jacket water coolers and auxiliary engine coolers. Sea water is corrosive and brings marine growth, silt and shells, so every cooler and every metre of sea water pipe was a maintenance liability.

A central cooling system limits sea water to a short circuit:

  • sea chest
  • strainers
  • sea water pump
  • central cooler(s)
  • overboard

Everything else is cooled by treated fresh water. The advantages are:

  • Fewer components in contact with sea water, so less corrosion, erosion and fouling.
  • Cheaper materials can be used in the fresh water circuits.
  • Coolers stay cleaner for longer, so heat transfer is more stable.
  • Temperatures are easier to control.
  • The HT circuit's heat can be recovered, usually in the fresh water generator.

The trade-off is an extra stage of heat exchange. You also have a closed water system that needs chemical treatment and testing. Neglect it and you get corrosion inside the engine itself.

Main components

Layouts vary by builder and engine maker, so always check your ship's piping diagrams. A typical arrangement has three circuits.

Sea water (SW) circuit

  • High and low sea chests, each with a suction valve and strainer.
  • Sea water pumps, normally at least one running and one on standby. Some ships have variable-speed drives.
  • Central coolers, usually titanium plate-type heat exchangers, often two in parallel.
  • Overboard valve.
  • A marine growth prevention system (MGPS), either copper/aluminium anodes or electro-chlorination.

Low-temperature (LT) fresh water circuit

  • LT pumps (duty and standby).
  • A three-way temperature control valve (TCV) that mixes water from the central cooler with water bypassing it.
  • The consumers, typically:
    • main engine scavenge air cooler
    • main engine lube oil cooler
    • auxiliary engines
    • air compressors
    • stern tube oil cooler
    • air conditioning and provision plant condensers
    • the HT (jacket water) cooler

High-temperature (HT) jacket water circuit

  • HT pumps.
  • Main engine jackets, liners, cylinder covers and exhaust valve cages.
  • A deaerator or air separator.
  • An HT TCV and the jacket water cooler (cooled by LT water).
  • A jacket water preheater.
  • The fresh water generator, which uses HT heat.

Common items

  • Expansion tank(s): these absorb volume changes, keep a static head on pump suctions, vent air, and give you a quick visual check.
  • Chemical dosing point, sampling cocks, thermometers, pressure gauges and alarms.

How it works, step by step

  1. Sea water suction. At sea the low sea chest is normally used. It stays submerged when the ship rolls and pitches, so air is not drawn in. In shallow water, rivers and canals the high sea chest is selected, which reduces the intake of sand and silt.
  2. Strainers and pumps. Sea water passes the strainer. The pump delivers it through the central cooler(s) and then overboard. On many ships an overboard or recirculation valve keeps the sea water side from over-cooling in cold seas.
  3. Central cooler. Heat passes through the plates from the LT fresh water to the sea water. The LT side is normally kept at a higher pressure than the sea water side. If a plate leaks, fresh water then escapes outward rather than sea water getting into the engine system.
  4. LT temperature control. The LT TCV senses the outlet temperature. It sends just enough water through the central cooler and bypasses the rest, holding the maker's set point. That set point is commonly in the mid-30s °C, but use your engine maker's figure.
  5. LT consumers. LT water flows in parallel or series through the consumers, including the HT cooler, picks up heat and returns to the LT pump suction.
  6. HT circuit. The HT pumps circulate jacket water through the main engine. The HT TCV keeps the engine outlet temperature within the maker's range. For slow-speed two-strokes this is typically around 80 to 90 °C. Surplus heat goes to the fresh water generator first, then to the HT cooler.
  7. Air and expansion. Air is separated in the deaerator and vented to the expansion tank. The expansion tank level shows whether the system is losing or gaining water.
  8. In port or on standby. The preheater keeps the HT water warm so the engine can be started without thermal shock. This also limits cold corrosion and liner stress.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch or rounds

  • Check LT and HT temperatures and pressures at pump discharge and at the main consumers.
  • Note the temperature difference across the central cooler on both the SW and FW sides. Look at trends, not single readings.
  • Check expansion tank levels and the colour of the water. Look for an oil sheen.
  • Look at strainer differential pressure, where fitted, and the sea water pump discharge pressure.
  • Listen for cavitation noise at the pumps. Check the seals and the pump and motor temperatures.
  • Check the TCV position indicator against the load.

Water treatment (per supplier and company SMS)

  • Test the HT and LT fresh water at the frequency in your SMS. The usual parameters are inhibitor concentration (commonly nitrite-based), pH and chlorides.
  • Rising chlorides point to sea water getting in. Falling inhibitor with frequent top-ups points to a leak.
  • Record results and dosing. Inspectors and superintendents look for trends.

Planned maintenance

  • Clean the sea water strainers. Change over to the standby strainer first, and confirm the valves are shut and holding before opening.
  • Open, inspect, clean and pressure-test the central cooler plates. Renew gaskets as required.
  • Inspect the sea chest gratings, zinc anodes and MGPS. The underwater parts are often only reachable in dry dock.
  • Overhaul the pumps (seals, bearings, impeller clearance).
  • Function-test the TCVs, the low-pressure and high-temperature alarms, and the standby pump auto-start.

Intervals come from the maker and your planned maintenance system, not from a fixed rule.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes First actions
LT temperature rising, TCV fully open to cooler Fouled central cooler, choked SW strainer, worn SW pump, high sea temperature Change over strainer, start standby SW pump, check ΔT across cooler, plan cooler cleaning
Chlorides rising in LT water Leaking central cooler plate or gasket, other SW-cooled exchanger, contaminated make-up water (check FWG salinity / top-up source) Check make-up water source, isolate coolers one at a time, pressure test, check FW/SW pressure relationship
Oil sheen in LT expansion tank Leaking lube oil cooler or stern tube oil cooler Isolate suspect cooler, check sump levels, pressure test
Bubbles in HT expansion tank, fluctuating HT pressure, HT level rising Exhaust gas leakage from a cracked cylinder cover or liner, or a cracked exhaust valve seat/cage or failed combustion-side sealing face Inform chief engineer, identify the unit, reduce load, investigate per maker's procedure
HT expansion tank level falling External leak, liner O-ring leak into crankcase or scavenge space, FWG leak Inspect scavenge spaces and crankcase, check FWG
Low LT/HT pump pressure, noisy pump Low expansion tank level, air lock, cavitation, worn impeller Top up, vent, check suction valves, change over pump
Temperature hunting TCV sticking or poorly tuned, sensor fault Switch TCV to manual and hold temperature, then investigate

The rule of thumb is to trend everything. A slow climb in LT temperature over weeks usually means fouling. A sudden jump means something has failed or a valve is wrong.

Safety precautions and the regulatory picture

Safety

  • Sea water side work. Before opening a strainer, cooler or pump on the sea side:
    • shut and lock the sea chest and overboard valves according to your lock-out tag-out procedure;
    • drain down and confirm there is no ingress. A passing sea valve can flood the engine room. Know where the emergency bilge suction is.
  • Hot water and pressure. HT water is near 90 °C and under pressure. Isolate it, let it cool, depressurise and drain before breaking any joint.
  • Chemicals. Treatment chemicals can be toxic or corrosive. Wear the PPE the safety data sheet calls for. Never dose by mouth-siphoning or improvised methods.
  • Sea chest blowing-through with steam or air should be done under a permit and controlled, according to company procedure.
  • Plate coolers. Follow the maker's tightening dimension and sequence. Over-tightening damages the plates.

Regulations, at chapter level

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 (machinery installations) sets general requirements that essential machinery systems be reliable and arranged so a single failure does not disable essential services. The detailed rules on standby pumps, materials and piping come mainly from the classification society and the flag administration. Requirements therefore differ by class and flag.
  • STCW Code Section A-III/1 covers the competence of engineering watchkeepers to operate and monitor auxiliary systems, including cooling.
  • MARPOL Annex I becomes relevant if a cooler leak puts oil into water that ends up in bilges or overboard. Oily bilge water must be handled through the approved route.

Your company SMS will set the specific test frequencies and limits.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to start simple and then push you on faults. Be ready for these:

  • Q: Why is FW pressure kept above SW pressure in the central cooler? A: So that a plate leak lets fresh water out rather than sea water into the engine cooling system.
  • Q: Which sea chest would you use entering a river port, and why? A: The high sea chest, to avoid drawing in sediment from near the bottom.
  • Q: How do you know there is exhaust gas in the jacket water? A: Gas bubbles at the expansion tank vent, fluctuating HT pressure, and a rising expansion tank level. You might confirm it by venting at individual cylinders.
  • Q: Why preheat the jacket water before starting? A: To reduce thermal stress and avoid cold corrosion and liner or piston problems when load is applied.
  • Q: The LT temperature keeps rising and the TCV is fully open to the cooler. What do you check? A: Work through these in order:
    1. SW strainers and SW pump pressure.
    2. Sea suction and overboard valve positions.
    3. Sea water temperature.
    4. ΔT across the cooler, as an indication of fouling.
    5. Air in the system. Then start the standby pump and plan cleaning.
  • Q: What are the main water treatment tests? A: Inhibitor concentration, pH and chlorides, to the supplier's limits.
Key points
  • On a typical central cooling design, sea water is confined mainly to the sea chests, strainers, SW pumps and central coolers; treated fresh water cools almost everything else.
  • The LT circuit cools the consumers and the HT jacket water cooler; the HT circuit cools the main engine and feeds the fresh water generator.
  • Three-way temperature control valves hold the set points; always use the engine maker's figures.
  • Use the low sea chest at sea and the high sea chest in shallow or silty water.
  • Rising chlorides mean sea water ingress; oil sheen means a leaking oil cooler; bubbles in the HT expansion tank suggest exhaust gas leakage.
  • Trend temperatures, pressures, ΔT and water test results; slow drift usually means fouling.
  • Never open the sea water side without positive isolation and confirmation of no ingress.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the LT and HT cooling circuits?

The HT circuit cools the hot parts of the main engine, such as the jackets, cylinder covers and exhaust valves, and runs near 80–90 °C. The LT circuit runs cooler and serves the air coolers, oil coolers, auxiliary engines and the HT cooler itself. The LT circuit then rejects heat to sea water in the central cooler.

Why are central coolers usually titanium plate type?

Titanium resists sea water corrosion very well. Plate exchangers are compact and efficient, and they can be opened for cleaning without removing tubes. Plate gaskets do need renewing during overhauls.

How often should cooling water be tested?

That is set by the chemical supplier's guidance and your company SMS, not a single international figure. Many companies test weekly and after any large top-up, but follow your own procedure and record every result.

What does a rising expansion tank level mean?

Something is entering the system. On the HT side it can be exhaust gas or combustion pressure pushing water up. On the LT side it can be oil or sea water from a leaking exchanger. Test the water and isolate coolers one at a time to find the source.

Can I run the main engine if one central cooler is out of service?

Often yes, at reduced load or in cool sea water, if the remaining cooler has enough capacity. Some designs have each cooler rated for the full duty. Check the maker's data and get the chief engineer's agreement before running on one cooler.

Test yourself

Chloride content in the LT fresh water is rising steadily. What is the most likely cause?

Chlorides come from sea water, and the central cooler is where LT water meets sea water. Oil and exhaust gas leaks show as a sheen and bubbles instead (maker's manual / company water treatment procedure).

Which sea chest should normally be used when transiting a shallow, silty river?

The high sea chest draws water further from the bottom, which reduces sand and silt ingress. The low chest is preferred at sea to avoid air when rolling (ship operating manual).

How does the LT temperature control valve hold the set point?

A three-way valve sends just enough LT water through the central cooler and bypasses the rest. Throttling SW pumps is a separate measure and is not normal temperature control (system piping diagram and TCV manual).

Bubbles at the HT expansion tank vent, fluctuating HT pressure and a rising level suggest what?

Combustion gas entering the cooling spaces pushes gas and water into the expansion tank. It needs immediate investigation (main engine maker's instruction manual).

What is the main reason for preheating jacket water before starting the main engine?

Starting and loading a cold engine causes thermal stress on liners and covers and promotes cold corrosion. Makers specify a minimum jacket temperature before loading (main engine maker's instruction manual).
