Controllable Pitch Propeller (CPP) on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

A controllable pitch propeller (CPP) gives ahead, zero and astern thrust by hydraulically turning the blades about their own axes, while the shaft keeps turning in one direction. Pitch is set by a hydraulic servo in the hub, supplied through an oil distribution box and monitored by a feedback system. Watchkeepers need to know the oil circuit, the feedback loop, the emergency control mode and the faults that show up first.

What a CPP does and why ships use it

With a fixed pitch propeller (FPP), you change thrust by changing shaft speed, and you go astern by stopping and reversing the engine. With a controllable pitch propeller, the shaft turns in one direction only. Thrust and direction come from changing the pitch angle of the blades.

Why owners fit them:

  • Manoeuvrability. Pitch can be moved from full ahead to full astern quickly, typically within tens of seconds depending on the installation and load-control limits. There is no stop–start–reverse sequence and no starting air is used. This is why CPPs are common on ferries, Ro-Ro and Ro-Pax ships, tugs, offshore vessels and many feeders.
  • Constant-speed operation. The shaft can run at fixed RPM while pitch varies. This allows a shaft generator to supply the main switchboard at steady frequency.
  • Non-reversing engines. Medium-speed four-stroke engines driving through a reduction gearbox are commonly paired with a CPP. The engine never needs to reverse.
  • Load matching. Pitch and RPM can be combined to keep the engine within its load limits in different sea and loading conditions.

The trade-offs:

  • The hub is more complex and has more seals.
  • The hub is larger, which costs some efficiency at the design point.
  • You depend on a hydraulic system that must be maintained properly.

Main components

Layouts differ between makers. The building blocks are broadly the same:

Component Function
Hub and blades Blades are bolted to blade carriers (crank discs) that rotate in the hub. Seals at the blade foot keep hub oil in and seawater out.
Servo piston and crosshead A hydraulic piston, usually inside the hub, moves a crosshead axially. Pins or sliding blocks convert this linear movement into blade rotation.
Hollow shaft and oil tube Concentric passages carry pressure oil to each side of the servo piston and back.
Oil distribution (OD) box Fitted on the shaft line or at the gearbox. It transfers oil from the stationary pipework into the rotating shaft through sealing rings or bushes.
Hydraulic power unit Tank, main and standby pumps, filters, cooler, relief valves, and pressure and temperature sensors. Some systems also have an emergency pump.
Control valve A proportional or solenoid valve that directs oil to the ahead or astern side of the servo.
Feedback system Reports actual blade position back to the control system. It may be a mechanical feedback rod and linkage, or an electronic transmitter.
Hub static head / gravity tank Keeps hub oil pressure slightly above outside sea pressure, so any leak goes outward rather than letting water in. Not every design uses one.
Remote control system Bridge, ECR and local stations; pitch indicators; alarm panel; combinator and load-control logic.

Take time on joining to trace each of these on your own ship. Use the maker's drawings, not a generic diagram.

How it works, step by step

Take a pitch order from the bridge lever, say from zero to half ahead:

  1. Order. The lever sends a pitch set-point to the remote control system. In combinator mode it also sends an RPM set-point.
  2. Comparison. The control system compares the ordered pitch with the actual pitch from the feedback transmitter.
  3. Valve shifts. Because there is a difference, the control valve opens to send pressure oil to the ahead side of the servo. The other side is opened to drain.
  4. Oil transfer. Oil passes through the OD box into the rotating shaft and along the oil tube to the hub.
  5. Servo moves. The servo piston and crosshead move axially. The crank mechanism rotates every blade together to the new angle.
  6. Feedback. As the blades move, the feedback signal changes. When actual pitch equals ordered pitch, the valve returns to centre and the servo is hydraulically locked in position.
  7. Load control. If the engine approaches its load limit, many systems automatically reduce pitch or hold it back. This depends on the installation.

Operating modes vary by installation but typically include:

  • Constant-speed mode. Fixed RPM, often with the shaft generator on line. Only pitch changes.
  • Combinator mode. Pitch and RPM follow a programmed curve for better efficiency.
  • Backup or emergency mode. Pitch is controlled directly by push-buttons or manual valves at the ECR or the local station, with the normal electronics bypassed.

A practical point for the bridge: at zero pitch with the shaft turning, the propeller disc can disturb flow to the rudder. This may reduce steering effect, and a small residual thrust may remain. Transverse thrust when going astern may also differ from what FPP experience suggests. Check the pilot card and the manoeuvring information for your ship, not general rules of thumb.

Routine checks and maintenance

Intervals and limits come from the maker's manual and your planned maintenance system. The typical items are below.

Watchkeeping rounds

  • Hydraulic tank level.
  • System pressure and oil temperature.
  • Filter differential pressure indicators.
  • OD box for leakage, temperature and abnormal noise.
  • Hub static head tank level, where fitted. A falling level suggests leakage outboard. A rising level or water in samples suggests seawater ingress.
  • Standby pump selected and in auto.

Before departure / arrival tests

  • Move pitch ahead and astern from each control station.
  • Confirm the bridge, ECR and local indicators agree with each other and with the order.
  • Test changeover between control stations and to backup control.
  • Confirm the alarms are working.
  • Record the tests as your SMS requires.

Periodic

  • Hydraulic and hub oil sampling and analysis, looking for water, particles and degradation.
  • Filter element changes.
  • Pump changeover and testing of the emergency pump.
  • Calibration check of the pitch indication and feedback.
  • Test of the emergency or manual pitch control procedure.

Dry-dock

  • Blade bolt and blade seal inspection.
  • Hub oil tightness test.
  • Blade surface and edge condition check.
  • Measurement of blade pitch against indicated pitch, including the zero point.
  • OD box ring overhaul as scheduled.
  • Coordination with the stern tube seal work. This is usually carried out with the class surveyor and maker's service engineer.

Pollution angle: oil leaking from the hub or its seals goes straight into the sea. Treat any oil sheen astern as an incident to investigate and report, not a nuisance. Some regions and port states require environmentally acceptable lubricants at oil-to-sea interfaces. Check what applies to your trade.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes to check
Pitch hunting or oscillating about the set-point Air in the hydraulic oil; worn or slack feedback linkage; faulty control valve or transmitter; control tuning
Slow pitch response Low system pressure; worn pump; clogged filter; oil too cold and viscous; internal leakage past the servo or OD box rings
Pitch drifts away from the set-point Internal leakage in the servo or OD box; control valve not sealing in centre position
No response to the bridge lever Control signal or power failure; pump stopped; valve solenoid fault; station not transferred
Bridge, ECR and local indicators disagree Feedback transmitter or linkage fault; calibration error; wiring fault
Water in hub oil sample, or rising head tank level Damaged blade foot seal or hub seal
Oil sheen astern, or falling head tank level Blade or hub seal leakage outboard; stern tube seal (cross-check)
High OD box temperature or leakage Worn sealing rings or bushes; misalignment; inadequate lubrication
New vibration Blade damage from a strike, rope or debris; uneven blade pitch; bearing problems

What the pitch does on total loss of hydraulic pressure depends on the design. It may hold its last position, move towards a set direction, or need to be mechanically locked. Know your own ship's answer from the maker's manual before you need it.

Safety precautions and a worked scenario

Precautions

  • Before working on the hub, OD box, oil tube or pitch linkage:
    • Stop the propulsion and make sure it cannot restart.
    • Engage the turning gear and lock it, or lock the shaft by the approved method.
    • Depressurise the hydraulics, including any accumulators.
    • Isolate electrically under your permit-to-work and lock-out tag-out procedures.
  • Divers alongside or under the hull: the propeller, shaft and pitch control must be isolated and tagged, under a permit, with the bridge and ECR informed.
  • High-pressure oil can cause injection injuries. Never feel for a leak with your hand. Hot oil and oil mist near the OD box are also fire risks.
  • Start interlock: many installations prevent starting or clutching in unless pitch is at or near zero. Never defeat this without the chief engineer's authority and a clear reason.
  • Before using the shaft generator in constant-speed mode, confirm what changes in RPM do to the switchboard. Expect consequences on the electrical side.

Scenario: pitch stops responding during berthing

The ship is entering port with the pilot on board. The bridge orders slow astern, but the indicator stays at slow ahead.

  1. Bridge: tell the master and pilot immediately. Use the rudder, bow thruster and tugs as available, and consider anchors. Inform the ECR.
  2. ECR: check the station in control, the alarms, hydraulic pressure and pump status. Start the standby pump if the running pump has failed.
  3. If control electronics are suspect: transfer to backup or emergency pitch control as per the maker's procedure. Confirm with the bridge which pitch you are setting.
  4. If pitch cannot be changed: stopping the engine or declutching removes thrust. Do this only on the master's order, because it also removes steering effect from the propeller wash.
  5. Afterwards: log the times and actions, record the event in the SMS, and investigate before the next departure.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to test the oil path, the feedback loop and emergency action.

"Trace the oil from the pump to the blades." The oil goes from the tank to the pump, then through the filter and cooler to the control valve. From there it passes through the OD box into the hollow shaft and oil tube, and on to one side of the servo piston. The other side of the servo is open to drain, and the return oil comes back the same way.

"Why is hub oil pressure kept above sea pressure?" So that any seal leakage is outward. This protects the hub from water ingress and corrosion. Mention the head tank and oil sampling.

"How do you know the pitch indicator is true?" Compare the bridge, ECR and local readings during daily or pre-departure tests. Confirm the zero pitch point and blade angles physically in dry-dock.

"What happens if the feedback fails?" The control loop can no longer tell where the blades are. You may see hunting, runaway movement or a pitch deviation alarm. Change to backup control and verify pitch with the local mechanical indicator, where fitted.

"Why does steering suffer at zero pitch?" The turning propeller at zero pitch can disturb flow over the rudder and give little useful wash.

"Which SOLAS chapter covers this machinery?" Chapter II-1, Part C, on machinery installations. It covers astern power and propulsion controls, including bridge control and indication.

Key points
  • A CPP reverses thrust by reversing blade pitch; the shaft keeps turning in one direction.
  • The pitch order goes from the control valve, through the OD box and oil tube, to the hub servo, with feedback closing the loop.
  • Bridge, ECR and local pitch indicators must agree; check them in every pre-departure test.
  • Water in hub oil points to seal damage; oil astern is a pollution incident to investigate.
  • Behaviour on loss of hydraulics depends on the design, so learn your ship's maker's emergency procedure.
  • Isolate the shaft, hydraulics and controls under permit before hub or OD box work or diving operations.
  • Zero pitch with the shaft turning can reduce rudder effect, so know your ship's handling characteristics.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a CPP and a fixed pitch propeller?

An FPP has fixed blades. Thrust changes with RPM, and going astern means reversing the engine. A CPP keeps the same rotation and changes blade angle hydraulically to vary or reverse thrust. This gives faster manoeuvring and allows constant-speed running for a shaft generator.

What is an OD box on a CPP?

The oil distribution box transfers hydraulic oil from the stationary piping into the rotating shaft. It uses sealing rings or bushes to keep the ahead and astern oil passages separate. Wear in the OD box shows up as leakage, heat, slow response or pitch drift.

What should I do if CPP pitch control fails while manoeuvring?

Inform the master or bridge at once and check the hydraulic pumps, pressure and alarms. If the fault is in the control system, transfer to backup or emergency pitch control. Stopping the engine or declutching is a master's decision, because it removes propeller wash over the rudder. Follow your ship's maker procedure and SMS checklist.

Why do we take oil samples from the CPP hub?

Samples show seawater ingress through damaged blade or hub seals, wear particles and oil degradation before they cause damage. Sampling intervals and acceptance limits come from the maker and your planned maintenance system.

Can a CPP ship have a shaft generator?

Yes, and this is one of the main reasons for fitting a CPP. The shaft can run at constant speed while pitch varies the thrust, so the shaft generator gives steady frequency. Watchkeepers must still understand how changes in propulsion mode affect the switchboard.

Test yourself

How does a controllable pitch propeller produce astern thrust?

On a CPP, ahead and astern thrust come from changing blade angle; shaft rotation does not reverse. This is the basic operating principle in any CPP maker's manual.

What is the main purpose of the hub static head tank on many CPP installations?

The head tank keeps a slight overpressure in the hub so seawater cannot enter through seals, and its level trend shows leakage. Details vary by maker.

Pitch slowly drifts away from the ordered value with no change at the lever. What is the most likely cause?

Internal leakage lets oil bypass the servo, so the blades creep from the set position. The control valve not sealing in its centre position gives the same symptom.

Which SOLAS chapter contains the requirements for machinery installations, including astern power and propulsion control?

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C covers machinery installations. Chapter II-2 is fire protection, Chapter III is life-saving appliances and Chapter V is safety of navigation.

Before divers work near the propeller of a CPP ship, what is the essential precaution?

Zero pitch does not make a turning propeller safe, and interlocks are not isolation. Shaft and hydraulics must be stopped, locked and tagged under the SMS permit-to-work.
