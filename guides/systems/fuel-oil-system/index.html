Fuel Oil System on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

The fuel oil system takes fuel from the bunker tanks, cleans it by settling and centrifuging, heats it to the right injection viscosity, and delivers it at steady pressure to the main and auxiliary engines and boilers. Most problems come from water, cat fines, incompatible fuels, wrong viscosity or leaks. Leaks onto hot surfaces are one of the most common causes of engine room fires.

What the fuel oil system does

Residual fuel arrives on board far from engine-ready. It carries water, sludge, sediment and catalytic fines (cat fines: hard aluminium and silicon particles left over from refinery cracking). At ambient temperature it is too viscous to pump well or atomise properly.

The fuel oil system has four jobs:

  • Store fuel safely in bunker, settling and service tanks.
  • Clean it by gravity settling, centrifugal separation and filtration.
  • Condition it by heating (or cooling, for distillates) to the injection viscosity the engine maker specifies.
  • Deliver it at steady pressure and flow to the consumers, with surplus returned and vented.

Many ships also run separate distillate (MGO/MDO) lines and tanks. These serve emission control areas (ECAs), the emergency generator, boiler pilot burners and manoeuvring where company or engine maker practice calls for it. Very low sulphur fuel oils (VLSFO) have added their own handling issues since the 2020 global sulphur limit.

Main components

Layouts vary by ship and maker, but you will find most of these on any motor ship burning residual fuel:

How it works, step by step

Follow a litre of heavy fuel from bunker tank to cylinder:

  1. Storage. Fuel sits in a bunker tank with enough heating to keep it pumpable. Follow the SMS and fuel documentation on heating limits, and never heat close to the flashpoint.
  2. Transfer. The transfer pump moves it to the settling tank. This is often done on level alarms, and on some ships automatically.
  3. Settling. In the heated settling tank, free water and heavy dirt sink. The engineer drains the bottom through self-closing drain valves.
  4. Purification. The separator feed pump takes fuel through the heater to the purifiers. These spin out water and solids and discharge sludge periodically to the sludge tank. Clean fuel goes to the service tank.
  5. Service tank. The service tank keeps a ready supply. Its overflow back to the settling tank means the separators can run continuously, re-cleaning surplus fuel.
  6. Supply circuit. The supply pumps push fuel through the automatic filter and flow meter into the mixing tank.
  7. Circulating circuit. The circulating pumps take from the mixing tank and push fuel through the heaters, past the viscometer and through the fine filter to the engine.
  8. Viscosity control. The controller adjusts steam (or electric heating) so fuel reaches the engine at the viscosity the maker specifies. Viscosity, not temperature, is the controlled variable, because different fuels need different temperatures to reach it.
  9. Injection and return. The engine fuel pumps take what they need. The surplus returns to the mixing tank, which vents released gas. A pressure-control valve holds circuit pressure steady.

Worked scenario: You change to a new bunker with a higher viscosity grade. If you hold the old temperature, viscosity at the engine rises, atomisation suffers and exhaust temperatures and smoke go up. In viscosity mode, the controller simply opens the steam valve further. Check that the heaters have the capacity, and watch the viscometer trend after the changeover.

Fuel changeover: HFO to distillate and back

Under MARPOL Annex VI, the global sulphur limit is 0.50% and the ECA limit is 0.10%. Unless the ship uses an approved equivalent such as an exhaust gas cleaning system (scrubber), or burns a compliant fuel throughout, it must change to compliant fuel before entering an ECA. Annex VI also requires:

  • a written changeover procedure on board, and
  • a record of the changeover, including tank volumes, date, time and position.

Practical points every engineer should know:

  • Thermal shock. Hot HFO switched suddenly to cold distillate can make fuel pump plungers seize. Change temperature gradually at the rate in the engine maker's or company procedure.
  • Low viscosity. Distillates are thin. Engine makers set a minimum viscosity at the engine inlet. Some ships need a fuel cooler or chiller to stay above it.
  • Heating off. Shut off or bypass the heaters and steam tracing on distillate lines, or the fuel may gas or fall below minimum viscosity.
  • Flushing time. Work out how long the system takes to flush to compliant fuel. Start early enough that you are compliant at the ECA boundary.
  • Compatibility. Mixing incompatible fuels, typically during changeover or in a shared tank, can drop out asphaltenic sludge. This rapidly chokes filters and separators.

The detail varies by engine maker and company SMS, so always follow the ship's own changeover procedure.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch or engine room round:

  • Drain the settling and service tanks of water, and note how much comes out.
  • Check purifier operation: back pressure, feed temperature, sludge discharge and no overflow loss.
  • Check filter differential pressures and auto-filter flushing frequency. A rising flushing rate is an early warning.
  • Check that viscosity, temperature and pressures are at their set points.
  • Look and listen for leaks, especially at high-pressure pipe connections and in leak-alarm tanks.
  • Check tank levels and heating, and make sure transfers are not left unattended.

Periodic, per planned maintenance system:

  • Overhaul the purifiers (bowl, discs and seals) at the maker's running hours.
  • Clean the filter elements and check the back-flushing mechanism.
  • Calibrate or verify the viscometer.
  • Test the quick-closing valves and remote stops for the fuel pumps and separators.
  • Overhaul the injectors and test them for opening pressure and spray pattern.
  • Inspect the spray shields, anti-spray tape and hot-surface insulation.
  • Confirm that the designated fuel sampling points are clear and accessible.

After bunkering:

  • Keep new fuel segregated where possible until the lab analysis is back.
  • Bring the new fuel into use carefully, and watch filters and separators closely for the first hours.

Safety precautions and the regulations behind them

Fuel spraying or dripping onto hot surfaces such as turbochargers, exhaust manifolds and indicator cocks is a classic cause of engine room fires. The main SOLAS requirements:

  • Flashpoint. SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4 generally prohibits oil fuel with a flashpoint below 60°C. A lower limit applies in specific cases, such as emergency generators.
  • Jacketed high-pressure lines. The same regulation requires external high-pressure fuel lines between the fuel pumps and injectors to be jacketed, with leak alarms.
  • Hot surfaces. Surfaces above 220°C that fuel could reach after a failure must be insulated.
  • Quick-closing valves. Outlets from storage, settling and service tanks above the double bottom need valves that can be closed from a safe position outside the space.
  • Remote stops. SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 5 requires remote stops outside the space for oil fuel transfer pumps, fuel unit pumps and similar.
  • Two service tanks. SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires at least two service tanks for each type of fuel used for propulsion and vital systems, or an equivalent arrangement.

Records and pollution prevention:

  • MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18:
    • Keep the bunker delivery note for three years.
    • Retain the MARPOL sample under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and for at least 12 months.
  • MARPOL Annex I: Record fuel bunkering, sludge disposal and related machinery-space operations in Oil Record Book Part I.

On-board habits that prevent incidents:

  • Never leave a fuel transfer unattended.
  • Restore spray shields and insulation after every job.
  • Isolate, depressurise and drain before breaking any fuel joint.
  • Wipe up leaks immediately and keep the bilges clean.
  • Know where the quick-closing valve and remote stop controls are before you need them.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to start with a sketch. Practise drawing the system from bunker tank to injector and back in under five minutes, then expect follow-ups like these:

  • Why control viscosity rather than temperature?
    • Because fuels differ. The same temperature gives different viscosities on different bunkers.
  • Why does the service tank overflow to the settling tank?
    • So the separators can run continuously at reduced throughput. Lower throughput gives better separation, and the overflow re-cleans surplus fuel.
  • What is the purpose of the mixing tank?
    • To receive return fuel from the engine, release gas and air, and blend return with fresh supply fuel.
  • What do you do if the jacketed pipe leak alarm sounds?
    • Inform the bridge and the chief engineer.
    • Identify the leaking line, and stop and isolate as procedures allow.
    • Treat any fuel mist as a fire risk.
    • Rectify before restart.
  • Where are the quick-closing valves operated from, and when were they last tested?
    • Know the answer for your own ship.
  • What records apply to bunkering?
    • The bunker delivery note, the MARPOL sample and the Oil Record Book Part I entry.
  • What are the risks when changing from HFO to MGO?
    • Thermal shock, low viscosity, incompatibility and missing the ECA compliance point.
Key points
  • Fuel is stored, cleaned (settling, separation, filtration), conditioned to the correct viscosity, then delivered at steady pressure.
  • The viscosity controller adjusts heating so fuel reaches the engine at the maker's specified viscosity, whatever the bunker grade.
  • Water, cat fines and incompatible fuels cause most fuel-related engine problems; regular draining and well-run separators are the first defence.
  • HFO-to-distillate changeovers need a gradual temperature change, the heating off and a check on minimum viscosity.
  • SOLAS II-2 requires jacketed high-pressure fuel lines, insulated hot surfaces, quick-closing valves and remote pump stops.
  • MARPOL Annex VI requires the bunker delivery note to be kept for three years and the MARPOL sample to be retained for at least 12 months.
  • Never leave a fuel transfer unattended, and restore spray shields and insulation after every job.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a settling tank and a service tank?

The settling tank receives fuel from the bunker tanks and holds it hot so water and sediment settle out before separation. The service (day) tank holds fuel that has already been through the purifiers, ready for the engines. The service tank overflows back to the settling tank.

Why is heavy fuel oil heated before injection?

Residual fuel is far too viscous at ambient temperature to be atomised properly by the injectors. Heating lowers it to the injection viscosity set by the engine maker, giving a fine spray and good combustion. The viscosity controller adjusts the heating automatically.

What are cat fines and why are they dangerous?

Cat fines are hard aluminium and silicon particles left in residual fuel from the refining process. They cause rapid abrasive wear of fuel pumps, injectors, piston rings and liners. Good settling tank practice and correctly run separators are the main way to remove them.

How long must the MARPOL fuel sample be kept on board?

Under MARPOL Annex VI, the sample must stay under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, and in any case for at least 12 months from delivery. The bunker delivery note must be kept for three years.

Why do engines sometimes have trouble after changing from HFO to MGO?

Common causes are fuel pump plunger sticking from a too-rapid temperature change, distillate viscosity falling below the engine maker's minimum, and sludge from mixing incompatible fuels. Following the ship's written changeover procedure, at the rate and temperatures it specifies, avoids most of these problems.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS, what is the general minimum flashpoint for oil fuel used on board?

SOLAS II-2 Regulation 4 generally prohibits oil fuel with a flashpoint below 60°C. Lower flashpoints, such as 43°C, are only allowed in specific cases like emergency generators. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4.

In a typical HFO booster circuit, what normally controls the heat input to the fuel heaters?

The heaters are controlled by viscosity, measured by the viscometer, so different fuels reach the engine at the maker's specified viscosity. Ref: engine maker's instructions; system design practice.

How long must the MARPOL fuel sample be retained under the ship's control?

Annex VI requires the sample to be retained until the fuel is substantially consumed and in any case for at least 12 months. The three-year period applies to the bunker delivery note. Ref: MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 18.

What is the main purpose of the quick-closing valves on fuel tank outlets?

Quick-closing valves allow the fuel supply from tanks above the double bottom to be stopped from outside the space, cutting fuel to a fire or burst pipe. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4.

How must external high-pressure fuel lines between the fuel pumps and injectors be protected?

SOLAS requires these lines to be jacketed so the jacket can contain fuel from a high-pressure line failure, with an alarm to warn of leakage. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4.
