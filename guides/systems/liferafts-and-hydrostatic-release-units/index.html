Liferafts and Hydrostatic Release Units (HRU): How They Work, Checks and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

An inflatable liferaft is a survival craft packed in a container on deck. It is launched by hand or by davit, or it floats free if the ship sinks. The hydrostatic release unit (HRU) frees the raft from its cradle once water pressure builds at a depth of not more than 4 m. The raft then rises, the painter pulls tight and fires the gas cylinder, and a weak link parts so the inflated raft can break away from the sinking ship.

What liferafts and HRUs are for

On most cargo ships the liferafts back up the lifeboat. On ships with free-fall lifeboats and on many passenger ships, they are a main part of the abandon-ship capacity. They also give you a craft on the side away from a fire, or on whichever side the ship is listing towards.

The HRU covers the worst case: the ship sinks so fast that nobody can launch anything. The raft must then float free and inflate on its own, so that survivors in the water have something to swim to.

The requirements come from two instruments:

  • SOLAS Chapter III covers how many rafts, where they are stowed, inspection and servicing.
  • LSA Code Chapter IV covers construction, inflation, float-free arrangements and equipment.

The number and type of rafts depend on ship type, size and the arrangement the flag has approved. Do not guess. The authoritative list for your ship is the Record of Equipment attached to the ship's safety certificate (Cargo Ship Safety Equipment, Cargo Ship Safety or Passenger Ship Safety Certificate).

Main components

Component What it does
Container (GRP canister or valise) Protects the packed raft and splits open on inflation
Cradle / stowage frame Holds the container, usually on a ramp or rail at the ship's side
Lashing (gripe) with slip hook Holds the container in the cradle; the slip hook allows quick manual release
Hydrostatic release unit Cuts or releases the lashing when water pressure acts on its diaphragm
Weak link Holds the painter to the ship until the inflated raft's buoyancy breaks it
Painter Line from the raft to the ship; pulling it fires the gas cylinder
Gas cylinder and operating head Compressed non-toxic gas that inflates the buoyancy tubes
Buoyancy tubes, floor, canopy At least two separate buoyancy compartments, an insulated floor and a canopy for protection
Emergency pack SOLAS A pack (international voyages) or SOLAS B pack (short international voyages)
Embarkation ladder, boarding ramp, righting strap Getting in from the water, and righting a raft that inflates upside down

The container is marked with:

  • maker and serial number
  • capacity
  • type of emergency pack
  • date of last service
  • painter length
  • maximum permitted stowage height above the waterline
  • launching instructions

Check the stowage height marking against the actual stowage position on your ship.

How the float-free sequence works, step by step

  1. The ship sinks and the raft container goes under with it.
  2. Water pressure acts on the HRU diaphragm. At a depth of not more than 4 m the unit releases. On the common disposable type, a spring-loaded knife cuts the lashing rope.
  3. The container floats up, paying out the painter, which stays fixed to the ship through the weak link.
  4. The painter comes taut as the ship keeps sinking. The pull fires the gas cylinder.
  5. The raft inflates and bursts the container open. The LSA Code requires inflation within 1 minute at an ambient temperature of 18–20 °C, and within 3 minutes at –30 °C.
  6. The weak link parts. The buoyancy of the inflated raft overcomes the weak link (designed to break at 2.2 kN ± 0.4 kN), so the raft floats free instead of being dragged down.
  7. The raft floats on the surface, ready for survivors to board. It may have inflated inverted, in which case someone must right it using the righting strap.

The whole system depends on correct rigging:

  • The lashing must pass through the HRU's release point.
  • The painter must go to the weak link, never to the ship's structure.

If the painter is made fast to a bollard or the cradle, the raft will inflate underwater and be held down.

Manual launching and boarding (throw-over raft)

Always follow the instructions posted at the station and the muster list. The general sequence is:

  1. Check the water below is clear of people, debris and burning oil. Choose the lee side if you can.
  2. Check the painter is secured to the ship, as per the posted instructions.
  3. Release the lashing with the slip hook. Do not cut it, and do not touch the HRU.
  4. Throw the container overboard. Use two people, or let it roll off a ramp cradle.
  5. Pull the painter out to its full length hand over hand, then give it a sharp tug to fire the cylinder.
  6. Bring the raft alongside under the embarkation ladder or chute. Board dry if at all possible. Do not jump onto the canopy unless the instructions and the height allow it.
  7. Cut the painter with the safety knife stowed near the entrance. Paddle clear of the ship's side.
  8. Stream the drogue (sea anchor). It reduces drift and steadies the raft, and keeps you nearer the distress position.
  9. Look for others, then close up. Pick up survivors, close the entrances, bail and dry the floor, and take anti-seasickness tablets early.

Davit-launched rafts are inflated alongside at the embarkation deck and lowered on an automatic release hook. Train on your ship's specific davit and hook. The details vary by maker.

Routine checks and servicing

Weekly and monthly (onboard). SOLAS Chapter III requires:

  • a weekly visual inspection of survival craft and launching appliances
  • a monthly inspection against a checklist, recorded in the official logbook

Your company SMS will have its own form. For each raft, check:

  • The container is intact: no cracks, no gaps at the seal band, and drain holes not blocked.
  • The raft is seated correctly in the cradle.
  • The lashing is tight, routed through the HRU, and the slip hook is free and not painted over.
  • The HRU is the right type, fitted the right way up, and within its expiry date.
  • The painter is connected to the weak link, and the weak link is connected to the deck point.
  • The next service date on the container has not passed.
  • Instructions and IMO symbols are posted and legible.
  • Nothing is stowed on or around the raft that could stop it floating free.

Servicing (ashore).

  • Inflatable liferafts must be serviced at an approved service station at intervals not exceeding 12 months. Where this is impracticable, the Administration may extend the interval to 17 months.
  • Some Administrations accept extended service intervals for rafts approved for that scheme. This depends on the flag, so check before assuming.
  • Ship's crew must not open, repack or modify a raft.

HRUs.

  • Disposable HRUs are replaced when their expiry date passes. The life is set by the manufacturer and marked on the unit.
  • Non-disposable HRUs are serviced on the same 12-month basis as rafts, extendable to 17 months.
  • When you replace an HRU, the weak link usually comes with it. Fit them exactly as the maker's diagram shows.

Common faults and their symptoms

What you find Why it matters
Painter tied to the cradle, rail or bollard The raft inflates underwater and is held down as the ship sinks
Lashing not passing through the HRU The HRU fires but the raft stays lashed in the cradle
HRU fitted upside down or wrong model It may not release, or may release early
HRU past its expiry date Its performance is not guaranteed; a common PSC finding
Slip hook seized, painted or wired shut You cannot launch manually in an emergency
Container band corroded, cracks, water inside The raft may be damaged or the container may not open
Raft fouled by pipes, cargo lashings or a cradle rail The raft cannot float free or roll off
Service date expired The certificate condition is not met; expect detention risk
Raft painted over, or HRU painted Hides markings and can jam the mechanism

A quick test on every round: trace the painter by eye from the container to the weak link, and from the weak link to the deck. Then trace the lashing through the HRU knife or release. If either path is wrong, report it to the officer responsible for LSA before you leave the station.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

Expect these in CoC orals and in PSC or vetting walk-rounds:

  • Walk me through float-free release. Answer with the sequence: HRU releases at no more than 4 m, raft floats up, painter tightens and fires the cylinder, raft inflates, weak link breaks.
  • What is the weak link for, and what happens without it? It holds the painter until inflation, then breaks so the raft is not dragged down. Without it, the raft goes down with the ship.
  • Show me where the painter is made fast. To the weak link, not to the ship's structure.
  • How often is the raft serviced, and by whom? At an approved station, not exceeding 12 months, extendable to 17 months by the Administration.
  • This HRU expired last month. What do you do? Report it, replace it with the correct approved unit, and record it. Flag-specific dispensation is only through the company and the flag.
  • What is in a SOLAS A pack versus a SOLAS B pack? The A pack is the full pack for international voyages. The B pack is a reduced pack for short international voyages. The full lists are in the LSA Code.
  • First actions after boarding? Cut the painter, clear the ship, stream the drogue, pick up survivors, close entrances, keep warm and dry, take seasickness tablets, set lookouts and organise the people on board.
Key points
  • The HRU releases the raft lashing at a depth of not more than 4 m.
  • The painter must be attached to the weak link, never directly to the ship's structure.
  • Painter tension fires the gas cylinder; the weak link then parts so the inflated raft floats free.
  • Inflatable liferafts are serviced at an approved station at intervals not exceeding 12 months, extendable to 17 months by the Administration.
  • Disposable HRUs are replaced by their expiry date; non-disposable HRUs are serviced like the raft.
  • Weekly and monthly LSA inspections should trace the lashing through the HRU and the painter to the weak link.
  • The Record of Equipment on the safety certificate is the authority on how many rafts your ship must carry.

Frequently asked questions

At what depth does a liferaft hydrostatic release unit activate?

The LSA Code requires the HRU to release the raft at a depth of not more than 4 m. In practice it releases within the first few metres of the ship going under. That gives the raft time to rise and inflate before the weak link parts.

How often must an inflatable liferaft be serviced?

SOLAS Chapter III requires servicing at intervals not exceeding 12 months, at a service station approved for that make of raft. Where this is impracticable, the Administration may extend the interval to 17 months. Some flags accept extended service schemes for specially approved rafts, so check your flag's position.

Can ship's crew repack or open a liferaft?

No. Rafts may only be opened, inspected and repacked by an approved service station. Crew may carry out external inspections, and may replace an HRU with the correct approved unit following the maker's instructions.

What happens if the liferaft painter is tied directly to the deck?

In a float-free situation the raft would inflate while still held by the painter. The sinking ship would then drag it down. That is why the painter must go to the weak link, which breaks under the raft's buoyancy.

Does a disposable HRU need servicing?

No. It is not serviced. It is replaced when it reaches the expiry date marked on it, which is set by the manufacturer. Expired HRUs are a common port state control deficiency.

Test yourself

Under the LSA Code, when must a hydrostatic release unit release the liferaft?

Float-free arrangements require the HRU to release at a depth of not more than 4 m. Painter tension fires the inflation; it does not release the HRU. Reference: LSA Code, Chapter IV.

Where should the end of a float-free liferaft's painter be attached?

The weak link holds the painter until inflation and then breaks, so the raft is not dragged down. Reference: LSA Code, Chapter IV float-free arrangements.

What is the normal SOLAS servicing interval for an inflatable liferaft?

Inflatable liferafts are serviced at approved stations at intervals not exceeding 12 months, with possible extension to 17 months. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20.

What is the requirement for a disposable hydrostatic release unit?

Disposable HRUs are not serviced; they are replaced when their expiry date passes. Reference: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20.

In the float-free sequence, what fires the liferaft's gas cylinder?

Painter tension operates the cylinder head; the weak link breaks only after inflation. Reference: LSA Code, Chapter IV.
