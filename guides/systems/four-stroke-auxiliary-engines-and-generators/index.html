Four-Stroke Auxiliary Engines and Generators: A Practical Guide for Ship's Crew

Quick answer

Ship's auxiliary engines are four-stroke diesels that drive alternators to make the vessel's main electrical power. SOLAS requires at least two generating sets, so if one stops the others can still keep propulsion and safety services running. To run them well you need the four-stroke cycle, the support systems, watchkeeping checks, the method for paralleling and load sharing, and the hazards of crankcases, hot surfaces and high-pressure fuel.

Purpose: why the auxiliary engines matter

On most merchant ships the main engine only turns the propeller. Everything else runs on electricity from the diesel generators (DGs, or "auxiliaries"). That includes steering gear, cooling and lube oil pumps, purifiers, compressors, bridge equipment, galley, reefers and accommodation.

Lose the generators and you lose the ship. That is why SOLAS requires the main source of electrical power to have at least two generating sets. Their capacity must be enough that, with any one set stopped, the rest still supply the services for normal propulsion and safety.

Typical arrangements:

  • Three or four DGs. One or two run at sea and more run during manoeuvring, cargo work or bow thruster use.
  • Sometimes a shaft generator takes the sea load.
  • A separate emergency generator sits outside the machinery space. It is a different system with its own SOLAS requirements and must not be confused with the main DGs.

Most auxiliaries are medium-speed, trunk-piston, four-stroke engines. Many burn heavy fuel oil as well as distillate, depending on design and company practice.

Main components of a generating set

A generating set is the engine (prime mover) and the alternator on a common base frame.

Engine structure and running gear - Bedplate/frame and crankcase. Larger engines have crankcase explosion relief valves and, on many, an oil mist detector. - Crankshaft with main bearings, connecting rods and trunk pistons. There is no crosshead, so the piston skirt takes the side thrust. - Cylinder liners and cylinder heads. Each head usually has inlet and exhaust valves, an injector, an indicator cock and often a safety valve. - Camshaft, driven by gears from the crankshaft. It operates the valves through push rods and rocker arms, and drives the fuel pumps.

Fuel system - An individual jerk-type injection pump per cylinder on conventional engines. Common-rail designs exist. - High-pressure fuel pipes, which are jacketed with leak detection. - Injectors. - Fuel rack linkage controlled by the governor.

Air and exhaust - Exhaust gas turbocharger. - Charge air cooler. - Exhaust manifold with a temperature reading for each cylinder and before/after the turbocharger.

Support systems - Lube oil: usually a wet sump with an engine-driven pump, filter and cooler, plus an electric pre-lubrication pump for starting. - Cooling: high-temperature (HT) water for jackets and heads, and low-temperature (LT) water for the charge air cooler and LO cooler. These usually come off the ship's central cooling system. - Starting: compressed air, either direct into the cylinders or through an air motor on the flywheel.

Control, protection and alternator - Governor, hydraulic-mechanical or electronic. - Safety devices: typically overspeed trip, low LO pressure shutdown and high cooling water temperature alarm/shutdown. - Alternator: usually a brushless synchronous machine with an AVR (automatic voltage regulator).

How it works, step by step

The four-stroke cycle. Each cylinder fires once every two crankshaft revolutions, so the camshaft turns at half crankshaft speed.

  1. Induction: the piston moves down with the inlet valve open, drawing in pressurised charge air from the turbocharger.
  2. Compression: both valves close. The piston rises and compresses the air until it is hot enough to ignite fuel.
  3. Power: fuel is injected near top dead centre, burns and drives the piston down.
  4. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens and the piston pushes out the gases to the turbocharger.

There is a period of valve overlap around top dead centre, when both valves are open. This helps scavenge the cylinder and cool the exhaust valve.

Constant speed. The alternator frequency depends on speed and the number of poles: frequency = (speed × poles) ÷ 120. A 60 Hz ship therefore runs at fixed speeds such as 720, 900 or 1,200 rpm, depending on the alternator. The governor holds that speed as electrical load changes by moving the fuel racks.

Who controls what when running in parallel:

Quantity Controlled by
Frequency and active load (kW) sharing Engine governor (fuel)
Voltage and reactive load (kVAr) sharing Alternator AVR (excitation)

Paralleling an incoming generator. On most ships the power management system does this automatically, but you must be able to do it by hand:

  1. Start the engine, warm it through and check that pressures and temperatures are normal.
  2. Match voltage to the busbar.
  3. Set the frequency slightly higher, so the synchroscope rotates slowly in the "fast" direction.
  4. Close the breaker just before the pointer reaches the in-phase (12 o'clock) position. Many ships also use synchronising lamps.
  5. Transfer load with the governor controls until the kW is shared as required. Check that the kVAr/current is balanced.

The reason for running the incoming set slightly fast is that it picks up a little load as soon as it is connected. If it ran slow, it would take reverse power and could trip on reverse power protection. That relay exists to stop a generator being motored by the busbar.

Routine checks and maintenance

Before starting - Check the sump level, and the governor oil level if it is hydraulic. - Check the cooling water expansion tank level. - Line up the fuel, and confirm the fuel temperature/viscosity if the engine is on HFO. - Confirm starting air pressure. Make sure the turning gear is disengaged. - Run the pre-lube pump. - If the engine has been stopped for a while, or after maintenance, blow through on air with the indicator cocks open. Water or oil coming out means find the cause before you start. Close the cocks before starting.

Running checks on watch - LO pressure and temperature, and filter differential pressure. - HT/LT water temperatures and pressures. - Charge air pressure and temperature. - Turbocharger rpm, where it is fitted with an indicator. - Exhaust temperatures for each cylinder and the deviation from the mean. - kW, current, voltage, frequency and power factor. - Walk round for fuel, oil, water and exhaust leaks. Check the fuel pipe leak alarm, vibration and unusual noises. - Log the readings and compare trends, not just against alarm limits.

Planned maintenance Intervals come from the manufacturer's manual and the company's planned maintenance system. Do not use figures from another ship or engine. Typical jobs include:

  • Injector pressure testing and overhaul.
  • Valve clearance (tappet) checks.
  • Cylinder head and valve overhaul.
  • Turbocharger washing and overhaul.
  • Air cooler cleaning.
  • Unit overhaul: piston, rings, liner calibration, bearings.
  • Crankcase inspections, including crankshaft deflections.
  • Governor and safety device tests.
  • Regular lube oil analysis.

Low-load running. Avoid long periods of very low load. It leads to poor combustion, fouling of the turbocharger and exhaust, and lube oil contamination. Manuals usually give minimum load guidance.

Worked scenario: one high exhaust temperature

On watch you see No. 4 cylinder reading about 60 °C above the others at a steady load.

  1. Confirm the reading is real: compare the thermocouple with a portable reading or check its trend.
  2. Check that No. 4 fuel rack position matches the others.
  3. Listen at the cylinder head. Check for a hot or leaking injector pipe.
  4. Inform the 2nd Engineer.
  5. Bring another set on line and take the suspect engine off load.
  6. On the permitted maintenance, the usual first suspect is the injector. Change and pressure-test it, then check valve clearances.
  7. Record the findings in the PMS.

Safety precautions

Crankcase hazards - If high oil mist or an overheated bearing is suspected, stop the engine and keep people clear of the relief valves. - Do not open crankcase doors until the engine has cooled for the period the manufacturer specifies. Letting air into hot oil mist can cause a secondary explosion. - Never use naked lights near an open crankcase.

Fire - Fuel spray onto hot exhaust surfaces is a classic engine room fire cause. - SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires jacketed high-pressure fuel pipes and insulated hot surfaces. - Report and fix any missing lagging, leaking jacket pipe or oil-soaked insulation at once.

Isolation before maintenance (lock-out tag-out under the permit system) - Breaker open, locked and tagged. - Auto-start inhibited in the power management system. - Starting air shut and the line vented. - Turning gear engaged. - Fuel shut off. - Cooling water and lube oil isolated as needed.

A generator that auto-starts during a blackout while someone is inside it is a fatal scenario.

Electrical - Treat the alternator terminal box and switchboard as live until proven dead. - On high-voltage installations only authorised, trained persons may switch or work.

General - Wear hearing protection. - Watch out for hot surfaces, rotating flywheels and couplings. - Use proper lifting gear for heads and pistons.

Regulations and blackout response

Regulations to know at chapter level - SOLAS Chapter II-1: - Part C covers machinery installations, including crankcase explosion protection. - Part D covers the main source of electrical power (minimum two generating sets) and the emergency source. - SOLAS Chapter II-2: fire safety, including fuel oil arrangements and hot surfaces. - MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 13: controls NOx from marine diesel engines above 130 kW. Whether it applies depends on the ship's construction date, the engine installation date and any major conversion, so most auxiliaries on modern ships are covered. Such engines need an EIAPP certificate and a NOx Technical File. Changes to NOx-critical components (such as injectors, turbocharger or camshaft) must stay within the Technical File. - Class rules and flag requirements add detail. These differ between societies and administrations, so follow your ship's documents.

Worked scenario: blackout at sea

The running generator trips and the lights go out. Your ship's emergency procedures take precedence, but the general sequence is:

  1. The emergency generator should start and supply the emergency switchboard automatically.
  2. Tell the bridge immediately. The ship may lose propulsion and steering.
  3. On most ships the standby DG auto-starts and closes onto the dead bus. If it does not, start one manually and close its breaker.
  4. Confirm the steering gear pumps have restarted automatically. Then restart other essential services in sequence, starting with the main engine's auxiliaries, so you do not overload the single set.
  5. Find the cause before restarting the tripped set. Common causes include:
    • fuel supply problems such as a choked filter or wrong changeover
    • a low LO pressure trip
    • overload on a large consumer starting
    • a protection fault

On many ships preferential trips shed non-essential loads during an overload to keep the essential ones running.

Oral exam questions with model answers

Q: Why does the camshaft run at half crankshaft speed? Each valve and fuel pump acts once per four-stroke cycle, and the cycle takes two crankshaft revolutions.

Q: What conditions must be met before closing an incoming generator's breaker? - Same voltage. - Same frequency, with the incoming set slightly fast. - Same phase sequence. - In phase at the moment of closing.

Q: Two generators are in parallel and the kW is unequal. What do you adjust? The governors, because active load follows fuel. Unequal current with equal kW points to excitation and the AVRs.

Q: What is the reverse power trip for? It stops a generator running as a motor if its prime mover fails while in parallel. This protects the engine and the healthy set.

Q: Why must you not open a crankcase immediately after an oil mist alarm shutdown? Fresh air entering hot oil mist with a hot spot present can ignite it and cause an explosion.

Q: What is the significance of the EIAPP certificate and NOx Technical File for an auxiliary engine? They show the engine meets the MARPOL Annex VI NOx limits. Components and settings must stay as approved in the file.

Key points
  • SOLAS requires at least two main generating sets so that losing one does not stop propulsion and safety services.
  • Four-stroke engines fire once every two revolutions, and the camshaft runs at half crankshaft speed.
  • The governor controls frequency and kW sharing, while the AVR controls voltage and kVAr sharing.
  • When paralleling, bring the incoming set in slightly fast and close the breaker just before the in-phase position.
  • Watch the deviation in each cylinder's exhaust temperature, as it is the best early warning of injector, fuel pump or valve faults.
  • Never open a crankcase until it has cooled for the time the manufacturer specifies, and always isolate start air, fuel, breaker and auto-start before maintenance.
  • Auxiliary engines above 130 kW on most modern ships fall under MARPOL Annex VI NOx rules and need an EIAPP certificate and Technical File.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an auxiliary engine and a generator?

The auxiliary engine is the diesel prime mover and the generator (alternator) is the electrical machine it drives. Together on a common frame they form a generating set, commonly called a DG on board.

How many generators must a merchant ship have?

SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires the main source of electrical power to include at least two generating sets. Their capacity must cover normal propulsion and safety services with any one set stopped. Most ships carry three or four, plus a separate emergency generator.

Why do we blow through an engine before starting?

Turning the engine on air with the indicator cocks open shows whether water, fuel or oil has collected in a cylinder. Trying to compress a liquid can cause serious damage. Company procedures and the manual state when this is required.

What causes a generator to trip on reverse power?

It trips when, in parallel, the engine stops producing enough power and the alternator starts drawing power from the busbar. Typical causes are fuel starvation, a governor fault or poor paralleling technique, such as closing the breaker with the incoming set running slow.

How often should auxiliary engine overhauls be done?

Intervals are set by the engine manufacturer's running-hour recommendations and the ship's planned maintenance system, adjusted by condition monitoring. They vary by engine model and fuel, so always work from your own ship's manual and PMS rather than a general figure.

Test yourself

In a four-stroke auxiliary engine, at what speed does the camshaft rotate?

Each cylinder completes one cycle in two crankshaft revolutions, so valves and fuel pumps act once per two revolutions. This is basic four-stroke engine principle as covered in STCW engineering competence.

When paralleling manually, what is the correct moment to close the incoming generator's breaker?

The incoming set should be slightly fast so it takes load on closing, and the breaker is closed just before the in-phase position. A slow set risks a reverse power trip, and closing out of phase causes damaging current surges.

Two generators in parallel are sharing kW unequally. What should be adjusted?

Active load (kW) depends on the fuel supplied to each prime mover, which the governor controls. The AVR adjusts excitation and so the reactive load sharing.

What is the main purpose of the reverse power protection on a ship's generator?

Reverse power protection trips a generator that starts drawing power from the busbar. Load shedding is done by preferential trips and over-speeding by the engine's overspeed trip.

After an oil mist alarm shutdown, why must the crankcase doors not be opened straight away?

Fresh air entering a hot crankcase full of oil mist can trigger a secondary explosion. Wait for the cooling period stated by the manufacturer. Crankcase explosion protection is addressed in SOLAS Chapter II-1.
