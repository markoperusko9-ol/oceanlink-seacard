Sewage Treatment Plant on Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

Most merchant ships use a biological sewage treatment plant. Bacteria break down toilet waste using air from blowers, solids settle out, and the clear effluent is disinfected before discharge. MARPOL Annex IV sets where treated, comminuted or untreated sewage may be discharged, and the plant needs steady air, the right biology and regular attention to stay compliant.

What the plant does and why it is fitted

Under MARPOL Annex IV, sewage means drainage from toilets, urinals and WC scuppers. It also covers drainage from medical premises, from spaces carrying live animals, and any other waste water mixed with these. Grey water from showers, galley and laundry is not sewage under Annex IV unless it is mixed with sewage. Many plants still receive grey water by design.

Annex IV applies to ships of 400 GT and above on international voyages. It also applies to smaller ships certified to carry more than 15 persons. Such a ship must have one of the following:

  • an approved sewage treatment plant (STP)
  • a sewage comminuting and disinfecting system, with a holding tank for use when close to land
  • a holding tank, sized for the crew, the ship's operation and other relevant factors.

The ship also needs a pipeline to deck ending in a standard discharge connection for shore reception. Compliance is shown by the International Sewage Pollution Prevention (ISPP) Certificate.

The STP is the most common option on cargo ships. Large passenger ships often have advanced systems such as membrane bioreactors.

Main components of a typical biological STP

Most cargo-ship plants use extended aeration activated sludge. Designs differ by maker, but you will usually find:

  • Inlet screen or macerator/comminutor. Breaks up or removes solids and rags before the tank.
  • Aeration chamber. The main biological stage, where sewage mixes with bacteria-rich sludge.
  • Air blowers and diffusers. Usually duty and standby blowers. They supply oxygen to the bacteria and keep the contents mixed.
  • Settling (clarification) chamber. A quiet zone where activated sludge settles out of the treated water.
  • Sludge return (air lift) and skimmer. Air-driven lines that send settled sludge and scum back to aeration.
  • Disinfection stage. Chlorine tablets, dosed hypochlorite solution or UV lamps. Some plants add dechlorination.
  • Discharge pump with level switches or floats. Pumps effluent overboard or to a holding tank. High-level alarm fitted.
  • Vent pipe. Leads to open deck. It removes gases produced in the tank.
  • Holding tank and shore connection. For areas where discharge is not allowed.

Many ships feed the plant from a vacuum toilet system with its own vacuum units. That system has its own faults (leaking valves, blocked lines) that show up as plant problems.

How it works, step by step

  1. Collection. Sewage arrives by gravity or vacuum. It is screened or macerated so solids are small enough for bacteria to work on.
  2. Aeration. Aerobic bacteria (the activated sludge) feed on the organic matter, using oxygen from the blowers. This is the heart of the plant. Stop the air and the bacteria die or turn anaerobic.
  3. Clarification. Mixed liquor flows into the settling chamber. Healthy sludge forms flocs that sink, leaving clearer water on top.
  4. Sludge return. The air lift sends settled sludge back to aeration to keep the bacteria population up. The skimmer returns floating scum.
  5. Disinfection. Clear effluent passes to the disinfection chamber, where chlorine or UV kills harmful organisms.
  6. Discharge. Level switches start and stop the discharge pump. Effluent goes overboard where allowed, or to the holding tank where it is not.
  7. Desludging. Excess sludge builds up over time. It is removed as the maker's manual directs, either to shore or overboard where discharge of untreated sewage is permitted.

A biological plant is a living system. After commissioning or a long shutdown, the bacteria need time to build up before effluent quality is reliable. The manual will say how to start up and whether seeding is recommended.

Discharge rules you must know (MARPOL Annex IV)

Annex IV Regulation 11 allows discharge in three cases:

What is discharged Condition
Comminuted and disinfected sewage (approved system) More than 3 nautical miles from the nearest land
Sewage not comminuted or disinfected (e.g. holding tank, desludging) More than 12 nautical miles from the nearest land, ship en route at not less than 4 knots, at a moderate rate approved by the Administration
Effluent from an approved STP in operation No distance limit in Annex IV, provided the effluent produces no visible floating solids and does not discolour the surrounding water. Passenger ships in the Baltic special area must meet stricter Reg. 11.3 standards

Points that catch people out:

  • Special area. The Baltic Sea is a special area for passenger ships. Stricter treatment standards (including nutrient removal) apply there, with dates depending on ship and voyage. Check your ship's documentation.
  • Local rules. Coastal and port states may set tighter rules in their waters, including no-discharge zones. The company SMS and the port information decide what you actually do.
  • Records. Annex IV does not prescribe a sewage record book. Most company SMS procedures still require logging discharges with time and position.
  • Port State Control. Inspectors will ask whether the plant runs, look at the effluent and check the ISPP Certificate.

Worked scenario: You are 8 nm off the coast and the holding tank is near full. Its contents are untreated sewage, so you cannot pump out here. Shift flow back through the running STP if the design allows, or keep holding. Pump out only once beyond 12 nm and making at least 4 knots, at the approved rate, and only if no local rule forbids it. Otherwise arrange shore reception.

Routine checks and maintenance

Exact intervals come from the maker's manual and the planned maintenance system. Typical routine items:

Daily or each watch - Blower running, normal current and pressure, and no unusual noise. Change over to standby as scheduled. - Air lift and skimmer returns visibly flowing. - Disinfectant supply topped up (tablets present, dosing pump working, or UV lamps on). - Discharge pump cycling normally, with no high-level alarms. - Smell and appearance. A healthy plant has an earthy smell and brown mixed liquor. A rotten-egg smell or black liquor means trouble.

Periodic - Settleability test. Draw a sample from aeration into a graduated cylinder, let it stand for the time stated in the manual, and note how well and how much sludge settles. This is the quickest guide to plant health. - Clean the inlet screen or macerator, and clear blockages in the air lift and diffusers. - Clean UV sleeves and replace lamps on hours. Service blower filters and belts, and check non-return valves. - Test the level switches and high-level alarm. - Desludge as directed. - Effluent sampling or testing if company or flag requires it.

Good housekeeping on board matters as much as the engine room. Strong disinfectants, bleach, solvents, cooking oil, wipes, sanitary items and paper towels kill the bacteria or block the plant. Remind the crew, especially after a crew change.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause Action
Rotten-egg smell, black liquor Air supply lost (blower stopped, blocked diffusers), plant gone anaerobic Restore air, clear diffusers, and allow time to recover. Keep effluent in the holding tank meanwhile
Cloudy effluent, solids carried over Poor settling (sludge bulking), overload, broken air lift Do a settleability test, restore sludge return, and desludge if sludge is excessive
Heavy foaming Detergent overload, young or unstable biomass Check what is entering from galley and laundry. Follow manual guidance
Plant biology dies off Toxic chemicals, long shutdown, too much disinfectant reaching aeration Identify the source, then restart as per the manual
High-level alarm Discharge pump failure, stuck float, blocked discharge line Check pump and switches. Clear the line. Avoid overflow into the space
No disinfection Tablets used up, dosing pump failed, UV lamp failed or sleeve fouled Restore disinfection before discharging overboard
Toilets not flushing (vacuum system) Vacuum unit fault, leaking valve, blocked line Locate the leak or blockage section by section

Treat any repair that needs the tank opened, or entry into the compartment, as a planned job under the permit system.

Safety precautions

  • Toxic and flammable gases. Sewage tanks can produce hydrogen sulphide and methane and can be oxygen deficient. Entry into an STP or holding tank is an enclosed space entry. Follow the full procedure: permit, ventilation, gas testing, standby person and rescue arrangements.
  • Keep the vent clear. A blocked vent lets gas build up and can push it back into accommodation through dry traps. Keep all water seals in drains topped up.
  • Biological hazard. Wear gloves, eye protection and overalls. Wash thoroughly and keep work clothes away from the mess. Vaccination policy is set by the company and medical adviser.
  • Chlorine tablets. These are typically strong oxidisers. Store them dry, away from oil, rags and other combustibles, and handle them as the safety data sheet says.
  • Electrical isolation. Lock out blowers, pumps and macerators before working on them. Check that the UV unit is isolated before opening it.
  • Pollution. An overflow or a wrong valve line-up can put raw sewage overboard in prohibited waters. Check the line-up before any pump-out.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Explain how a biological sewage plant works. Walk through screening, aeration, settling, sludge return, disinfection and discharge. Say why air is essential.
  • What is sewage under Annex IV? Toilet and urinal drainage, medical premises, live-animal spaces and waste water mixed with these. Grey water alone is not sewage.
  • What options does a ship have to comply? Approved STP, comminuting and disinfecting system, or holding tank. State the discharge conditions for each.
  • Your plant smells of rotten eggs. What do you do? Check the blowers and diffusers, restore air, divert to the holding tank, and investigate any chemicals entering the plant.
  • How do you judge plant health? By the settleability test, the appearance and smell of the liquor, the clarity of the effluent and whether the returns are flowing.
  • Which certificate is issued? The ISPP Certificate, kept valid by surveys.
  • Hazards of entering the tank? H₂S, methane and oxygen deficiency, so enclosed space entry procedure applies, plus the biological hazard.

Answer with the process first, then the regulation, then the safety angle. Examiners want to hear that you understand all three.

Key points
  • Most cargo ships use an extended aeration activated sludge plant; bacteria need continuous air to work.
  • Annex IV options are an approved STP, a comminuting and disinfecting system, or a holding tank, with compliance shown by the ISPP Certificate.
  • Comminuted and disinfected sewage may go overboard beyond 3 nm; untreated sewage only beyond 12 nm, en route at 4 knots or more, at the approved rate.
  • Approved STP effluent has no Annex IV distance limit but must show no floating solids or discoloration; Baltic special-area rules for passenger ships and local or port rules can be stricter.
  • The settleability test and the smell and colour of the liquor are the quickest checks on plant health.
  • Bleach, solvents, oil, wipes and sanitary items kill or block the plant, so crew awareness matters.
  • Sewage tanks can hold H2S, methane and low oxygen; entry is always an enclosed space entry.

Frequently asked questions

Can a ship discharge from the sewage treatment plant in port?

Annex IV sets no distance limit for effluent from an approved plant that is working properly. Many ports and coastal states ban or restrict any sewage discharge in their waters. Always check local regulations and the company SMS, and use the holding tank when in doubt.

Is grey water covered by MARPOL Annex IV?

Not on its own. Annex IV covers toilet and urinal drainage, medical premises, live-animal spaces and other waste water mixed with these. If grey water is mixed with sewage, the mixture is treated as sewage. Some coastal states regulate grey water separately.

Why does the sewage plant smell like rotten eggs?

That smell is usually hydrogen sulphide from anaerobic conditions. It most often means the aeration air has been lost or reduced, through a stopped blower, a blocked diffuser or a broken air line. Restore the air, hold effluent in the holding tank, and treat the gas as a toxic hazard.

Is a sewage record book mandatory?

MARPOL Annex IV does not prescribe a sewage record book. Most company SMS procedures, and some flag or local rules, require discharges to be logged with time and position. Follow your company's requirement.

How long does a sewage plant take to start working after a shutdown?

It depends on the plant design, temperature and loading, so follow the maker's manual. The bacteria population has to rebuild, and effluent quality is not reliable until it does. Some makers recommend seeding to speed this up.

Test yourself

Under MARPOL Annex IV, sewage that has not been comminuted or disinfected may be discharged when the ship is:

Untreated sewage needs more than 12 nm, the ship en route at 4 knots or more, and an approved moderate discharge rate. The 3 nm limit applies to comminuted and disinfected sewage. (MARPOL Annex IV, Reg. 11)

What is the main purpose of the air blowers in a biological STP?

The aerobic bacteria need oxygen to break down organic matter, and the air also keeps solids in suspension. Venting is done by the vent pipe, and discharge by the pump. (Maker's manual)

Which of the following is NOT sewage under MARPOL Annex IV?

Grey water such as shower water is not sewage unless mixed with sewage. Hospital and live-animal space drainage is included in the definition. (MARPOL Annex IV, Reg. 1)

Which certificate shows a ship complies with MARPOL Annex IV?

The ISPP Certificate is issued after survey under Annex IV. The IOPP covers Annex I and the IAPP covers Annex VI. (MARPOL Annex IV, Reg. 5)
