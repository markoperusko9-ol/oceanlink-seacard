Refrigeration and Provision Plant on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

The provision plant keeps the ship's food stores frozen or chilled using a vapour-compression cycle: compressor, condenser, receiver, drier, expansion valve and evaporator coolers in each cold room. Engineers must read pressures, sight glass and superheat to diagnose faults, manage refrigerant under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 12, and keep crew safe from refrigerant gas and cold-room entrapment.

What the provision plant does

The provision (domestic) refrigeration plant keeps the ship's food stores at the right temperature for the length of the voyage. A typical layout has:

  • Frozen rooms (meat, fish), held well below freezing, commonly around −18 °C or colder.
  • Chill rooms (vegetables, dairy, eggs), held just above freezing.
  • Sometimes a handling room or lobby at a moderate temperature that acts as a buffer and limits warm, humid air entering the colder rooms.

The exact set points come from the plant maker, the company SMS and the cook's needs. Spoiled stores on a long passage are a health problem and a morale problem. Under MLC 2006 the food has to be of suitable quality and quantity. That makes the provision plant a system that matters, even though it is small next to the main engine.

Most ships have two compressor units (one running, one standby) serving all rooms through a common condenser and receiver. Each room has its own evaporator coolers and control valves. Some ships use separate condensing units per room. The principles are the same.

Main components

Follow the refrigerant round the circuit and you meet these items:

Component Job
Compressor (reciprocating or screw) Draws low-pressure vapour from the evaporators and compresses it to high pressure and temperature
Oil separator (if fitted) Returns oil carried over in the discharge gas back to the crankcase
Condenser Removes heat so the gas condenses to liquid. On ships it is usually water-cooled, from sea water or the central cooling FW system depending on design
Receiver Stores liquid refrigerant and gives a liquid seal to the liquid line. Used for pump-down
Filter drier Removes moisture and dirt from the liquid
Sight glass / moisture indicator Shows full liquid flow and whether moisture is present
Solenoid valve (per room) Opens or shuts liquid supply on the room thermostat's signal
Thermostatic expansion valve (TEV) Throttles liquid to evaporator pressure and meters flow to hold a set superheat at the evaporator outlet
Evaporator / air cooler with fans Refrigerant boils and absorbs heat from the room air
Back-pressure / evaporator pressure regulator (on multi-temperature plants) Stops a chill room evaporator running too cold when it shares a compressor with frozen rooms

Protective devices usually include:

  • a high-pressure (HP) cut-out
  • a low-pressure (LP) cut-out or control
  • an oil pressure differential switch
  • a crankcase heater
  • a cooling water flow or pressure alarm
  • room high-temperature alarms

Refrigerant varies with the age of the ship. Older plants may still run on R22, which is an HCFC and an ozone-depleting substance. Newer plants commonly use HFCs or HFC blends such as R404A, R507 or R134a. Always check the plate on the plant before handling or topping up.

How it works, step by step

The cycle:

  1. The compressor discharges hot, high-pressure vapour.
  2. In the condenser, cooling water removes the superheat and the latent heat, and the refrigerant condenses to liquid.
  3. The liquid collects in the receiver, then passes through the filter drier and sight glass.
  4. With the room calling for cooling, the solenoid valve is open. Liquid reaches the TEV, where its pressure drops sharply and part of it flashes off.
  5. In the evaporator the low-pressure liquid boils, taking heat from air blown across the coil by the cooler fans.
  6. The TEV bulb, clamped to the evaporator outlet, senses superheat and opens or closes the valve. This ensures only vapour, slightly superheated, returns to the compressor.
  7. The suction vapour returns to the compressor and the cycle repeats.

Typical control by pump-down:

  • When a room reaches its set point, the room thermostat closes that room's solenoid valve.
  • When all solenoids are closed, the compressor keeps running and pumps refrigerant out of the evaporators into the condenser and receiver.
  • Suction pressure falls until the LP switch stops the compressor.
  • When a room warms up, its solenoid opens and suction pressure rises. The LP switch then restarts the compressor.

This keeps liquid out of the crankcase during the off cycle. It also explains why a leaking solenoid valve can cause the compressor to short-cycle.

Defrost:

  • Frozen-room coolers ice up and need periodic defrosting. This is normally done with electric heaters on a timer, or with hot gas on some plants.
  • During defrost the liquid solenoid shuts and the fans stop. Heaters warm the coil and drip tray, and the melt water drains through a heated drain line.
  • After a delay, cooling resumes and the fans restart once the coil is cold again, so the fans do not blow water droplets into the room.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch or daily round:

  • Check room temperatures against set points and look at the recorder or log.
  • Note suction and discharge pressures and compare them with the normal values for the current sea water temperature.
  • Check the compressor oil level in the sight glass and the oil pressure.
  • Look at the liquid sight glass. It should run clear, with no continuous bubbles, and the moisture indicator should show 'dry'.
  • Look for frost or ice in the wrong places, such as heavy frost on the compressor body or a frosted liquid line.
  • Check the cooling water flow and condenser temperatures.
  • Look for oil stains on joints, which often mark a refrigerant leak.
  • Check that cold-room door seals, door heaters and drain heaters are working.

Periodic, per the planned maintenance system and maker's manual:

  • Clean condenser tubes. Fouling raises head pressure.
  • Inspect zinc anodes on sea-water-cooled condensers.
  • Leak test with an electronic detector or soap solution.
  • Change the filter drier core after any opening of the system or if moisture shows.
  • Overhaul the compressor: valves, shaft seal, and oil change.
  • Function test the HP and LP cut-outs and the oil pressure switch.
  • Check the TEV superheat setting and the room alarm function, including the personnel-trapped alarm.

Before an overhaul:

  • Pump the refrigerant down into the receiver or condenser rather than releasing it.
  • Isolate and lock out the electrical supply.
  • Confirm that the section is at zero pressure before opening it.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms
Undercharge / leak Bubbles in sight glass, low suction pressure, high superheat, long running hours, rooms slow to pull down, oil stains at leak point
Fouled condenser or poor cooling water High discharge pressure, high discharge temperature, HP cut-out trips
Air / non-condensables in system High discharge pressure even with a clean condenser and good water flow, condenser pressure above saturation pressure for the condensing temperature
Moisture in system Moisture indicator colour change, TEV freezing intermittently (ice choke), erratic suction pressure
Choked filter drier Temperature drop or frost across the drier, starved evaporator
Iced evaporator / defrost failure Poor air flow, room temperature rising, low suction pressure
Liquid floodback (TEV stuck open or superheat set too low) Frost on suction line up to compressor, cold crankcase, knocking, foaming oil
Leaking solenoid or compressor valves Short-cycling, failure to pump down, poor capacity
Oil logging in evaporators Falling crankcase oil level, low oil pressure trips

Worked scenario. On the 0400–0800 round you find the meat room at a few degrees above set point.

  • Suction pressure is low and the sight glass shows a stream of bubbles.
  • Discharge pressure is lower than normal for the sea temperature.
  • The compressor has run continuously since midnight.

This pattern points to a shortage of refrigerant.

  1. Report to the second engineer.
  2. Leak test the joints, starting where you see oil stains, then common leak points such as the shaft seal and flare connections.
  3. Repair the leak before recharging.
  4. Enter the refrigerant added in the records the company and flag require.

Adding gas without finding the leak is the classic mistake. It also counts as an avoidable emission.

Safety precautions and regulation

Refrigerant hazards:

  • Halocarbon refrigerants are heavier than air. A large leak in a small or low compartment can displace oxygen. Ventilate the space and treat it with enclosed-space caution if a big release is suspected.
  • Liquid refrigerant causes frostbite and eye injury. Wear goggles and gloves when charging or opening the system.
  • Halogenated refrigerants can decompose in flame or on hot surfaces into toxic and corrosive products. Do not do hot work on or near a charged system. Do not smoke where a leak is suspected.
  • Never heat a refrigerant cylinder with a flame. Do not overfill recovery cylinders.
  • Ammonia plants are rare for provisions but exist on some reefer and fishing ships. Ammonia is toxic and flammable, and needs its own precautions.

Cold-room hazards:

  • A person can be trapped inside a cold room. Rooms are normally fitted with an inside door release and a personnel-trapped alarm. Exact fit requirements depend on flag and class, so test both as the PMS requires.
  • Tell someone before entering a freezer for any length of time.
  • Watch for slippery, iced floors.

Regulation (MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 12):

  • Deliberate emissions of ozone-depleting substances are prohibited. This includes emissions during maintenance, servicing, repair or disposal. Minimal releases during recapture or recycling are allowed.
  • New installations containing ODS are prohibited. HCFCs such as R22 were permitted in new installations only until 1 January 2020.
  • Ships within the regulation's scope keep a list of equipment containing ODS.
  • If such ships have rechargeable systems containing ODS, they also keep an ODS record book. It records:
    • recharges
    • repairs
    • discharges, deliberate or not
    • landing of ODS to shore reception facilities
  • ODS removed from the ship goes to an appropriate reception facility.

HFCs are not ozone-depleting, but some flag states and regions (for example EU rules on fluorinated gases) impose their own leak-check, record and certification requirements. Follow your flag and company SMS.

Food and catering (MLC 2006, Regulation 3.2):

  • Food must be of appropriate quality, nutritional value and quantity.
  • The master, or someone working on the master's behalf, must carry out frequent, documented inspections of food stores and storage spaces. This includes the cold rooms.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why does the TEV control superheat and not evaporator pressure? So the evaporator is fully used while only vapour returns to the compressor. This protects the compressor from liquid slugging.

Q: Discharge pressure is high. What do you check? - Cooling water flow and temperature - Condenser fouling - Cooling water valves left shut - Air or non-condensables in the system - Overcharge

Q: Explain the pump-down cycle. The thermostat closes the solenoid. The compressor pumps refrigerant into the receiver, then the LP switch stops it. Rising suction pressure restarts it.

Q: Why is a crankcase heater fitted? To stop refrigerant migrating into and dissolving in the oil during standstill. Otherwise the oil foams and loses lubrication when the compressor starts.

Q: How do you know there is moisture in the system, and what does it cause? - Signs: the moisture indicator changes colour, and the TEV freezes intermittently. - Effects: acid formation, corrosion and motor or valve damage. - Remedy: change the drier and find the source.

Q: What records apply to refrigerant? - The ODS record book under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 12, where the plant contains ODS. - Any refrigerant log required by flag state, regional rules (e.g. EU F-gas rules) or the company SMS, which normally covers all refrigerants.

Key points
  • The provision plant is a vapour-compression cycle: compressor, condenser, receiver, drier, TEV, evaporator.
  • Rooms are controlled by thermostat and solenoid, usually with pump-down and an LP switch starting and stopping the compressor.
  • Bubbles in the sight glass with low suction pressure usually mean undercharge, so find the leak before recharging.
  • High head pressure points to the condenser side: cooling water, fouling or non-condensables.
  • Frost on the suction line up to the compressor warns of liquid floodback.
  • MARPOL Annex VI Reg 12 bans deliberate ODS emissions and requires an ODS record book for rechargeable ODS systems.
  • Test the cold-room inside door release and personnel-trapped alarm, and never do hot work on a charged system.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main components of a ship's provision refrigeration plant?

The compressor, condenser, liquid receiver, filter drier, sight glass, solenoid valves, thermostatic expansion valves and evaporator coolers in each room. Protective devices include the HP and LP cut-outs, oil pressure switch and crankcase heater. Larger plants with mixed-temperature rooms often add evaporator pressure regulators.

Why are there bubbles in the refrigeration sight glass?

Continuous bubbles usually mean the liquid line is not full of liquid. The most common cause is a shortage of refrigerant from a leak. A choked filter drier upstream or flash gas from a pressure drop can give a similar picture. Check suction pressure and superheat together before deciding.

Can I top up refrigerant without logging it?

No. If the plant contains an ozone-depleting substance such as R22, recharges must be entered in the ODS record book under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 12. For other refrigerants, flag state, regional rules and the company SMS normally require a refrigerant log as well. Find and repair the leak first.

Is R22 still allowed on ships?

Existing systems with R22 may continue in service. However, new installations containing HCFCs have been prohibited under MARPOL Annex VI since 1 January 2020, and deliberate emissions are banned. Some flags and regions restrict servicing further, so check your flag and company rules.

Why does the meat room cooler keep icing up?

Usual causes are failed defrost heaters or timers, a blocked or frozen drain line, faulty door seals, or doors left open letting in humid air. Poor fan operation or reduced air flow can make it worse. Check the defrost cycle actually runs and the drain heater works.

Test yourself

What does the thermostatic expansion valve primarily control?

The TEV meters refrigerant flow to hold a set superheat at the evaporator outlet. Room temperature is controlled by the thermostat and solenoid valve (plant design principle; maker's manual).

Discharge pressure is high, cooling water flow is good and the condenser was recently cleaned. What is a likely cause?

Non-condensables collect in the condenser and raise head pressure above the saturation pressure for the condensing temperature. Undercharge and an iced evaporator tend to lower pressures (refrigeration practice).

In a pump-down control system, what normally stops the compressor when all rooms are satisfied?

With all solenoids closed, the compressor pumps down and the LP switch stops it on falling suction pressure. The HP and oil switches are safety trips, not normal controls (plant design principle).

Under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 12, which statement about ozone-depleting substances is correct?

Regulation 12 prohibits deliberate ODS emissions, including during maintenance, and requires an ODS record book for rechargeable ODS systems. New HCFC installations were permitted only until 1 January 2020 (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg 12).

Heavy frost on the suction line up to the compressor and a knocking compressor most likely indicate:

Liquid returning to the compressor, often from a TEV stuck open or set with too little superheat, frosts the suction line and can damage valves. Shortage gives high superheat instead (refrigeration practice).
