Boil-off Gas Management on Gas Carriers

Quick answer

Boil-off gas (BOG) is cargo vapour produced continuously as heat leaks into refrigerated cargo tanks. If nothing removes it, tank pressure rises towards the relief valve settings. Under the IGC Code, cargo tank pressure and temperature must be controlled by approved means (unless the tanks are designed to withstand the full vapour pressure at the upper ambient design temperature), such as reliquefaction, burning in engines or boilers, or a gas combustion unit, and routine venting to atmosphere is not an accepted method.

What boil-off gas is and why it must be managed

LNG is carried at roughly -162°C, close to atmospheric pressure. No insulation is perfect, so heat from the sea, air and sun constantly enters the tanks. Some cargo boils as a result. This is natural boil-off gas (NBOG). Sloshing in a seaway, cargo pumps and spray cooling add to it.

If the vapour is not removed, tank pressure rises. Membrane and other low-pressure containment systems have small margins between normal operating pressure and the relief valve setting. Pressure control is therefore a continuous watchkeeping duty, not something you do now and then.

Why it matters:

  • Safety: relief valve lifts discharge flammable cargo vapour through the vent masts.
  • Containment: overpressure and underpressure both stress the containment system.
  • Commercial: BOG is cargo the charterer has paid for. On most LNG carriers it is also the main fuel, so managing it is closely tied to the fuel plan agreed for the voyage.

LPG carriers face the same physics, but the solution is different. Fully refrigerated and semi-refrigerated ships usually run a reliquefaction plant that compresses the vapour, condenses it and returns it to the tank. Fully pressurised ships with Type C tanks can often hold the pressure rise within the tank design for the voyage. Your ship's cargo manual is the authority for which method applies.

Main components of a BOG system

Layouts vary with the propulsion type and builder, but these items appear on most LNG carriers:

  • Vapour header and domes: collect vapour from each tank's vapour space.
  • High-duty (HD) compressors: large-volume compressors, mainly used to return vapour to shore during loading and for warming-up and gassing-up operations.
  • Low-duty (LD) or fuel gas compressors: send BOG to the engines or boilers at sea. Two-stroke high-pressure gas injection engines use multi-stage high-pressure compressors or a high-pressure pump-and-vaporiser train instead.
  • BOG heater: warms the gas before it enters the engine room so that cold gas does not reach machinery-space piping and burners.
  • Forcing vaporiser: turns LNG into extra fuel gas (forced boil-off, FBOG) when the natural boil-off cannot meet the fuel demand.
  • LNG vaporiser: produces vapour for operations such as gassing-up.
  • Mist separator and spray (desuperheating) arrangements: remove liquid droplets and control compressor suction temperature.
  • Gas combustion unit (GCU): burns surplus BOG safely when the engines cannot use it.
  • Reliquefaction plant: fitted on some ships. It may be a closed nitrogen (Brayton) cycle or a partial reliquefaction system using the BOG's own cold.
  • Consumers: dual-fuel diesel generators, dual-fuel two-stroke main engines, or steam boilers on turbine ships.
  • Safety layer: gas valve unit, master gas valve, double-walled or ducted gas piping in machinery spaces with ventilation and gas detection, and the ESD system.

How it works: laden passage step by step

A typical sequence on a dual-fuel ship at sea is below. Always follow your cargo and engine operating manuals for the actual valve line-up and set points.

  1. Pressure control mode selected. The cargo control room selects a mode, for example fuel gas with GCU backup. The automation then controls tank pressure by adjusting how much BOG is drawn off.
  2. Vapour drawn from the header. The LD or fuel gas compressor takes suction from the vapour header. Spray or desuperheating may be used to hold suction temperature within the compressor's range.
  3. Compression and heating. The gas is compressed and warmed in the BOG heater to the temperature the consumer requires.
  4. Delivery to the engine room. The gas passes the master gas valve and gas valve unit into the double-walled or ventilated supply line, then to the engines or boilers.
  5. Balancing supply and demand:
    • If the engines need more gas than the natural boil-off provides, the forcing vaporiser makes up the shortfall.
    • If the engines need less, the surplus goes to the GCU or the reliquefaction plant. On steam ships, the boilers burn the gas and excess steam is dumped to the condenser.
  6. Monitoring. The cargo officer of the watch trends tank pressures, temperatures, compressor parameters and gas consumption, and logs them as the company procedures require.

Worked scenario: generator trip while laden. A gas-burning generator trips and the remaining engines cannot take the full BOG flow. Tank pressure starts climbing.

  • The control system should automatically divert surplus gas to the GCU, or increase reliquefaction load.
  • The CCR officer checks that the GCU has actually started and is accepting gas, not just that it was commanded.
  • The officer monitors the pressure trend against alarm and relief settings and informs the chief officer and the engine room.
  • If the GCU also fails, the options in the manual may include stopping any forced vaporisation, increasing gas consumption on running consumers (for example raising engine or generator load on gas, or starting another dual-fuel generator in gas mode), restarting tripped consumers on gas once they are recovered, and allowing tank pressure to accumulate within approved limits.
  • Relief valve lifting is the last line of defence. It is not a planned outcome.

Ballast passage, heel and arrival cooldown

After discharge, a quantity of LNG called the heel is usually retained. It provides fuel and keeps the tanks cold during the ballast leg. The heel quantity is planned against voyage length, fuel consumption and the loading terminal's arrival temperature requirements.

Before arrival at the loading port, tanks may be cooled with the spray pumps through the spray lines. This brings tank temperatures within the terminal's acceptance criteria and avoids excessive vapour generation when loading starts.

During loading, the displaced vapour and flash gas are normally returned to shore by the HD compressors. Some may also be burned or reliquefied, depending on the terminal's arrangements. Poor cooldown means more vapour, slower loading and possible delays, so arrival condition is a real commercial concern.

Routine checks and maintenance

Exact intervals come from the planned maintenance system and makers' manuals. Typical items include:

  • Pressure and temperature trends: compare tank pressure, vapour temperature and BOG flow against previous days. A changing boil-off rate in steady conditions deserves investigation.
  • Compressors:
    • lube oil and seal gas systems
    • vibration and bearing temperatures
    • anti-surge valve function
    • inlet guide vane or speed control response
  • BOG heater and vaporisers: check outlet temperatures. Check heating medium drains or condensate for gas contamination, which indicates a tube leak.
  • Mist separator and drains: check for liquid accumulation and test level alarms.
  • GCU: burner and ignition checks, fan and dilution air, and flame scanner function. Periodically prove that it will accept gas.
  • Gas detection: calibrate fixed detectors and test the alarms and automatic shutdowns of the master gas valve and gas valve unit.
  • Double-wall and duct ventilation: check fan running and the low-flow alarm.
  • ESD and trip testing: as required by the cargo manual and terminal requirements before cargo operations.
  • Nitrogen system: check purity and supply pressure, because nitrogen is used for purging gas lines and seal gas.

Safety precautions and regulatory framework

  • Flammability: methane is flammable between roughly 5% and 15% by volume in air. Treat every BOG system leak as a potential fire.
  • Cold vapour behaviour: very cold methane vapour is denser than air and spreads at low level until it warms up. Do not assume it will rise away from you.
  • Cryogenic hazards: contact with cold liquid or uninsulated pipework causes cold burns. Brittle fracture of ordinary steel is a risk if LNG spills onto the deck, so water curtains and drip trays must be ready during cargo operations.
  • Purging and isolation: gas lines must be purged with nitrogen and proven gas-free before maintenance. Apply permit to work and isolation procedures.
  • Never defeat trips: bypassing gas detection or master gas valve shutdowns to keep an engine on gas is a serious breach.

Regulatory framework, in outline:

  • SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C makes the IGC Code mandatory for gas carriers.
  • IGC Code Chapter 7 covers cargo pressure and temperature control. It accepts methods such as reliquefaction, thermal oxidation (including use as fuel), pressure accumulation and cargo cooling. It treats venting to atmosphere as an emergency measure only.
  • IGC Code Chapter 8 covers vent systems and relief valves.
  • IGC Code Chapter 16 governs use of cargo as fuel on gas carriers. Gas-fuelled ships that are not gas carriers fall under the IGF Code instead.
  • STCW Section A-V/1-2 sets the liquefied gas tanker training required for crew.

Company and flag requirements may add to these, so check your SMS.

Oral exam questions to prepare

Examiners like to test understanding, not just recall. Be ready for questions such as:

  • Why does boil-off occur and what increases it? Heat ingress through insulation, plus sloshing, pump heat and spray operations.
  • Difference between natural and forced boil-off? NBOG comes from heat leaking into the tanks. FBOG is deliberately produced in the forcing vaporiser to meet fuel demand.
  • Why is BOG heated before the engine room? To protect machinery-space piping and consumers from cryogenic temperatures and to meet the consumer's inlet requirements.
  • What happens if gas is detected in the double-wall pipe ventilation? Automatic closure of the master gas valve and changeover to fuel oil. The cause must be found and the line purged before gas is reintroduced.
  • Why keep a heel on the ballast passage? To provide fuel and keep tanks cold for arrival at the loading port.
  • Is venting cargo to atmosphere an acceptable way to control pressure? Not routinely. Relief valves are an emergency safeguard, and the IGC Code requires an approved pressure control method.
  • Explain compressor surge and its protection. Surge is flow reversal at low flow and high pressure ratio. It is prevented by an anti-surge recycle valve controlled from flow and pressure measurement.
Key points
  • Boil-off is continuous: heat ingress always vaporises some cargo, so tank pressure must be actively controlled.
  • On LNG carriers, BOG is usually burned as fuel, with a GCU or reliquefaction plant handling any surplus.
  • The forcing vaporiser makes extra fuel gas when natural boil-off is not enough.
  • BOG is heated before entering the engine room, and gas piping there is double-walled or ducted with gas detection.
  • Gas detection in machinery spaces automatically closes the master gas valve and changes engines to fuel oil.
  • The heel is retained after discharge to keep tanks cold and provide fuel on the ballast passage.
  • IGC Code Chapter 7 requires approved pressure control; venting to atmosphere is for emergencies only.

Frequently asked questions

What is boil-off gas on an LNG carrier?

It is cargo vapour produced as heat leaks through the insulation into LNG stored at around -162°C. It forms continuously throughout the voyage. If it is not removed by burning, reliquefying or using it as fuel, tank pressure rises.

What is the difference between NBOG and FBOG?

NBOG (natural boil-off gas) is generated naturally by heat ingress and cargo motion. FBOG (forced boil-off gas) is produced deliberately by vaporising LNG in a forcing vaporiser when the engines need more gas than the natural boil-off can supply.

What does a gas combustion unit (GCU) do?

A GCU is a dedicated burner that safely oxidises surplus BOG when the engines or boilers cannot use it all, for example during low speed or after a consumer trips. It keeps tank pressure under control without venting cargo to atmosphere.

Can an LNG carrier vent boil-off gas to atmosphere?

Routine venting is not an accepted means of pressure control under the IGC Code. Relief valves and vent masts exist as an emergency safeguard against overpressure. Port and terminal rules may impose further restrictions.

Which training do I need to work with BOG systems?

Crew with cargo-related duties on gas carriers need liquefied gas tanker training under STCW Section A-V/1-2: basic training for officers and ratings with specific duties related to cargo or cargo equipment, and advanced training for officers with immediate responsibility for cargo operations. Companies usually add type-specific familiarisation on the ship's own BOG and fuel gas plant.

Test yourself

What is the main cause of natural boil-off in an LNG cargo tank at sea?

No insulation is perfect, so heat from the surroundings continuously vaporises some cargo. Sloshing and pump heat add to this.

The engines need more fuel gas than the natural boil-off supplies. Which equipment makes up the difference?

The forcing vaporiser converts LNG into additional fuel gas (FBOG). The GCU destroys surplus gas rather than producing it.

Gas is detected in the ventilated annular space of the double-walled fuel gas pipe in the engine room. What should happen?

Gas detection in machinery-space gas piping triggers automatic shutdown of the gas supply. The cause must be found before gas is reintroduced (IGC Code, Chapter 16).

Under the IGC Code, how is routine venting of cargo vapour to atmosphere treated as a method of tank pressure control?

IGC Code Chapter 7 requires approved means such as reliquefaction, thermal oxidation or use as fuel. Venting is reserved for emergencies.

Why is a heel of LNG normally retained after discharge?

The heel supplies fuel and keeps the containment cold so the ship arrives within the loading terminal's temperature requirements. It is a cargo and voyage planning measure, not a MARPOL requirement.
