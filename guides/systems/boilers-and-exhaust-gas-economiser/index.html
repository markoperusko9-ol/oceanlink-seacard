Boilers and Exhaust Gas Economiser on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

Most motor ships carry an oil-fired auxiliary boiler for steam in port and an exhaust gas economiser that makes steam at sea from main engine exhaust heat. Together they supply steam for fuel heating, tank heating, purifiers and accommodation. Running them safely comes down to three things: water level, water chemistry and clean, well-managed combustion.

What the boiler plant does and why it matters

On a typical motor ship, steam is a utility. It heats heavy fuel in settling and service tanks, warms fuel before the purifiers and the main engine, heats cargo on many tankers, and supplies the accommodation and galley. On some ships it also drives cargo pumps or a turbo-generator.

There are usually two sources of steam:

  • Oil-fired auxiliary boiler: used in port, at anchor and at low engine loads.
  • Exhaust gas economiser (EGE), also called an exhaust gas boiler: sits in the main engine uptake and recovers waste heat while at sea.

Some ships have a composite boiler, where one pressure vessel has both an oil-fired section and an exhaust gas section. At steady sea speed the economiser alone often meets demand, so the oil-fired burner stays off. As engine load drops, the burner cuts in automatically to hold steam pressure.

If steam is lost, heavy fuel cools and thickens, purifiers lose separation, and the main engine can be starved of properly heated fuel. That is why boiler problems quickly become propulsion problems.

Main components

Auxiliary boiler (water-tube or fire-tube/smoke-tube type)

  • Furnace and pressure parts: the steam drum, plus water drum and tubes on water-tube types.
  • Burner unit: pressure-jet, rotary cup or steam/air-atomising type, depending on the maker.
  • Forced draught (FD) fan and air register.
  • Ignition: igniter electrodes and often a diesel pilot burner.
  • Flame scanner (flame eye), which proves the flame is present.
  • Fuel solenoid or quick-closing valves, a fuel pump and a fuel heater.
  • Two independent water level indicators, usually gauge glasses, often with a remote level transmitter.
  • Safety valves fitted with easing gear.
  • Main steam stop valve, feed check valve and feed stop valve.
  • Scum (surface) blowdown and bottom blowdown valves.
  • Low-low water level trip, high and low level alarms, and high pressure switches.
  • Sampling cooler and chemical dosing point.

Feed system

  • Hot well or cascade tank. Most systems include an observation section where returning drains can be checked for oil.
  • Feed pumps, normally duplicated.
  • Atmospheric or dump condenser, plus drain coolers.

Exhaust gas economiser

  • Tube banks in the engine uptake. These are often finned or pinned to increase heat transfer.
  • Forced-circulation pumps, which draw water from the boiler drum, pass it through the economiser and return a steam/water mix to the drum.
  • Soot blowers and water-washing connections.
  • Gas temperature sensors at inlet and outlet. Many installations also have fire or high-temperature alarms.
  • Exhaust bypass dampers on some installations.

How it works, step by step

Oil-fired boiler automatic firing sequence (typical)

  1. Steam pressure falls to the cut-in setting, or the burner is started manually.
  2. Interlocks are checked: water level normal, fuel temperature and pressure correct, FD fan running, air damper proven.
  3. Pre-purge: the fan runs with the damper open to clear any fuel vapour from the furnace. The duration is set by the maker.
  4. The igniter and pilot light. The flame scanner must prove the pilot flame.
  5. The main fuel valve opens. The scanner must prove the main flame within the safety time, or the burner locks out.
  6. The burner modulates, or steps high and low, to hold pressure.
  7. At cut-out pressure the fuel shuts off, followed by a post-purge.

Feed and condensate loop

Steam goes to consumers, condenses, and returns through drain coolers to the observation tank and hot well. Feed pumps then return the water to the boiler, with the flow controlled by the level control.

Economiser loop

The circulating pump forces boiler water through the economiser tubes. Exhaust heat produces a steam/water mixture, which goes back to the drum where the steam separates. The circulating pump should keep running while the engine is running, and for a period after it stops, so the tubes are never dry and hot.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch or daily round

  • Check steam pressure and water level on both indicators, and compare them with the remote reading.
  • Look at the flame through the sight glass. A good flame is stable, bright and not smoky. Check the funnel for smoke.
  • Check fuel temperature and pressure at the burner, and look for leaks around the burner.
  • Confirm the economiser circulating pump is running. Check gas inlet and outlet temperatures and the gas-side pressure drop if it is monitored.
  • Inspect the observation tank for oil, which indicates a leaking heating coil.
  • Check hot well level and temperature, and how much make-up water you are using.

Regular tasks (intervals per company PMS and maker)

  • Boiler water tests, typically pH or alkalinity, chlorides, phosphate or other treatment reserve, hardness and conductivity. Dose chemicals according to the supplier's control limits. Do not use limits from another ship.
  • Gauge glass blowdown, following the maker's sequence of cocks. The level should return quickly. A sluggish return suggests a choked passage.
  • Scum and bottom blowdown, as the water tests dictate.
  • Soot blowing of the economiser at sea, at the frequency set by the SMS, usually more often during low-load or slow steaming.
  • Water washing of the economiser in port with the engine stopped. Drain the wash water properly, then dry the tubes out.
  • Burner maintenance: clean the nozzle or rotary cup, set the electrode gap and clean the flame scanner.
  • Function tests of the low water trip, flame failure trip and alarms.

Statutory and class items

Safety valves are set and sealed under survey. Internal and external boiler surveys follow class rules and the flag state's rules, and so do hydraulic tests after repairs. Your company PMS will reflect those dates.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms Usual causes
Flame failure lockout Burner trips, alarm Dirty scanner or nozzle, water in fuel, wrong fuel temperature, igniter fault
Black smoke Dark funnel, sooting Too little air, poor atomisation, worn nozzle, cold fuel
Low water level Low and low-low alarm, burner trip Feed pump failure, level control fault, leaking tubes, large steam demand
Priming / carry-over Water in steam lines, water hammer, level fluctuating High dissolved solids, oil contamination, level too high, sudden steam demand
Rising chlorides Chloride test climbing Seawater ingress, usually at the condenser or drain cooler, or contaminated make-up water
Oil in feed system Oil film in the observation tank, foaming Leaking fuel or cargo tank heating coil, or a leaking fuel heater
Economiser fouling Rising gas outlet temperature, rising pressure drop, falling steam output Soot build-up at low load, missed soot blowing
Economiser soot fire Gas outlet temperature rising sharply, sparks or smoke at the funnel, fire alarm Ignition of oily soot deposits, often after low-load running

Furnace explosion risk comes from fuel entering a hot furnace without a proper purge. Typical causes are a leaking fuel valve, repeated relight attempts, or bypassed interlocks. Never bypass the purge or the flame scanner to get the boiler going.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1: Low-low water trip at 0300

The burner has tripped and the gauge glass shows no water.

  1. Do not pump cold feed into a boiler whose level you cannot see. Hot, uncovered tubes or furnace surfaces may be overheated, and sudden cold feed can damage them.
  2. Confirm the fuel is off and keep it off. Inform the Chief Engineer and the bridge, because heating and fuel supply will be affected.
  3. Blow down the gauge glass to confirm whether the reading is true or a choked glass.
  4. If the level really was lost, let the boiler cool. Find the cause: feed pump, level control or tube leak. The Chief Engineer decides whether to inspect before refiring.
  5. If the level is only just out of sight and was lost briefly, follow the company and maker's procedure. Experience and the SMS govern this. Do not guess.

Scenario 2: Economiser outlet temperature climbing at sea

  1. Treat it as a possible soot fire. Inform the Chief Engineer and the bridge.
  2. Keep the circulating pump running. Never stop circulation.
  3. Reduce engine load or stop the engine in consultation with the bridge, as navigation allows. This removes the oxygen and heat supply.
  4. Do not soot blow with air into a fire. Firefighting with water uses the installed system according to the maker's instructions. With a very hot fire, a small amount of water can make things worse, so the SMS procedure matters.
  5. Boundary cool the uptake if required, and keep watch until temperatures fall.

Safety precautions and regulations

Working on the plant

  • Boiler internal entry is an enclosed space entry. You need a permit, ventilation, gas testing and a standby person.
  • Before entry, isolate and lock out the steam, feed, blowdown and fuel valves, and the burner electrics. Confirm the boiler is depressurised and drained, and that the vents are open.
  • Remember that steam leaks can be invisible. Wear proper PPE and never open a flange on a line that has not been proven depressurised.
  • When raising steam from cold, open the air vents and warm through slowly, following the maker's instructions.

Regulations (chapter level)

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C covers steam boilers and feed systems. This includes safety valves, at least two means of water level indication (at least one a direct-reading gauge glass), and feed water quality control. For oil-fired boilers intended to operate without manual supervision, it also requires automatic fuel shut-off with alarm on low water level, air supply failure or flame failure. Part E adds requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2 covers fire safety in machinery spaces, including oil fuel arrangements.
  • MARPOL Annex VI fuel sulphur limits apply to fuel burned in boilers as well as engines. Changeover procedures in emission control areas therefore include the boiler.
  • Design, construction and survey intervals are mainly set by the classification society and the flag state, so the exact details vary.

Key points
  • The oil-fired auxiliary boiler covers port and low-load steam; the exhaust gas economiser recovers main engine heat at sea.
  • Never pump cold feed into a boiler whose water level has been lost out of sight; shut the fuel off and let it cool.
  • Pre-purge and flame-proving interlocks prevent furnace explosions, so never bypass them.
  • Boiler water chemistry follows the chemical supplier's limits; rising chlorides mean seawater ingress.
  • Oil in the observation tank usually points to a leaking heating coil and risks foaming and priming.
  • Soot builds up fastest at low load; regular soot blowing and washing prevent economiser fires.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 Part C sets the core boiler safety requirements; class and flag set the survey details.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an exhaust gas economiser and an auxiliary boiler?

The auxiliary boiler burns fuel in its own furnace to make steam. The economiser has no burner and recovers heat from main engine exhaust gas. On many ships the economiser sends its steam/water mixture back to the auxiliary boiler drum, so the two work as one steam system.

Why does an economiser soot fire happen?

Unburnt fuel and lube oil residues from the engine collect as soot on the economiser tubes, especially during low-load running and slow steaming. With enough heat and oxygen these deposits can ignite. Good engine combustion, regular soot blowing and keeping circulation running are the main defences.

Which boiler water tests are done on board?

Common tests include alkalinity or pH, chlorides, the treatment chemical reserve (often phosphate), hardness and conductivity. Some systems also test condensate. The exact tests and control limits depend on your chemical supplier and the boiler maker, so use the ship's test kit instructions.

Who is responsible for the boilers in the engine room?

The Chief Engineer has overall responsibility. Day-to-day care is commonly given to one engineer, often the 3rd Engineer, but this depends on company and ship. Your SMS and the Chief Engineer's standing orders define who does what.

How often are boiler safety valves checked?

Safety valves are set and sealed under survey. Survey intervals follow classification society and flag state rules, which differ. On board, the easing gear and function checks follow the company planned maintenance system.

Test yourself

The water level is out of sight in both gauge glasses and the burner has tripped. What is the correct first action?

Adding cold feed to possibly overheated surfaces can cause serious damage, so the fuel stays off while the level is verified. SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C requires oil-fired boilers intended to operate without manual supervision to shut off fuel automatically and alarm on low water level.

What is the main purpose of the pre-purge in a boiler firing sequence?

Pre-purging removes any accumulated fuel vapour that could cause a furnace explosion on ignition. The duration is set by the burner maker.

During a suspected economiser soot fire, what should be done with the circulating pump?

Stopping circulation leaves hot tubes without water and greatly increases the risk of tube damage and fire escalation. Follow the maker's and SMS procedures.

A steadily rising chloride reading in the boiler water most likely indicates:

Chlorides come mainly from seawater, typically through a leaking condenser or drain cooler. Oil contamination shows in the observation tank rather than in the chloride test.

Why must the auxiliary boiler be included in fuel changeover planning before entering an emission control area?

The fuel sulphur limits in MARPOL Annex VI cover fuel oil used on board, not just main engine fuel. NOx limits apply to diesel engines, not boilers.
