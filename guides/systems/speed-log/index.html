Speed Log on Merchant Ships: How It Works, Checks, Faults and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

The speed log measures the ship's speed and distance run, normally through the water and, on Doppler logs, over the ground as well. It feeds radar/ARPA, ECDIS and the VDR, and on some installations the gyrocompass, so a wrong log reading spreads errors across the bridge. Know which type you have, which mode it is in, how to check it against GNSS, and how to withdraw the sensor safely.

What the speed log does and why it matters

The speed log gives the officer of the watch speed and distance travelled. Two quantities matter, and candidates often mix them up:

  • Speed through the water (STW) is the ship's movement relative to the water around her. This is what the hull and engine actually produce, and it is what counts for collision avoidance.
  • Speed over the ground (SOG) is the movement relative to the seabed. It is what you need for navigation, ETA and berthing.

The difference between them is current and tidal stream. GNSS gives you SOG. A single-axis electromagnetic log gives you STW. A Doppler log can give either, depending on its tracking mode.

The log output does not stay on its own display. Depending on the bridge configuration it may feed:

  • Radar/ARPA, for sea-stabilised presentation and true vectors.
  • The gyrocompass, for speed error correction. Some installations use the log for this, while others take GNSS speed.
  • ECDIS and the conning display.
  • The VDR, which records speed as one of its data items.

A log that over-reads by one knot does more than give you a wrong ETA. It can corrupt your ARPA target aspects in a crossing situation.

Types of log and how each works

Electromagnetic (EM) log. This is the most common type on merchant ships. It works on Faraday's law of induction.

  • A coil in the sensor sets up a magnetic field in the water next to the hull.
  • Seawater is a conductor. When it flows past the sensor, a small voltage is induced between two electrodes.
  • That voltage is proportional to the water speed past the sensor, and the electronics convert it to knots.
  • An EM log measures STW. Most fitted units are single-axis (fore-and-aft).

Doppler log. This is an acoustic log that uses the frequency shift of reflected sound.

  • Transducers send sound pulses at an angle down and forward, and usually also aft. The forward-and-aft (Janus) arrangement reduces errors caused by pitch and trim.
  • The returning echo is shifted in frequency in proportion to the ship's speed along the beam.
  • Bottom-track mode uses echoes from the seabed and gives SOG. It only works within the equipment's depth range.
  • Water-track mode uses echoes from a water layer below the hull and gives STW. In deep water the log falls back to this mode.
  • Dual-axis Doppler logs show fore-and-aft and athwartships speed. This is valuable when berthing large ships, and some installations also show transverse speed at the bow and stern.

Pitot (pressure) log. This is an older type. A tube projecting below the hull senses dynamic pressure, which is compared with static pressure. The difference is proportional to the square of speed, so the mechanism extracts the square root. Some older tonnage still carries them.

Others. You may also meet acoustic correlation logs, impeller logs and the traditional towed patent log. The towed log is now mostly a training curiosity.

Main components and where they sit

Layouts vary by maker, but a typical installation has four parts.

Component Function Typical location
Sensor / transducer Measures water flow (EM) or transmits and receives sound (Doppler) Bottom plating, usually forward, often in a log trunk or void space
Sea valve / gate valve and housing Lets a retractable sensor be withdrawn without drydocking Same compartment as the sensor
Electronics / processing unit Signal processing, calibration settings, output generation Near the sensor or in the bridge equipment room
Display and distribution unit Shows speed and distance, sends data to other equipment Bridge

Sensor fitting. Retractable EM sensors sit in a tube through the hull with a valve below them. Flush-mounted sensors and most Doppler transducers are fixed. They can only be worked on in drydock or by divers.

Outputs. Data normally goes out as NMEA 0183 sentences, or the equivalent on a networked bridge:

  • VHW carries water speed and heading.
  • VBW carries dual ground and water speed.
  • VLW carries distance travelled.

If the ARPA or ECDIS shows no log input, the distribution unit and the cabling are among the first things to check.

Routine checks and calibration

Each watch (OOW):

  • Compare log speed with GNSS SOG. A steady difference is expected where current is running. A sudden jump, or a difference that makes no sense against the known set, is not.
  • On a Doppler log, check the mode indicator (bottom or water track). A change of mode on the shelf edge changes what the number means.
  • Confirm that the radar/ARPA speed source is the one you intend: log, GNSS or manual.

Periodic checks (per company planned maintenance):

  • Check that display lamps, alarms and the distance counter work.
  • Check the cable glands and the housing in the sensor compartment for leakage.
  • Confirm the calibration settings recorded in the electronics unit match the last calibration record.

Worked scenario: checking calibration with reciprocal runs. Use a steady sea, constant engine setting and a current running along the track.

  1. Run on course 000°. GNSS SOG is 15.0 kn and the log reads 14.4 kn.
  2. Turn onto the reciprocal, 180°, and settle at the same engine setting. GNSS SOG is 13.0 kn and the log reads 14.4 kn.
  3. Take the mean of the two SOG figures: (15.0 + 13.0) / 2 = 14.0 kn. If the current stays constant, it cancels out, so 14.0 kn is your best estimate of true STW.
  4. The log reads 14.4 kn, so it is over-reading by 0.4 kn, about 3%.
  5. Report to the Master. The correction is entered following the maker's procedure, often by the ETO or a service engineer. Record it.

Formal calibration is usually done on sea trials and after sensor renewal. Where the procedure and the authority to change settings are laid down by the company SMS or the maker, follow those.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause What to do
EM log gradually under-reads over months Marine growth or fouling on the sensor face, or sensor not fully extended Compare with GNSS on reciprocal runs; arrange cleaning or check the extension
Erratic or zero reading in heavy weather or light ballast Aeration under the hull (bubbles); Doppler loses its echo Expect it; switch ARPA to another speed source and note it in the log book
Doppler suddenly shows a different speed near the shelf edge Change between bottom-track and water-track mode Check the mode indicator before trusting the figure
Steady offset at all speeds Calibration error or wrong setting after a service Recalibrate and check the settings record
Reading changes with trim or draught Boundary-layer and flow effects at the sensor Known limitation; compare under different loading conditions
Radar shows no log speed Distribution unit fault, cabling, or source deselected in settings Check the input selection, then the connections
Water in the log compartment Gland or valve leakage Report at once; treat as a hull integrity issue

One pattern is worth remembering. Fouling makes an EM log read low, because the water right at the sensor is slowed by the rough surface. If speed through the water appears to have dropped but fuel consumption and RPM are unchanged, suspect the sensor before the hull.

Safety precautions

Withdrawing a retractable sensor at sea or in port. This means opening a hole in the bottom plating, so plan it with the same care as any hull work.

  1. Obtain permission and a permit to work as the SMS requires. Inform the bridge and the engine room.
  2. If the compartment is a void, trunk or forepeak space, apply the enclosed space entry procedure. That means testing the atmosphere, ventilating and having a standby person in place.
  3. Read the maker's instructions. Have the blank flange or cover and the tools ready before you start.
  4. Withdraw the sensor fully past the valve, then close the gate valve before removing the sensor from its housing.
  5. Never force a sensor or valve that binds. Stop and report.
  6. Refit in the reverse order. Open the valve only once the housing is secured, then check for leaks.

In drydock:

  • Retract or protect the sensors before blasting and painting.
  • Do not paint EM sensor electrodes or Doppler transducer faces unless the maker's instructions allow it. Paint and antifouling can degrade or kill the signal.
  • Do not power up acoustic transducers out of the water unless the maker permits it.

Electrical. Isolate the equipment before working on the electronics, following lock-out tag-out procedures.

Regulations and performance standards

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 sets the carriage requirements. In summary:

  • Ships of 300 gross tonnage and upwards, and all passenger ships: must have a speed and distance measuring device, or other means, to indicate speed and distance through the water.
  • Ships of 50,000 gross tonnage and upwards: must additionally have a device, or other means, indicating speed and distance over the ground in the fore-and-aft and athwartships directions. In practice this is usually a dual-axis Doppler log.

The equipment must meet the IMO performance standards for speed and distance measuring equipment and be type-approved. Your flag administration may add its own requirements or interpretations, so check the ship's Safety Equipment Certificate record and the flag's instructions. Port State Control officers do check that the log works and feeds the equipment it should.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why should ARPA use speed through the water for collision avoidance? A target's aspect, and therefore the COLREG situation, depends on its heading through the water. A ground-stabilised display in a strong current can make a crossing vessel look as if she is on a different heading from her real one.

Q: What is the principle of the EM log? Faraday's law. Seawater, a conductor, moves through a magnetic field set up by the sensor. This induces a voltage across the electrodes that is proportional to speed.

Q: Your Doppler log reading jumps by 1.5 kn as you leave the continental shelf. Why? It has probably switched from bottom track (SOG) to water track (STW). The difference between the two readings is the current.

Q: How would you check the log's accuracy at sea? Run reciprocal courses at a constant engine setting and compare with GNSS. The mean SOG cancels a steady current. A measured mile can be used where one is available.

Q: Which other equipment is affected if the log fails? Radar/ARPA true vectors and sea stabilisation, ECDIS, and the speed data recorded by the VDR. It also affects gyro speed error correction where the gyro takes its speed from the log. Switch to an alternative or manual input and log the failure.

Key points
  • Speed through the water (STW) and speed over the ground (SOG) differ by the current; know which one your log is showing.
  • The EM log works on Faraday's law and measures STW; it under-reads when the sensor is fouled.
  • The Doppler log measures SOG in bottom-track mode and STW in water-track mode, so always check the mode indicator.
  • The log feeds ARPA, ECDIS, the VDR and, on some installations, gyro speed correction, so one error spreads across the bridge.
  • Reciprocal runs compared against GNSS cancel a steady current and reveal log error.
  • Withdrawing a sensor means a hole in the hull: use a permit, follow the maker's procedure, and use enclosed space precautions where needed.
  • SOLAS V/19 requires a log through the water on ships of 300 GT and over and all passenger ships; ships of 50,000 GT and over also need fore-and-aft and athwartships speed over the ground.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a speed log and GPS speed?

GNSS (GPS) gives speed over the ground. A speed log normally gives speed through the water, and a Doppler log can give either. The difference between the two is the set and rate of current.

Which speed should be used on radar for collision avoidance?

Speed through the water, with a sea-stabilised presentation, gives the target's true heading through the water and so its correct aspect. Ground stabilisation suits navigation and parallel indexing. Company procedures may set out how the input is selected.

Why does my EM log read lower than expected?

The usual causes are marine growth on the sensor face, or a retractable sensor that is not fully extended. Calibration error and changes in trim or draught can also play a part. Confirm with reciprocal runs against GNSS before blaming the hull or the engine.

Is a Doppler log mandatory on all ships?

No. SOLAS V/19 requires ships of 50,000 GT and upwards to have speed and distance over the ground in both the fore-and-aft and athwartships directions, and a dual-axis Doppler log is the usual way to meet this. Ships of 300 GT and upwards and all passenger ships need speed and distance through the water, which an EM log usually provides.

Can the speed log sensor be removed at sea?

Retractable types can, using the gate valve, provided you follow the maker's instructions and the company permit system. Fixed or flush-mounted sensors need drydock or divers. Always close the valve before removing the sensor from its housing.
