Emergency Generator on Merchant Ships: Purpose, Operation, Checks and Faults

Quick answer

The emergency generator is an independent diesel-driven set, located above the uppermost continuous deck and away from the main machinery spaces, that starts automatically on loss of main power and feeds the emergency switchboard. Under SOLAS Chapter II-1 it must take its load within a maximum of 45 seconds and run essential services for at least 18 hours on cargo ships or 36 hours on passenger ships. Your job is to keep it in auto, fuelled, warm, ventilated and proven by regular blackout tests.

What the emergency generator is for

When the main generators trip and the ship blacks out, you lose propulsion, steering, lighting and most of your alarms within seconds. The emergency generator keeps power on the services needed to keep people alive, raise the alarm and abandon ship.

SOLAS Chapter II-1 covers this in Regulation 42 for passenger ships and Regulation 43 for cargo ships. In outline, the emergency source must be self-contained, with its own fuel, cooling and starting arrangements. It must also be:

  • located above the uppermost continuous deck
  • arranged so that a fire or other casualty in the main generating space or category A machinery spaces cannot affect it (as far as practicable, not contiguous with category A machinery space boundaries)
  • readily accessible from the open deck
  • not forward of the collision bulkhead (on cargo ships the Administration may permit otherwise in exceptional circumstances)

The aim is that a fire or flood that takes out the engine room does not also take out the emergency power.

On a cargo ship the emergency generator typically supplies the following, for the period the regulation specifies (18 hours for most of them):

  • emergency lighting at muster and embarkation stations, alleyways, stairways, machinery spaces and control stations
  • navigation lights
  • radio equipment
  • the emergency fire pump
  • fire detection and alarm
  • general alarm, whistle, daylight signalling lamp and internal communications
  • required navigational equipment
  • in many designs, one steering gear power unit

The exact load list for your ship is on the emergency switchboard drawings. Know where they are kept.

Main components

Layouts differ by builder, but most sets you will meet have these parts:

Component What it does
Prime mover Usually a high-speed four-stroke diesel, often radiator-cooled so it needs no sea water
Alternator with AVR Produces the supply; the automatic voltage regulator holds the voltage steady
Starting system Electric starter with battery banks, a hydraulic accumulator, compressed air or a spring starter
Battery chargers Keep the starting batteries topped up from the emergency switchboard
Jacket water preheater Keeps the engine warm so it starts and loads quickly
Service fuel tank Gravity-fed day tank in the emergency generator room, with a gauge and drain
Ventilation louvres / dampers Supply combustion and cooling air; often closable for fire-fighting
Emergency switchboard (ESB) Distributes emergency power; located as near as practicable to the generator
Interconnector (bus-tie) breaker Feeds the ESB from the main switchboard in normal service
Control panel Auto/manual/off selector, start/stop, alarms, test switch

Passenger ships must always have a transitional source of emergency power, usually a battery bank. Cargo ships need one if the generator is not arranged to take load within the time limit. The transitional source bridges the gap until the generator comes on.

How it works: the blackout sequence step by step

In normal service the ESB is fed from the main switchboard through the interconnector. The emergency generator sits in AUTO, warm and stopped.

  1. Main power fails. Voltage on the main bus, and therefore on the ESB, drops away.
  2. Detection. An undervoltage relay on the ESB senses the loss. After a short time delay that filters out momentary dips, it sends a start signal.
  3. Interconnector opens. The bus-tie breaker on the ESB opens automatically. This isolates the emergency bus from the dead main switchboard, so the emergency generator cannot try to feed the whole ship.
  4. Engine cranks and fires. The starter engages and the engine runs up to rated speed.
  5. Voltage builds. The AVR brings the alternator up to rated voltage and frequency.
  6. Generator breaker closes. Once voltage and frequency are within limits, the emergency generator breaker closes onto the dead emergency bus.
  7. Loads restored. Emergency lighting, the fire pump supply and the other services come back. Some loads may be restored in sequence to avoid a heavy starting surge.

The whole sequence must be complete within 45 seconds at the most, and as quickly as is safe and practicable.

When main power is restored, transfer back depends on the design:

  • On some ships the transfer is automatic. The generator breaker opens, the interconnector recloses and the engine runs on to cool down before stopping.
  • On others it is a manual changeover.

Check your ship's operating manual. Do not guess during a real blackout.

The emergency generator is also commonly part of the dead-ship recovery plan. It powers what is needed to bring the main plant back without outside help. Your engine room black-out and dead-ship procedures will show the order.

Starting arrangements and readiness

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 44 deals with starting. In summary:

  • Cold start. The set must start readily in cold conditions down to 0°C. If that is impracticable, or lower temperatures are likely, heating arrangements must be fitted. This is why the jacket water preheater matters.
  • First set of starts. An automatically started set must have stored energy for at least three consecutive starts.
  • Second source. A second source must be able to give a further three starts within 30 minutes, unless manual starting is shown to be effective.
  • Recharging. Stored energy must be kept charged. Electrical and hydraulic starting systems are maintained from the emergency switchboard. Air systems may be charged from the main or auxiliary air receivers through a non-return valve, or by an emergency compressor supplied from the ESB.

Regulations 42 and 43 also require the prime mover's fuel to have a flashpoint of not less than 43°C.

In practice, 'ready' means all of the following:

  • selector in AUTO
  • fuel valves open
  • day tank above the minimum level set in your SMS
  • preheater on
  • batteries charged, or accumulator at pressure
  • louvres open
  • no alarms standing on the panel

The most common way an emergency generator fails a real blackout is that someone left it in MANUAL after maintenance.

Routine checks, testing and maintenance

SOLAS requires that the complete emergency system, including the automatic starting arrangements, can be periodically tested. The frequency and method of testing are set by your company SMS, the maker's manual and class. They differ between companies, so follow your own planned maintenance system.

Typical daily or watch round checks

  • Selector in AUTO, no alarms on the panel
  • Day tank level; drain water from the tank bottom if the SMS calls for it
  • Lube oil and radiator coolant levels
  • Preheater working (engine block warm to the touch)
  • Battery charger output normal; accumulator or air pressure in range
  • Louvres and dampers open, room clean, nothing stowed against the radiator

Typical periodic tests

  • No-load run. Start manually and check pressures, temperatures, voltage and frequency.
  • Simulated blackout test. Use the test facility or open the interconnector to prove the auto-start and auto-close sequence. Note the time taken.
  • On-load test. Run the generator supplying the emergency bus with its real loads. This proves the engine and alternator under load, not just idling.
  • Second starting source. Test it as required.

Always warn the bridge and the engine control room before a blackout test. Navigation equipment and alarms may briefly drop out. Record results in the log and the PMS.

Maintenance items

  • Lube oil and filter changes
  • Fuel filter changes; bleed the system afterwards
  • Coolant condition
  • Belt tension
  • Battery electrolyte and terminals, where applicable
  • Insulation resistance of the alternator
  • Breaker and relay checks, usually with the ETO

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
No start on blackout test Selector in MANUAL/OFF, flat batteries, charger failed, starter solenoid fault, low accumulator pressure, start signal not reaching the panel
Cranks but will not fire Fuel valve shut, empty or contaminated day tank, blocked filter, air in fuel after filter change, fuel shut-off solenoid stuck
Starts but breaker will not close Voltage or frequency out of limits, AVR fault, loss of residual magnetism (no voltage build-up), breaker undervoltage coil or mechanism fault
Slow to come on load (near or over 45 s) Cold engine (preheater off), weak batteries, governor sluggish, incorrect time delay settings
High coolant temperature on load Louvres or fire dampers shut, radiator fins clogged, slack fan belt, low coolant
Frequency hunting Governor fault or wrong setting, fuel supply restriction
Water in fuel Tank not drained, condensation, contaminated bunkers transferred

Note on shutdowns: class rules usually limit the automatic shutdowns that act in emergency mode, so that the set keeps running when it is needed most. Overspeed is the typical exception, and other conditions often give an alarm only. Know which trips are active on your set and how they are reset.

Safety precautions

  • Isolate properly before maintenance. Lock out and tag out the start circuit and the battery or accumulator. An auto-start while your hand is on a fan belt will injure you badly.
  • Return to AUTO after work. Make returning the selector to AUTO a written step in the permit or job card, and have a second person check it.
  • Fire-fighting in the space. Know where the fuel shut-off and ventilation closures for the emergency generator room are operated from. After a drill, confirm they are reset and open.
  • Battery rooms and boxes. Lead-acid batteries can give off hydrogen when charging. Keep the space ventilated and keep ignition sources away.
  • Hot surfaces and exhaust. Keep fuel and oil leaks cleaned up and lagging intact.
  • Electrical work on the ESB. Treat it as live unless proven dead. The emergency bus can be fed from two directions.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to test whether you understand why each requirement exists, not just the number.

  • 'Where must the emergency generator be located and why?' Above the uppermost continuous deck and readily accessible from the open deck. It must be arranged so that a fire or casualty in the main generating space or category A machinery spaces cannot affect it, and as far as practicable its space must not be contiguous with those spaces. It must also not be forward of the collision bulkhead. This is so that a casualty in the engine room or forward does not disable both power sources.
  • 'Describe what happens on the ESB when the ship blacks out.' Walk through the sequence: undervoltage detected, interconnector opens, set starts, voltage builds, generator breaker closes, loads restored within 45 seconds.
  • 'Why must the interconnector open first?' To stop the emergency generator from trying to supply the dead main switchboard and its faults, which would overload and trip it.
  • 'How long must it supply its loads?' At least 18 hours on cargo ships and 36 hours on passenger ships for the main services. Some individual services have shorter periods in the regulation.
  • 'What starting arrangements are required?' At least three consecutive starts from stored energy. A second source must give a further three starts within 30 minutes, unless manual starting is shown to be effective. The set must also be able to start cold at 0°C.
  • 'You find the generator in MANUAL during your round. What do you do?' Find out why. If no work is in progress, put it back to AUTO, inform the duty engineer or chief engineer and record it. If work is in progress, confirm that the bridge and the chief engineer know the ship has no automatic emergency power.
Key points
  • The emergency generator must start and take its load automatically within a maximum of 45 seconds after blackout.
  • It must supply essential services for at least 18 hours on cargo ships and 36 hours on passenger ships.
  • It must be self-contained, above the uppermost continuous deck, accessible from open deck, and arranged so an engine room fire or casualty cannot affect it.
  • On blackout the interconnector opens before the emergency generator breaker closes onto the emergency bus.
  • Starting needs stored energy for at least three consecutive starts plus a second source, and the set must start cold at 0°C.
  • Test frequency and method come from your SMS, maker and class, and should include on-load and simulated blackout tests.
  • The most common real-life failure is a set left in MANUAL; restoring AUTO should be a written step after every job.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly must the emergency generator come on load after a blackout?

SOLAS Chapter II-1 requires an automatically started emergency generator to supply its load as quickly as is safe and practicable, subject to a maximum of 45 seconds. Passenger ships must always have a transitional source of emergency power, usually a battery. On cargo ships a transitional source is required if the emergency generator is not arranged to take load within 45 seconds.

How long must the emergency generator run on a cargo ship?

For most of the listed services it must supply power for at least 18 hours on cargo ships, compared with 36 hours on passenger ships. Some services, such as steering gear, have their own shorter periods in the regulations. The day tank must hold enough fuel for the required duration.

How often should the emergency generator be tested?

SOLAS requires that the complete system, including the automatic starting arrangements, can be periodically tested. The actual frequency is set by your company SMS, maker's instructions and class. Many companies test weekly, but follow your own PMS. Tests should include proving the auto-start sequence and running on load, not just idling.

Can the emergency generator be used in port to supply the ship?

SOLAS allows limited use of the emergency generator in port under specific conditions agreed by the Administration, including protection arrangements so that emergency services are not compromised. Whether your ship is approved for this depends on flag and class. If it is not written into your ship's documentation and SMS, do not do it.

Why is there a preheater on the emergency generator engine?

SOLAS requires the set to start readily at 0°C. A warm engine also starts faster and accepts load sooner, which helps meet the 45-second limit. Preheating also reduces wear on cold starts. A failed preheater is a common reason for a slow or failed blackout test.

Test yourself

What is the maximum time allowed for an automatically started emergency generator to supply its required load?

The generator must take its load as quickly as is safe and practicable, up to a maximum of 45 seconds. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulations 42 and 43.

For how long must the emergency source supply its main listed services on a cargo ship?

Cargo ships require 18 hours; 36 hours applies to passenger ships. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 43.

What is the minimum flashpoint of fuel for the emergency generator prime mover?

The emergency generator prime mover must use fuel with a flashpoint of not less than 43°C. 60°C is the general limit for fuel oil in machinery. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulations 42 and 43.

On loss of main power, which breaker on the emergency switchboard opens automatically before the emergency generator connects?

The interconnector opens so the emergency generator cannot feed the dead main switchboard. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulations 42 and 43 (emergency switchboard arrangements).

How many consecutive starts must the stored energy of an automatically started emergency generator provide?

Stored energy must give at least three consecutive starts. Six is the total including the second source, which gives a further three within 30 minutes. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 44.
