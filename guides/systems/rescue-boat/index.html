Rescue Boat on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

A rescue boat is a small, manoeuvrable boat carried under SOLAS Chapter III to recover persons from the water and to marshal liferafts. It must be ready to launch in not more than five minutes, and it must be launched and handled in the water at regular drills. This guide covers the main components, the launch and recovery sequence, the routine checks, common faults and the questions examiners ask.

What a rescue boat is for

A rescue boat has two main jobs. The first is to recover people from the water, which mostly means man overboard. The second is to marshal survival craft, which means towing and gathering liferafts after abandonment. A lifeboat is built to keep a crowd alive for days. A rescue boat is built to be quick, nimble and easy to handle alongside a person in the water.

Key points from SOLAS Chapter III and the LSA Code (Chapter V), in paraphrase:

  • Cargo ships must carry at least one rescue boat. A lifeboat can be accepted as the rescue boat if it also meets the rescue boat requirements. Many bulk carriers and tankers with a totally enclosed lifeboat on each side use this arrangement.
  • Passenger ships of 500 GT and over carry at least one rescue boat on each side. Smaller passenger ships carry at least one.
  • Ro-ro passenger ships must also carry a fast rescue boat. The fast rescue boat has its own LSA Code requirements and its own STCW training.
  • Hull length must be between 3.8 m and 8.5 m.
  • Capacity must be at least five seated persons plus one person lying on a stretcher.
  • Construction can be rigid, inflated, or a rigid hull with inflated tubes (RIB).
  • Speed and towing: the boat must manoeuvre at speeds of at least 6 knots and keep that speed for at least 4 hours. It must also be able to tow the largest liferaft carried on board, loaded with its full complement and equipment, at a speed of at least 2 knots.

The exact boat type, engine and davit fitted depend on the ship's build specification and flag approval. Always go by the ship's training manual and the maker's instructions.

Main components

Component What to know
Hull Rigid GRP, inflatable, or rigid hull with inflatable collar. Inflated sections are split into separate buoyancy compartments.
Propulsion Inboard diesel or outboard engine. A petrol outboard is permitted only if the fuel system is specially protected against fire and explosion.
Launching appliance Usually a single-arm davit (often slewing) with a single fall, a winch and a gravity or powered lowering system. A stored-power or hand recovery arrangement is also fitted.
Release hook A single-point hook with an off-load or on-load release, depending on the design. Know which type your boat has.
Painter Rigged from well forward and fitted with a quick release, so the boat can be launched while the ship still has headway.
Bowsing / tricing gear Holds the boat to the embarkation position while the crew board.
Equipment Items required by the LSA Code (see below), stowed and secured.

Typical equipment on board includes:

  • buoyant oars or paddles
  • a bailer
  • a buoyant bucket
  • a compass
  • a sea-anchor
  • a painter
  • a buoyant towline at least 50 m long for towing liferafts
  • a waterproof torch
  • a searchlight
  • a whistle
  • a first-aid kit
  • buoyant rescue quoits on lines
  • a radar reflector
  • thermal protective aids
  • portable fire-extinguishing equipment suitable for oil fires
  • a boat hook and knife

Inflated boats also carry a repair kit, a pump or bellows and a safety knife. Check the full list against the LSA Code and your maker's inventory rather than from memory.

Launch and recovery, step by step

The company SMS and the muster list set the actual roles. A typical sequence for a single-fall davit is set out below.

Launch

  1. Raise the alarm and inform the bridge. For a man overboard, the bridge starts the manoeuvre and keeps the casualty in sight or marked. The bridge decides whether, and on which side, to launch.
  2. Muster the crew. The boat crew dress in immersion suits or anti-exposure suits, lifejackets and helmets, and take a portable VHF.
  3. Check the boat. Plug in, battery isolator on, fuel on and painter rigged. Disconnect the charging and shore connections.
  4. Swing out and lower to the embarkation level. The crew board and the bowsing gear is released.
  5. Start the engine before the boat reaches the water, or as the maker specifies. Check that it runs.
  6. Lower to the water under control, as the ship's motion allows.
  7. Release the hook once the boat is waterborne (for off-load gear). Steer away using the painter and the engine, then slip the painter.
  8. Keep VHF contact with the bridge. Approach the casualty from downwind or down-sea so the boat does not drift onto them. Recover the person as horizontally as practicable to reduce the risk of post-rescue collapse.

Recovery

  1. The ship provides a lee and keeps headway to a minimum.
  2. Come alongside under the davit and pass the painter. Hook on and make sure the hook is fully reset and locked.
  3. Hoist steadily. Stop at embarkation level to land the casualty and crew if the procedure requires it.
  4. Stow the boat, reconnect charging, and restore the equipment and fuel.

SOLAS requires that a rescue boat loaded with its full complement and equipment can be recovered within 5 minutes in moderate sea conditions. It must also be possible to launch it while the ship makes headway of up to 5 knots in calm water.

Routine checks, drills and servicing

Under SOLAS Chapter III, the main routine is as follows.

  • Weekly
    • Visually inspect the rescue boat and its launching appliance to confirm they are ready for use.
    • Run the engine for a total of not less than 3 minutes (ambient temperature permitting) and check the gearbox engages ahead and astern.
    • Outboards that cannot safely run out of the water are run as the maker's handbook prescribes, for example with a flushing muff or in a test tank.
  • Monthly
    • Inspect the boat, equipment and launching appliance against the ship's checklist and record it.
  • Drills
    • Launch the rescue boat with its assigned crew and manoeuvre it in the water each month as far as is reasonable and practicable.
    • Do this at least once every three months in all cases.
    • Where the rescue boat is also a lifeboat, the lifeboat launching rules apply as well.
  • Falls
    • Turn the falls end for end at intervals not exceeding 30 months.
    • Renew them when necessary, or at intervals not exceeding 5 years, whichever comes first.
  • Thorough examination
    • Launching appliances and release gear get an annual thorough examination and a five-yearly thorough examination and test. These are carried out by an authorised service provider in line with the IMO requirements made mandatory under SOLAS (MSC.402(96)).

Practical extras most chief officers add:

  • Inflatable tubes: check pressure, chafe, valve condition and any seam lifting.
  • Batteries: check charging and terminal condition.
  • Fuel: check the fuel is fresh. Old petrol in outboards is a classic non-starter.
  • Hook: check hook reset indicators, grease points, and wire condition at the fall terminal.

The exact checklist content and intervals beyond the SOLAS minimum are set by the SMS and the maker. Record every check, because port state control officers often ask to see the rescue boat engine run.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause What to check
Outboard will not start or starts then dies Stale or water-contaminated fuel, closed tank vent, fuel line not primed, kill-cord not fitted Fuel condition, vent screw, primer bulb, kill-cord clip
Engine overheats or no tell-tale water Blocked cooling intake, impeller worn, run out of water too long Intake strainer, impeller, test method used
Inflatable tube soft Leaking valve, seam failure, temperature change overnight Re-pressurise, soapy-water test, repair kit or shore service
Davit lowers jerkily or creeps Brake lining contamination or wear, centrifugal brake fault Do not use until serviced; report to the chief officer
Hook will not reset or indicator not showing locked Corrosion, misalignment, debris, worn linkage Clean and grease to maker's procedure; do not hoist with crew on board if in doubt
Fall wire kinked or with broken strands Poor spooling, age, corrosion Report it and renew according to the maker's discard criteria
Flat battery, no searchlight or start Charging lead left disconnected, failed charger Charging connection, charger output

The golden rule is simple. If the hook or brake is doubtful, the boat does not go out with people in it until the gear is fixed. Many lifeboat and rescue boat injuries have come from release gear and falls, not from the sea.

Safety precautions

  • Personal protective equipment: crew wear lifejackets, helmets and suitable thermal or immersion protection for the conditions. They carry a knife and a portable VHF.
  • Kill-cord: the coxswain on an outboard wears it at all times.
  • Ship handling: the bridge must give a lee and keep speed as low as the manoeuvre allows during launch and recovery. Keep the propeller well clear of the boat.
  • Communications: agree the VHF channel and hand signals before launching.
  • Competence: crew should hold Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (PSCRB, STCW A-VI/2). Fast rescue boat crews need the separate Proficiency in Fast Rescue Boats (STCW Reg. VI/2, Section A-VI/2 paras 7–10).
  • Weather: the master decides whether launching is safe. In heavy weather, recovering a person with the ship itself may carry less risk than putting the boat out.
  • Drills: drills must be realistic but never put people at risk. Use hoisting with crew on board only when the gear is proven sound.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Can a lifeboat be your rescue boat? Yes, if it also meets the rescue boat requirements. This is common on cargo ships with an enclosed lifeboat on each side.

Q: What is the minimum capacity of a rescue boat? Five seated persons and one person on a stretcher.

Q: How long may launching and recovery take? The boat must be ready for launching in not more than 5 minutes. Recovery with a full complement in moderate seas must be achievable within 5 minutes.

Q: Why is the painter led well forward? It lets the boat sheer away from the ship's side when there is headway. Once slipped, it releases cleanly.

Q: How often is the rescue boat launched? Each month with its crew where reasonable and practicable, and at least every three months.

Q: How do you approach a person in the water? Come from downwind or down-sea with the engine at slow speed. Keep the propeller away from the casualty, and lift them as horizontally as practicable.

Q: What do you check before accepting the boat for a drill? - Hook locked and indicator correct - Falls and brake sound - Plug in - Fuel and battery - Equipment complete - Inspection records up to date

Key points
  • Every cargo ship carries at least one rescue boat; a lifeboat may double as one if it meets the rescue boat requirements.
  • LSA Code limits: hull 3.8–8.5 m, at least five seated plus one stretcher, at least 6 knots for 4 hours.
  • The boat must be ready to launch in not more than 5 minutes and recoverable within 5 minutes in moderate seas.
  • Weekly visual check and engine run, monthly checklist inspection, launch monthly where practicable and at least every three months.
  • Falls are turned end for end within 30 months and renewed by 5 years, whichever comes first.
  • Never put crew in the boat if the hook or davit brake is in doubt.
  • Boat crew should hold PSCRB; fast rescue boats need the separate fast rescue boat certificate.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a rescue boat and a lifeboat?

A lifeboat is a survival craft sized to carry the ship's complement and sustain them until rescue. A rescue boat is a small, fast, manoeuvrable boat for recovering people from the water and marshalling liferafts. A lifeboat can serve as the rescue boat only if it also meets the rescue boat requirements of the LSA Code.

How often must the rescue boat be launched?

SOLAS requires rescue boats to be launched with their assigned crew and manoeuvred in the water each month as far as reasonable and practicable. In all cases this must happen at least once every three months. Your SMS may set a stricter routine.

Can a rescue boat have a petrol outboard engine?

Yes. The LSA Code allows petrol-driven outboards provided the fuel tanks are specially protected against fire and explosion. Many RIB-type rescue boats use them, which is why fuel freshness and storage need attention.

Which certificate do I need to crew the rescue boat?

Rescue boat crew should hold Proficiency in Survival Craft and Rescue Boats (PSCRB) under STCW A-VI/2. If the ship carries a fast rescue boat, the crew assigned to it need the separate Proficiency in Fast Rescue Boats under STCW Section A-VI/2 (paragraphs 7–10). Flag states and companies may add their own requirements.

Who services the rescue boat davit and release hook?

Ship's crew carry out the weekly and monthly inspections and routine greasing to the maker's instructions. The annual thorough examination and the five-yearly examination and test are done by a service provider authorised under the IMO requirements made mandatory by SOLAS.

Test yourself

What is the minimum carrying capacity of a rescue boat under the LSA Code?

The LSA Code requires a rescue boat to carry at least five seated persons and a person lying on a stretcher. Ref: LSA Code, Chapter V.

What is the maximum interval between launches of the rescue boat with its assigned crew?

Launching is required monthly where reasonable and practicable, but in all cases at least once every three months. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 19.

Why is the rescue boat painter rigged from well forward on the ship?

A painter led well forward lets the boat sheer clear of the side when the ship has headway. Ref: LSA Code, Chapter V and the ship's training manual.

How often must the falls of a rescue boat launching appliance be turned end for end?

Falls are turned end for end at intervals not exceeding 30 months and renewed when necessary or within 5 years, whichever is earlier. Ref: SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20.

What is the minimum speed a rescue boat must manoeuvre at, and maintain for at least 4 hours?

Rescue boats must manoeuvre at speeds of at least 6 knots and keep that speed for at least 4 hours. The 20-knot figure is for fast rescue boats. Ref: LSA Code, Chapter V.
