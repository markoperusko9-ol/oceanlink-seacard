Mooring Winches and Windlass on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

The windlass heaves and lowers the anchor cable through a gypsy, and mooring winches hold and adjust the ship's mooring lines on drums with band brakes. Both are simple machines that can kill you if a brake, clutch or line is mismanaged, so brake condition, correct reeling and clutch discipline matter most. Your company PMS, class rules and the maker's manual set the exact test intervals and limits.

What the windlass and mooring winches do

On most merchant ships the forecastle carries one or two windlasses for the anchors. The forecastle and poop decks carry mooring winches for the lines. On many ships the two are combined: a windlass with a gypsy for the cable and one or more mooring drums driven by the same motor.

  • Windlass: heaves up and lowers (walks back) the anchor and cable. Its brake can also hold the cable when letting go.
  • Mooring winch: pays out, heaves in and holds mooring lines. It lets you tend moorings as draught and tide change.
  • Warping ends (drum ends): smooth heads used with a few turns of rope for handling lines that are not stowed on a drum.

One point to fix in your head early: the windlass brake is not intended to be the primary means of holding the ship at anchor. Once brought up, the load goes onto the chain stopper (bow stopper/guillotine) with the brake also applied. On a mooring winch, the brake is meant to render (slip) before the line parts. It acts as a safety fuse for the line and the fittings.

Main components

Component Purpose
Prime mover Hydraulic motor (from a power pack or central pump unit), electric motor (often pole-changing), or steam on some older tankers
Gearbox Reduces speed and increases torque to the gypsy or drum
Gypsy / cable lifter Shaped wheel whose snugs grip the chain links. It must match the cable size.
Dog clutch Engages or disengages the gypsy or drum from the drive. It is operated only with no load on it.
Band brake Steel band lined with friction material, tightened by a screw spindle or hydraulic/spring actuator
Mooring drum Often split: a storage section and a tension section that works the line
Motor brake On electric winches, usually a spring-applied, electrically released disc brake. It holds the motor, not the line.
Controls Local lever or pedestal with emergency stop, and sometimes remote or self-tension controls
Chain stopper Separate deck fitting that takes the anchor cable load off the windlass

Alongside these sit the hawse pipe, spurling pipe, chain locker and bitter end fitting for the anchor side. The mooring side has fairleads, rollers, chocks and bitts. Those fittings are part of the mooring arrangement, and the winch is only as good as the weakest of them.

How it works: step by step

Heaving up the anchor (typical sequence; follow your SMS checklist):

  1. Confirm power is on, the hydraulic system is running (oil level and temperature normal) and communications with the bridge are tested.
  2. Run the windlass out of gear to check it turns freely. Then stop it.
  3. Engage the gypsy clutch with the brake still on, and check it is fully home.
  4. Take the weight on the motor, release the brake, then open the bow stopper.
  5. Heave in steadily. Report the cable direction, strain and number of shackles to the bridge. Wash the cable as it comes in if fitted.
  6. When the anchor is sighted clear and housed, apply the brake, close the stopper and fit the lashings or devil's claw. Take the clutch out of gear.

Mooring winch, holding a line alongside:

  1. Reel the line onto the tension drum in the direction that tightens the brake band when the line comes under load. The maker usually marks the correct lead on the drum or brake.
  2. Keep only one layer on the tension drum where possible. Extra layers reduce the brake's holding capacity.
  3. Once the line is tended, apply the brake firmly to the specified torque or mark. Then take the winch out of gear. The brake, not the motor or gearbox, holds the line.
  4. To tend a line: keep the brake on, stop the motor and engage the clutch fully. Start the motor and take the weight, then release the brake and heave or pay out. When finished, re-apply the brake and take the clutch out of gear.

Self-tensioning (auto-tension) mode lets lines pay out and heave in on their own, and the ship can walk along the berth. Industry guidance (OCIMF MEG4) advises against leaving winches in self-tension mode when moored, and many terminals and companies prohibit it. Check your company and terminal rules.

Routine checks and maintenance

Exact intervals come from your planned maintenance system, the maker's manual and class. These tasks are standard on almost every ship:

Before arrival and departure (pre-use checks): - Test-run each winch and windlass in both directions. Check the emergency stops work. - Operate the clutches and brakes, and confirm they engage fully. - Check for hydraulic leaks and correct oil level. Make sure guards are in place and the deck is clear of grease and loose gear.

Periodic: - Brake lining: check thickness, look for glazing and oil or grease contamination, and check the band, pins and linkage for corrosion or seizing. Contaminated linings must be cleaned or renewed. They cannot be trusted. - Brake holding test: carried out with a test kit to confirm the brake renders at its design setting. Do it at the interval in your PMS, often annually, and after renewing linings or working on the brake. - Lubrication: grease bearings, clutch slides and brake spindles. Check gearbox oil level and condition. - Hydraulics: renew or clean filters, sample oil for analysis if your PMS requires it, and check coolers and the oil temperature in service. - Gypsy and drums: check for wear, cracks and grooving. Check the gypsy fits the cable correctly. - Foundations and seatings: look for corrosion, cracks and loose holding-down bolts. Check the deck below the seating where you can reach it. - Electric winches: check motor insulation, the motor brake air gap and condition, and terminal boxes for water ingress.

Record defects and repairs. Under the ISM Code, maintenance of critical equipment must be planned and recorded. Many companies list windlass and mooring winches as critical equipment.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Brake slipping at low load Oil/grease on lining, worn or glazed lining, line reeled the wrong way, too many layers on drum, brake not tightened enough
Winch slow or weak under load Low hydraulic oil, internal leakage in pump or motor, relief valve set low or leaking, clogged filter
Noisy hydraulic pump, foaming oil Air ingress on the suction side, low oil level, choked suction strainer, cavitation
Hydraulic oil overheating Cooler fouled or no cooling water, continuous running near relief pressure, wrong oil grade
Electric motor tripping Overload, single phasing, motor brake not releasing, water ingress
Clutch will not engage or disengage Teeth misaligned, corroded or ungreased sliding parts, attempting it under load
Cable jumping the gypsy Worn gypsy snugs, cable size mismatch, heaving against a bad lead
Windlass brake not holding while letting go Lining wet with oil or seawater, band not adjusted, excessive cable speed

Worked scenario. Alongside, at 0300, the AB reports a stern line has paid out a metre and the brake was tight. The officer checks the drum. The line has been reeled so the load is trying to open the band, with three layers on the tension side. The fix is to put out an extra line first to take the load. Then re-reel the slipping line with the correct lead and a single layer. Report it so the mooring plan and lining condition are checked in daylight. Do not simply wind the brake tighter and walk away.

Safety precautions

  • Never stand in the bight of a line or in line with a rope under tension. Treat the whole mooring deck as a potential snap-back area. Current industry guidance warns that painted snap-back zones give false confidence.
  • Never engage or disengage a clutch under load. Take the weight on the brake first.
  • Do not leave the winch holding on the motor. Apply the brake and take it out of gear.
  • Only competent, authorised crew operate controls. One person gives signals, and the operator watches only that person.
  • Keep hands, gloves and loose clothing clear of drums, gypsies and warping ends. When using a warping end, the person tending the turns stands back from the drum.
  • For maintenance, isolate and lock out the power and hydraulic supply. Secure the cable on the stopper and any drum load before work starts.
  • Synthetic and HMPE lines behave differently from wire and stretch differently. Never mix line types on the same lead.
  • Wear a helmet, safety shoes and gloves, plus eye protection when washing cable or handling greasy wires.

Regulations and oral exam questions

Where the rules sit: - SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 3-8 covers towing and mooring equipment. Since 1 January 2024, new ships of 3,000 GT and above must have mooring arrangements designed and equipment selected in line with MSC.1/Circ.1619. All ships, new and existing, must have mooring equipment, including lines, inspected and maintained in line with MSC.1/Circ.1620. - MSC.1/Circ.1619 gives guidelines on the design of mooring arrangements and selection of equipment. - MSC.1/Circ.1620 gives guidelines on inspection and maintenance of mooring equipment, including lines. It also covers making a maintenance record and replacement criteria. - MSC.1/Circ.1175 (as revised) gives guidance on shipboard towing and mooring equipment. - Anchoring equipment and winch design loads are largely set by classification society rules. Exact brake and stopper capacities depend on class and the maker.

Typical examiner questions: - Why must the chain stopper be on when riding at anchor? The windlass brake is not intended to be the primary means of holding the ship at anchor; the stopper is the primary holding device, with the brake also applied. - Why does the reeling direction matter? A band brake holds best when the load tightens the band; wrong lead greatly reduces holding. - Why should the brake render before the line breaks? So the line, fittings and people are protected; the brake is a controlled release. - What do you check before arrival? Power, hydraulics, free running, clutches, brakes, emergency stops, communications, deck clear. - What would make you suspect brake contamination? Slipping at modest loads, oily lining, recent gearbox or hydraulic leak above the brake.

Key points
  • The windlass heaves and lowers the anchor; mooring winches hold and tend lines.
  • Riding at anchor, the chain stopper is the primary holding device, with the windlass brake also applied.
  • Reel lines so the load tightens the brake band, and keep one layer on the tension drum.
  • Hold lines on the brake with the winch out of gear, never on the motor.
  • Never engage or disengage a clutch under load.
  • Contaminated or worn brake linings are the classic cause of slipping. Test brakes per your PMS.
  • SOLAS II-1/3-8, with guidelines MSC.1/Circ.1619 (design) and MSC.1/Circ.1620 (inspection and maintenance), covers mooring arrangements; anchoring equipment is mainly covered by class rules.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a windlass and a mooring winch?

A windlass has a gypsy (cable lifter) shaped to grip anchor chain and is used to heave up and lower the anchor. A mooring winch has drums for mooring lines and is used to hold and adjust the ship alongside. Many ships combine both on one machine, with mooring drums and warping ends on the windlass shaft.

How often should mooring winch brakes be tested?

The interval is set by your company's planned maintenance system, the maker and class. Industry practice is commonly at least annually and after any work on the brake or lining renewal. Check your SMS and the winch's test records for the actual requirement.

Why should mooring winches not be left in self-tension mode alongside?

In self-tension mode, winches at both ends pay out and heave in on their own. The ship can creep along the berth, and the lines may not hold her in position. Industry guidance (OCIMF MEG4) advises against leaving winches in self-tension mode when moored, and many terminals and companies prohibit it. Check your company and terminal rules.

Why does a mooring line slip even when the brake is tight?

Common causes are oil or grease on the lining, worn or glazed lining, and the line reeled in the wrong direction. Too many layers on the tension drum is another. Each of these reduces holding capacity well below design, so tightening harder will not fix the root cause.

Can you heave the ship up to her anchor with the windlass?

The windlass is designed to lift the anchor and a given length of cable, not to drag the ship against strong wind or current. Use the engine to ease the strain so the cable leads up and down while heaving. Report cable direction and strain to the bridge throughout.

Test yourself

When riding at anchor, what should take the load of the cable?

The chain stopper is the primary holding device at anchor, with the windlass brake also applied as backup. Reference: classification society rules for anchoring equipment and the maker's manual.

Which practice reduces the holding capacity of a mooring winch band brake?

Band brakes are directional and hold best when the load tightens the band. Wrong-way reeling greatly reduces holding. Reference: OCIMF Mooring Equipment Guidelines (MEG4) and maker's instructions.

What is the correct way to hold a tended mooring line alongside?

The band brake is designed to hold and render; the motor and gearbox are not meant to hold static mooring loads. Reference: OCIMF Mooring Equipment Guidelines (MEG4) and maker's instructions.

A hydraulic windlass pump is noisy and the tank oil is foaming. What is the most likely cause?

Noise and foaming point to air ingress, low oil or a choked suction, which causes cavitation. Reference: maker's manual, planned maintenance under ISM Code section 10.

Why should a mooring winch brake render before the line breaks?

The brake is set to slip at a load below the line's strength, acting as a safety fuse for the mooring system. Reference: OCIMF Mooring Equipment Guidelines (MEG4) and maker's instructions.
