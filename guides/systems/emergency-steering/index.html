Emergency Steering: How It Works, How to Test It and What Goes Wrong

Quick answer

Emergency steering is the means of controlling the rudder locally from the steering gear compartment, and of keeping it powered, when normal bridge control is lost. SOLAS requires the steering gear to be checked and tested within 12 hours before departure and an emergency steering drill at least every three months, with the change-over procedure displayed on the bridge and in the steering gear room. Every watchkeeper should be able to carry out the change-over without reading the instructions.

What emergency steering actually means

Seafarers use "emergency steering" loosely for three different things. In an oral exam you should separate them clearly:

  • Local (emergency) control. Steering the ship from the steering gear compartment when the bridge control system has failed. Orders are passed by telephone and the rudder is moved by local push-buttons, a manual lever on the directional valve, or a trick wheel on older installations.
  • Auxiliary steering gear. A second means of moving the rudder, required by SOLAS unless the main gear already has duplicated power units and meets the single-failure standard. On most modern ships this is the second hydraulic pump unit.
  • Alternative power supply. On ships where the rudder stock exceeds the SOLAS size threshold, a supply from the emergency source of power, or from an independent source in the steering gear compartment, that keeps at least one power unit, its control and the rudder angle indicator running after a main power failure.

The purpose is simple: a ship that cannot steer in a TSS, a narrow channel or close to a lee shore is in serious trouble within minutes. Emergency steering buys you control until the fault is found or the ship is brought to safety.

Main components in the steering gear compartment

Layouts vary by maker (ram type, rotary vane) and by age, but you will usually find:

  • Two (or more) hydraulic power units, each an electric motor and pump, with separate starters fed from the main switchboard and, where required, the emergency switchboard.
  • Directional control (solenoid) valves, often with manual override pins or a lever for local operation.
  • Local control panel with port and starboard push-buttons or a lever, plus a selector to take control from the bridge.
  • Feedback unit and linkage connected to the rudder stock or tiller, which tells the control system where the rudder actually is.
  • Rudder angle indicator visible in the compartment, and a mechanical scale on the tiller or rudder carrier.
  • Heading information: a gyro repeater or other means at the emergency steering position.
  • Communication: a sound-powered telephone or talk-back to the bridge. Portable UHF radios are often kept as a backup.
  • Hydraulic oil storage tank, fixed and piped, big enough to recharge at least one power actuating system.
  • Limit switches and rudder stoppers. The electrical limits cut the gear off before the rudder reaches the mechanical stops.
  • Change-over instructions and block diagram posted at the local control position.
  • Emergency lighting, gratings and handrails, so you can work safely with the ship rolling.

Change-over to local control: step by step

Scenario: You are OOW in coastal waters. The rudder stops responding in both autopilot and hand steering, and the rudder angle indicator is frozen at 5° starboard.

On the bridge

  1. Try the obvious first. Change over to the other steering system or pump, then try NFU (non-follow-up) control. Many "steering failures" are cured at this stage.
  2. Call the master and the engine room, and sound the alarm in line with your SMS.
  3. If control is not regained, assess the traffic. Use the engine to reduce speed or take way off if it is safe to do so. Remember that going astern produces transverse thrust that swings the head, usually to starboard with a right-handed fixed-pitch propeller, and can make the situation worse.
  4. If you are unable to manoeuvre, exhibit the not-under-command lights or shapes. Warn traffic by VHF and, where required, inform VTS. Navigational warnings and broadcasts follow company and coastal-state procedures.
  5. Send a competent person, usually an officer or engineer plus a helmsman, to the steering gear compartment.

In the steering gear compartment

  1. Establish communications with the bridge and test them before doing anything else.
  2. Check which pump is running and whether it shows fault alarms or low oil level. Start the standby pump if needed.
  3. Select local control on the panel, following the displayed block diagram.
  4. Steer by order from the bridge. Use the local push-buttons or valve override, watching the rudder angle indicator and gyro repeater.
  5. Repeat every helm order back, and report "wheel 10° port" only when the rudder is actually there.
  6. Keep the bridge informed of anything abnormal, such as noise, oil leaks, overheating or rudder creep.

Key point: helm orders from the bridge must be clear and given in the standard format. The person on the local control cannot see the traffic, so the bridge must think for them.

Routine checks, tests and drills

SOLAS Chapter V sets out the operational minimum. Your company SMS will normally add to it.

Within 12 hours before departure, the steering gear is checked and tested. The test covers, as applicable:

  • the main and auxiliary steering gear;
  • the remote control systems and the bridge steering positions;
  • the emergency power supply;
  • the rudder angle indicators compared with the actual rudder position;
  • the remote control power failure alarms and power unit failure alarms;
  • the automatic isolating arrangements and other automatic equipment.

The checks also include full movement of the rudder, a visual inspection of the gear and linkage, and a test of bridge to steering gear room communications.

Emergency steering drills are held at least once every three months. They include direct control from the steering gear compartment, the communications procedure with the bridge and, where applicable, operation of the alternative power supply. The date and details are recorded in the log book.

The flag Administration may waive the 12-hour pre-departure checks for ships regularly engaged on short voyages, but such ships must still carry out the checks and tests at least once every week. Check your flag's rules.

Typical engineer's routine (frequency per the SMS and the maker's manual):

  • Check the hydraulic oil level and the storage tank level.
  • Look for leaks at rams, vanes, pipes and valve blocks.
  • Grease the rudder carrier and linkages.
  • Check the feedback linkage for slack.
  • Take oil samples for analysis.
  • Check filter differential pressure.
  • Test the low-level and overload alarms.
  • Confirm the emergency lighting and telephone work.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Rudder does not move in follow-up but does in NFU Follow-up amplifier or feedback unit fault, broken feedback linkage
Rudder hunts or oscillates around the ordered angle Air in the hydraulic system, worn feedback linkage, maladjusted control settings
Slow rudder movement One pump not delivering, internal leakage, low oil level, relief valve passing
Rudder moves without an order (creep) Solenoid valve sticking or leaking, contamination
Rudder will not move at all, pumps running Hydraulic lock (often signalled by a dedicated alarm where fitted), valve stuck in the wrong position
Indicator on bridge disagrees with tiller scale Indicator transmitter or linkage fault, or bridge indicator fault; check and recalibrate
No communication with bridge Sound-powered phone fault, damaged handset cable
Pump trips on overload Motor fault, mechanical binding, rudder bearing problem

Air in the system after maintenance and contaminated oil are behind a large share of real failures. After any work on the hydraulic circuit, bleed the system thoroughly and test it through the full range before handing back.

Safety precautions

  • Isolate before working. Never work near the tiller, rams or linkages without the gear isolated and locked out under the permit-to-work system. A bridge-initiated movement can crush a hand or worse.
  • Inform the bridge before any test or isolation, and make sure the OOW knows when the gear is out of service.
  • Mind the conditions. The compartment is often hot, noisy and slippery with oil. Keep gratings clean and use the handrails, especially in a seaway.
  • Keep the posted instructions legible and current. If the system is modified, the block diagram must be updated.
  • Do not use the compartment as a store. It must stay readily accessible.
  • Avoid alarm fatigue. Test the alarms properly; do not just acknowledge and silence them.
  • Drill realistically. Use the real telephone, steer real helm orders, and rotate officers and ratings so that everybody has done it, not just the bosun and 3rd Engineer.

Regulatory framework (chapter level)

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulations 29 and 30. These cover design and installation: main and auxiliary gear capability, bridge and local control, rudder angle indication, communication with the bridge, alarms, and the alternative power supply for larger rudder stocks.
    • The main gear must be capable of putting the rudder over from 35° on one side to 35° on the other, and from 35° on either side to 30° on the other within 28 seconds, at the deepest seagoing draught and maximum ahead service speed. Where testing at the deepest draught is impractical, SOLAS allows the capability to be demonstrated under specified alternative conditions.
    • The auxiliary gear must manage 15° to 15° within 60 seconds, at half maximum service speed or 7 knots, whichever is greater.
    • Where an alternative power supply is required, it must come on automatically within 45 seconds. It must run continuously for at least 30 minutes on ships of 10,000 GT and above, and at least 10 minutes on other ships.
    • Tankers, chemical tankers and gas carriers of 10,000 GT and above, and other ships of 70,000 GT and above, have additional duplication and single-failure requirements.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26. This covers operational testing, drills, the posted change-over instructions and officer familiarisation.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19. Its carriage requirements include heading information at the emergency steering position, if one is fitted.

Detailed requirements for your ship also depend on its build date, classification society rules and flag Administration interpretations.

Oral exam: questions examiners like to ask

  • "You lose steering in a TSS. What do you do?" Walk through the bridge actions, the NUC signals and communications, then local control. Mention reducing speed safely and warning other traffic.
  • "What tests are done before departure?" List the SOLAS V/26 items. Do not forget the rudder angle indicator against the actual rudder position, and the communications.
  • "How often is the emergency steering drill, and what does it include?" Every three months: local control, communications, and the alternative power supply where applicable. Record it in the log book.
  • "What is the auxiliary steering gear performance requirement?" 15° to 15° in 60 seconds at half speed or 7 knots, whichever is greater.
  • "Why do limit switches cut out before the rudder stops?" So the gear is stopped electrically before the rudder hits the mechanical stoppers. This prevents structural damage and hydraulic overload.
  • "What causes rudder hunting?" Air in the oil, slack in the feedback linkage, control maladjustment.
  • "How is heading information provided in the steering gear room?" Usually by a gyro repeater. Describe what you would use if it failed: a magnetic compass reading relayed by phone, and steering by order from the bridge.

Examiners want a calm, sequenced answer that keeps the bridge in command, not a list of buttons.

Key points
  • Emergency steering means local control from the steering gear room, auxiliary gear, and an alternative power supply. Know the difference.
  • Steering gear must be checked and tested within 12 hours before departure under SOLAS Chapter V.
  • Emergency steering drills are held at least every three months and recorded in the log book.
  • Change-over instructions with a block diagram must be displayed on the bridge and in the steering gear compartment.
  • Auxiliary gear must achieve 15° to 15° in 60 seconds at half speed or 7 knots, whichever is greater.
  • Test communications first when taking local control, and keep the bridge in command of every helm order.
  • Air in the hydraulic system and feedback linkage faults are common causes of hunting and erratic rudder response.

Frequently asked questions

How often must the emergency steering drill be carried out?

SOLAS Chapter V requires emergency steering drills at least once every three months. The drill must include direct control from the steering gear compartment, the communication procedure with the bridge and, where applicable, the alternative power supply. Many company SMS schedules require more frequent practice.

What has to be tested before departure?

Within 12 hours before departure, the main and auxiliary gear, remote controls, bridge steering positions, emergency power supply, rudder angle indicators and alarms are tested. Full rudder movement, a visual inspection of the linkage and bridge to steering room communications are also checked. The flag Administration may waive the 12-hour pre-departure checks for ships regularly engaged on short voyages, but such ships must still carry out the checks and tests at least once every week.

How do you steer if the bridge steering completely fails?

First try the alternative pump or system and NFU control from the bridge. If that fails, send a team to the steering gear room, establish telephone communication, select local control and steer by order using the push-buttons or valve override. The bridge keeps the con and exhibits NUC signals if the ship cannot manoeuvre as required.

Who is responsible for emergency steering on board?

The master is responsible for ensuring tests and drills are done and that officers know the change-over procedure. In practice, the chief engineer's department maintains the gear and the navigating officers conduct the tests and log them. The exact division is set by the company SMS.

Does every ship have an emergency power supply to the steering gear?

SOLAS requires an automatic alternative power supply where the rudder stock diameter exceeds the size threshold in Chapter II-1. It comes from the emergency source of power or an independent source in the steering compartment. Ships whose required rudder stock diameter is 230 mm or less (excluding ice strengthening) may not need it, so check your ship's documentation and class records.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS, how often must an emergency steering drill be held?

Emergency steering drills are required at least once every three months. Pre-departure testing is a separate requirement. Reference: SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26.

The steering gear must be checked and tested how long before departure?

SOLAS requires the steering gear to be checked and tested within 12 hours before departure. Reference: SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26.

What is the minimum performance required of auxiliary steering gear?

The 28-second, 35° to 30° figure belongs to the main steering gear. Auxiliary gear needs 15° to 15° in 60 seconds. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 29.

Where an alternative power supply to the steering gear is required, within what time must it come on automatically?

The alternative power supply must be provided automatically within 45 seconds. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 29.

Where must the steering gear change-over instructions and block diagram be displayed?

Simple operating instructions with a block diagram of the change-over procedures must be permanently displayed on the bridge and in the steering gear compartment. Reference: SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 26.
