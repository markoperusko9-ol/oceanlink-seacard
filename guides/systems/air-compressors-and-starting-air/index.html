Air Compressors and Starting Air: A Practical Guide for Engineers and Cadets

Quick answer

The starting air system compresses air, stores it in receivers and admits it to engine cylinders in firing order to turn a diesel engine until it fires. On most ships the main engine cannot be started without starting air, so engineers must keep the compressors, receivers, valves and drains in good order. The greatest hazard is a starting air line explosion caused by a leaking cylinder starting valve.

What the starting air system does and why it matters

Most merchant ship main engines and many auxiliary engines are started by compressed air. Air at high pressure is admitted to the cylinders, pushes the pistons down and turns the crankshaft. Once the engine is turning fast enough, fuel is injected and it fires on its own.

If the starting air fails, the main engine cannot be started or reversed. On a direct-reversing two-stroke engine, that means no astern movement either. This is why the system is built with duplicated compressors and receivers, and why the duty engineer watches receiver pressure closely during manoeuvring.

Compressed air on board is usually split into separate services:

  • Starting air: high pressure, stored in the main air receivers.
  • Control air: reduced pressure, filtered and dried, used for engine remote control, valve actuators and instruments.
  • Service or working air: used for pneumatic tools, cleaning and deck use, often from a separate compressor.

Working pressures depend on the engine maker and the ship's design. Read them from your machinery manuals and the receiver nameplates, not from memory of a previous ship.

Main components

A typical installation contains the following:

  • Main air compressors: normally two or more, usually reciprocating two-stage machines with water or air cooling.
  • Intercooler and aftercooler: cool the air between stages and after the final stage. This improves efficiency and condenses moisture.
  • Stage relief valves: protect each stage against overpressure.
  • Drain valves and unloaders: remove condensed water and oil, and unload the compressor so it starts without load.
  • Non-return valve: on the compressor discharge, stops receiver air flowing back.
  • Air receivers: pressure vessels that store starting air. Each has a safety valve, pressure gauge, drain valve and, depending on design and class, a fusible plug.
  • Emergency air compressor: a small unit, often hand-started or diesel-driven, or supplied from the emergency switchboard. It fills an emergency receiver so a generator can be started from dead ship.
  • Main starting valve (automatic valve): a large valve, opened by control air, that admits receiver air to the starting manifold.
  • Starting air distributor: driven from the camshaft or crankshaft. It sends pilot air to open each cylinder's starting valve at the correct crank angle.
  • Cylinder starting air valves: fitted to each cylinder head and opened by pilot air during the starting period.
  • Flame arresters and/or bursting discs: fitted in the branch pipes to the cylinders. They limit the effect of a starting air line explosion.
  • Turning gear interlock: blocks starting air while the turning gear is engaged.
  • Control air reduction station and air dryer: supply clean, dry, reduced-pressure air to the controls.

How it works, step by step

Charging the receivers

  1. The compressor starts on pressure switch demand with the unloader open, so it runs up without load.
  2. The unloader closes. The first stage draws air through the suction filter and compresses it.
  3. The air passes through the intercooler, where moisture condenses and is drained.
  4. The second stage compresses the air to final pressure. The air then passes through the aftercooler and the moisture separator.
  5. The air goes through the non-return valve into the receivers. When the cut-out pressure is reached, the compressor stops and the drains open briefly.

Starting a main engine

  1. The engineer checks four things: the receiver is at working pressure, the receiver outlet valve is open, the starting manifold is drained and the turning gear is disengaged.
  2. A start order from the bridge or engine control room opens the main starting valve.
  3. Air fills the starting manifold up to each cylinder starting valve. These valves stay closed for now.
  4. The distributor sends pilot air to open the starting valve of whichever cylinder is in the correct position, just after top dead centre on its power stroke.
  5. Cylinders receive air in turn, in firing order, and the engine turns.
  6. At the firing speed set by the maker, fuel is admitted. Shortly after, starting air is cut off, the main starting valve closes and the manifold vents.
  7. If the engine fails to fire, the control system or the engineer stops the air. Investigate before the receivers run down.

On a direct-reversing engine, the distributor and fuel cams are shifted for astern before air is admitted. Every reversal uses air, so heavy manoeuvring can drain the receivers quickly. Start the second compressor early.

Routine checks and maintenance

Each watch

  • Check receiver pressures, compressor running hours and duty cycles. A compressor that runs too often points to a leak or a worn compressor.
  • Check compressor stage pressures and temperatures, cooling water flow, and lube oil level and pressure.
  • Drain the receivers and the control air system as per the vessel's routine. Confirm that automatic drains actually discharge.
  • Before manoeuvring, drain the starting manifold and make sure the standby compressor is on auto.

Planned maintenance (intervals per maker and your company PMS)

  • Overhaul compressor suction and delivery valves, piston rings and bearings, and check bumping clearance.
  • Clean intercoolers and aftercoolers, and inspect them for leaks between the air and water sides.
  • Clean or renew suction filters.
  • Test receiver safety valves, either in place or by bench testing as the PMS requires.
  • Inspect receivers internally during surveys. Look for corrosion and oil or water accumulation.
  • Overhaul cylinder starting valves and the main starting valve, and check the distributor timing.
  • Inspect flame arresters and bursting discs, and check that the turning gear interlock works.
  • Test the emergency compressor and dead-ship starting arrangement, and record the results.

Detailed intervals and test methods for safety valves and receivers vary by class society, flag and company SMS. Follow your own documents.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Compressor runs long and fills slowly Leaking suction or delivery valves, worn piston rings, choked suction filter, unloader stuck open, leaking drains
Intercooler pressure higher than normal Second-stage (HP) valves leaking. Air passes back into the intercooler, or the HP stage cannot take its share
Intercooler pressure lower than normal First-stage (LP) valves leaking, choked LP suction, worn LP rings
High air discharge temperature Fouled coolers, poor cooling water flow, leaking valves recompressing hot air
Water or oil in receivers and control air Poor draining, failed separator or dryer, excessive cylinder lubrication of the compressor
Air in compressor cooling water, or bubbling in the header tank Cracked cooler tube or cylinder liner, letting air leak into the water side
Receiver pressure drops in port with no use Leaking main starting valve, drains, fittings or consumers
Engine turns slowly or stops during start on air Low receiver pressure, a cylinder starting valve not opening, distributor fault, sticking main starting valve
One starting air branch pipe hot to the touch That cylinder's starting valve is leaking or stuck open, so combustion gas is entering the air line

The last item is the one that matters most for safety. Treat a hot branch pipe as an emergency warning, not a maintenance note.

Safety precautions and starting air line explosions

How a starting air explosion happens. Lubricating oil carried over from the compressor collects as a film in the starting manifold. If a cylinder starting valve leaks or sticks open, hot combustion gas enters the manifold during firing. The oil film can ignite. With high-pressure air present, the flame can travel along the pipe and cause an explosion that damages the manifold and bursting discs and can injure nearby crew.

Prevention

  • Keep oil out of the air. Maintain the compressor, drain frequently and do not over-lubricate the compressor.
  • Overhaul cylinder starting valves on schedule and lap them properly.
  • Feel or check branch pipe temperatures after starting and during manoeuvring.
  • Keep flame arresters and bursting discs in good order. Never replace a disc with a solid blank.

Personal safety

  • Before working on any part of the system, isolate it, lock and tag the valves, and vent it to zero. Confirm zero pressure on a gauge or by opening a drain. Work under the permit to work and lock-out tag-out procedures.
  • Never point compressed air at a person or use it to clean clothing or skin. Air can enter the body through cuts and cause serious injury.
  • Before working on the main engine, engage the turning gear and shut and lock the main starting air valve so the engine cannot be turned on air.
  • Before blowing the engine through on air, open the indicator cocks and watch them for water or fuel. Follow the vessel's procedure.
  • Respect receiver safety valves. Never gag them or set them above the stamped working pressure.

Regulation and survey: what is certain

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C, Regulation 34 (air pressure systems) sets four main requirements:
    • means to prevent overpressure in compressed air systems, including protection of compressor water jackets and coolers against overpressure from air leakage;
    • compressor discharge pipes that lead directly to the receivers;
    • starting pipes from the receivers to the engines that are entirely separate from the compressor discharge piping;
    • arrangements to reduce oil entering the air system and to drain the system.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C, Regulation 26 requires means to bring machinery into operation from the dead ship condition without external aid. The emergency compressor and emergency receiver commonly serve this purpose.
  • Classification society rules set the detailed requirements. These include the number and capacity of compressors and receivers, the minimum number of consecutive starts without recharging, and protection devices on starting air lines. Figures differ between class societies and engine types, so check your class rules and machinery documentation.
  • ISM Code, Section 10 requires the company to maintain the ship and equipment under planned maintenance, including critical systems. Many companies include the starting air system among the critical equipment identified under ISM 10.3. Check your SMS.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners like this system because it tests both theory and watchkeeping sense. Practise answering these aloud:

  1. Why are air compressors multi-stage with intercooling? Intercooling brings the compression closer to isothermal. This reduces the work needed and lowers the final temperature, which reduces the risk of oil ignition. Moisture also drops out between stages.
  2. What is bumping clearance and how do you check it? It is the small clearance between the piston and the cylinder head at top dead centre. Too much clearance reduces volumetric efficiency, and too little risks contact. To check it, insert lead wire, turn the compressor over by hand and measure the squashed lead.
  3. Your intercooler pressure is rising. What do you suspect? The HP stage valves.
  4. How does a starting air explosion occur, and what protection is fitted? Explain the oil film, the leaking starting valve and the hot gas entering the line. Then describe the flame arresters, bursting discs and draining.
  5. During manoeuvring, one starting air branch pipe is hot. What do you do? Inform the bridge and the chief engineer, and follow the engine maker's and company's instructions. That usually means stopping the engine when it is safe to do so and changing or overhauling that cylinder's starting valve.
  6. How would you start the ship from dead ship? Explain your own ship's arrangement. A typical sequence is emergency compressor, emergency receiver, emergency generator or auxiliary engine start, then main compressors.
  7. Why must the starting air pipes from the receivers to the engines be separate from the compressor discharge pipes? SOLAS II-1 Reg 34 requires the compressor discharge to lead directly to the receivers. Keeping the starting lines separate isolates the compressor side from a starting line explosion and from hot or oily air backflow.
Key points
  • Starting air turns the engine on compressed air until it fires on fuel. Without it, most ships cannot start the main engine or, on a direct-reversing engine, go astern.
  • Main compressors are usually two-stage with intercooling and aftercooling, and each stage has relief protection.
  • Abnormal intercooler pressure helps diagnose faults: high suggests HP valve leakage, low suggests LP valve leakage.
  • Drain receivers and control air regularly, because oil and water carry-over cause corrosion and explosion risk.
  • A hot starting air branch pipe means a leaking cylinder starting valve and is an explosion warning.
  • Always isolate, lock and vent to zero before work, and keep the turning gear and starting air interlock intact.
  • SOLAS II-1 Regulation 34 covers air pressure systems. Class rules set detailed numbers such as compressor capacity and consecutive starts.

Frequently asked questions

What pressure is starting air on a ship?

It depends on the engine maker and the ship's design, so read the figure from the engine manual and the receiver nameplate. Control air is reduced to a much lower pressure through a reducing station and dryer. Never set relief valves above the stamped working pressure.

Why do starting air explosions happen?

Lubricating oil carried over from the compressor forms a film in the starting manifold. If a cylinder starting valve leaks or sticks open, hot combustion gas enters the line and can ignite that oil. Good draining, well-maintained starting valves and intact flame arresters and bursting discs are the defences.

How many starts must the air receivers provide?

Classification society rules set the minimum number of consecutive starts without recharging. The number differs between reversible and non-reversible engine arrangements. Check your class rules and the ship's machinery documentation rather than quoting a figure from another ship.

What is the emergency air compressor used for?

It charges an emergency receiver so that a generator or engine can be started from the dead ship condition without external power. SOLAS Chapter II-1 Regulation 26 requires means to bring machinery into operation from dead ship without external aid. Test it regularly as your PMS requires.

Why does my air compressor keep running and fill slowly?

Common causes are leaking suction or delivery valves, worn piston rings, a choked suction filter, a stuck unloader or leaking drains. Stage pressures and temperatures will help you pinpoint which stage is at fault. Also check the system for air leaks downstream of the receivers.

Test yourself

On a two-stage air compressor, the intercooler pressure is higher than normal. Which fault is most likely?

If the HP stage valves leak, the HP stage cannot take its share and air builds up in the intercooler. LP valve leakage or a choked suction lowers intercooler pressure (general compressor theory, maker's manuals).

What is the most common source of fuel for a starting air line explosion?

Compressor lube oil collects in the starting line and can be ignited by hot gas from a leaking cylinder starting valve. SOLAS II-1 Regulation 34 requires arrangements to reduce oil entering the air system.

During manoeuvring you notice the starting air branch pipe to one cylinder is very hot. What does this indicate?

Hot combustion gas passing a leaking cylinder starting valve heats that branch pipe. This warns of a possible line explosion (engine maker's instructions).

According to SOLAS Chapter II-1 Regulation 34, how should starting air pipes from the receivers to the engines be arranged?

SOLAS II-1 Regulation 34 requires compressor discharge pipes to lead directly to the receivers. It also requires starting pipes from receivers to engines to be separate from the compressor discharge system.

Why is the main engine starting air blocked when the turning gear is engaged?

The turning gear interlock protects the gear and personnel by preventing the engine being started or turned on air while the gear is engaged (class rules and engine maker's requirements).
