Inert Gas System on Tankers: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

An inert gas system (IGS) fills the space above the cargo in tanker tanks with low-oxygen gas so the atmosphere cannot burn. Gas is supplied at no more than 5% oxygen by volume, and the tanks are kept at no more than 8% oxygen under positive pressure. The gas comes from boiler flue gas, an inert gas generator or a nitrogen plant, and both the deck crew who run it during cargo work and the engineers who maintain the plant need to understand it.

Why tankers carry an inert gas system

A fire or explosion needs three things: fuel, oxygen and a source of ignition. In a cargo tank the fuel (hydrocarbon vapour) is always there. Ignition sources such as static discharge, pyrophoric iron sulphide and mechanical sparks can never be ruled out completely. The one thing you can reliably control is oxygen.

If you replace the air in the tank with gas low in oxygen, the atmosphere becomes too oxygen-poor to burn, whatever the hydrocarbon concentration. That is the whole purpose of the IGS.

The IGS is also used to:

  • Keep a positive pressure in the tanks, so air cannot be drawn in during discharge or as the ship cools at night.
  • Make crude oil washing (COW) safe. COW must never be carried out in a tank that is not inerted.
  • Purge hydrocarbons out of a tank before gas freeing, so the atmosphere never passes through the flammable range when air is let in.

The carriage requirement sits in SOLAS Chapter II-2, and the engineering specification is in the FSS Code, Chapter 15. Which ships need an IGS depends on ship type, deadweight, cargo flashpoint and build date. The thresholds were tightened by amendments that apply to tankers built from 2016. For a particular ship, check the Safety Construction certificate record of equipment and the flag's requirements rather than relying on a general rule of thumb.

Main components, from source to tank

There are three common sources of inert gas:

  • Flue gas from the main or auxiliary boiler uptakes. This is typical on crude carriers.
  • An oil-fired inert gas generator (IGG) with its own burner. This is typical on product tankers without large boilers.
  • A nitrogen generator (membrane or pressure-swing adsorption). This is common on chemical tankers and gas carriers, where cargo purity matters.

A flue gas system has the following main components, in order of gas flow:

Component Job
Boiler uptake valves Isolate the IGS from the boiler. They must be kept clean so they seal properly.
Scrubber Cools the gas and washes out soot and most of the sulphur oxides with sea water.
Demister Removes water droplets carried over from the scrubber.
Blowers (fans) Deliver gas to the deck. At least two are fitted.
Gas pressure regulating valve Controls flow to the deck and automatically isolates on shutdown.
Deck water seal The main non-return barrier. It stops hydrocarbon gas flowing back to the machinery spaces. It may be wet, semi-dry or dry type.
Mechanical non-return valve The second non-return device, downstream of the deck seal.
Deck isolating valve Separates the deck main from the plant.
IG main and branches to each tank Distribute the gas, with tank isolating arrangements as fitted.
P/V breaker A liquid-filled device that protects tanks and the main against excessive pressure or vacuum if the P/V valves cannot cope.
Fixed oxygen analyser and pressure recorders Continuously monitor oxygen content and deck main pressure, with indication in the cargo control room and on the bridge or as fitted.

The system must be able to deliver gas at a rate of at least 125% of the ship's maximum cargo discharge rate. This lets it keep up with the pumps and maintain positive pressure.

How it works, step by step

This is a typical flue gas sequence for discharge. Always follow the ship's operating manual and the company's procedures, because valve sequences differ between plants.

  1. Pre-start checks. Confirm sea water supply to the scrubber and deck seal is available. Check the P/V breaker liquid level. Calibrate the fixed oxygen analyser (zero with nitrogen, span with fresh air). Confirm alarms are tested and the deck main is lined up.
  2. Start scrubber water first. Never send hot flue gas into a dry scrubber.
  3. Open the boiler uptake valve. Make sure the boiler is firing steadily with good combustion. The boiler load and air-fuel ratio decide the oxygen content you get.
  4. Start a blower and recirculate or vent to atmosphere until the oxygen reading is steady at or below 5%.
  5. Open the gas regulating valve and deck isolating valve. Gas then passes through the deck seal and non-return valve into the main.
  6. Start discharging cargo. The IGS replaces the volume of cargo pumped out. Watch the deck main pressure and keep it positive. Coordinate pumping rate with IGS output, and never pump faster than the IGS can replace.
  7. Monitor continuously. Watch oxygen content, deck pressure, gas temperature and deck seal level, and log readings at the intervals in your procedures.
  8. Shut down in reverse order. Close the deck isolating valve, stop the blower, close the uptake valve, then stop scrubber water once the gas has cooled.

There are other operations as well:

  • Initial inerting. Done by displacement or dilution until every tank reads 8% oxygen or less.
  • Topping up at sea. Restores positive pressure lost through cooling or small leaks.
  • Purging. Inert gas is blown through the tank to reduce hydrocarbon content before gas freeing. Industry guidance (ISGOTT) is to bring hydrocarbon below about 2% by volume first, so that diluting with air never creates a flammable mixture.

Routine checks and maintenance

Most IGS problems come from soot, salt water and neglect. Good routine work prevents nearly all of them.

  • Oxygen analyser. Calibrate before every use, and cross-check against a calibrated portable meter. A drifting analyser is the most dangerous fault because it gives false confidence.
  • Deck water seal. Check the water level and supply. In cold climates, guard against freezing, for example with heating arrangements or by keeping the supply flowing, as fitted.
  • P/V breaker. Check the liquid level, and maintain the antifreeze mixture in cold weather.
  • Scrubber. Check water flow and inspect spray nozzles for blockage. Look for corrosion, since scrubber water becomes acidic.
  • Demister. Clean or renew it when the pressure drop rises or water carry-over appears downstream.
  • Blowers. Water-wash to remove soot build-up, which causes imbalance. Monitor vibration and bearing temperatures.
  • Boiler uptake valves. Operate them regularly and keep seats clean, using air blowing or soot cleaning where fitted, so they seal properly and do not seize.
  • Mechanical non-return valve and regulating valve. Confirm free movement.
  • Alarms and trips. Test them as per the planned maintenance system. Many terminals and charterer inspectors will ask for test records.

The planned maintenance intervals are set by the maker's manual and the company's PMS. They are not fixed by the convention.

Common faults and what you will see

Symptom Likely causes
High oxygen reading Poor combustion or low boiler load. Air leaking in at blower suction or seals. Analyser out of calibration. On an IGG, a burner air-fuel ratio fault.
Blower vibration Soot deposits unbalancing the impeller. Bearing wear. Water carry-over.
Water in the IG main or deck seal flooding Clogged demister. Scrubber level control fault. Deck seal level control fault.
High gas temperature alarm or trip Loss or reduction of scrubber cooling water.
Low deck main pressure Discharge rate higher than IGS output. Regulating valve fault. Leaking tank openings, P/V valves or hatches.
Hydrocarbon smell near the IG room Deck seal level lost. Non-return valve not seating. This is a serious defect, so stop the operation and investigate.
Uptake valve will not close fully Soot build-up on the seat or spindle.

The FSS Code requires alarms for conditions such as:

  • low scrubber water pressure or flow
  • high scrubber water level
  • high gas temperature
  • blower failure
  • high oxygen content
  • abnormal deck main pressure
  • low deck seal level

Some of these conditions also shut the plant down automatically. Know which ones on your own ship, and know where the alarm panels are in the engine room and the cargo control room.

If the IGS fails during discharge, stop cargo discharge. Do not let air into the tanks. Follow the ship's emergency procedures before resuming.

Safety precautions

  • Inert gas is an asphyxiant. An inerted tank contains almost no oxygen, and one breath can cause collapse. Treat it as an enclosed space, and use the full permit and entry procedure.
  • Flue gas is toxic as well as oxygen-deficient. It contains sulphur oxides, nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide. Before entry, test for oxygen, hydrocarbon and toxic gases, not just one of them.
  • Never vent inert gas at low level. Use the designated mast risers or vent outlets. Keep people clear of the vent outlets.
  • Do not let the tanks go into vacuum. If the P/V valves and the breaker fail, tanks can be structurally damaged.
  • Watch the deck seal. It is the barrier between the cargo deck and the machinery spaces. Losing the seal can bring cargo vapour back towards the boilers.
  • Dip and sample through closed or restricted systems where the tanks are inerted. Avoid opening tanks to atmosphere.
  • Portable gas meters must be calibrated and suitable for measuring in inert atmospheres. A standard catalytic sensor reads incorrectly when there is not enough oxygen.

Oral exam: what the examiner will ask

These questions come up often in CoC orals for both deck and engine candidates. Answer in your own words and refer to your own ship where you can.

  • What is the purpose of the IGS? It reduces oxygen in cargo tanks below the level that will support combustion, keeps the tanks at positive pressure, and allows COW and safe gas freeing.
  • What oxygen limits apply? Gas supplied at 5% or less by volume, and tanks kept at 8% or less with positive pressure.
  • Name the two non-return devices. The deck water seal and the mechanical non-return valve.
  • Why have a scrubber? It cools the gas and removes soot and sulphur compounds, protecting the blowers, the tanks and the people.
  • What is the P/V breaker for? It is the final protection against over-pressure or vacuum if the P/V valves cannot cope.
  • Your fixed analyser shows the oxygen content of the inert gas being supplied rising to 9% during discharge. What do you do? Stop delivering gas to the tanks (the regulating valve and deck isolating valve close, and gas is vented to atmosphere), stop cargo discharge, find the cause (combustion, air leakage or analyser calibration), and resume only when the supplied oxygen is back to 5% or less and the tanks are confirmed at 8% or less under positive pressure.
  • Why purge before gas freeing? So that adding air never takes the tank atmosphere through the flammable range.
  • Why calibrate the analyser before each use? A drifting reading can show a safe value when the atmosphere is not inert.
Key points
  • The IGS removes oxygen from cargo tanks so the atmosphere cannot burn, whatever the hydrocarbon level.
  • Inert gas is supplied at no more than 5% oxygen, and tanks are kept at no more than 8% oxygen under positive pressure.
  • The deck water seal and the mechanical non-return valve stop cargo vapour flowing back to the machinery spaces.
  • The system must deliver at least 125% of the maximum cargo discharge rate.
  • Crude oil washing must only be done in inerted tanks.
  • Calibrate the oxygen analyser before every use, because a false low reading is the most dangerous fault.
  • Inerted tanks are deadly to enter: test for oxygen, hydrocarbon and toxic gases under a permit.

Frequently asked questions

What is the maximum oxygen content allowed in an inerted cargo tank?

Cargo tanks must be kept at an oxygen content of not more than 8% by volume, with positive pressure maintained. The inert gas itself should be supplied at 5% or less. Company procedures may set tighter working limits.

Do all tankers need an inert gas system?

No. It depends on ship type, deadweight, the flashpoint of the cargoes carried and the build date, under SOLAS Chapter II-2. Newer tankers fall under tighter thresholds than older ships. Check the ship's certificates and the flag administration's requirements for the exact application.

What is the difference between a flue gas system and an inert gas generator?

A flue gas system takes exhaust gas from the ship's boiler uptakes and cleans it in a scrubber. An inert gas generator burns fuel in its own combustion chamber specifically to produce inert gas. Generators are common on product tankers that do not have large boilers.

Why does a deck water seal sometimes freeze, and what is done about it?

The seal holds water, so in cold climates it can freeze and lose its function. Ships fitted for cold-weather trading have heating or anti-freeze arrangements. Crew should monitor the seal closely when operating in low temperatures, following the operating manual.

Can I enter a tank once the inert gas has been purged out?

Not just on that basis. Purging reduces hydrocarbons, but the tank is still oxygen-deficient until it has been gas freed with air. Entry needs a permit, ventilation, and tests confirming oxygen at 21%, no flammable gas and toxic gases below exposure limits, as per the company's enclosed space procedure.

Test yourself

What is the main reason a tanker uses an inert gas system?

Inerting removes oxygen so the tank atmosphere cannot burn, whatever the hydrocarbon level. Any reduction in vapour emission or corrosion is incidental (SOLAS Ch. II-2; FSS Code Ch. 15).

What is the maximum oxygen content to which cargo tanks must be maintained?

Tanks are kept at 8% oxygen or less with positive pressure. The 5% figure applies to the gas as supplied (FSS Code Ch. 15).

Which two devices together prevent hydrocarbon gas flowing back from the deck to the machinery spaces?

The deck water seal and the mechanical non-return valve are the two required non-return devices (FSS Code Ch. 15).

During discharge, the fixed analyser shows a sudden rise in oxygen content. Which is the most likely plant cause to check first?

Oxygen content depends on combustion quality and on air leaking in. The analyser calibration should also be verified (FSS Code Ch. 15; ship's IGS manual).

Why are tanks purged with inert gas before gas freeing with air?

Purging brings hydrocarbon content down, typically below about 2% by volume as per industry guidance, so dilution with air stays outside the flammable range (ISGOTT).
