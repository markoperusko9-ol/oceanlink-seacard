SART (Search and Rescue Transponder): A Practical Guide for Ship's Crew

Quick answer

A SART is a portable search and rescue locating device. It is carried on the bridge and taken into survival craft so rescuers can find your exact position in the final stage of a search. A radar SART shows a line of 12 dots on an X-band radar, and an AIS-SART shows a distinctive target on AIS and ECDIS. SOLAS Chapters III and IV require it, and it only works if it is switched on, held high and has a battery within its expiry date.

What a SART is and why you carry it

The EPIRB tells the world that you are in distress and roughly where you are. The SART does the next job: it guides the rescue ship or aircraft onto your liferaft or lifeboat when they are close. In GMDSS terms it is the homing device.

Two types are accepted:

  • Radar SART: a transponder that answers X-band (9 GHz) radar pulses and paints a distinctive signal on the searching vessel's radar screen.
  • AIS-SART: a self-contained transmitter with its own GNSS receiver. It broadcasts its position as AIS messages, which appear on any AIS display, ECDIS or AIS-enabled chart plotter in range.

On board, SARTs are normally stowed on the bridge. This is either one on each bridge wing or inside the wheelhouse near the exits. They usually come with a mounting pole (or the survival craft has a mounting point) and a lanyard.

The person detailed on the muster list grabs the SART on the way to the survival craft. If nobody owns that duty, the SART stays on the bridge, so check your muster list.

Main components

Component Purpose
Buoyant, high-visibility casing Floats if dropped and is easy to spot. Watertight to the depth set in its performance standard.
Antenna (inside the casing) Receives radar pulses and transmits responses (radar SART), or transmits AIS (AIS-SART).
Primary battery (lithium) Non-rechargeable. Expiry date is marked on the unit.
Switch (OFF / TEST / ON) Guarded or sealed against accidental operation. Some units also activate automatically when deployed.
Indicator light and/or buzzer Shows the unit is on and, for a radar SART, that a radar is interrogating it.
Telescopic or sectional pole (usually supplied; some craft have a mounting point instead) Lifts the SART above the survival craft canopy to improve range.
Lanyard Secures the unit to the craft so it is not lost.
GNSS receiver (AIS-SART only) Supplies the position that is broadcast.

The label shows the battery expiry date and the operating instructions. On an AIS-SART it also shows the unit's identity number. AIS-SART identities begin with 970, not a normal ship MMSI.

How it works, step by step

Radar SART

  1. When switched on, the SART goes into standby and listens for X-band radar pulses.
  2. A ship or aircraft radar sweeps over it. The SART detects the pulse and immediately transmits a response that sweeps the 9.2–9.5 GHz band, so it shows up whatever the radar's exact frequency.
  3. The searching radar displays a line of 12 dots stretching outward from the SART's position along its bearing, roughly 8 nm long.
  4. The dot closest to own ship marks the SART. The SART itself is just slightly nearer than that first dot because of the transponder's response delay. Steer for that end of the line, not the middle.
  5. At about 1 nm the dots turn into arcs. When the searcher is very close, they become concentric circles around own ship's centre. At that point, look out of the window.
  6. The SART's light or buzzer changes pattern to tell survivors that a radar is interrogating them. Rescue is close.

An S-band (10 cm) radar will not trigger a radar SART. You must use the X-band (3 cm) set.

AIS-SART

  1. When switched on, the unit gets a GNSS fix.
  2. It transmits position reports in bursts at frequent intervals.
  3. Receiving vessels see a special SART symbol (a circle with a cross), labelled as an active SART. Bearing and range to it are available like any AIS target.
  4. It does not depend on the searcher's radar. It does depend on VHF line of sight and on the searcher watching their AIS or ECDIS.

Detection ranges depend mainly on the height of both antennas. Performance standards are based on a SART mounted at least 1 m above sea level.

  • From a ship's radar, expect only a few miles.
  • From an aircraft at altitude, expect much more.
  • Lying flat in the bottom of a liferaft, the SART performs very poorly.

Using a SART in a survival craft: practical scenario

Scenario: You are the 3rd Officer. The Master orders abandon ship, and your muster duty is the port SART.

  • Collect it: take the SART from its bracket with its pole and lanyard. You do not need to switch it on while still on board with the ship's own radar working nearby. It will not harm anything, but there is no need.
  • Switch it on once in the craft, and secure the lanyard.
  • Mount it high. Assemble the pole and fit the SART at the highest point of the craft or through the canopy port. Never leave it inside the canopy, where the fabric and the people around it reduce performance.
  • Keep it upright and clear of the radar reflector if one is also deployed. A strong reflector echo can mask the SART response.
  • Leave it on. The battery is designed for days of standby and hours of continuous interrogation. Switching it on and off to save power only creates gaps when a searcher might be passing.
  • Watch and listen to the indicator. When it shows interrogation, prepare flares and smoke. A visual signal brings the rescuer the last few hundred metres.

If you are the searching ship (radar SART):

  • Use the X-band radar on the 6 or 12 nm range scale.
  • Reduce or switch off sea and rain clutter suppression. They can wipe out the weak SART dots.
  • Slightly detuning the radar can help pick out the broadband SART response from clutter. Follow your radar maker's guidance.
  • Once you see the 12-dot line, steer for the inner dot and post extra lookouts.

Routine checks and maintenance

Test frequency and the checklist come from your company SMS, the flag administration and the maker's manual. Many fleets use a monthly GMDSS checklist. Typical items:

  • Visual inspection: casing undamaged, no cracks, label legible, switch guard or seal intact, pole and lanyard present.
  • Battery expiry date: in date, with a clear margin before the next survey.
    • The expiry is checked at the annual radio survey.
    • The maker sets the replacement interval.
    • Plan replacements well ahead, because shore service is not available everywhere.
  • Self-test:
    • Radar SART: switch to TEST briefly while watching own X-band radar. A correct test shows concentric rings or arcs and operates the indicator. Keep the test short so you do not alarm other ships in the area.
    • AIS-SART: test mode transmits messages flagged as a test, so they are not taken as a real distress. A full test may need a clear view of the sky to get a GNSS fix. Some units test the GNSS fix only at longer intervals, so follow the manual.
  • Stowage: in its bracket, readily accessible and not locked away. It must be able to go to any survival craft quickly.
  • Records: log tests in the GMDSS radio log or the planned maintenance system, as your SMS requires.

Shore servicing and battery replacement are done only as the manufacturer specifies. Crew do not open sealed units.

Common faults and what they look like

Symptom Likely cause Action
No indication on TEST Exhausted or expired battery, or failed switch Report to the Master, arrange a spare or shore service, and note the defect in the log.
Battery found flat although well within date Unnoticed accidental activation (switch knocked, seal broken) Check the switch guard and stowage. Replace the battery or the unit.
Radar SART test shows nothing on radar but the light works Testing with S-band radar, clutter controls too high, or unit screened by bridge structure Retest on X-band with reduced clutter and the unit in clear view of the scanner.
AIS-SART test shows no position No GNSS fix (tested indoors or under steel) Retest with a clear sky view and allow time for the fix.
Water inside casing or cracked housing Physical damage, often from poor stowage Take it out of service and replace.
Pole or lanyard missing Borrowed or lost Replace before sailing. A SART without its pole loses much of its range.

If the SART is activated by accident, switch it off and inform the Master. Follow company and RCC guidance on whether to notify the coast station.

Regulations (where certain)

  • SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 6 covers carriage of search and rescue locating devices for survival craft:
    • Passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and upwards carry at least one on each side.
    • Cargo ships of 300 GT and upwards but less than 500 GT carry at least one.
    • They must be stowed so they can be rapidly placed in any survival craft.
  • SOLAS Chapter IV, Regulation 7 lists a search and rescue locating device among the radio equipment required on ships to which Chapter IV applies (passenger ships and cargo ships of 300 GT and upwards). The devices carried under III/6 may satisfy this.
  • Either a radar SART or an AIS-SART may meet the requirement.
  • Performance standards:
    • Radar SARTs: IMO Resolution A.802(19) as amended.
    • AIS-SARTs: IMO Resolution MSC.246(83).

The SART is not covered by the LSA Code's appliance chapters in the way lifeboats and liferafts are. Its requirements sit in SOLAS III and IV and the IMO performance standards. Survey and test intervals beyond the annual radio survey vary with your flag administration's requirements and company SMS.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • Q: What frequency band does a radar SART work on? X-band, 9 GHz. S-band radar cannot trigger it.
  • Q: What does a SART look like on the radar screen? A line of 12 dots radiating outward on the SART's bearing. The inner dot is the SART. Close in, the dots become arcs and then concentric circles.
  • Q: How would you make a SART easier to detect on your radar? Use the X-band radar on a 6 or 12 nm range, reduce clutter suppression, and consider slight detuning.
  • Q: How should survivors deploy it? Switched on, on its pole, as high as possible (at least 1 m above the sea), outside the canopy, and left running.
  • Q: How do you identify an AIS-SART? It shows the AIS-SART symbol and has an identity starting with 970. Test transmissions are flagged as tests.
  • Q: How many SARTs does a 30,000 GT bulk carrier need? At least one on each side under SOLAS III/6. Confirm against the Record of Equipment.
  • Q: What is the difference between an EPIRB and a SART? The EPIRB sends the distress alert via satellite. The SART provides local homing in the final stage of a search.

Examiners often follow up with: "Where is the SART on your last ship, and who takes it to the survival craft?" Know the answer.

Key points
  • A SART is the homing device: the EPIRB alerts, the SART guides rescuers to the survival craft.
  • A radar SART answers X-band (9 GHz) radar only and shows 12 dots on the screen. The dot nearest own ship is the SART.
  • An AIS-SART broadcasts its GNSS position, shows a special symbol, and has an identity starting with 970.
  • Mount it high on its pole, outside the canopy, and leave it switched on.
  • Check battery expiry, casing, pole and lanyard regularly, and keep radar tests brief.
  • SOLAS Chapter III Reg 6 and Chapter IV Reg 7 require carriage. Check numbers against the Record of Equipment.
  • Searching ships should use X-band on the 6–12 nm range and reduce clutter suppression.

Frequently asked questions

Will a SART show up on S-band radar?

No. A radar SART only responds to X-band (9 GHz, 3 cm) radar, so use the X-band set when searching for one. If you only watch the S-band display, you can pass close by survivors without seeing anything.

How long does a SART battery last once switched on?

The IMO performance standards require enough capacity for days in standby plus a period of continuous radar interrogation. Exact figures and the battery replacement interval are on the maker's label and in the manual. Never use a unit past its marked battery expiry date.

Can I test the SART at sea?

Yes. Most SMS checklists include a periodic test. For a radar SART, keep the test brief and watch your own X-band radar for the ring pattern, because ships nearby may also see the signal. AIS-SART test messages are flagged as tests, but still follow the maker's instructions and company procedure.

What is the difference between a SART and an EPIRB?

An EPIRB sends a distress alert with your identity and position via satellite to rescue coordination centres anywhere in the world. A SART has a short range and works locally, so the searching ship or aircraft can home onto the survival craft. You need both.

Is an AIS-SART acceptable instead of a radar SART?

Yes. SOLAS allows either a radar SART or an AIS-SART to meet the search and rescue locating device requirement. The Record of Equipment shows which type the ship carries, and some operators carry both.

Test yourself

On the searching ship's radar, a radar SART first appears as a line of dots. Where is the SART actually located?

The 12 dots extend outward from the SART along its bearing, so the inner dot marks its position; rings appear only when the searcher is very close. (IMO SART operational guidance)

Which radar will trigger a radar SART?

Radar SARTs operate in the 9 GHz X-band and do not respond to S-band radars. (IMO Res A.802(19) as amended)

In a liferaft, how should the SART be deployed for best detection?

Range depends heavily on height: performance standards assume mounting at least 1 m above sea level, and the canopy and water reduce performance. (IMO Res A.802(19) as amended)

How can you recognise an AIS-SART on AIS or ECDIS?

AIS-SARTs use identities beginning with 970 and are displayed with a distinctive SART symbol on AIS-capable equipment. (IMO Res MSC.246(83))

Under SOLAS, how many search and rescue locating devices must a cargo ship of 20,000 GT carry as a minimum?

Passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and upwards must carry at least one on each side, stowed for rapid placing in any survival craft. (SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 6)
