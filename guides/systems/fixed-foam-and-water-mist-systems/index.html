Fixed foam and water-mist fire-extinguishing systems on merchant ships

Quick answer

Fixed foam systems smother a fire under a blanket of foam. Low-expansion foam is used mainly on tanker cargo decks, and high-expansion foam can fill machinery spaces. Water-mist systems fight fire with very fine droplets that cool it, displace oxygen with steam and block radiant heat, either across a whole space or on local hot spots such as purifiers and engine fuel pumps. Both are approved under SOLAS Chapter II-2 and the FSS Code, and both depend on routine checks of nozzles, valves, pumps and foam concentrate to work when they are needed.

Purpose and where you will find them

Fixed foam and water-based systems are alternatives or supplements to CO2 and other gas systems. They are chosen because they can be used without first evacuating and sealing a space, or because foam is the right medium for a large hydrocarbon spill fire.

Typical fits on merchant ships:

  • Deck foam system (low-expansion): tanker cargo decks, with monitors and hand applicators covering the cargo area and manifolds.
  • High-expansion foam (total flooding): some machinery spaces and cargo pump-rooms. The space is filled with a large volume of light foam.
  • Local application water-mist (often called LAFFS): category A machinery spaces above a certain volume on ships above certain tonnages, covering fire hazard areas such as the fuel injection areas of engines, boiler fronts, incinerators and heated fuel oil purifiers.
  • Total flooding water mist: an approved alternative to gas systems in some engine rooms, pump-rooms and generator spaces.
  • Water mist in accommodation: approved as an equivalent to conventional sprinklers on many passenger ships and some cargo ships.

The exact system on your ship depends on its type, size, flag and the approved design. The fire control plan and the system manual are your authority, not a generic textbook.

Main components

Fixed foam system

  • Foam concentrate tank. The concentrate type matters (AFFF, fluoroprotein, alcohol-resistant and so on), as does the mixing ratio, commonly 3% or 6%.
  • A water supply from the fire main or a dedicated foam pump, plus a concentrate pump where fitted.
  • A proportioner, which mixes concentrate into water at the correct ratio. Common types are the venturi inductor, the balanced-pressure proportioner and the bladder tank.
  • A foam main with isolating valves, deck monitors and portable applicators or branch pipes.
  • For high-expansion foam: foam generators (fan or water-driven) that aerate the solution, ducting to the protected space, and venting so that air displaced by the foam can escape.

Expansion ratio is the volume of foam produced per volume of solution. As a rough guide, low-expansion foam is up to about 20:1 in industry standards (the FSS Code limits fixed deck foam to not more than 12:1), and high-expansion foam runs from a few hundred up to about 1,000:1. The exact bands differ between standards.

Water-mist system

  • A fresh water tank, often with a sea water backup for prolonged operation where the design allows it.
  • A pump unit. High-pressure systems use positive-displacement pumps. Low-pressure systems use centrifugal pumps. A jockey pump or pressurised accumulator keeps the line charged.
  • Section valves, normally solenoid-operated with manual override, one per protected zone.
  • Nozzles, many with built-in strainers, designed to produce a specific droplet size and spray pattern.
  • Detection (flame and/or heat detectors) and a control panel, with release points on the bridge, in the engine control room and locally.

How they work, step by step

Water mist (local application, typical sequence)

  1. A fire starts at a protected hot spot, for example a heated fuel oil purifier.
  2. Two detectors in that zone confirm the fire. Many systems use coincidence logic to avoid spurious release. The panel alarms on the bridge and in the ECR.
  3. Depending on the approved arrangement and how the space is manned, release is automatic or manual. Manual release is from the panel or from a local push button.
  4. The pump starts and the section valve for that zone opens. Mist discharges only in that zone.
  5. The fine droplets absorb heat fast. Some flash to steam, which locally reduces oxygen, and the mist curtain blocks radiant heat to nearby equipment.
  6. The crew carry out the fire plan in parallel: stop the fuel supply, isolate the purifier, muster, and send a team to boundary cool.

Deck foam on a tanker

  1. Start the fire pump or foam pump and confirm main pressure.
  2. Open the concentrate supply and line up the proportioner.
  3. Open the foam main valve to the required monitor or hydrant.
  4. Aim the monitor so the foam lands on a deck structure or the far side of the spill and flows gently over the fuel. Do not plunge it into the burning liquid.
  5. Build and maintain an unbroken blanket. Keep applying after the flames are out, because re-ignition from hot surfaces is common.

High-expansion foam

After the space is evacuated and a full head count is taken, ventilation is shut down and fuel is stopped. The generators are then started and the space is filled. Foam smothers the fire, excludes air and cools it. Release follows the same discipline as a CO2 release: confirm everyone is out first.

Routine checks and maintenance

IMO guidelines on the maintenance and inspection of fire protection systems set a schedule running from weekly items up to multi-year items. Your planned maintenance system (PMS) should reflect these, together with the maker's manual and any flag or class requirements. Typical shipboard items:

Water mist

  • Check the tank level and that the supply valves are in the correct position, locked or sealed where required.
  • Check that system pressure is in range and that the jockey pump is not cycling excessively.
  • Run the pump unit on test and confirm the alarms and indications at each panel.
  • Inspect nozzles for paint, oil film, damage or missing caps, and clean or replace strainers.
  • Test the section valves and confirm the detection-to-panel logic.
  • Flow test zones as per PMS, usually into a test line or with the agreement of the chief engineer where discharge is involved.

Foam

  • Check the concentrate tank level and confirm the tank is sealed against water ingress and evaporation.
  • Send concentrate samples ashore for analysis at the intervals in the IMO guidelines for periodic control of foam concentrates. Retain the reports.
  • Exercise and grease monitors, valves and swivels, and check that drains are clear.
  • Check that the proportioner and inductors are not blocked.
  • Fresh water flush lines and equipment after any foam discharge or test, because foam residues corrode.
  • Check that high-ex generators and fans run, and that inlets and outlets are unobstructed.

Record everything. Port State Control inspectors and class surveyors often ask for concentrate test certificates and evidence of nozzle and pump tests.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Symptom / how you notice
Blocked nozzles or strainers Poor or uneven spray pattern on test; pressure higher than expected at the pump
Passing section valve Wet nozzles or drips in a zone with no release; jockey pump runs often
Leaks in pipework Pressure drop, frequent jockey pump starts, tank level falling
Detector false alarms Alarms during welding, near hot work or in strong sunlight on flame detectors
Pump fails to start or trips Pump alarm on test, low pressure alarm; check power supply and overload
Foam concentrate degraded Sediment, separation or water contamination; sample fails analysis
Wrong or mixed concentrate Poor foam quality; different types topped up into one tank
Proportioner blocked or mis-set Foam too watery or too stiff; concentrate consumption not matching the design
Seized monitor or valves Cannot train or elevate; valve stiff after long periods of no use

Most of these show up only if you actually run and inspect the system. A pressure gauge in the green tells you very little about nozzle condition.

Safety precautions

  • Isolation for hot work. Hot work near flame detectors may require temporary isolation under the permit to work. Restore it immediately afterwards and log both actions.
  • Blocking release during maintenance. Lock out the system per procedure before working on section valves or nozzles. Tell the bridge and ECR which zones are out of service.
  • Personnel in high-ex foam. A space full of foam gives zero visibility and causes disorientation. Never enter without breathing apparatus and a lifeline, and evacuate fully before release.
  • Water mist and people. Water-based local application systems are considered safe for occupied spaces, unlike CO2. Still clear the area, because a fire is burning there.
  • Electrical equipment. Approved mist systems are designed with electrical hazards in mind, but follow the manual on isolating affected equipment.
  • Fuel source. No extinguishing system holds a pressurised fuel spray fire for long. Use the quick-closing valves and stop the fuel pumps early.
  • Concentrate handling. Wear the PPE given in the safety data sheet and avoid skin and eye contact.
  • PFOS foams. IMO has adopted amendments prohibiting fire-fighting foams containing PFOS. Check with your company and class how and when these apply to your ship's concentrate.

Oral exam: what examiners ask

Expect questions like these at the operational and management level. Answer from your own ship where you can.

  • Where is the water-mist release on your last ship, and what does it protect? Name the zones, the release points and what happens when you press the button.
  • Why water mist rather than CO2 for local application? It can be released without evacuating and sealing the whole engine room. It acts quickly on the hot spot, and it is not an asphyxiation hazard.
  • How does water mist extinguish fire? By cooling, by local oxygen displacement through steam, and by attenuating radiant heat.
  • What is expansion ratio and where would you use low versus high expansion? Low expansion goes on tanker decks and spill fires. High expansion is for total flooding of enclosed spaces.
  • How do you know the foam concentrate is still good? Through periodic sampling and shore analysis per the IMO guidelines and the PMS, plus visual checks of the tank.
  • What do you do after a foam test? Flush with fresh water, restore the valve line-up, top up the concentrate and record it.
  • What must happen before releasing high-ex foam into the engine room? A full head count, ventilation and fuel stopped, openings closed except design vents, and the master's authority.

Examiners look for a clear sequence and for you naming the fuel shut-off. Reciting the regulation alone is not enough.

Key points
  • Foam smothers and cools; water mist cools, displaces oxygen locally with steam and blocks radiant heat.
  • Low-expansion foam is used on tanker decks; high-expansion foam totally floods enclosed spaces.
  • Local application water mist protects engine room hot spots and can be released without evacuating the whole space.
  • Foam concentrate must be periodically sampled and analysed ashore; keep the reports for surveys and PSC.
  • Blocked nozzles and passing section valves are the classic water-mist faults; find them only by testing.
  • Always stop the fuel source; no fixed system will hold a pressurised fuel spray fire on its own.
  • Flush foam lines with fresh water after any use or test and restore the valve line-up.

Frequently asked questions

Is a water-mist system safe to release with people in the engine room?

Water-based local application systems are not an asphyxiation hazard like CO2, so they can be released with the space still occupied. You should still clear the immediate fire area and follow the fire plan. Total flooding systems and high-expansion foam call for full evacuation before release.

What is the difference between high-pressure and low-pressure water mist?

High-pressure systems use positive-displacement pumps to produce very fine droplets with small pipework and nozzles. Low-pressure systems use centrifugal pumps, larger droplets and larger pipe sizes. The pressure bands vary between manufacturers and standards, so check your system manual.

How often is foam concentrate tested?

IMO guidelines for the periodic control of foam concentrates set an initial period after supply and then regular sampling and shore analysis. Your PMS, flag and class requirements govern the exact interval on your ship. Keep the test certificates on board.

Can I top up the foam tank with any foam concentrate?

No. Use only the type and mixing ratio approved for that system. Mixing different concentrates can ruin foam quality and may not meet the approval. Check the label, the system manual and the last analysis report before topping up.

Which regulations cover fixed foam and water-mist systems?

SOLAS Chapter II-2 sets where fixed systems are required. The FSS Code gives the engineering standards: Chapter 6 for fixed foam, Chapter 7 for fixed pressure water-spraying and water-mist systems, and Chapter 14 for fixed deck foam systems. IMO circulars give maintenance and approval guidance.

Test yourself

Which FSS Code chapter covers fixed deck foam systems on tankers?

Fixed deck foam systems are covered in FSS Code Chapter 14. Chapter 5 is fixed gas systems, Chapter 8 is sprinklers and Chapter 9 is fire detection (FSS Code, Chapter 14).

What is the main reason local application water mist is used on engine room hot spots instead of CO2?

Local application water mist attacks the hot spot early and is not an asphyxiation hazard, so the space need not be evacuated and sealed (SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 10).

What should be done after any foam system test or discharge?

Foam residues corrode and block equipment, and the system must be returned to ready status with the correct approved concentrate (system maker's manual and the ship's PMS).
