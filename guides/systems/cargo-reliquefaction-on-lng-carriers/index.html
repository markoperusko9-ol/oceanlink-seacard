Cargo Reliquefaction on LNG Carriers

Quick answer

A reliquefaction plant takes boil-off gas from the cargo tanks, cools it until it condenses, and returns it to the tanks as LNG. This controls tank pressure without burning or wasting cargo. Most plants use a closed nitrogen refrigeration cycle or the boil-off gas itself as the refrigerant, and every plant demands careful cooldown, gas-tight integrity and close watch on tank pressure.

Why LNG carriers need reliquefaction

LNG is carried at about -162°C, close to atmospheric pressure. However good the insulation, some heat always leaks into the tanks. The cargo boils, and this boil-off gas (BOG) pushes tank pressure up. Something has to remove that vapour, or the pressure will eventually lift the tank relief valves.

The boil-off rate is a small fraction of a percent of the cargo per day. The exact figure depends on the containment system, the insulation design and the voyage conditions, so check your ship's cargo manual for the design value.

There are four basic ways to deal with BOG:

  • Burn it as fuel: in boilers, dual-fuel engines or high-pressure gas-injection engines.
  • Burn it without recovering energy: in a gas combustion unit (GCU).
  • Let pressure build within the tank design limits (pressure accumulation).
  • Reliquefy it and send it back to the tanks.

Older steam turbine ships burned almost all their BOG in the boilers. Conventional slow-speed diesel ships cannot burn BOG at all. Dual-fuel ships cannot always use all of it, especially at low speed or in port. On these ships the reliquefaction plant recovers the surplus, and the cargo delivered at discharge stays closer to the cargo loaded.

Types of plant and main components

There are many designs, but most fall into a few families. Always work from your own plant's maker manual and the ship's cargo operations manual.

Plant type Refrigerant Typical application
Full reliquefaction, reverse Brayton cycle Nitrogen in a closed loop Large slow-speed diesel LNG carriers; some dual-fuel ships
Partial reliquefaction (Joule-Thomson type) The BOG itself, compressed for high-pressure gas engines Ships with high-pressure gas-injection main engines
Mixed-refrigerant cycle Blend of hydrocarbons and nitrogen Some newer designs

Main components of a typical nitrogen-cycle plant:

  • BOG compressor: usually a multi-stage centrifugal machine. It takes vapour from the cargo vapour header and raises its pressure so it can condense at a workable temperature.
  • Cold box: insulated housing containing the main cryogenic heat exchanger. This is usually a brazed aluminium plate-fin unit, which is sensitive to thermal shock. The cold box is normally purged or blanketed with nitrogen.
  • Nitrogen compander: compressor stages and an expander on a common gearbox. Energy recovered by the expander helps drive the compressor.
  • Intercoolers and aftercooler: water-cooled exchangers that remove heat of compression from the nitrogen loop.
  • Liquid (phase) separator: separates condensed LNG from non-condensable gas, which is mainly nitrogen.
  • LNG return line: sends the condensed LNG back to the cargo tanks through the cargo piping.
  • Nitrogen make-up and drying: supplied from the ship's nitrogen generator to replace small losses from the loop.
  • Control and safety systems: anti-surge control, temperature and pressure trips, gas detection and emergency shutdown links.

How a nitrogen-cycle plant works, step by step

The plant has two separate circuits that exchange heat inside the cold box: the BOG circuit and the nitrogen circuit.

BOG circuit

  1. Vapour leaves the cargo tanks through the vapour header and reaches the BOG compressor.
  2. The compressor raises the BOG pressure. A higher pressure means the methane condenses at a higher (less cold) temperature, which cuts the refrigeration needed.
  3. The compressed BOG enters the cold box. It is cooled by the cold nitrogen until most of it condenses.
  4. The two-phase mixture goes to the separator. LNG drops out at the bottom.
  5. The LNG is returned to the tanks under level control.
  6. The non-condensable gas, rich in nitrogen, is taken off the separator top. It is usually routed to the GCU or another approved disposal route. Your ship's arrangement decides where it goes.

Nitrogen circuit (closed loop)

  1. Nitrogen is compressed in stages, with cooling after each stage to remove the heat of compression.
  2. The high-pressure nitrogen is pre-cooled in the cold box by returning cold nitrogen.
  3. It then passes through the expander. The expansion drops its temperature well below the condensing point of the BOG.
  4. This very cold nitrogen flows back through the cold box. It absorbs heat from the BOG and from the incoming warm nitrogen.
  5. The warmed nitrogen returns to the compressor suction, and the cycle repeats.

Capacity control. The plant matches its output to the BOG available, so that tank pressure stays steady. Typical methods are:

  • changing the nitrogen inventory in the loop (more nitrogen gives more capacity);
  • adjusting the compressor guide vanes;
  • using recycle or bypass valves.

The cargo control system normally manages the split between fuel consumption, reliquefaction and the GCU.

Start-up, running checks and routine maintenance

Before start-up

  • Check the permit and status of the cargo vapour lines, and the valve line-up against the cargo operations manual.
  • Confirm the cooling water supply, the lube oil levels and the seal gas supply to the compressors.
  • Confirm the nitrogen supply and dryness. Moisture in the loop can freeze in the cold box.
  • Test the gas detection and confirm the ESD links are healthy.

Cooldown

  • Cool the cold box slowly, at or below the maker's maximum rate.
  • Brazed aluminium exchangers can be damaged by large temperature differences between streams. Watch the inlet and outlet temperature spread closely.
  • Do not introduce BOG until the cold box has reached the temperature the maker specifies.

Running checks (each watch or round)

  • Cargo tank pressures and their trend.
  • BOG compressor suction pressure and temperature.
  • Nitrogen loop pressures and temperatures, and the expander outlet temperature.
  • Liquid level in the separator.
  • Compander and BOG compressor vibration and bearing temperatures.
  • Lube oil pressure and temperature.
  • Seal gas pressures.
  • Cooling water temperatures at the intercoolers.
  • Nitrogen make-up consumption. A rising trend points to a loop leak.
  • Gas detector readings in the compressor room and around the cold box.

Maintenance

The maker's planned maintenance schedule and the ship's PMS take precedence. Typical items are:

  • lube oil sampling and filter changes;
  • vibration monitoring;
  • calibration of instruments and trips;
  • inspection of intercooler tubes for fouling;
  • function tests of anti-surge and shutdown systems;
  • leak checks on flanges and seals.

Common faults and symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms Usual cause / first check
Compressor surge Pressure and flow fluctuation, noise, vibration, anti-surge valve hunting Low flow at suction, faulty anti-surge control, restricted suction
Reduced capacity Tank pressure creeping up with the plant apparently running normally Fouled intercoolers, high cooling water temperature, low nitrogen inventory, high nitrogen in BOG
Cold box blockage Rising differential pressure across a stream, poor cooling Moisture freezing in the nitrogen loop; contaminants in BOG
Nitrogen loop leak Frequent make-up, falling loop pressure Seal wear, flange or valve leakage
Expander or compander trip High vibration or bearing temperature alarm, sudden loss of refrigeration Bearing wear, lube oil problem, seal gas failure
Excess non-condensables High flow from separator top, low LNG return High nitrogen content in BOG, typical early in a laden voyage

Nitrogen in the cargo is more volatile than methane, so it boils off first. Early in the laden voyage the BOG can be nitrogen-rich. This reduces the amount that will condense and sends more gas to the GCU.

Worked scenario: plant trips mid-ocean on a laden voyage

  1. Acknowledge the alarm and identify the trip cause from the control system.
  2. Inform the Chief Officer and Chief Engineer as per the company procedures.
  3. Watch tank pressure and its rate of rise. You have time, but it is not unlimited.
  4. Use the alternative routes in your ship's design to take the BOG. Depending on the arrangement, this may mean increasing gas burning in the engines or boilers, or starting the GCU.
  5. Investigate the fault before restarting. Restarting a tripped compander without finding the cause risks damaging it.
  6. When restarting, cool down again to the maker's limits.
  7. Record events in the cargo and engine logs.

Venting cargo to atmosphere is a last-resort safety measure, not a pressure control method.

Safety precautions

Cryogenic hazards

  • LNG and cold nitrogen cause severe cold burns.
  • Spilled LNG can cause brittle fracture of ordinary hull steel. Drip trays and spill protection must be in place where leaks can occur.
  • Wear the PPE the company requires for cryogenic work.

Asphyxiation

  • The cold box and refrigerant loop contain large amounts of nitrogen.
  • A leak into an enclosed or poorly ventilated space can displace oxygen without any warning smell.
  • Treat the inside of the cold box enclosure and any confined area around it as an enclosed space. Test the atmosphere before entry and follow the permit system.

Flammable gas

  • The BOG side is natural gas. The compressor room lies within the cargo area and is a hazardous area.
  • Keep the ventilation running, the gas detection working and doors closed as designed.
  • Use only certified safe equipment in hazardous zones.
  • The gas-tight bulkhead between the compressor room and the motor room, and its shaft seals, must be kept intact.

Trapped liquid

  • LNG trapped between closed valves will expand as it warms and can over-pressurise the pipe.
  • Never isolate a liquid-filled section without a relief path.
  • Never gag or disable relief valves.

Isolation

  • Use lock-out tag-out and the permit to work before opening any part of the system.
  • Purge with nitrogen and gas-free to the levels your company SMS specifies.

Regulation and training (chapter level)

  • SOLAS Chapter VII, Part C makes the IGC Code mandatory for ships carrying liquefied gases in bulk.
  • IGC Code, Chapter 7 (Cargo pressure/temperature control) covers the permitted means of keeping tank pressure below the MARVS. These include reliquefaction, thermal oxidation, use of cargo as fuel and pressure accumulation. It also covers capacity and availability requirements.
  • IGC Code, Chapter 16 covers the use of cargo as fuel. This is relevant because fuel burning and reliquefaction usually work together.
  • IGC Code, Chapter 13 covers instrumentation and automation, including gas detection.
  • The exact arrangement accepted for your ship is set by the flag administration and the classification society. Your cargo operations manual reflects it.

Masters, chief engineer officers, chief mates, second engineer officers and any person with immediate responsibility for cargo operations on liquefied gas tankers need advanced liquefied gas tanker training under STCW Regulation V/1-2. Basic training applies to other officers and ratings assigned specific cargo duties. Endorsement procedures and sea-service requirements vary by administration.

Plant-specific training, often from the maker or the company, is normally required before you stand a cargo watch on a reliquefaction plant. Check your company SMS.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners for gas tanker endorsements and senior certificates often ask questions like these.

Q: Why compress BOG before condensing it? At higher pressure, methane condenses at a warmer temperature. That cuts the refrigeration work needed.

Q: What happens to the nitrogen in the BOG? Most of it does not condense. It is separated in the phase separator and sent to the GCU or another approved route.

Q: Why is cold box cooldown rate limited? Brazed aluminium exchangers can crack under thermal stress if temperature differences are too large.

Q: Name three alternatives if the reliquefaction plant fails. The three main alternatives, as fitted, are:

  • burning more gas in the engines or boilers;
  • using the GCU;
  • controlled pressure accumulation within the tank limits.

Q: What is the main personnel hazard in the cold box area besides cold? Oxygen deficiency from nitrogen leakage.

Q: What does a rising nitrogen make-up rate tell you? There is a leak in the closed nitrogen loop, often at seals or flanges.

Key points
  • Reliquefaction condenses boil-off gas and returns it to the tanks, controlling pressure without losing cargo.
  • Most plants use a closed nitrogen reverse Brayton cycle or the BOG itself as refrigerant.
  • BOG is compressed before cooling because higher pressure raises its condensing temperature.
  • Nitrogen-rich BOG early in a laden voyage reduces condensation and increases flow to the GCU.
  • Cold boxes must be cooled down slowly to the maker's limits to avoid thermal stress damage.
  • Main hazards: cryogenic burns, brittle fracture of steel, flammable gas and nitrogen asphyxiation.
  • IGC Code Chapter 7 governs cargo pressure/temperature control; the ship's approved arrangement is set by flag and class.

Frequently asked questions

Do all LNG carriers have a reliquefaction plant?

No. Many steam turbine ships burn their boil-off gas in the boilers and have no reliquefaction plant. Some dual-fuel ships rely on fuel use and a GCU. Reliquefaction is common on slow-speed diesel ships and on ships with high-pressure gas engines, but the fit depends on the design and owner.

What is the difference between full and partial reliquefaction?

A full reliquefaction plant has its own refrigeration cycle, usually nitrogen. It can condense the BOG without depending on fuel consumption. A partial plant uses the compressed BOG itself as refrigerant. It is usually linked to the high-pressure fuel gas system, so its capacity depends on engine gas consumption.

Why does the GCU run even when the reliquefaction plant is working?

Some gas cannot be condensed, mainly nitrogen. It must be disposed of, often in the GCU. The GCU may also take surplus BOG when the plant and engines together cannot handle all of it. The ship's cargo operations manual sets the actual arrangement.

Who operates the reliquefaction plant on board?

It depends on the company. Typically, the cargo engineer or a designated engineer runs the machinery. The Chief Officer, who is responsible for cargo, controls tank pressure. The division of duties is set in the company SMS and the cargo operations manual.

Can tank pressure simply be allowed to rise if the plant fails?

Pressure accumulation is a recognised means of control under the IGC Code, but only within the tank design limits and the ship's approved arrangement. It buys time; it is not a permanent solution. Follow your ship's procedures and keep the Master and Chief Officer informed.

Test yourself

Why is boil-off gas compressed before it enters the cold box?

Higher pressure raises the condensing temperature of methane, so less refrigeration is needed to condense it. This is a basic thermodynamic principle used in the plant design.

In a nitrogen reverse Brayton plant, where is the lowest temperature in the nitrogen loop produced?

Expansion of the pre-cooled nitrogen in the expander produces the coldest stream. That stream then condenses the BOG in the cold box.

Early in a laden voyage, the plant sends more gas to the GCU than usual. What is the most likely cause?

Nitrogen boils off preferentially and does not condense readily, so more non-condensables leave the separator top and go to the GCU or other approved disposal route.

What is the main reason for limiting the cooldown rate of the cold box?

Plate-fin brazed aluminium exchangers can crack if temperature differences are too large. The maker sets cooldown limits.

A crew member must work near the cold box enclosure. Which hazard is most often underestimated?

Nitrogen leaks displace oxygen without warning. Atmosphere testing and enclosed space procedures under the company SMS apply.
