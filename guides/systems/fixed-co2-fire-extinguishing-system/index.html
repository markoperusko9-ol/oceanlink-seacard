Fixed CO2 Fire-Extinguishing System on Ships

Quick answer

A fixed CO2 system puts out a fire by flooding a closed space with carbon dioxide, which lowers the oxygen level until combustion cannot continue. It is lethal to anyone left inside. It only works if the space is evacuated, sealed and the ventilation and fuel are stopped before release. SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires fixed systems in spaces such as machinery spaces of category A, and the FSS Code, Chapter 5, sets out how gas systems must be designed.

What the system does and where you will find it

CO2 is an inert, colourless and odourless gas that is heavier than air. Discharged into a closed compartment, it pushes the oxygen concentration below the level a fire needs. It also gives some cooling as it expands, but the cooling is minor. The main effect is smothering.

Typical protected spaces on merchant ships:

  • Engine room (machinery space of category A), often including the purifier room and the funnel casing
  • Cargo holds on bulk carriers and general cargo ships
  • Vehicle and ro-ro spaces
  • Paint stores, emergency generator rooms and similar smaller spaces, depending on the design

SOLAS Chapter II-2 says which spaces need a fixed system. It allows CO2, high-expansion foam or water-based systems as alternatives in some spaces, so not every ship's engine room will have CO2. The FSS Code, Chapter 5, covers the engineering of gas systems.

The quantity of gas depends on the volume of the largest single protected space, not the total of all spaces. The FSS Code sets this as a percentage of gross volume:

  • Cargo spaces: 30% of the largest cargo space.
  • Machinery spaces: the larger of 40% of the gross volume excluding part of the casing, or 35% of the gross volume including the casing.

For the engine room, the Code also requires 85% of the gas to be discharged within two minutes.

These are design figures. You do not need to calculate them at sea. You do need to understand that a system sized for the largest space has no reserve for a second discharge.

Main components

There are two types of installation:

  • High-pressure systems use banks of steel cylinders in a dedicated CO2 room.
  • Low-pressure systems keep the CO2 in a refrigerated, insulated tank at a controlled low temperature and pressure. They are more common on large installations.

On a typical high-pressure system you will find:

  • CO2 cylinders, secured in racks, each with a cylinder valve and a discharge head.
  • Pilot (actuating) cylinders, usually small CO2 or nitrogen bottles. Their gas, piped through actuation lines, opens the main cylinder valves.
  • Flexible discharge hoses with non-return valves connecting each cylinder to the manifold. A cylinder can then be removed without the gas escaping through that connection during discharge.
  • Manifold with a safety relief device (bursting disc or relief valve) to protect it from overpressure.
  • Main (master) valve and section/distribution valves. These direct gas to the engine room or to a particular hold.
  • Distribution piping and nozzles inside the protected space.
  • Release cabinet or cabinets, usually one per protected space and clearly marked. Opening the door operates a switch that starts the pre-discharge alarm and, on most ships, stops the ventilation fans.
  • Pre-discharge alarm: a siren and flashing lights inside the protected space.
  • Weighing equipment or a liquid-level gauge for checking the contents.

On many bulk carriers and general cargo ships, the hold CO2 piping is shared with the smoke-detection sampling system. Air is drawn from each hold through the same pipes, and a three-way valve per hold changes the line from detection to CO2 discharge.

On ships built to the current FSS Code, two separate release controls are required for spaces that are normally manned or accessible, such as the engine room and ro-ro spaces:

  1. One control opens the valve to the protected space.
  2. The other discharges the gas from the containers.

The arrangement must ensure they can only be operated in that order. Both controls sit in a release box for that space. If the box is locked, the key must be in a break-glass enclosure next to it.

Older ships and some cargo-hold arrangements may use different release arrangements. Always follow the instructions posted at your ship's release station.

Step-by-step: releasing CO2 into the engine room

The exact sequence is in your ship's fire plan, the operating instructions posted at the release station and the company SMS. Follow those. The general logic is always the same:

  1. Decision. The Master decides to release, normally on the Chief Engineer's advice. CO2 is used when other attempts have failed or the fire is clearly beyond control.
  2. Evacuate and muster. Sound the alarm and get everyone out. Take a full headcount. Nobody releases gas until every person is accounted for.
  3. Stop the air. Stop all ventilation fans and close the fire dampers, skylights, funnel flaps, doors and any other openings.
  4. Stop the fuel. Operate the remote quick-closing valves. Stop the fuel transfer, purifier and boiler fuel pumps, and the lube oil pumps if required, using the emergency stops.
  5. Open the release cabinet. The pre-discharge alarm sounds inside the space. This gives anyone missed a last warning and time to escape.
  6. Operate the controls in sequence. First open the main or section valve to the engine room. Then actuate the pilot cylinders to discharge the bank.
  7. Confirm discharge. Check by gauge, the sound of gas flowing, frosting on the manifold or the indicator, depending on the system.
  8. Keep it sealed. Cool the boundaries with hoses and watch the adjacent spaces for heat spread.

Do not open up to check too early. Hot metal and smouldering material can re-ignite as soon as fresh air gets in. The time to wait depends on the circumstances and is the Master's decision, often with shore and fire-fighting advice.

When re-entering:

  • Use breathing apparatus and a controlled team, with charged hoses ready.
  • Ventilate thoroughly afterwards.
  • Test the atmosphere before anyone enters without breathing apparatus, as for an enclosed space.

Worked scenario: cargo hold fire on a bulk carrier

You are 2nd Officer on watch. The smoke-detection panel alarms for No. 3 hold, and you can see smoke at the sampling cabinet.

  • Confirm and raise the alarm. Call the Master, sound the fire alarm and identify the hold and the cargo. Check the cargo documents and any IMSBC Code guidance for that cargo. For some cargoes, such as ammonium nitrate based fertilisers, CO2 is not effective. Follow the IMSBC Code and EmS advice for that cargo before deciding to release.
  • Seal the hold. Close and secure the hatch covers and stop any hold ventilation. Close the ventilator flaps and access hatches. Make sure nobody is in the hold or trunk.
  • Prepare the release. Change the three-way valve for No. 3 hold from detection to CO2 so gas goes to the right space. Open only that hold's section valve.
  • Release the quantity on the fire plan. Hold systems are often designed so that only part of the bank goes to one hold, and further gas may be released later as the plan allows. Follow the posted instructions.
  • Monitor. Check hatch cover and deck temperatures and look for smoke escaping through leaks. Seal any leaks you find.

A hold fire may need the space kept closed until arrival in port. Do not open the hatches at sea to have a look.

The lesson is this: CO2 only works in a tight space, and a single open ventilator can defeat the whole bank.

Routine checks and maintenance

SOLAS Chapter II-2 requires fire-protection systems to be kept in good order and ready for use, under an onboard maintenance plan. IMO guidance in MSC.1/Circ.1318 sets out inspection and maintenance items for CO2 systems. Your flag state, class society and maker's manual decide the exact intervals and who may do each task. Some tasks must be done by an approved service company.

Typical onboard checks (crew):

  • Release cabinets: Doors close properly, keys are in their break-glass boxes and instructions are posted and legible.
  • Valves: Section valves are in the correct position, with locks or seals intact.
  • CO2 room: Clean and dry, ventilation working, door opens outwards, no stores kept in it and lighting works.
  • Cylinders: Secure in their racks, with no corrosion or damage.
  • Lines: Actuation lines intact and connected, and flexible hoses not kinked or chafed.
  • Alarm test: Pre-discharge alarm and fan stops tested by opening the cabinet, with the release system isolated in accordance with the maker's procedure.
  • Low-pressure tanks: Check the tank level and pressure, and that the refrigeration units and alarms are working.

Pipework: Blow through the distribution piping and nozzles with dry air or nitrogen at the interval in MSC.1/Circ.1318 and the maintenance plan. This is done by crew or a service company, as flag or class require.

Typical service-company tasks:

  • Contents: Check by weighing or by liquid-level indicator. A cylinder that has lost more than the permitted amount is recharged or replaced. A 10% loss is the commonly applied limit, but check your manual.
  • Hoses: Inspect flexible hoses and replace them at the maker's interval.
  • Cylinders: Pressure-test or inspect them at the intervals the flag or class requires.

Record everything in the planned maintenance system. Port State Control inspectors frequently check CO2 rooms, release cabinets and service records.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault What you see
Slow leak from a cylinder valve Cylinder underweight or low reading on the level indicator
Actuation line disconnected or crushed Found on inspection; on discharge, some or all cylinders do not open
Blocked nozzles or piping (scale, debris, paint) Fails the blow-through test
Pre-discharge alarm not sounding Silent or weak siren and dead lights when the cabinet is opened
Fans not tripping on cabinet opening Ventilation still running during the alarm test
Seized section or three-way valve Hard or impossible to turn; corrosion on the spindle
Missing release key or broken glass not replaced Release delayed in an emergency
Low-pressure tank losing refrigeration Tank pressure rising, high-pressure alarm
Accidental discharge into the CO2 room Hissing sound, frost, CO2 room alarm (if fitted)

Each of these can turn a controllable fire into a total loss. They can also cause an accidental release with people in the space. Report defects at once and log them. The Master must know if the system is not fully ready for use.

Safety precautions

  • CO2 kills. At the concentration used for fire fighting, a person in the space loses consciousness quickly and dies. There is no smell to warn you.
  • Headcount first, always. Several fatal accidents have involved crew who were still inside when gas was released.
  • Treat the CO2 room as a hazardous space. Ventilate it before entering. Do not work in it alone unless the SMS specifically permits it.
  • Isolate before maintenance. Before any work on cylinders, actuation lines or the manifold, follow the maker's isolation procedure. This usually means disconnecting or locking out the pilot lines, under a permit to work. Accidental releases have happened during routine servicing.
  • Never test by discharging gas into an occupied space or for drill purposes.
  • Expect gas to travel. CO2 sinks and can collect in lower spaces, bilges, pipe tunnels and adjacent compartments. Test before entering any space near a discharge.
  • Re-entry is enclosed space entry. Use breathing apparatus, atmosphere testing and a permit.
  • Drills. Include the CO2 release sequence in fire drills: musters, headcounts, closing dampers, the quick-closing valves and walking through the release station. Do not discharge any gas.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners like to test the release sequence and the reasons behind each step. Practise answering these aloud:

  • "Walk me through releasing CO2 into the engine room." Answer in order: decision, evacuation and headcount, ventilation and openings, fuel, cabinet and alarm, valve then cylinders, boundary cooling, keeping the space closed.
  • "Why is there a delay and an alarm before release?" To give anyone still inside time to escape.
  • "Why two controls?" So gas cannot be released by a single accidental action. The valve to the space must be open before the cylinders discharge. Under the current FSS Code this applies to spaces that are normally manned or accessible, such as the engine room.
  • "How is the quantity of CO2 determined?" It is based on the gross volume of the largest protected space, using percentages from FSS Code Chapter 5.
  • "How do you check the contents?" By weighing or with a liquid-level indicator, at the intervals in the maintenance plan.
  • "When can you re-enter?" Only when the Master decides. The space must be cool, entered with breathing apparatus and hoses ready, then ventilated and tested before normal entry.
  • "What precautions do you take before working in the CO2 room?" Ventilate, isolate the actuation per the maker's procedure, issue a permit to work and inform the bridge and engine room.

A candidate who forgets the headcount usually does not pass that question.

Key points
  • Fixed CO2 smothers a fire by reducing oxygen in a sealed space and is lethal to anyone left inside.
  • Never release CO2 until a full headcount confirms the space is empty.
  • Stop ventilation, close all openings and shut off fuel before release, otherwise the gas will not hold.
  • On modern installations, release needs two separate controls operated in order: open the valve to the space, then discharge the cylinders.
  • The bank is sized for the largest single protected space, so there is no reserve for a second discharge.
  • Isolate the actuation per the maker's procedure before any maintenance in the CO2 room.
  • Re-entry after discharge is an enclosed space entry: breathing apparatus, testing and the Master's authority.

Frequently asked questions

How long should the engine room be kept closed after a CO2 release?

There is no single fixed time. The Master decides based on boundary temperatures, signs of continuing fire and expert advice. Opening too early lets in fresh air and can cause re-ignition, so the space is usually kept closed well beyond the time the fire seems to be out.

Can the crew release CO2 without the Master's order?

Normally the decision rests with the Master, as set out in the company SMS and the fire plan. Some company procedures cover what to do if the Master cannot be reached. Know your ship's instructions before you need them.

Who weighs or checks the CO2 cylinders?

Contents checks are done by weighing or by a liquid-level indicator, at intervals in the onboard maintenance plan, following IMO guidance and flag or class requirements. Many flags and classes require an approved service company for some tasks. Crew carry out the routine visual and alarm checks.

What is the difference between high-pressure and low-pressure CO2 systems?

High-pressure systems store CO2 in many steel cylinders at ambient temperature. Low-pressure systems store it in one refrigerated, insulated tank at a much lower pressure. Low-pressure systems need their refrigeration and pressure alarms monitored continuously.

Why does the smoke detection system share piping with the hold CO2?

On many cargo ships, air is sampled from each hold through the CO2 distribution pipes, which saves a separate pipe system. A three-way valve for each hold changes the line from detection to discharge. Setting it correctly is part of the release procedure.

Test yourself

What is the main way a fixed CO2 system extinguishes a fire?

CO2 works mainly by smothering: it displaces oxygen in a closed space. Its cooling effect is minor.

Before releasing CO2 into the engine room, which step must come first?

No release may take place until everyone is out and accounted for, because the concentration used is lethal (company fire plan/SMS; posted release instructions).

Why does the FSS Code require two separate release controls?

For spaces normally manned or accessible, one control opens the valve to the protected space and the other discharges the containers, in that order (FSS Code, Chapter 5).

The quantity of CO2 carried is based on what?

The FSS Code sets the required free gas volume as a percentage of the gross volume of the largest protected space (FSS Code, Chapter 5).

Before servicing cylinders in the CO2 room, what should be done?

Accidental releases have happened during maintenance, so the actuation must be isolated first (maker's instructions; company SMS permit-to-work).
