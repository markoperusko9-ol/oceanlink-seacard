Anchoring Equipment on Merchant Ships: How It Works and How to Look After It

Quick answer

A ship's anchoring equipment includes the anchors, chain cable, hawse and spurling pipes, chain stoppers, windlass (gypsy, brake and clutch) and chain locker with its bitter end release. The classification society sizes it from the ship's Equipment Number, and it is built to hold the ship in reasonably sheltered waters, not to stop a moving ship. This guide covers the components, operation, maintenance, faults and typical oral-exam questions.

What anchoring equipment is for

The anchor and cable hold the ship in position at an anchorage, in sheltered water, in reasonable weather. Most of the holding comes from the weight and catenary of the chain lying on the seabed, not from the anchor alone. The anchor stops the cable dragging, and the catenary keeps the pull on the anchor close to horizontal so the flukes stay dug in.

What it is not designed for:

  • Stopping a ship that still has way on. Letting go at speed can strip the brake, part the cable or damage the windlass.
  • Riding out open-sea heavy weather. If a storm is forecast, heaving up and going to sea is often the safer option.
  • Being dropped from the hawse pipe in deep water. The cable picks up speed and the brake may not hold it.

The classification society sets the number and mass of anchors and the size and length of cable from the Equipment Number. This is worked out from moulded displacement, breadth, freeboard plus superstructure and deckhouse heights, and the side profile (windage) area. The common basis for these class rules is IACS Unified Requirement A1. Its design assumptions are reasonably sheltered water and moderate wind and current. Check your class rules for the exact figures that apply to your ship.

Main components, from anchor to bitter end

Component What it does
Anchors Usually stockless types such as Hall, Spek or AC-14 (a high holding power design). Two bower anchors are stowed in the hawse pipes. Some ships also carry a spare or a stern anchor.
Anchor shackle and swivel piece Connect the head of the anchor shank to the cable. The swivel stops turns building up in the cable.
Chain cable Usually stud-link, in grades U1, U2 or U3 with U3 the strongest. It comes in lengths called shackles or shots of 15 fathoms (about 27.5 m), joined by joining shackles.
Joining shackles Usually the lugless Kenter type. Two halves and a stud are held by a tapered pin, which is locked with a lead pellet. D-type end shackles, where fitted, are usually secured with a spile pin.
Hawse pipe Leads the cable from the deck to the shell. It usually has a cover and a hawse wash.
Chain stopper Takes the load off the windlass once the anchor is holding or stowed. It may be a guillotine (bar) stopper or a compressor/bow stopper.
Windlass Hydraulic or electric drive. The gypsy (cable lifter or wildcat) has pockets shaped to fit the links. The band brake holds the gypsy. The dog clutch connects the gypsy to the drive. The windlass usually also has warping ends or drums for mooring.
Spurling (navel) pipe Leads the cable down into the chain locker. Covers or plugs are fitted to stop water getting in at sea.
Chain locker Stows the cable. It has a drain or bilge and a mud box.
Bitter end The inboard end of the cable, secured to the ship's structure. It has a release that can be operated from outside the chain locker, so the cable can be slipped in an emergency.
Lashings / devil's claw Secure the stowed anchor tight in the hawse pipe at sea.

Marking the cable lets the forward team report how many shackles are out. A common method is to paint the joining shackle and a number of links either side of it, and to wind seizing wire round the studs so they can be felt at night. The number of marked links matches the shackle number. Colours and details vary by company, so learn your own ship's system during familiarisation.

How it works: letting go and heaving up, step by step

Preparing the anchor (well before arrival):

  1. Start the windlass power pack or motor and confirm communication with the bridge.
  2. Check that the brake is on and the stopper is engaged. Then remove the lashings and devil's claw.
  3. Open the spurling pipe covers and check the hawse pipe is clear.
  4. Engage the clutch, release the brake, take off the stopper and walk the anchor out under power until it is clear of the hawse pipe. Usually this means near the waterline, or as the Master or SMS directs.
  5. Apply the brake, take out the clutch and report 'anchor ready to let go'.

Letting go in moderate depth:

  1. When ordered, release the brake. The cable runs out under the anchor's weight.
  2. Control the speed with the brake. Check it at the required shackle and report the shackles out as each mark passes.
  3. Once the required scope is out, apply the brake hard, then engage the stopper so the windlass is not left holding the load.
  4. Report the lead and strain of the cable. Leads are usually given as clock positions or 'ahead / on the bow / up and down', with strain as 'tight / slack'.
  5. When the cable rises taut and then dips and stays slack while the ship holds position, report 'brought up'. The bridge confirms the anchor is holding by bearings and position.

Deep-water anchoring: walk the anchor back under power all the way to the bottom, then continue walking back or let go under brake as the Master decides. Each company sets the depth at which this becomes mandatory in its SMS.

Heaving up:

  1. Start the windlass and engage the clutch. Release the brake, heave a little to take the weight off the stopper, then open or remove the stopper.
  2. Heave in. Report shackles remaining, the cable lead and the strain. Ask for engine or thruster help if the cable leads hard.
  3. Report 'anchor aweigh' when the anchor breaks out of the bottom. Report 'anchor sighted and clear' (or fouled) when you can see it.
  4. Wash the cable and anchor with the hawse wash as it comes in.
  5. Stow the anchor home. Apply the brake and stopper, take out the clutch, fit the lashings and devil's claw, and close the spurling pipe covers before sea.

Routine checks and maintenance

The ship's planned maintenance system sets the actual intervals. Under the ISM Code, maintenance must follow that system and be recorded. Typical items:

  • Brake: check the band lining thickness, the linkage and the adjustment. Many companies require periodic brake holding tests to class or maker guidance.
  • Clutch: check the dogs engage fully and move freely. Grease the sliding parts.
  • Gypsy: check the pockets for wear and the whelps for damage.
  • Stoppers: check the pins, bars and hinges move freely and are not wasted.
  • Hydraulics/electrics: check for leaks, oil level, hose condition, motor insulation, and that the limit and overload protection work.
  • Cable: during dry dock ranging, or when the opportunity allows, check:
    • link diameter at the wear points;
    • loose or missing studs;
    • cracks;
    • Kenter shackle pins and lead pellets.
  • Wear limits: class sets the allowable reduction in mean link diameter. Many societies use about 12%, but always measure against your own class's limit.
  • Chain locker: check it is clear of mud, the drains work and the bitter end release operates. Entering the locker is enclosed space entry.
  • Greasing: grease all nipples on the windlass, stoppers and bitter end release. Lubricate the brake and clutch linkages as the maker recommends.
  • Hawse and spurling pipe covers: check they are in place and the seals are intact.

At dry dock, the cable is usually ranged out on the dock bottom, cleaned, gauged and remarked. Shackle lengths are often swapped end for end so wear is spread evenly. The anchors are inspected at the same time.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause
Cable creeps out with the brake fully on Worn or oily brake lining, brake out of adjustment, damaged band
Cable jumps or rides out of the gypsy pockets Worn gypsy pockets, worn or stretched links, cable size not matching the gypsy, cable leading at a bad angle
Clutch will not engage or jumps out Dogs not aligned, worn dogs, seized sliding sleeve, operating linkage bent
Windlass slow or stalls under load Hydraulic pump wear, low oil, relief valve setting, motor overload, or an attempt to break out a heavily set anchor on the windlass alone
Hydraulic oil on deck, overheating Leaking hoses or seals, cooler fouled, prolonged heavy heaving
Anchor will not house snugly Fluke turned the wrong way, mud on the crown, damaged hawse pipe lip
Water in the chain locker / forward space Spurling pipe covers left open in heavy weather, blocked drains
Kenter shackle parts come loose Lead pellet missing, so the pin has worked out

A fouled anchor, with the cable round the stock or flukes, is a common heaving-up problem. Report it to the bridge straight away; do not just keep heaving.

Safety precautions on the forecastle

  • Wear full PPE: helmet, goggles or face shield, gloves and safety shoes. Rust and mud fly off the cable when it runs.
  • Keep out of the bights and the line of the cable. Never stand where a parting cable or a slipping stopper could whip.
  • Keep reliable communication with the bridge. Use a backup radio channel or agreed hand signals. Report clearly and repeat the orders you receive.
  • Never let go with headway beyond what the Master intends. In deep water, walk back.
  • Do not use the windlass to haul the ship up to its anchor in strong wind or current. Ask the bridge for engine help.
  • Isolate the windlass under lock-out tag-out before working on the gypsy, brake or hydraulics.
  • Treat the chain locker as an enclosed space. Use a permit, test the atmosphere and post an attendant.
  • Secure for sea before departure: lashings on, devil's claw on, stopper on, spurling pipe covers closed. Green water on the forecastle can flood the locker through open pipes.
  • Keep the anchor ready to let go in pilotage waters and coastal approaches when the Master or SMS requires it. It is an emergency tool if you lose power or steering.

Worked scenario: dragging at anchor at night

You are OOW at anchor with 6 shackles on deck in 30 m of water. The wind is rising and the anchor watch alarm on the ECDIS or GNSS goes off. The bearings confirm the ship is moving.

  1. Call the Master at once. Ask for the engine to be put on standby.
  2. Send the forward team to the forecastle with radios. Have them report the lead and strain of the cable.
  3. Plot your position against hazards downwind and inform VTS or the port if required.
  4. The Master decides between two options:
    • veer more cable to increase the catenary, if room and conditions allow; or
    • heave up and re-anchor or proceed to sea, using the engine to ease the load on the windlass while heaving.
  5. Show the correct signals and record the events in the logbook.

The lesson for exams: dragging is usually down to too little scope, poor holding ground or weather beyond the design basis. The equipment is rarely the cause.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners for watchkeeping and Chief Mate certificates often ask:

  • 'Name the parts of the windlass and explain what the clutch does.' Gypsy, brake, clutch, drive and warping ends. The clutch connects or disconnects the gypsy from the drive, so you can let go on the brake alone.
  • 'Why do we walk back in deep water?' The cable gains too much speed and energy for the brake to stop it safely, risking a parted cable or a lost anchor.
  • 'Where does the holding power come from?' Mainly the weight and catenary of the cable on the seabed, plus the anchor's grip.
  • 'How long is a shackle?' 15 fathoms, about 27.5 m.
  • 'How do you know the anchor is holding?' Cable behaviour (brought up), steady bearings or GNSS position, and the anchor watch alarm.
  • 'Why must the bitter end release be outside the chain locker?' So the cable can be slipped in an emergency without anyone entering the locker.
  • 'What do you check before going to sea?' Anchor home, brake on, stopper on, lashings and devil's claw on, spurling pipe covers closed, windlass isolated as required.
Key points
  • The class society sizes anchors and cable from the Equipment Number, mainly via IACS UR A1 rules.
  • Holding power comes largely from the cable's weight and catenary, not just the anchor.
  • The equipment is designed for sheltered anchorages, not for stopping a ship or riding out open-sea storms.
  • One shackle of cable is 15 fathoms, about 27.5 m. Marking systems vary by company.
  • In deep water, walk the anchor back under power instead of letting it run from the hawse pipe.
  • Once brought up, the stopper, not the windlass brake, should take the load.
  • Before sea, check: lashings and devil's claw on, stopper on, spurling pipe covers closed.

Frequently asked questions

How many shackles of anchor chain does a ship carry?

It depends on the ship's Equipment Number and class rules, so there is no single figure. Larger ships carry more cable, divided between the two bower anchors. The exact length is on the class equipment documentation.

What is the difference between the windlass brake and the chain stopper?

The brake controls and holds the gypsy while letting go or walking out. The stopper is a separate device that takes the cable load directly to the hull structure. Once the ship is brought up or the anchor is stowed, the stopper should take the load so the windlass and brake are not left carrying it.

How much cable should I put out when anchoring?

Rules of thumb exist, but the right scope depends on depth, holding ground, weather, current, swinging room and company or Master's standing orders. More cable gives a better catenary but a bigger swinging circle. Always follow the Master's orders and your SMS anchoring procedure.

What is a Kenter shackle?

It is a lugless joining shackle used to connect lengths of chain cable. It is about the same size and shape as a common link, so it passes over the gypsy smoothly. It comes apart into two halves and a stud, held by a tapered pin locked with a lead pellet.

Is entering the chain locker considered enclosed space entry?

Yes. A chain locker can have an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, especially where steel is corroding or mud is rotting. Follow your enclosed space entry procedure: permit, ventilation, atmosphere testing and an attendant at the entrance.

Test yourself

Where does most of an anchored ship's holding power normally come from?

The cable lying on the seabed provides much of the holding and keeps the pull on the anchor close to horizontal. This is basic seamanship.

Why is the anchor walked back under power in deep water?

In deep water, a freely running cable builds up energy that can overrun the brake, part the cable or damage the windlass. Company SMS procedures set the depth at which walking back is required.

What is the main reason the bitter end release is fitted outside the chain locker?

Class rules normally call for the inboard end to be releasable from an accessible position outside the locker, so the cable can be slipped quickly and safely.

Once the ship is brought up at anchor, what should be taking the cable load?

The stopper transfers the load to the hull structure, protecting the windlass and brake. This is standard practice in company anchoring procedures.

The cable keeps jumping out of the gypsy pockets while heaving. Which is the most likely cause?

If the links and the gypsy pockets no longer match because of wear, the cable rides out. Gypsy and cable wear are checked under the planned maintenance system required by the ISM Code, Section 10.
