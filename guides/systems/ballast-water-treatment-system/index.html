Ballast Water Treatment System (BWTS) on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

A ballast water treatment system (BWTS, or BWMS in IMO wording) removes or kills organisms in ballast water so the discharge meets the Ballast Water Management Convention's D-2 performance standard. Most shipboard systems filter on uptake and then disinfect with UV or electrochlorination, with neutralisation or a second UV pass on discharge. Crew must run it correctly, keep it maintained, and record every operation, including bypasses, in the Ballast Water Record Book.

Purpose and the rules behind it

Ballast water carries plankton, larvae, bacteria and pathogens from one coast to another. When these are released they can establish themselves as invasive species. The International Convention for the Control and Management of Ships' Ballast Water and Sediments, 2004 (BWM Convention) addresses this. It is a stand-alone convention, not part of SOLAS or MARPOL.

The key regulations you will be asked about:

  • Regulation D-1 (exchange standard): ballast exchange at sea with at least 95% volumetric exchange, or pumping through three times the tank volume. Most ships have now moved past this as their sole method.
  • Regulation D-2 (performance standard): limits on viable organisms and indicator microbes in the discharge. The ship needs an approved treatment system to meet it.
  • Regulation D-3: systems that use Active Substances need extra IMO approval.
  • Regulations B-1 and B-2: each ship carries an approved Ballast Water Management Plan and a Ballast Water Record Book.
  • Regulations E-1 and E-2: surveys (E-1) and issue of the International Ballast Water Management Certificate (E-2) for ships of 400 GT and above.

The D-2 limits are:

  • fewer than 10 viable organisms per m³ of 50 micrometres or more
  • fewer than 10 viable organisms per ml between 10 and 50 micrometres
  • limits on toxicogenic Vibrio cholerae, E. coli and intestinal Enterococci

Systems are type-approved by the flag administration under the IMO BWMS Code. The United States runs its own separate type-approval regime. Some other port states and local authorities add their own requirements as well.

Always check what applies to your ship's trade, flag and next port. Do not assume that IMO approval alone covers you.

Main components

Makers differ a lot, but most systems combine mechanical separation with a disinfection stage. Typical components:

  • Automatic back-flushing filter: a screen or disc filter that removes larger organisms and sediment. A differential pressure (DP) sensor triggers back-flushing, and the back-flush water goes overboard at the uptake location.
  • UV reactor (UV-type systems): medium- or low-pressure UV lamps inside quartz sleeves.
    • A UV intensity sensor monitors the dose.
    • A wiper or clean-in-place (CIP) arrangement keeps the sleeves clear.
    • Lamp power supplies sit in a separate panel.
  • Electrochlorination (EC) unit (EC-type systems): an electrolytic cell powered by a rectifier. It generates oxidant from seawater, either in a side stream or in the full flow.
    • Fitted with a hydrogen degassing/separation arrangement.
    • Fitted with a TRO (total residual oxidant) analyser.
  • Chemical dosing and neutralisation unit: on systems using active substances. A reducing agent, commonly a sulphite or thiosulphate-based chemical, is injected on discharge to bring TRO down to the permitted limit.
  • Flow meter and flow control valve: treatment is only valid within the approved flow range.
  • Control panel / PLC: runs the sequence and logs operating data. It records alarms, bypass events and treated volumes for inspection.
  • Sampling points: for port state or commissioning samples.
  • Bypass valves: usually monitored. Opening them is logged and alarmed.

The system's System Design Limitations are stated on the type-approval certificate and in the manual. Typical examples are salinity, temperature, UV transmittance and maximum flow. Know them for your system.

How it works step by step

The exact sequence depends on the maker's manual and your company SMS. The outline below covers the two most common types.

Filter + UV system, ballasting:

  1. Check that the system is healthy: no active alarms, lamps available, filter clean, sufficient power.
  2. Start the BWTS from the panel. Lamps need warm-up time before full flow is allowed.
  3. Start the ballast pump. Water passes through the filter, then the UV reactor, then into the tanks.
  4. The control system adjusts flow to keep the UV dose above minimum. In murky water with low UV transmittance, it will throttle flow.
  5. When ballasting is complete, stop the pump and stop the system. Some systems run a flush cycle.

Filter + UV system, deballasting:

  1. Water is drawn from the tanks and usually bypasses the filter.
  2. It passes through the UV reactor a second time to kill organisms that regrew in the tanks.
  3. It then goes overboard.

Electrochlorination system, ballasting:

  1. Water is filtered.
  2. Oxidant is generated, either in a side stream or directly, and dosed to a target TRO.
  3. The TRO analyser confirms the dose.
  4. Hydrogen produced at the cell is separated and vented to a safe location.

Electrochlorination system, deballasting:

  1. TRO is measured.
  2. Neutralising agent is injected so the discharge is below the TRO limit set in the system's approval.
  3. Some EC systems need a minimum holding time in the tanks between treatment and discharge.

Worked scenario: you are loading in a river port with very low salinity. A side-stream EC system may not generate enough oxidant from fresh water. Depending on design, makers provide:

  • a seawater or brine supply from a dedicated tank
  • a stored salt solution

The procedure for this must be in the manual and the BWM Plan. Plan it during passage planning, not at the berth.

Routine checks and maintenance

Follow the maker's planned maintenance. Typical items are listed below.

Every operation:

  • Check for alarms.
  • Check filter DP trend, flow rate, and UV intensity or TRO reading.
  • Check chemical levels where applicable.
  • Make the Ballast Water Record Book entry.

UV systems:

  • Record lamp running hours and replace lamps at the maker's interval.
  • Clean quartz sleeves, by wiper check or CIP with the specified cleaning agent.
  • Verify the UV sensor and calibrate as specified.

EC systems:

  • Inspect and clean the electrode cell (scale build-up is common).
  • Check rectifier cooling.
  • Check the hydrogen detection and venting arrangement.
  • Calibrate the TRO analyser and replace reagents before expiry.

Filter:

  • Inspect the screen for tears or blockage.
  • Check that the back-flush motor and valves work.

Long idle periods:

  • Some systems need flushing or must be kept filled with fresh water to protect sensors and sleeves.
  • Follow the lay-up procedure in the manual.

Keep spares for the critical items: lamps, sleeves, sensors, reagents and neutralising chemical. A dead TRO analyser or a shortage of neutralising chemical can stop deballasting just as effectively as a pump failure.

The control system's data logs are evidence. Port state control inspectors may ask to see them alongside the record book, and the two should agree.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptom Usual cause / first check
Filter high DP Continuous back-flushing, flow reduced, system trips Turbid or silty water, damaged back-flush mechanism, clogged screen
Low UV intensity Flow throttled or system stops Fouled sleeves, aged lamps, low UV transmittance water, sensor fault
Lamp failure Lamp alarm, reduced capacity End of life, power supply fault, cooling problem
Low TRO on uptake Dose alarm, system cannot reach setpoint Low salinity, low temperature, scaled electrodes, rectifier fault
High TRO on discharge Discharge blocked Neutraliser empty or dosing pump failure, analyser fault
Hydrogen alarm Shutdown of EC unit Degassing fault, ventilation failure, leak
Flow outside approved range Alarm or interlock Wrong pump combination, valve line-up error, flow meter fault
Communication / PLC fault No data logging, cannot start Power supply, network, software

When the system fails, do not simply open the bypass and carry on.

  1. Inform the Master and the Chief Engineer.
  2. Follow the BWM Plan.
  3. Contact the company.
  4. Notify the relevant port or coastal state as required.

IMO guidance on contingency measures exists, but what is acceptable is decided by the port state concerned. Options may include:

  • exchange where permitted
  • discharge to a reception facility or another ship
  • retaining ballast

Record everything.

Safety precautions

  • Electrical: UV power supplies and EC rectifiers carry high currents. Isolate and lock out before opening panels or reactors.
  • UV radiation: never view a lit lamp. Reactors are interlocked for a reason, so do not defeat them.
  • Mercury: UV lamps contain mercury. Handle broken lamps per the maker's instructions and dispose of them as hazardous waste.
  • Hydrogen: EC systems produce hydrogen. Ventilation and gas detection must work, and no hot work goes near the vent outlets.
  • Chemicals: oxidants, neutralisers and cleaning acids are hazardous. Read the SDS and wear the correct PPE: goggles, gloves and apron. Store chemicals separately as the manual specifies.
  • Tankers and gas carriers: where BWTS components sit in or near hazardous areas, equipment must be suitable for that zone. Follow the ship's specific arrangements.
  • Ballast tanks: treated tanks may hold residual oxidant, and tanks generally can be oxygen-deficient. Ballast tank entry is enclosed space entry, with a permit, gas testing and a standby person.

Oral exam style questions

Q: What is the difference between D-1 and D-2? D-1 is ballast exchange, a volumetric standard. D-2 is a performance standard on the discharge itself. It limits viable organisms by size class and limits indicator microbes, and in practice needs a type-approved treatment system.

Q: Why does a UV system treat on both ballasting and deballasting? Organisms can survive or regrow in the tanks. A second UV pass on discharge ensures the water leaving the ship meets D-2. The filter is normally bypassed on discharge.

Q: What is TRO and why neutralise it? TRO is total residual oxidant from chlorination. Discharging it would harm the receiving environment, so it is reduced to below the approved limit with a neutralising agent before overboard discharge.

Q: Your BWTS fails in port before deballasting. What do you do? 1. Do not discharge untreated water on your own decision. 2. Inform the Master, Chief Engineer and company. 3. Follow the BWM Plan contingency section. 4. Contact the port state. 5. Attempt repair. 6. Record the failure and the actions taken.

Q: What documents does the PSC officer expect? - International Ballast Water Management Certificate (or flag equivalent) - approved BWM Plan - Ballast Water Record Book - BWTS type-approval certificate and manual - crew familiarity with operation - often the system's logged data

Q: What are System Design Limitations? The parameters within which the system was proven to work, such as salinity, temperature, flow and UV transmittance. Operating outside them may mean the discharge does not meet D-2.

Key points
  • A BWTS treats ballast so the discharge meets the D-2 performance standard of the BWM Convention 2004.
  • Most systems use filtration plus UV or electrochlorination; chemical-based systems neutralise TRO before discharge.
  • UV systems normally treat on uptake and again on discharge, bypassing the filter on discharge.
  • Know your system's design limitations: salinity, temperature, flow and UV transmittance.
  • Every operation, failure and bypass goes in the Ballast Water Record Book and should match the control system log.
  • On failure, follow the BWM Plan and contact the port state; contingency options are decided by that state.
  • Hazards include high current, UV radiation, mercury, hydrogen and treatment chemicals.

Frequently asked questions

Is ballast water treatment covered by MARPOL?

No. Ballast water is covered by the separate International Convention for the Control and Management of Ships' Ballast Water and Sediments, 2004. It has its own certificate, plan and record book requirements.

Can we use the BWTS bypass if the system trips?

Only in a genuine emergency or as permitted under the BWM Plan and the port state's direction. Bypass events are logged by the system and must be recorded and reported. Discharging untreated ballast where D-2 applies can lead to detention or penalties.

Why does the UV system reduce ballast flow in some ports?

Turbid or coloured water has low UV transmittance, so the lamps cannot deliver the required dose at full flow. The control system throttles flow to keep the dose above the approved minimum. Clean sleeves and healthy lamps help, but water quality is the main factor.

Who operates the ballast water treatment system on board?

It varies by company. Typically the Chief Officer is responsible for ballast operations and the record book, while the engine department maintains the equipment. The Ballast Water Management Plan names the designated officer responsible.

Is an IMO type-approved BWTS accepted in US waters?

Not automatically. The US runs its own type-approval regime for ballast water management systems. Check your system's approvals and current US requirements before trading there.

Test yourself

Which regulation of the BWM Convention sets the ballast water performance standard?

D-2 is the performance standard; D-1 is exchange, B-1 the BWM Plan and E-1 surveys (BWM Convention 2004).

On a typical filter + UV system, what happens during deballasting?

UV leaves no residual, so organisms surviving or regrowing in tanks are treated by a second UV pass on discharge (maker's manual / type-approval documentation).

What is the main purpose of neutralisation in an electrochlorination BWTS?

Residual oxidant would harm the receiving environment, so it is neutralised to below the limit set in the system's approval before discharge (systems using Active Substances are approved under BWM Convention Regulation D-3).

A side-stream electrochlorination system cannot reach its TRO setpoint while ballasting in a river port. What is the most likely cause?

Electrolysis relies on chloride in the water, so low salinity reduces oxidant generation; this is one of the system design limitations (BWMS Code).

Which document must record ballast operations, including treatment failures and bypasses?

Ballast operations are recorded in the Ballast Water Record Book (BWM Convention Regulation B-2).
