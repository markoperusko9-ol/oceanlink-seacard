Shaft Generator on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

A shaft generator is an alternator driven by the main engine or propulsion shafting. It supplies the ship's electrical load at sea so the diesel generators can be stopped, which saves fuel and running hours. Because its output depends on shaft speed, it is managed by frequency control, interlocks and strict changeover routines. It has clear limits during manoeuvring, heavy weather and engine slowdowns.

What a shaft generator does and why ships fit one

A shaft generator (SG) converts some of the main engine's output into electrical power. On a long sea passage it can carry the whole hotel and machinery load, so the auxiliary diesel generators can be shut down.

The case for fitting one is practical:

  • Fuel efficiency. A large slow-speed main engine usually has a lower specific fuel consumption than the four-stroke auxiliaries.
  • Fewer running hours on the generators. This means fewer overhauls, less lube oil consumption and less spare-part use.
  • Lower noise and maintenance load in the engine room during long passages.

The trade-off is that the SG is tied to propulsion. When the shaft slows down, stops or goes astern, the SG may not be able to supply power. Almost every operating rule around shaft generators comes back to this one fact.

Many installations can also run in reverse as a motor:

  • PTO (power take-off): generator mode.
  • PTI (power take-in): motor mode, boosting propulsion using electrical power from the generators.
  • Power take-home (PTH): some designs can provide limited propulsion if the main engine is unavailable.

Main types and components

Designs vary widely, so always learn the arrangement on your own ship from the manufacturer's manual. The common arrangements are:

Arrangement How frequency is held Typical fit
Gear-driven SG with controllable pitch propeller (CPP) Engine runs at constant speed; thrust is changed by pitch Ferries, offshore vessels, some tankers
SG with static frequency converter Variable-frequency output is rectified, then inverted to a fixed frequency Fixed pitch propeller (FPP) ships needing a usable rpm range
Constant-speed mechanical drive A controlled planetary gear holds generator speed while engine rpm varies Various, less common now
Shaft-mounted alternator on the intermediate shaft Low-speed, many-pole machine, normally with a converter Newer builds, PTO/PTI hybrids

Typical components:

  • PTO gearbox or step-up gear. Driven from the engine's free end or from the shafting. It has its own lube oil pump, filter and cooler.
  • Clutch. Usually a hydraulically or pneumatically operated multi-disc clutch, plus a flexible coupling. It lets you disconnect the SG from the shaft.
  • Alternator. Usually a brushless synchronous machine with an exciter, rotating diodes, an AVR and anti-condensation heaters. It is air or water cooled.
  • Frequency converter (if fitted). Contains a rectifier, DC link and inverter, with its own cooling and fault monitoring.
  • Synchronous condenser (on older line-commutated thyristor systems). This rotating machine supplies reactive power. It also gives the converter its voltage reference and provides fault current to trip breakers. Modern PWM (IGBT) converters often do not need one.
  • Main switchboard section. Includes the SG air circuit breaker, synchroscope or auto-synchroniser, and protection relays.
  • Interface to the power management system (PMS) and main engine control. This handles rpm and pitch permissives, load limits and standby generator start.

How it works and a typical changeover

The SG cannot supply anything until the shaft is turning within its permitted speed range. That speed range, and any clutch-in limits, are set by the maker and your ship's procedures.

Worked scenario: full away, transferring load from the diesel generators to the SG (typical PMS-assisted sequence)

  1. Bridge confirms full away. The engine is at steady sea rpm, or in constant-speed mode for a CPP ship.
  2. Check the PTO gearbox lube oil pressure, the clutch hydraulic or air pressure, and the cooling to the SG and converter.
  3. Engage the clutch. Some designs only allow clutching in at standstill or at low rpm. Others allow it under way. Follow the interlocks; never bypass them.
  4. Build up voltage and check the frequency. Excitation comes on, and on converter systems the inverter starts. Confirm that voltage and frequency are steady.
  5. Synchronise and close the SG breaker. Use the auto-synchroniser or the manual synchroscope according to the procedure.
  6. Transfer load. The PMS ramps load onto the SG and unloads the diesel generators. Watch for reverse power on the outgoing set.
  7. Open the diesel generator breaker and stop the set. Leave it in standby with auto-start selected, unless the SMS says otherwise.
  8. Log the changeover and confirm the main engine load is within its limits with the SG load added.

Reverse sequence before manoeuvring (end of sea passage): start the diesel generators in good time and parallel them. Take the load off the SG, open its breaker and declutch if required. Do all of this before the bridge starts reducing speed for the pilot station.

On many fixed-ratio systems, paralleling the SG with diesel generators is only meant for brief load transfer. Converter-type systems may allow longer parallel running. Your PMS and maker's manual are the authority here.

Operating limits you must respect

  • Manoeuvring on FPP ships. A direct-reversing engine stops and reverses during manoeuvring, so the SG will drop out. Many company procedures require the diesel generators to carry the load in port approaches, narrow waters and during manoeuvring.
  • CPP ships are different. With the engine at constant speed, the SG can often run during manoeuvring and may even feed the bow thruster. That is a design feature, not a general rule.
  • Main engine slowdown or shutdown. If the engine safety system slows or stops the engine, the SG can trip on underfrequency or undervoltage. If no diesel generator is standing by, the result is a blackout.
  • Heavy weather. Propeller racing and governor action cause frequency and voltage swings. Many ships change back to the diesel generators when the motion becomes heavy.
  • Main engine margin. The SG load is added to the propeller load. With a fouled hull, heavy weather or reduced engine performance, you may need to limit the SG load.
  • Barred speed range. Fitting a PTO changes the torsional behaviour of the shafting. Respect any rpm restrictions marked at the control stations.

Routine checks and maintenance

Exact intervals come from the planned maintenance system, the maker and class. The following are the typical watch and routine items.

Each watch or round:

  • PTO gearbox lube oil pressure, temperature and sump level.
  • Bearing temperatures on the alternator and gearbox.
  • Stator winding temperature.
  • Cooling air or water temperatures, and the cooler leak detector if fitted.
  • Abnormal noise, vibration or oil leaks around the gearbox and clutch.
  • Converter cabinet alarms, cooling fans or water cooling, and room temperature.
  • Load, power factor and frequency stability at the switchboard.

Periodic:

  • Gearbox oil analysis. Inspect the filters and magnetic plugs for metal particles.
  • Check clutch operating pressure and engagement time.
  • Check alignment and the condition of the flexible coupling.
  • Insulation resistance test of the windings, done only with the machine isolated. Disconnect the AVR, converter and other electronics first so the test voltage does not damage them.
  • Confirm the anti-condensation heaters work when the machine is idle.
  • Clean the converter filters and heat sinks.
  • Test the protection relays: reverse power, overcurrent, under/over voltage and under/over frequency. Do this during class or shore-supported testing as scheduled.
  • Functional test of the standby generator auto-start and blackout recovery with the SG on load. Do this only under a controlled, planned procedure.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes to check
Frequency or voltage hunting at sea Heavy weather and propeller racing, governor issues, AVR fault, converter control fault
SG trips when bridge reduces rpm Below minimum permitted speed, underfrequency or undervoltage protection acting as designed
Clutch will not engage, or slips Low hydraulic or air pressure, interlock not satisfied, worn plates, solenoid valve fault
High gearbox bearing temperature or new noise Lube oil pressure or cooling problem, contamination, bearing or gear damage
Reverse power trip during changeover Poor load sharing during transfer, wrong sequence, governor response
Low insulation resistance after a long idle period Moisture in the windings, heaters not working, cooler leak
Converter fault alarm or derating Cooling failure, blocked filters, power semiconductor fault, DC link fault
Breakers fail to trip selectively on a downstream fault (older thyristor plants) Insufficient short-circuit contribution from the synchronous condenser or system (e.g. condenser undersized, faulty excitation, or incorrect protection settings)

If the SG fails at sea, confirm the standby diesel generator has taken the load. Inform the bridge and the chief engineer. Do not attempt to restore the SG until the cause is understood.

Safety precautions and regulations

  • A shaft can turn the SG even when the engine is stopped. Water flow over the propeller or tidal current alongside can rotate the shafting, and residual or permanent magnetism can then produce a dangerous voltage. Before working on the SG, gearbox or converter:
    • declutch the SG where a clutch is fitted (shaft-mounted alternators usually have none, so the shaft lock/turning gear is essential);
    • engage the turning gear or shaft lock as per procedure;
    • rack out and lock the breaker;
    • apply lock-out tag-out under a permit to work.
  • Converter DC links store energy. Wait the discharge time stated by the maker and prove the circuit dead before touching it.
  • Keep coupling and shaft guards in place. Never reach near rotating shafting.
  • Never defeat interlocks or protections to keep the SG online.
  • Treat manoeuvring power changeovers as a bridge/engine team activity, agreed before the pilot station.

Regulations, at the level of certainty:

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part D sets the general requirements for the main source of electrical power. That includes having sufficient generating sets so the ship is not dependent on a single one. Regulation II-1/41.1.3 requires the main source of electrical power to maintain essential services regardless of the speed and direction of rotation of the propulsion machinery or shafting, which is why a shaft generator cannot be the only source relied on.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part E adds requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces, including electrical supply continuity.
  • Whether a shaft generator may count as one of the required main generating sets depends on classification society rules and the flag administration. Check your ship's class documentation rather than assuming.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why is a shaft generator usually taken off load before manoeuvring on an FPP ship? A: The engine is reversed and stopped during manoeuvring, so shaft speed and frequency cannot be maintained. The diesel generators must be on load first to avoid a blackout.

Q: How is constant frequency achieved when engine rpm varies? A: There are three ways: running the engine at constant speed with a CPP, using a static frequency converter, or using a constant-speed mechanical gear.

Q: What is the purpose of a synchronous condenser? A: On line-commutated thyristor converter systems, it supplies reactive power and a voltage reference. It also provides short-circuit current so protective devices operate.

Q: What happens if the main engine goes into automatic slowdown with the SG carrying the load? A: The SG may trip on underfrequency or undervoltage. The standby diesel generator must auto-start and connect, or the ship blacks out. This is why standby selection is checked after every changeover.

Q: What precautions do you take before an insulation test on the SG? A: Isolate it, declutch it where a clutch is fitted and lock the shaft. Disconnect the AVR, converter and other electronics, then follow the permit and lock-out procedure.

Key points
  • A shaft generator turns main engine power into electricity so diesel generators can be stopped at sea.
  • Its output depends on shaft speed, so frequency is held by CPP constant speed, a frequency converter or a constant-speed gear.
  • On FPP ships the diesel generators normally take the load before manoeuvring, because the SG drops out when the engine stops or reverses.
  • A main engine slowdown can trip the SG, so standby diesel generator auto-start must always be available.
  • The shafting can turn the SG even with the engine stopped, so declutch where fitted, lock the shaft and isolate before maintenance.
  • Watch PTO gearbox lube oil, bearing and winding temperatures, clutch pressure and converter cooling on every round.
  • Whether an SG counts as a SOLAS main generating set is decided by class and flag, not assumed.

Frequently asked questions

Can a shaft generator run in parallel with diesel generators?

It depends on the design. Many fixed-ratio systems only allow brief paralleling for load transfer, because shaft speed varies with sea conditions and engine governing. Converter-type systems may allow longer parallel running. Follow the PMS and maker's instructions for your ship.

Why does the shaft generator trip in heavy weather?

Propeller racing and governor response cause shaft speed to fluctuate, which shows up as frequency and voltage swings. If these exceed the protection settings, the SG trips. Many ships transfer the load to the diesel generators before conditions get that bad.

Can the shaft generator be used during manoeuvring?

On FPP ships with a direct-reversing engine, generally not, and company procedures usually require diesel generators on load. On CPP ships running at constant engine speed, the SG is often designed to run during manoeuvring and may supply the bow thruster. Always follow your SMS.

What is PTO and PTI on a shaft generator?

PTO (power take-off) means the machine is generating electricity from the shaft. PTI (power take-in) means it is running as a motor, using electrical power to add propulsion power. Some systems also offer a power take-home mode if the main engine is unavailable.

Does a shaft generator count as one of the required main generators under SOLAS?

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part D requires adequate main generating capacity that does not rely on a single source. Whether a particular shaft generator is accepted as one of those sets is decided by the classification society and the flag administration. Check the ship's class approval documents.

Test yourself

On an FPP ship with a direct-reversing main engine, when should the diesel generators normally take over from the shaft generator?

The SG cannot hold frequency while the engine stops and reverses, so the diesel generators must be on load before manoeuvring. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 41.1.3 (main source of power independent of propulsion shaft speed and direction) and company SMS procedures.

What is the main purpose of a synchronous condenser in a thyristor-converter shaft generator system?

Line-commutated converters cannot supply reactive or short-circuit current, so a synchronous condenser provides it. Reference: shaft generator system design per maker's manual and class-approved arrangement.

Before working on the SG while alongside with the main engine stopped, which precaution addresses the risk of the shaft turning the generator?

Water flow over the propeller can rotate the shafting, and residual magnetism can produce voltage, so mechanical and electrical isolation are both needed. Reference: company permit to work and lock-out tag-out procedures.

The bridge reduces rpm and the SG trips, causing a blackout. Which item was most likely missed after the changeover?

Underfrequency or undervoltage trips on speed reduction are expected, so a standby set with auto-start is the defence against blackout. Reference: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Parts D and E on continuity of electrical supply.

Which arrangement allows a shaft generator to give fixed frequency while an FPP engine varies its rpm over a range?

The converter rectifies the variable-frequency output and inverts it to fixed frequency. Reference: shaft generator system design per maker's manual and class-approved arrangement.
