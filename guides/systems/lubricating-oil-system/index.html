Lubricating Oil System on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide

Quick answer

The lubricating oil system draws oil from the engine sump and pumps it through coolers and filters to the bearings, then lets it drain back. On a two-stroke main engine it often also cools the pistons. A purifier cleans the oil continuously. Your job on watch is to keep oil pressure, temperature, cleanliness and quantity within the maker's limits, and to treat oil mist or low-pressure alarms as serious warnings.

What the lubricating oil system does

Lube oil does far more than reduce friction. In a typical marine diesel it:

  • Lubricates main, crankpin, crosshead and camshaft bearings by keeping a hydrodynamic oil film between journal and shell.
  • Cools bearings and, on many two-stroke engines, the piston crowns (oil-cooled pistons).
  • Cleans the engine by carrying wear particles and combustion products to the filters and purifier.
  • Protects surfaces against corrosion. Detergent and alkaline additives also neutralise acids.
  • Acts as hydraulic oil on many electronically controlled engines, where system oil feeds the hydraulic power supply for fuel injection and exhaust valve actuation.

On a slow-speed two-stroke there are usually separate oils with separate systems:

Oil Where it goes Fate
System oil Bearings, crosshead, piston cooling, often turbocharger Recirculated
Cylinder oil Liner walls via cylinder lubricators Consumed (total loss)
Stern tube oil Stern tube bearing and seals Recirculated in its own small system

Some designs also have a separate camshaft or turbocharger lube system. Four-stroke auxiliary engines normally have a single system with a wet sump in the engine bedplate.

Main components

A typical main engine system oil circuit contains the following:

  • Sump (drain) tank. Usually a double-bottom tank under the engine, with oil draining into it from the crankcase. It has a sounding pipe with a self-closing cock and level alarms.
  • Suction strainers. Coarse strainers that protect the pumps.
  • Main LO pumps. Normally two electric pumps, one running and one on standby with automatic start on low pressure. Auxiliary engines usually have an engine-driven pump plus an electric pre-lubrication pump.
  • LO cooler. Plate or shell-and-tube, normally cooled by the central fresh water system.
  • Thermostatic (three-way) valve. Bypasses part of the oil around the cooler to hold inlet temperature at the set point.
  • Fine filters. Often an automatic back-flushing filter with a manual duplex or bypass filter. A differential pressure gauge or alarm is fitted.
  • Engine inlet main. Supplies the main bearings. Branches go to the crosshead and piston cooling through telescopic pipes or swinging links.
  • Purifier (separator) and heater. Takes oil from the sump, cleans it and returns it to the sump, running continuously at sea.
  • Storage and settling tanks. Hold oil for top-up and renewal.
  • Monitoring. Inlet pressure and temperature, bearing or outlet temperatures, sump level, filter differential pressure, oil mist detector and flow indications.
  • Crankcase explosion relief valves. Fitted on crankcase doors of engines above the size thresholds set by SOLAS and class.

How it works, step by step

Main engine system oil, in circulation order:

  1. Suction. The running main LO pump draws oil from the sump tank through the suction strainer.
  2. Pressure control. A pressure-regulating or relief valve sets system pressure and protects against overpressure.
  3. Cooling. Oil passes to the LO cooler. The thermostatic valve mixes cooled and bypassed oil so the engine inlet temperature stays at the maker's set point.
  4. Filtration. The automatic back-flush filter removes fine particles. Back-flushed dirty oil goes to the sump, sometimes via a separate chamber or settling arrangement, depending on design.
  5. Distribution. The inlet main feeds the main bearings. Oil then reaches the crankpin bearings through drilled passages, or from the crosshead side depending on design. Telescopic or articulated pipes deliver oil to the crosshead bearing and up the piston rod to cool the piston crown.
  6. Return. Oil drains from bearings and piston cooling outlets into the crankcase and falls back into the sump tank. Piston cooling outlets are often fitted with temperature sensing and flow indication.
  7. Cleaning in parallel. The purifier continuously draws from the sump, heats the oil and removes water and solids. Sludge goes to the sludge tank.

Before starting: run the LO pump to establish pressure and flow at all bearings, and confirm the purifier and alarms are in service. For auxiliary engines, pre-lubrication is normally continuous or intermittent while on standby. Follow the maker's instructions and your company SMS.

Routine checks and maintenance

Every watch / UMS round

  • Inlet pressure and temperature against the normal running values and the maker's limits.
  • Filter differential pressure and auto-filter back-flush frequency. Rising frequency is an early warning of contamination.
  • Sump level. Compare it with previous soundings and consumption. A sudden rise suggests water or fuel ingress. A sudden fall suggests a leak.
  • Purifier running, with normal back-pressure and temperature and no abnormal discharge.
  • Look and listen: leaks at flanges, filter covers, and around hot surfaces and turbocharger connections.

Periodic

  • Shore laboratory oil analysis at the interval set by the company, maker or oil supplier. Typical parameters are viscosity, water content, base number (BN), insolubles, wear metals and, for four-stroke engines, flash point (fuel dilution).
  • Onboard test kits between lab samples, for water, viscosity, BN and spot test.
  • Cleaning of the cooler, strainers and filter elements per the planned maintenance system.
  • Function tests of the standby pump auto-start, low-pressure alarms, slowdown and shutdown, and the oil mist detector. Do these as per PMS and class requirements.
  • Sump tank cleaning and inspection, usually during dry dock. This is an enclosed space entry.
  • Purifier overhaul per running hours.

Sample correctly. Take samples from the designated point on the engine inlet with the engine running, after flushing the sampling cock. Never take them from the sump bottom or the purifier outlet unless the sampling programme asks for it.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Low LO pressure Clogged filter or suction strainer, low sump level, pump wear, relief valve leaking or stuck open, high oil temperature (low viscosity), excessive bearing clearances, internal pipe leak, air in suction
High LO temperature Fouled cooler, thermostatic valve fault, high cooling water temperature, overloaded or damaged bearing
High filter differential pressure / frequent back-flush Contaminated oil, purifier not performing, cat fines or wear debris, oxidised oil
Water in oil Cooler leak, purifier water seal or operating fault, condensation, leaking piston cooling (water-cooled designs) or jacket water leak
Rising sump level and falling viscosity Fuel dilution, common on four-strokes from leaking injectors or pumps
Rising BN / viscosity in two-stroke system oil Cylinder oil and combustion residues passing the piston rod stuffing box
Oil mist / high bearing temperature alarm Hot spot from a failing bearing, piston blow-by, or seizure developing

Water ingress via the cooler is less likely if oil pressure is kept above cooling water pressure, as most designs intend. If you find oil traces in the fresh water expansion tank, suspect the cooler.

Worked scenario: low LO pressure alarm at sea

You are the duty engineer. The main engine LO inlet low-pressure alarm sounds and the standby pump has cut in.

  1. Confirm and protect. Check the local gauge against the remote reading. If pressure is still falling towards slowdown or shutdown level, inform the bridge and request a speed reduction. Do not override safety devices without the Chief Engineer's authority and SMS procedure.
  2. Check the obvious. Sound the sump. Check filter differential pressure and change over to the standby or bypass filter if the auto-filter is blocked. Check LO temperature and the cooler and thermostatic valve.
  3. Identify the first pump's fault. Look for abnormal noise, low current, or tripped breaker. Then check the suction strainer and look for air entrainment.
  4. Look for leaks. Check the external pipework, filter covers and flanges. Watch for oil spraying onto hot surfaces, because that is a fire in waiting.
  5. Monitor bearings. Watch bearing temperatures, piston cooling outlet temperatures and flows, and the oil mist detector.
  6. Record and report. Make entries in the engine logbook, inform the Chief Engineer, and raise a PMS or defect report.

If the oil mist detector alarms:

  • Reduce speed or stop as per the maker's and company emergency procedure.
  • Keep everyone away from the crankcase relief valves.
  • Do not open crankcase doors until the engine has cooled for the time the maker specifies. Fresh air entering a hot crankcase can trigger an explosion.
  • Normally keep the LO pumps running to help cool the engine, unless the maker says otherwise.

Safety precautions and regulations

Safety

  • Lube oil leaking onto exhaust manifolds or turbochargers is a leading cause of engine room fires. Keep lagging intact, spray shields on flanges, and drip trays clean.
  • Before opening any filter, cooler or pipe: change over correctly, isolate, depressurise, vent, and apply lock-out/tag-out on pumps.
  • Hot oil causes burns. Wear gloves and eye protection.
  • Sump tanks and crankcases are enclosed spaces. Entry requires a permit, ventilation and atmosphere testing.
  • Keep sounding pipe self-closing cocks closed after use.

Regulation (at chapter level)

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1 covers protection of internal combustion engines. Part C (machinery installations) includes crankcase explosion relief valves. Part E (periodically unattended machinery spaces) includes oil mist detection, bearing temperature monitoring or equivalent for engines above set size thresholds, and the alarm and safety systems for unattended operation. These rules require the equipment; the operational response comes from the engine maker and company procedures.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4 covers arrangements for oil fuel, lubricating oil and other flammable oils. This includes protecting hot surfaces and preventing oil spraying or leaking onto ignition sources.
  • MARPOL Annex I covers the oil residue (sludge) tank. Purifier sludge, sludge disposal and bunkering of bulk lubricating oil are recorded in the Oil Record Book Part I.

Detailed alarm set points, testing intervals and survey requirements come from the engine maker, class society, flag state and your company SMS. Check the documents for your own ship.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to test whether you understand the system, not just the drawing. Be ready for these:

  • Sketch and describe the main engine LO system from sump to bearings and back, including purifier and alarms. Label the standby pump auto-start, thermostatic valve and filter bypass.
  • Why is system oil separate from cylinder oil on a crosshead engine? The cylinder oil needs a high BN to neutralise sulphuric acid from combustion and is burned, while system oil must stay stable for recirculation. The stuffing box keeps them apart.
  • What causes a crankcase explosion, and what do you do on an oil mist alarm? A hot spot vaporises oil, which condenses into a flammable mist. The hot spot ignites the mist (primary explosion). The resulting pressure wave and partial vacuum can draw in air, causing a more violent secondary explosion. Relief valves with flame arresters limit this. Then describe slowdown, keeping clear, and not opening doors until cooled.
  • How do you know your LO is deteriorating? Point to analysis trends in viscosity, BN, water, insolubles and metals, plus filter behaviour and sump level.
  • Why must purifier sludge be logged? It is an oil residue under MARPOL Annex I and must be recorded in the Oil Record Book Part I.
  • Why keep oil pressure above cooling water pressure in the cooler? So any leak puts oil into water rather than water into the bearings.
Key points
  • System oil lubricates, cools (including pistons on many two-strokes), cleans and protects; cylinder oil on crosshead engines is a separate total-loss system.
  • The core circuit is sump, strainer, pump, cooler with thermostatic valve, fine filter, bearings, then drain back to the sump, with a purifier running continuously.
  • Trend pressure, temperature, filter differential pressure and sump level every round; changes matter more than single readings.
  • Water and fuel dilution are the commonest contaminants; regular lab analysis plus onboard tests catch them early.
  • On an oil mist alarm, slow down or stop per procedure, keep clear of relief valves and never open a hot crankcase.
  • Purifier sludge and bulk lube oil bunkering are Oil Record Book Part I entries under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Alarm set points and test intervals come from the maker, class and company SMS, not from memory.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between system oil and cylinder oil?

System oil circulates through the crankcase to lubricate and cool bearings and pistons, and is reused after purification. Cylinder oil is injected onto the liner walls of a crosshead engine, where it neutralises acids from fuel combustion, and is consumed. They have different BN and additive packages, so they must not be mixed or swapped.

Why does the main engine LO sump level rise?

A rising level usually means something is getting into the oil. On a main engine this is most often water from a leaking cooler, a purifier fault or (on water-cooled designs) piston cooling. On four-stroke engines, fuel dilution from leaking injectors or pumps is also common. Sample and test immediately, check the purifier and cooler, and inform the Chief Engineer.

How often should lube oil samples be sent ashore?

The interval is set by the company's oil analysis programme, the engine maker and the oil supplier, so it varies between ships. Follow your PMS. Send an extra sample after any contamination incident or abnormal alarm.

Why should you not open crankcase doors immediately after an oil mist alarm?

A hot spot may still be present, and opening the doors lets fresh air into an oil-mist-rich atmosphere. That can cause a crankcase explosion. Wait for the cooling time specified by the engine maker and follow the company emergency procedure.

Is purifier sludge recorded in the Oil Record Book?

Yes. Oil residues (sludge), including sludge from lube oil purifiers, are recorded in the Oil Record Book Part I under MARPOL Annex I. This covers collection, transfer, incineration or landing ashore. Accurate entries are routinely checked by port state control.

Test yourself

On a crosshead main engine, what is the main reason the piston rod stuffing box is fitted?

The stuffing box scrapes dirty cylinder oil and residues off the piston rod, keeping them out of the system oil. This is standard crosshead engine design described in maker manuals.

The main engine LO pressure is falling and the filter differential pressure alarm is active. What is the most likely cause?

High differential pressure across the filter, together with low downstream pressure, indicates a choked filter. Change over to the standby or bypass filter and investigate the contamination source, per the maker's manual.

What should be done when the crankcase oil mist detector alarms?

Opening a hot crankcase can admit air and cause an explosion. Reducing speed or stopping, keeping clear and waiting for the maker's cooling time is the response given in engine maker instructions and company emergency procedures.

Why is LO pressure in the cooler normally kept higher than cooling water pressure?

Water in system oil damages bearings and the oil itself. With higher oil-side pressure, a leak shows as oil in the cooling water system instead, which is the normal design intent in maker manuals.

Under MARPOL Annex I, where are disposal of purifier sludge and bunkering of bulk lube oil recorded?

Sludge (oil residues) and bulk lubricating oil bunkering are machinery space operations recorded in Oil Record Book Part I. Part II is for cargo and ballast operations on oil tankers (MARPOL Annex I).
