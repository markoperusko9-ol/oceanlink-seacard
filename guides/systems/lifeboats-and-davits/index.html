Lifeboats and Davits: How They Work, How to Check Them, What Goes Wrong

Quick answer

A lifeboat is lowered by gravity on its falls. The davit winch brake controls the descent, and a centrifugal governor limits the speed. On-load release hooks free the boat once it is waterborne. SOLAS Chapter III and the LSA Code set the design, testing and drill requirements. Your SMS and the maker's manual set the detailed routine, and most lifeboat accidents happen during drills and maintenance, not real abandonment.

Purpose and where the rules sit

The lifeboat is your primary means of leaving a ship that can no longer keep you alive. The davit is what gets the boat, and you, safely from the embarkation deck to the water with the ship possibly listing, trimmed and rolling.

The regulatory framework sits in three places:

  • SOLAS Chapter III covers carriage, readiness, drills, inspections and maintenance.
  • The LSA Code covers construction and performance. Chapter IV deals with survival craft and Chapter VI with launching and embarkation appliances.
  • IMO resolutions and circulars cover servicing of release gear and accident prevention.

Typical cargo ship arrangements are:

  • Totally enclosed lifeboats on each side. Each side can carry everyone on board.
  • One free-fall lifeboat over the stern. This can carry everyone on board and is backed up by liferafts.

Passenger ships follow different carriage rules. Always check the ship's Safety Equipment Certificate and the muster list for what your vessel actually carries.

Main components

The boat

  • GRP hull with built-in buoyancy.
  • Seating with seat belts.
  • Compression-ignition engine with fuel and starting arrangements.
  • Drain plug or valve.
  • Equipment as listed in the LSA Code, such as water, rations, pyrotechnics, first aid kit and bailer.

Tankers carrying low-flashpoint (≤60°C) cargoes must have fire-protected lifeboats with a water spray system and a self-contained air supply. Tankers carrying toxic cargoes need at least a self-contained air supply.

Release gear

  • On-load release hooks fore and aft, linked by cables or rods to a single release handle at the helm position.
  • A hydrostatic interlock stops the hooks being released under load until the boat is waterborne. An override is provided for emergencies.
  • Indicators or reset marks show whether the hooks are correctly closed.

Davit and winch

  • Gravity davits: either pivoting (luffing) arms or a roller-track type.
  • Falls: steel wire ropes from the winch drum, over the davit head sheaves, to the boat hooks.
  • Winch brake: a mechanical brake held on by a weight on the brake lever. Lifting the lever, locally or from inside the boat by remote control wire, lets the boat lower by gravity.
  • Centrifugal governor brake: limits the lowering speed.
  • Hoisting arrangements:
    • an electric motor for recovery;
    • a hand crank, with an interlock so the motor cannot run while the crank is engaged;
    • limit switches that stop power hoisting before the arms reach the stops. The final stage is usually by hand.

Securing and embarkation

  • Gripes with quick-release slips.
  • Harbour or securing pins.
  • Fall preventer devices (FPDs), where fitted.
  • Forward painter.
  • Embarkation ladder.
  • On some designs, tricing pendants and bowsing tackles hold the boat alongside at the embarkation deck.

Free-fall boats

  • The boat sits on a stern ramp and is released by one person from inside the boat, typically by a hydraulic release system.
  • A davit or crane with a hook provides secondary launching and is used for recovery.

Step by step: launching an enclosed lifeboat from gravity davits

Follow the instructions posted at the station. The order below is typical, not universal.

  1. Muster and headcount. Lifejackets on, plus immersion suits if the conditions call for them. Confirm communication with the bridge.
  2. Pre-launch checks.
    • Drain plug in.
    • Painter rigged and led well forward.
    • Area under the boat and the sea surface clear.
  3. Release securing devices.
    • Slip the gripes.
    • Remove harbour pins.
    • In a drill, handle FPDs exactly as the procedure says. In a real abandonment they come off before lowering.
  4. Embark. Crew board, sit in their allocated seats and fasten seat belts. The coxswain takes the helm position.
  5. Start the engine. Start the engine before lowering. The LSA Code requires lifeboat engines to be able to run for at least 5 minutes after a cold start with the boat out of the water. Follow the maker's limits on out-of-water running time.
  6. Lower from inside the boat. The coxswain pulls the remote brake wire. The arms swing out, then the boat descends. The governor controls the speed. If the boat stops, check that nobody is fouling the wire.
  7. Check the hooks once waterborne. Confirm the boat is actually afloat and the hydrostatic interlock indicator has moved over.
  8. Release and get clear.
    • Operate the release handle so both hooks open together.
    • Slip the painter.
    • Motor clear of the ship's side and the propeller.

Recovery is the high-risk phase:

  • Hook on and visually confirm that each hook is fully reset, using its indicator and marks.
  • Reset the interlock.
  • Hoist with the minimum people on board that your procedure allows.
  • Stop power hoisting at the limit switch and finish by hand.
  • Re-secure the gripes and pins before anyone relaxes.

Routine checks and maintenance

SOLAS Chapter III requires weekly and monthly inspections and records. The SMS planned maintenance system turns these into checklists.

Weekly (typical content)

  • Visual inspection of the boats, davits and launching appliances.
  • Lifeboat engine run for a total of not less than 3 minutes, demonstrating that the gearbox engages satisfactorily. In practice this means running ahead and astern.
  • Moving the boat from its stowed position to prove the appliance works, as the regulation and your SMS require. Free-fall boats are handled differently.

Monthly

  • Full inspection of the boat, its equipment and the launching gear against a checklist.
  • Check expiry dates on pyrotechnics, rations, water and first aid items.
  • Check battery charge and the bilge.
  • Inspect the falls and sheaves.
  • Record the inspection in the log.

Periodic, shore-based or specialist work

  • Falls: turned end for end at intervals not exceeding 30 months. Renewed when deterioration makes it necessary, or at intervals not exceeding 5 years, whichever is earlier.
  • Annual thorough examination of the release gear, davits and winches.
  • Five-yearly thorough examination and load testing.

Under IMO resolution MSC.402(96), this servicing must be done by the manufacturer or an authorised service provider. The crew assist but do not sign it off.

Ongoing good practice

  • Grease the davit sheaves, arm pivots and winch gearing to the maker's schedule.
  • Keep the brake lining dry and free of grease.
  • Check that the remote control wire runs freely through its guides.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Symptom Usual cause
Boat creeps down when stowed Arms move off stops, gap appears at securing pins Contaminated or glazed brake lining, brake weight or linkage out of adjustment
Lowering too fast or jerky Rapid uncontrolled descent, shock loads Centrifugal governor worn, pads contaminated, wrong grease
Boat will not lower Brake lifted but nothing moves Gripes or pins still in, seized sheaves or pivots, remote wire jammed
Hooks not properly reset Indicator not in the closed or safe position, cam not fully home Hurried recovery, worn linkage, cables out of adjustment
Hooks will not release when afloat Handle locked Hydrostatic interlock seized by fouling or corrosion, cable slack
Over-hoisting Arms hit stops hard, wire overloaded Limit switch failed or bypassed
Falls damage Broken wires, corrosion, kinks, flattening at sheaves Age, poor lubrication, seized sheaves
Engine will not start Cranks but no fire, or no cranking Water in fuel, flat battery, air in the fuel system, cold weather

The most dangerous fault is a hook that looks closed but is not fully reset. Investigations after drill accidents have repeatedly found this. Treat the reset check as a two-person job.

Safety precautions during drills and maintenance

IMO issued specific guidance on preventing lifeboat accidents (MSC.1/Circ.1206 as revised) because drills were killing seafarers.

The practical rules are:

  • Use FPDs during drills where fitted, as the procedure says. Never use them in a way that would stop a real launch.
  • Wear protective gear. Lifejacket and helmet, with seat belts fastened, for everyone in the boat.
  • Keep people clear.
    • Do not stand under the boat, in the swing path of the arms, or in the bight of a wire.
    • Do not tie or wedge the brake lever open.
    • Keep hands off the drum.
  • Do not let the drill lapse. SOLAS requires each lifeboat to be launched with its assigned crew and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months during an abandon ship drill. Free-fall boats have their own launching arrangements. Check the regulation and your SMS.
  • Maintenance safety.
    • Isolate the winch power under lock-out tag-out before working on the davit.
    • Secure the boat mechanically, not just on the brake.
  • Watch the weather and the ship. Don't launch in a seaway or when the ship is making way unless the procedure and the master explicitly allow it.
  • Hold a toolbox talk. Brief every drill on hook reset, communications and the abort signal.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

Q: How is lowering speed controlled on a gravity davit? A: The mechanical brake is released and the boat descends under its own weight. A centrifugal governor brake on the winch limits the speed. The LSA Code sets the lowering speed requirements.

Q: What is the purpose of the hydrostatic interlock? A: It prevents on-load release unless the boat is waterborne, so the hooks cannot be opened while the boat is hanging. It can be overridden in an emergency, and the override must be clearly marked and guarded.

Q: What list and trim must launching appliances cope with? A: The LSA Code requires lifeboat launching arrangements to work with the ship trimmed and listed. The usual figures quoted are up to 10° trim and 20° list either way. Know the exact wording for your certificate level and ship type.

Q: What do you check before hoisting a recovered boat? A: For both hooks:

  • each is fully reset, with indicators in the safe position;
  • the interlock is reset;
  • the release handle is secured;
  • the falls are seated in the sheaves;
  • the number of people on board is as the procedure allows.

Hoist on power to the limit switch and finish by hand.

Q: Who carries out the annual and five-yearly examinations? A: The manufacturer or an authorised service provider, under SOLAS III/20 and resolution MSC.402(96). The ship's crew do the weekly and monthly checks.

Key points
  • Gravity davits lower by the boat's own weight. The brake holds it and the centrifugal governor limits the speed.
  • The hydrostatic interlock prevents on-load hook release until the boat is waterborne.
  • The weekly and monthly SOLAS III inspections are crew tasks. Annual and five-yearly examinations need an authorised service provider (MSC.402(96)).
  • Falls are turned end for end within 30 months and renewed within 5 years, or sooner if deteriorated.
  • Each lifeboat must be launched with crew and manoeuvred in the water at least every three months.
  • Recovery and hook resetting are the highest-risk phases. Treat the hook check as a two-person job.
  • Never wedge brake levers or bypass limit switches, and isolate power before davit maintenance.

Frequently asked questions

How often must a lifeboat be lowered into the water?

SOLAS Chapter III requires each lifeboat to be launched with its assigned operating crew aboard and manoeuvred in the water at least once every three months during an abandon ship drill. Free-fall lifeboats have their own launching provisions. Your SMS may require more frequent drills.

What is an on-load release hook?

It is a hook that can be released while it is still carrying the boat's weight, so the boat can be freed in a seaway when the falls are not slack. A hydrostatic interlock normally prevents this until the boat is waterborne. Hooks must comply with the LSA Code requirements introduced by MSC.320(89).

Can ship's crew carry out the annual lifeboat and davit inspection?

Not on their own. Since MSC.402(96), the annual thorough examination and the five-yearly examination and load tests must be done by the manufacturer or an authorised service provider. The crew carry out the weekly and monthly inspections and the routine maintenance in the PMS.

Why does a lifeboat creep down from its stowed position?

The usual cause is a winch brake that is not holding properly. This may be due to an oil- or grease-contaminated or glazed lining, or a brake linkage out of adjustment. It must be reported and corrected at once, and the boat secured mechanically until then.

What are fall preventer devices and when are they used?

FPDs are slings or pins that hold the boat if the hooks release unintentionally. They are used during drills and maintenance according to IMO guidance and the maker's instructions. They must be removed before a real launch and must never be left in place in a way that would stop an emergency launch.

Test yourself

What is the maximum interval for turning lifeboat falls end for end?

Falls are turned end for end at intervals not exceeding 30 months and renewed within 5 years or when deteriorated (SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20).

What does the weekly routine for a lifeboat engine require?

SOLAS III/20.6 requires lifeboat engines to be run weekly for a total of not less than 3 minutes, demonstrating that the gearbox engages satisfactorily. In practice this means running ahead and astern.

On a gravity davit, which component limits the lowering speed?

The centrifugal brake on the davit winch controls the speed of gravity lowering (LSA Code, Chapter VI).

What is the purpose of the hydrostatic interlock on lifeboat release gear?

The interlock prevents premature on-load release of the hooks until the boat is afloat (LSA Code, Chapter IV).

Who must carry out the five-yearly examination and load test of lifeboat release gear and davit winches?

Under SOLAS III/20 and resolution MSC.402(96), annual and five-yearly servicing is by the manufacturer or an authorised service provider.
