Shipboard Incinerator: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

A shipboard incinerator burns oily sludge and permitted solid garbage at high temperature to reduce the volume landed ashore. MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 16 controls it: type approval, an operating manual, trained operators, continuous flue gas temperature monitoring and a list of substances you must never burn. Engineers run it, and every burn must be recorded correctly in the Garbage Record Book or Oil Record Book.

What the incinerator is for

Every ship produces two waste streams that are awkward to keep on board:

  • Oily sludge from fuel and lube oil purifiers, filters and drip trays, collected in the sludge tank.
  • Solid garbage such as oily rags, paper, cardboard, some food waste and some operational waste.

The incinerator burns these to reduce volume and save sludge tank space between ports. It does not replace port reception facilities. Ash, unburnable items and anything prohibited still go ashore.

In an exam you can put it simply: the incinerator is a waste-volume reduction plant. It is regulated as a source of air pollution under MARPOL Annex VI, and garbage handling is regulated under Annex V.

Under SOLAS Chapter II-2, a space containing oil-fired equipment such as an incinerator falls within the definition of a machinery space of category A. That definition drives the fire protection requirements for the space it sits in.

Main components

Makes differ, but a typical marine unit has these parts:

Component Function
Primary combustion chamber Refractory-lined chamber where solid waste is burnt. It has a loading door and an ash door.
Secondary chamber / afterburner Completes combustion of the gases from the primary chamber.
Diesel (pilot) burner Lights off and heats the chamber to working temperature. It also supports combustion.
Sludge burner Atomises and burns sludge once the chamber is hot enough.
Sludge service tank Heated and circulated, often with a mixing or grinding pump to homogenise the sludge and break up water and solids.
Combustion air fan Supplies air for burning and purging.
Flue gas / draught fan and dilution air damper Keeps the chamber under slight negative pressure. Mixes in cool air to bring the stack temperature down.
Flame scanner (photocell/UV) Proves the flame. Trips the burner on flame failure.
Thermocouples Measure chamber and flue gas outlet temperature for control, alarms and the regulatory monitoring.
Control panel and interlocks Handle the purge sequence, door interlocks, high-temperature trips, emergency stop and alarms.

The negative pressure matters. It stops hot gas and flame blowing back out of the doors when they are opened.

How it works: a typical operating sequence

Always follow the maker's operating manual, which Annex VI requires on board. A typical batch-plus-sludge cycle looks like this:

  1. Pre-start checks. Ash removed from the previous burn. Doors and seals in good condition. Diesel oil supply open. Sludge tank level adequate, and the sludge heated and circulated long enough to homogenise. Water drained off the sludge tank. Fans free. No alarms on the panel.
  2. Load solid waste. Load only permitted items (see the safety section), in sensible quantities, while the unit is cold or stopped. Then close and secure the loading door.
  3. Start. Fans start and the chamber is purged before ignition. The diesel burner lights off and the scanner proves the flame.
  4. Heat up. The chamber temperature rises. Annex VI requires batch-loaded incinerators to be designed so that the combustion chamber reaches 600 °C within 5 minutes after start-up.
  5. Sludge burning. The control system normally releases the sludge burner only when the chamber has reached its set temperature. Sludge is atomised and burnt, and the diesel burner cycles to hold temperature.
  6. Monitor. Watch the flue gas outlet temperature, chamber temperature, draught, stack colour and alarms. On continuous-feed units, waste must not be fed in when the temperature is below the minimum allowed, which is 850 °C.
  7. Shutdown. Stop the sludge burner first and flush the line as the manual directs. Then stop the diesel burner. The fans keep running to cool the unit.
  8. Ash removal. Open the ash door only when the unit is cool. Collect the ash in a metal container and store it for landing ashore.
  9. Record. Make the Garbage Record Book and/or Oil Record Book Part I entries the same watch.

Routine checks and maintenance

Planned maintenance intervals come from the maker and your company PMS. Typical items are:

  • Burners. Clean nozzles, check atomising air or pressure, check electrode gaps and ignition transformer.
  • Flame scanner. Keep the lens clean, since soot is the commonest cause of nuisance flame failures. Test the flame-failure trip.
  • Refractory. Inspect for cracks, spalling and loose bricks. Small cracks grow quickly with thermal cycling.
  • Door seals and interlocks. Test that the burner cannot run with the loading door open.
  • Sludge system. Clean strainers, check heating coils, drain water from the tank, and check the mixing/grinding pump.
  • Fans and dampers. Check bearings, belts and impeller fouling. Make sure dilution dampers move freely.
  • Instrumentation. Calibrate thermocouples and test alarms and trips, especially the high flue gas temperature trip.
  • Flue/uptake. Remove soot and ash build-up, which reduces draught and is a fire risk.

Log test results. A port State control inspector may ask you to demonstrate the incinerator and its safety trips.

Common faults and what they look like

Symptom Likely causes
Flame failure on start or during sludge burning Dirty scanner, blocked nozzle, water in sludge, sludge too cold or viscous, poor atomisation, ignition fault
Sludge burner will not release Chamber not up to set temperature, faulty thermocouple, interlock not satisfied
Black smoke from funnel Too much waste loaded, insufficient combustion air, poor atomisation, fouled fan
Smoke or flame puffing from doors Loss of negative pressure: flue fan fault, damper stuck, uptake blocked, door seal failed
High flue gas temperature alarm or trip Dilution damper fault, overloading, high-calorific waste, fan fault
Hot spots on the casing Refractory damage
Spitting or explosive bangs in chamber Water slugs in sludge, or sealed containers or aerosols loaded by mistake

Worked scenario. The sludge burner keeps tripping on flame failure about 30 seconds after release. The chamber temperature is normal.

  1. Stop and check the sludge tank first. Drain the water bottom.
  2. Confirm the heating is in service and circulation has run long enough.
  3. Clean the scanner lens and sludge strainer.
  4. Check the nozzle and atomising medium.
  5. If water keeps reappearing, look upstream. A leaking purifier operating water or a heating coil leak into the sludge tank are classic culprits.

Safety precautions

Never burn these (MARPOL Annex VI Reg 16 prohibitions, paraphrased):

  • Residues of cargoes covered by Annexes I, II and III, and packing contaminated with them.
  • PCBs.
  • Garbage containing more than traces of heavy metals.
  • Refined petroleum products containing halogen compounds.
  • Sewage sludge and sludge oil not generated on board.
  • Exhaust gas cleaning system residues.

PVC may only be burnt in an incinerator type-approved for it.

Practical hazards:

  • Never load aerosol cans, batteries, sealed drums, gas cartridges or pyrotechnics. They explode, and batteries count as heavy-metal waste anyway.
  • Never open the loading door while the burner is running or the chamber is hot. Do not defeat the door interlock.
  • Wear heat-resistant gloves, a face shield and overalls when handling ash or near open doors.
  • Treat ash as hot for longer than you think. Use metal containers only.
  • The sludge tank holds heated oil, so check temperature control and keep the area clean of drips.
  • Isolate electrical supply and fuel, and apply lock-out/tag-out, before working inside the chamber. Refractory dust and confined access make chamber entry a planned job.

Check local rules before burning. Many ports and some coastal States prohibit incinerator use in their waters, and the company SMS often restricts it further.

Regulations and record keeping

MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 16 is the core rule:

  • Shipboard incineration is permitted only in a shipboard incinerator. The exception is sludge oil and sewage sludge generated during normal operation, which may be burnt in main or auxiliary power plant or boilers, but not inside ports, harbours and estuaries.
  • Incinerators installed on or after 1 January 2000 must be type-approved to the IMO standard specification for shipboard incinerators. The current standard is resolution MEPC.244(66), and earlier units may be approved to MEPC.76(40) as permitted by Reg 16. Check the type approval certificate for your unit.
  • An operating manual must be on board, and personnel must be trained to use it.
  • Combustion flue gas outlet temperature must be monitored whenever the unit is operating.

The incinerator is listed in the supplement to the IAPP Certificate.

Records:

  • Garbage Record Book (Annex V). Record the date and time, ship's position, the categories burnt and the estimated amount incinerated.
  • Oil Record Book Part I (Annex I). Record sludge disposed of by incineration as a disposal of oil residues.
  • Ash. Incinerator ash is a garbage category in its own right. It is not among the discharges Annex V permits at sea, so land it ashore and record the delivery.

Follow your flag State's and company's current guidance on the exact ORB entry format. Mismatched sludge figures between the ORB, sounding records and incinerator running hours are a favourite PSC finding.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

  • Why must sludge be heated and circulated before burning? To lower viscosity for atomisation, release water, and homogenise water and solids so the flame is stable.
  • Why is the chamber kept under negative pressure? To prevent flue gas and flame escaping through the doors, and to give a steady draught.
  • Why is dilution air used? To cool the flue gas to a safe stack temperature and protect the uptake.
  • Name items you may not burn. Annex I/II/III cargo residues and contaminated packing, PCBs, garbage with more than traces of heavy metals, halogenated refined products, sludge not generated on board, EGCS residues. PVC only in units approved for it.
  • What temperatures does Annex VI specify? A minimum of 850 °C for feeding continuous-feed units. Batch-loaded units must be designed so that the combustion chamber reaches 600 °C within 5 minutes after start-up.
  • Where can sludge be burnt other than the incinerator? In main or auxiliary power plant or boilers, but not inside ports, harbours and estuaries.
  • What do you do with the ash? Store it in metal containers, land it to a reception facility and record it in the Garbage Record Book.
  • What type of space is the incinerator room under SOLAS II-2? It falls within the definition of a machinery space of category A, because it contains oil-fired equipment.
Key points
  • The incinerator reduces waste volume; it never replaces landing ash and prohibited waste ashore.
  • MARPOL Annex VI Reg 16 requires type approval, an operating manual, trained operators and continuous flue gas temperature monitoring.
  • Continuous-feed units must not be fed below 850 °C; batch units must be designed to reach 600 °C within 5 minutes of start-up.
  • Never burn cargo residues, PCBs, heavy-metal waste, halogenated products, EGCS residues, sealed containers or batteries.
  • Most sludge burner trips come from water in the sludge, cold sludge or a dirty flame scanner.
  • Negative pressure in the chamber prevents blowback; open doors only when the unit is stopped and cool.
  • Record every burn in the Garbage Record Book and/or Oil Record Book Part I, and keep sludge figures consistent.

Frequently asked questions

Can we use the ship's incinerator in port?

Annex VI does not ban incinerator use outright in port, but many port authorities and coastal States prohibit it locally, and company SMS often forbids it too. Burning sludge in boilers or power plant is specifically not allowed inside ports, harbours and estuaries. Check port regulations and your SMS before any burn.

Can incinerator ash be thrown overboard?

No. Incinerator ash is a garbage category under MARPOL Annex V, and it is not among the discharges Annex V permits at sea. Keep it in metal containers, land it to a port reception facility and record the delivery in the Garbage Record Book.

Can plastics be burnt in the incinerator?

Annex VI restricts only PVC, which may be burnt only in an incinerator type-approved for PVC. Other plastics are not prohibited by Annex VI. Many companies land all plastics ashore, so follow your garbage management plan.

Why does the sludge burner keep tripping on flame failure?

The usual causes are water in the sludge, sludge that is too cold or viscous to atomise, a blocked nozzle or strainer, or a sooted flame scanner. Drain the sludge tank, confirm the heating and circulation, clean the scanner and nozzle, then look for the source of any persistent water.

Which record book do I fill in after using the incinerator?

Burning solid garbage goes in the Garbage Record Book with date, time, position, categories and estimated amount. Burning sludge is a disposal of oil residues and goes in Oil Record Book Part I. If you burn both in one cycle, both books need entries.

Test yourself

Under MARPOL Annex VI, what is the minimum temperature below which waste must not be fed into a continuous-feed shipboard incinerator?

Annex VI requires that waste is not fed into a continuous-feed incinerator when the temperature is below 850 °C. The 600 °C figure is a design requirement for batch-loaded units at start-up (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg 16).

Which of the following may NOT be burnt in a shipboard incinerator?

Residues of Annex I, II and III cargoes and related contaminated packing are prohibited. Own-ship sludge, oily rags and paper are routinely incinerated (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg 16).

Where may sludge oil generated during normal operation be burnt in a boiler or power plant?

Annex VI allows own-generated sludge oil and sewage sludge to be burnt in main or auxiliary power plant or boilers, but not inside ports, harbours and estuaries (MARPOL Annex VI, Reg 16).

Smoke and flame puff out around the loading door during operation. What is the most likely cause?

The chamber is designed to run under slight negative pressure. Blowback through the doors points to lost draught from a fan, damper, uptake or seal fault (maker's manual; MARPOL Annex VI, Reg 16 operating manual requirement).

What should be done with incinerator ash?

Incinerator ash is a garbage category that Annex V does not permit to be discharged at sea, so it must be landed ashore and recorded (MARPOL Annex V).
