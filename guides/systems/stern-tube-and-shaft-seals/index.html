Stern tube and shaft seals on merchant ships

Quick answer

The stern tube carries the propeller shaft through the hull and holds its aft bearings. Seals at each end keep seawater out and lubricating oil in. On most large merchant ships the bearings are oil-lubricated white metal, the aft seal is usually a multi-ring lip seal running on a chrome steel liner, and oil pressure is held from a header tank. Watchkeepers monitor tank levels, aft bearing temperature and oil condition, because a seal failure means either pollution or water in the bearing.

What the stern tube and seals do

The propeller shaft has to leave the hull somewhere below the waterline, and that somewhere is the stern tube. It is a heavy tube built into the stern frame and passing through the aft peak area. It does three jobs:

  • Supports the shaft: it carries the aft (and usually forward) stern tube bearings, which take the weight of the shaft and the overhung propeller.
  • Lubricates the bearings: it holds oil, or on water-lubricated designs lets seawater through, to keep the bearings working.
  • Seals the hull opening: seals at each end stop the sea getting in and the lubricant getting out.

If the aft seal fails you lose oil to the sea, or you get seawater into the bearing. Either way you have a pollution problem, a machinery problem, and possibly a detention.

Two basic arrangements are found at sea:

Type Bearing material Typical sealing
Oil-lubricated (most deep-sea ships) White metal (Babbitt) lined bearings Lip-type or mechanical face seals at aft and forward ends
Water-lubricated (older, smaller, some newer designs) Rubber, polymer composites, historically lignum vitae Forward gland packing or a face seal; the aft end is open to the sea

The rest of this guide concentrates on the oil-lubricated arrangement, because that is what most cadets and junior engineers will meet.

Main components

Names and details vary by maker. Always read your own ship's manual. The building blocks are similar on most ships:

  • Tailshaft (propeller shaft): the aftmost length of shafting, carrying the propeller.
  • Aft and forward stern tube bearings: white-metal lined bushes. The aft bearing carries most of the load because of the propeller's overhung weight, so it is the one temperature-monitored and wear-measured.
  • Shaft liners (sleeves): hard sleeves, typically chrome steel (sometimes chrome-plated or coated), fitted to the shaft where the seal rings run. The liner rotates with the shaft. The lips run on it, not on the shaft itself.
  • Aft seal assembly: typically several elastomer sealing rings in a housing bolted to the stern frame. The outer ring or rings face the sea and the inner ring or rings face the oil. The chambers between rings may have their own oil or air supply and drain lines led forward.
  • Forward seal assembly: usually fewer rings, keeping stern tube oil from leaking into the engine room or shaft tunnel. It often has its own small supply tank.
  • Rope guard: a shroud between the propeller boss and the stern frame that protects the aft seal from ropes, fishing line and nets. Some ships also fit a rope cutter.
  • Header (gravity) tank or tanks: these set the oil pressure in the stern tube. Many installations have a high and a low tank, selected according to draught.
  • Sump or drain tank, circulation pump and cooler: fitted on some systems, to circulate and cool the oil.
  • Monitoring: aft bearing temperature sensor, tank level alarms and oil sampling points. Some ships have a built-in wear-down measuring device.
  • Shaft earthing device: a slip ring and brush that earths the shaft to the hull to limit spark erosion of bearings and other electrical effects. It is not part of the seal, but it is checked on the same rounds.

How it works, step by step

  1. Oil fills the stern tube between the forward and aft seals and lubricates the bearings. A hydrodynamic oil film forms once the shaft is turning at adequate speed.
  2. The header tank sets the pressure. On conventional lip-seal systems, the oil pressure at the aft seal is kept a little above the surrounding seawater pressure. The aim is that any leakage is outward rather than seawater coming in, which protects the bearing.
  3. Draught changes the balance. Seawater pressure at the seal rises as the ship loads. This is why many ships have high and low header tanks and switch between them for loaded and ballast conditions. The changeover is laid down in the maker's instructions and the company procedures.
  4. The seal lips ride on the liner. The rings are held against the rotating liner by their own shape, garter springs and the pressure difference. A thin lubricating film between lip and liner keeps friction and heat down.
  5. Ring chambers manage pressure across the rings. In many modern designs the chambers between rings are supplied with oil or air, or drained to a collection tank. This shares the pressure across the rings and keeps the oil-to-sea leak very small. Air-type designs exist specifically to reduce oil loss to the sea.
  6. The forward seal does the same job inboard, keeping oil in the stern tube and out of the bilge.

The key idea for orals: the pressure balance at the aft seal decides which way any leak goes. Too much oil head means more oil to sea. Too little means seawater into the bearing.

Routine checks and maintenance

On watch / daily rounds (follow your planned maintenance system and engine room standing orders):

  • Header tank level: note it and compare with the previous reading. A steady drop means oil is going somewhere.
  • Drain or collection tank level, if fitted: a rising level means the seal rings are passing.
  • Aft bearing temperature: compare with normal for that load and sea temperature. The trend matters more than a single value.
  • Forward seal: look for leakage, drips or oil in the tunnel or aft bilge well.
  • Shaft earthing device: brush condition and contact. Record the millivolt reading where a meter is fitted.
  • Correct header tank selected for the present draught.

Periodic:

  • Oil sampling and analysis at the interval in the PMS. The two things you want to know are water content and bearing metal content. Water-in-oil limits come from the seal and bearing makers. Use their figures, not a number someone remembers from another ship.
  • Draining off any water from the bottom of tanks where the system allows.
  • Alarm testing of level and temperature alarms.

In dry dock:

  • Wear-down (bearing clearance) measurement of the aft bearing. It is taken in dry dock with a poker gauge, or afloat using a built-in wear-down measuring device where fitted. Record it against previous readings.
  • Inspection of seal rings and liner. Lips wear grooves into the liner, and the rings are renewed per maker or class requirements.
  • Rope guard and rope cutter inspection. Remove any fishing line found wrapped near the seal.
  • Tailshaft survey when due. The survey interval and any extension through condition-monitoring schemes depend on the classification society's rules and the notations the ship holds. Check your class status, not a general rule of thumb.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Typical symptoms Likely causes
Aft seal leaking oil outward Header tank level falling, top-ups more frequent, possible sheen astern Worn or damaged rings, grooved liner, net or line damage, header too high for draught
Seawater ingress Collection or sump level rising, oil milky or emulsified, water in analysis Outer rings failed, oil head too low for draught, liner damage
Forward seal leak Oil in tunnel or aft bilge, header level slowly falling Worn rings, shaft misalignment, blocked drain
High aft bearing temperature Temperature alarm, rising trend Low oil level, water-contaminated oil, overload, misalignment (for example after grounding or a hull deflection change), bearing damage
Rapid bearing wear Increased wear-down reading, metals in oil analysis Poor lubrication, water in oil, misalignment, spark erosion

Worked scenario. On the 0400–0800 watch, a junior engineer notices that the stern tube header tank has dropped noticeably since the previous watch. Nothing else is alarming and the aft bearing temperature is normal.

  1. Confirm the reading. Check the gauge glass, then compare the logbook entries over the last few days.
  2. Check the forward seal and aft bilges for oil. If there is no inboard leak, suspect the aft seal.
  3. Confirm the correct header tank is in use for the present draught.
  4. Inform the 2nd Engineer and Chief Engineer. Do not simply top up and say nothing, because a loss to sea has pollution implications.
  5. The bridge can look astern for a sheen when conditions allow.
  6. Take an oil sample if instructed.
  7. Follow the company SMS and the maker's guidance. Options may include adjusting header tank selection, closer monitoring and planning repairs, which may be in-water work by divers on designs that allow it.

Record every top-up quantity accurately. Unexplained oil consumption is exactly what a port state inspector will ask about.

Safety and environmental precautions

  • Pollution: oil escaping at the aft seal is an oil discharge to the sea. MARPOL Annex I prohibits oil discharges except under its specific conditions, and a leaking stern tube does not meet them. Report losses within the ship and to the company as the SMS requires. Coastal and port authorities may also need to be informed, depending on local law.
  • Environmentally acceptable lubricants (EALs): the US Vessel General Permit stays in force until it is replaced by Coast Guard regulations under the Vessel Incidental Discharge Act (VIDA). Under it, vessels covered must use EALs in oil-to-sea interfaces, including stern tubes, unless technically infeasible. Check current US requirements and your seal maker's approval before trading there or changing oil type. Not every seal material is compatible with every EAL.
  • Never mix oil grades without approval. Use only the specified stern tube oil.
  • Shaft rotation: before any work on the seals, shafting or rope guard, the shaft must be prevented from turning. Engage the turning gear, block main engine starting and apply lock-out tag-out under a permit to work. Propeller wash or water flow can windmill a shaft that has not been secured.
  • Diver operations: where divers work on the aft seal or propeller, coordinate so the main engine, thrusters, sea suctions and overboard discharges are isolated as required by the procedures.
  • Confined spaces: shaft tunnels, aft peak areas and some stern tube access spaces may need enclosed space entry procedures. Treat them as the SMS designates.
  • Watertight integrity: SOLAS Chapter II-1 contains requirements on enclosing stern tubes within watertight spaces. Keep watertight doors and seals in those areas in good order.

Oral exam style questions

Examiners like to test understanding rather than memorised maker names. Be ready for:

  • Why is stern tube oil pressure kept slightly above seawater pressure on a conventional lip-seal system? So that any leakage across the aft seal is outward, protecting the bearing from seawater, while keeping that oil loss small.
  • Why do some ships have two header tanks? Seawater pressure at the seal changes with draught. The tank height is selected to keep the correct balance in loaded and ballast conditions.
  • How do you know water is getting into the stern tube? Rising sump or collection tank level, milky oil, and water content in the analysis. A rising aft bearing temperature may follow.
  • What is wear-down and how is it measured? It is the drop of the shaft in the aft bearing as the white metal wears. It is taken in dry dock with a poker gauge, or afloat using a built-in wear-down measuring device where fitted. The reading is compared with previous records and class limits.
  • What does the rope guard do? It shields the aft seal from ropes, fishing line and nets, which can cut or displace the seal rings.
  • Why fit a shaft earthing device? To earth the rotating shaft to the hull, reducing current through bearings that causes spark erosion and pitting.
  • What would you do with a sudden high aft bearing temperature? Inform the engineer in charge and the bridge, and reduce shaft speed as directed. Check oil level and condition, then follow the maker's and SMS instructions.

Expect a follow-up of "and what do you record?". The answer is levels, top-ups, temperatures, sample results and actions taken, in the engine room log.

Key points
  • The stern tube supports the tailshaft, lubricates the aft bearings and seals the hull opening around the shaft.
  • Most large ships use oil-lubricated white metal bearings, with the aft seal usually a multi-ring lip seal running on a chrome steel liner.
  • Header tank height sets the oil-to-seawater pressure balance at the aft seal, and it must suit the draught.
  • A falling header tank level means oil loss; a rising sump or collection level with milky oil means seawater ingress.
  • Aft bearing temperature trend, oil analysis and wear-down readings are the main condition indicators.
  • Oil escaping at the aft seal is a pollution issue, so record and report top-ups honestly.
  • Secure the shaft against rotation under permit and lock-out before any seal or propeller work.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a stern tube seal leak oil into the sea?

On conventional lip-seal systems the oil pressure is kept slightly above seawater pressure, so any leakage across worn or damaged rings goes outward. Wear, liner grooving, fishing line damage or a header tank set too high for the draught all increase the loss. Air-type seal designs exist to reduce this oil-to-sea leakage.

What are the signs of water in stern tube oil?

Look for a rising level in the sump or collection tank, oil that looks milky or emulsified, and water content reported in the oil analysis. If it continues, the aft bearing temperature may rise. Acceptable water content limits are set by the seal and bearing makers.

Can a stern tube seal be repaired without dry docking?

With some seal designs and arrangements, divers can renew the aft seal rings afloat, often using a habitat around the stern. Whether this is possible depends on the seal type, maker approval, class acceptance and local regulations. Forward seal work can usually be done from inside the ship.

What is a stern tube wear-down reading?

It is a measurement of how far the tailshaft has dropped in the aft bearing as the white metal wears. It is taken in dry dock with a poker gauge, or afloat using a built-in wear-down measuring device where fitted. The reading is compared with previous readings and with the class or maker's limits.

Do I need environmentally acceptable lubricant in the stern tube?

It depends on where the ship trades. The US Vessel General Permit stays in force until it is replaced by Coast Guard regulations under the Vessel Incidental Discharge Act (VIDA). Under it, vessels covered must use EALs in oil-to-sea interfaces, including stern tubes, unless technically infeasible. Check current US requirements, company policy and the seal maker's compatibility approval before any change of oil.

Test yourself

On a conventional oil-lubricated lip-seal stern tube, why is the oil head kept slightly above seawater pressure at the aft seal?

The pressure balance decides the direction of leakage. Slightly higher oil pressure protects the white metal bearing from seawater, as described in seal makers' manuals.

The stern tube header tank level has been dropping steadily and there is no oil in the aft bilge. What is the most likely cause?

Falling header level with no inboard leak points to oil loss at the aft seal. Seawater ingress would tend to raise the oil volume, not reduce it.

Why do many ships have high and low stern tube header tanks?

Seawater pressure at the aft seal varies with draught, so the header height is selected for loaded or ballast condition per the maker's instructions.

What is the main purpose of the rope guard at the aft end of the stern tube?

The rope guard shields the seal housing from entangled lines and nets that can cut or displace the rings. Earthing is done by the separate shaft earthing device.

Which measurement taken in dry dock indicates aft stern tube bearing wear?

The wear-down reading shows how far the shaft has dropped in the aft bearing and is compared with previous records and class or maker limits. Crankshaft deflection relates to the main engine, not the stern tube.
