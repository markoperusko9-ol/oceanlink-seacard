Fire Detection System on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

A ship's fixed fire detection and fire alarm system uses heat, smoke and flame detectors and manual call points, wired to a control panel on the bridge or in a continuously manned control station. It gives early warning of a fire and identifies the section or detector concerned. It also separately reports its own faults. The requirements sit in SOLAS Chapter II-2 Regulation 7 and the FSS Code Chapter 9, and the system must be tested and maintained under your company's planned maintenance system.

Purpose and where the rules come from

The detection system has one job. It finds a fire early, while it is still small enough to be fought with portable extinguishers or a hose, and it tells the right people exactly where it is.

In an unmanned machinery space at night, the detection system is effectively the only watchkeeper.

The regulatory framework, at chapter level:

  • SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 7 (Detection and alarm) sets out which spaces need fixed detection, manual call points and fire patrols.
  • The required level of detection depends on ship type, and on cargo ships on the accommodation protection method (IC, IIC or IIIC).
  • FSS Code, Chapter 9 gives the engineering standard for fixed fire detection and fire alarm systems.
  • FSS Code, Chapter 10 covers sample extraction smoke detection systems, typically fitted in cargo holds.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 14 requires fire protection systems to be kept in operational readiness and maintained.
  • IMO guidance in MSC.1/Circ.1432 is widely used as the basis for maintenance and inspection intervals.

Your flag administration and class may add their own requirements. Your company SMS will turn all of this into specific routines.

Main components

Control and indicating panel. This is located on the navigation bridge or in a continuously manned central control station. Many ships also have repeater panels in the ECR, fire control station or cargo control room. The panel shows:

  • fire alarms
  • fault alarms
  • disabled zones
  • power status

Detectors. The common types are:

  • Optical (photoelectric) smoke detectors. These are the standard choice in accommodation corridors, stairways and cabins.
  • Ionisation smoke detectors. These are found on older installations. They contain a small radioactive source, so a removed head must be handled and landed for disposal as the manufacturer and local rules require. It must never go in the general garbage.
  • Heat detectors. These are either fixed-temperature or rate-of-rise types. They are used in galleys, laundries, drying rooms and other places where smoke or steam would cause nuisance alarms.
  • Flame detectors. These sense ultraviolet, infrared or both. They are used in machinery spaces near high-risk equipment such as main engine fuel areas, purifiers, boilers and the incinerator.
  • Multi-sensor detectors. These combine smoke and heat to cut false alarms.

Manual call points. These are break-glass or push-button units. They are fitted throughout accommodation, service spaces and control stations, with one at each exit.

Wiring. Detectors are wired in loops or sections.

  • On a conventional system the panel identifies the zone.
  • On an addressable system it identifies the individual detector. Addressable systems are now standard on most newbuildings. Whether individual detector identification is mandatory depends on ship type, space and build date.

Power supplies. There are two sources: main and emergency. They have separate feeders and automatic changeover, and the panel normally has battery backup.

Outputs. On alarm, the panel can trigger:

  • sounders and the general alarm interface
  • the UMS alarm and extension alarm system
  • fire door release
  • ventilation fan stops
  • on some ships, fire damper closure

How it works, step by step

  1. Detection. A detector senses its stimulus:
    • smoke particles scattering light in an optical chamber
    • a temperature threshold or rapid temperature rise
    • flame radiation flickering at characteristic frequencies
  2. Signal to panel. The detector changes state (conventional) or sends a coded signal (addressable). Its local LED usually lights.
  3. Alarm at the panel. Audible and visual fire alarm sounds on the bridge or in the control station, and the zone or detector address is displayed.
  4. Escalation. If nobody acknowledges the alarm within two minutes, the FSS Code requires an audible alarm to sound automatically throughout the following areas:
    • crew accommodation
    • service spaces
    • control stations
    • machinery spaces of category A
  5. Interfaces operate. Depending on the design, fire doors are released, ventilation is stopped and the duty engineer is called on UMS ships.
  6. Investigation and response. The OOW acknowledges the alarm, sends someone to investigate and follows the ship's fire procedure. The panel is reset only after the cause is found.

Typical performance figures from FSS Code Chapter 9:

Detector Required behaviour
Smoke, accommodation escape routes Must operate before smoke density exceeds 12.5% obscuration per metre, but not before it exceeds 2% per metre
Heat Must operate between 54°C and 78°C when temperature rises slowly (below 1°C per minute)
Heat, high-ambient spaces (e.g. drying rooms) Operating temperature may be higher, within limits set in the Code

The Code also sets maximum floor area and spacing per detector, with different limits for heat and smoke detectors. Shipyard drawings apply these limits. Do not move or add detectors on board without an approved modification.

Sample extraction smoke detection in cargo holds

Many cargo ships use a sample extraction system for holds. Car carriers and ro-ro decks have their own arrangements. It works like this:

  • A fan continuously draws air from each hold through small-bore pipes to a central unit.
  • That unit is often in the CO2 room or near the bridge.
  • At the central unit a smoke sensor checks each sample, and there are usually sight cabinets or indicators for each hold.
  • On many ships the same pipes serve as CO2 discharge lines. A three-way valve switches each line from sampling to flooding.

Points to know:

  • The valves must be set correctly. If a line has been left on CO2 discharge, that hold is not being sampled.
  • Blocked pipes, a stopped fan or a wet filter mean no detection, even though the panel may look normal unless low-flow alarms are fitted.
  • Before fumigation or loading dusty cargo, follow the SMS and manufacturer guidance. Do not simply isolate the system and forget it.

Routine checks and maintenance

Intervals come from your PMS. Most companies base them on MSC.1/Circ.1432 and the maker's manual. A typical pattern:

Each watch / daily

  • Check the panel shows normal, with no fault, disablement or power-failure indication.
  • Check that any isolated zone is logged and has a valid reason, such as an active hot work permit.

Weekly

  • Use the panel's lamp and buzzer test function to confirm all indicators work.
  • Check the sample extraction fan is running and flow indicators are normal.

Periodic (often annual, by section)

  • Test detectors functionally with approved test equipment:
    • hot air for heat detectors
    • test aerosol or smoke for smoke detectors
    • a test lamp for flame detectors
  • Test a sample of detectors and manual call points, rotating through the system so that every device is tested over the cycle the guidance specifies.
  • Test changeover from main to emergency power, and battery backup.
  • Confirm that outputs operate: sounders, fire doors and fan stops.

Good practice when testing

  • Inform the bridge and ECR before starting, and agree how alarms will be handled.
  • Never use a naked flame or a cigarette lighter. Use only proper test kit.
  • Clean or replace dirty heads as the maker specifies. Engine room heads collect oily dust quickly.
  • Record each detector tested so the rotation is genuine, not the same three easy ones every year.
  • Keep the spares and test equipment the system requires, and keep them in good order.

Common faults and symptoms

Symptom Likely causes
Repeated false fire alarm, same detector Dirty or contaminated head, steam from galley or shower, cigarette smoke, aerosol sprays, detector past its service life
Fire alarms in engine room during maintenance Hot work fumes, grinding dust, exhaust leaks, steam leaks. Often a sign the job should have been under a permit with the zone properly managed
Earth fault alarm Water in junction boxes or detector bases (deck washing, condensation, sea spray), damaged cable insulation
Open-circuit / loop fault Detector head removed, broken cable, loose terminal, failed end-of-line device
Short-circuit fault Damaged cable, water ingress, failed detector base
Power supply / battery fault Charger failure, aged batteries, loss of one supply feeder
Hold sampling low-flow or no smoke indication Blocked pipes, wet filters, fan failure, three-way valve in wrong position
Flame detector nuisance alarms Welding arc, sunlight through an open door, reflections from hot surfaces

A fault alarm is distinct from a fire alarm, but it still matters. It means part of the ship may be unprotected. Treat it as a defect to be reported, logged and rectified, not as background noise.

Safety precautions and a worked scenario

Precautions

  • Never leave a zone isolated unattended. If a zone must be disabled for hot work or dusty jobs:
    • record it under the permit system
    • keep it isolated for the minimum time
    • post a fire watch
    • reinstate it and confirm the panel is normal when the job ends
  • Do not cover or bag detectors. If covering is permitted for a specific job, it must be under the permit, and the covers must be removed before the job is closed out.
  • Do not silence and reset without investigating. A false alarm is only false once someone has been to the space and confirmed it.
  • Ionisation heads are handled as radioactive items under maker and local requirements.

Scenario: UMS engine room alarm at 0300

The bridge panel shows fire alarm, engine room zone, a smoke detector near the purifier room. The OOW should:

  1. Acknowledge the alarm. This stops the two-minute escalation, but the alarm stays active on the panel.
  2. Call the duty engineer and inform the Master, following the SMS. Many companies require the general alarm or a fire party muster if there is any doubt.
  3. Ensure the engineer investigates with a radio. They must not enter alone into smoke, and must not open doors carelessly into a space that may be on fire.
  4. If fire is confirmed:
    • raise the general alarm and muster the fire parties
    • the Master decides on boundary cooling, ventilation shutdown and, if necessary, fixed system release
  5. If no fire is found, identify the cause, such as purifier sludge discharge vapour or a leaking hot pipe lagging. Rectify it, then reset the panel and log the event.
  6. If the detector is faulty, report it, raise a defect and arrange compensating measures, such as extra rounds, until it is repaired.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Examiners like these. Answer in your own words and connect them to your ship.

  • What types of detector are on your last ship and where? Name actual spaces: smoke in corridors, heat in the galley, flame near the main engine.
  • What happens if a fire alarm is not acknowledged? After two minutes it sounds automatically throughout crew accommodation, service spaces, control stations and category A machinery spaces.
  • How is the system powered? By main and emergency sources on separate feeders with automatic changeover, plus battery backup in the panel.
  • How do you test a smoke detector? With an approved test aerosol or smoke generator, after informing the bridge, and you record which head was tested.
  • What is the difference between a fire alarm and a fault alarm? A fire alarm means a detector or call point has operated. A fault alarm means part of the system cannot detect: open circuit, short circuit, earth fault or power loss.
  • Why heat detectors in the galley? Cooking smoke and steam would cause constant false alarms from smoke detectors.
  • How does a hold sample extraction system work, and what if a line is set to CO2? The fan draws samples to a central smoke sensor. If a line is set to CO2, that hold is not monitored.
  • Where are the regulations? SOLAS II-2 Regulation 7, and FSS Code Chapters 9 and 10.
Key points
  • Fixed detection is required by SOLAS II-2 Regulation 7; the technical standard is FSS Code Chapter 9, with Chapter 10 for sample extraction systems.
  • Smoke, heat, flame and multi-sensor detectors are chosen to suit each space; heat detectors go where smoke or steam is normal.
  • An unacknowledged fire alarm escalates automatically after two minutes to crew accommodation, service spaces, control stations and category A machinery spaces.
  • The system needs main and emergency power with automatic changeover, and its fault alarm is distinct from its fire alarm.
  • A fault or disabled zone means part of the ship is unprotected, so log it, minimise it and reinstate it.
  • Test with approved equipment on a recorded rotation set by your PMS and maker's manual, never with a naked flame.
  • Never reset a fire alarm until someone has physically confirmed the cause.

Frequently asked questions

Can I isolate a fire detector zone during hot work?

Only if your SMS permits it, and then under the permit to work system. Isolate for the minimum time, post a fire watch and record the isolation. Reinstate it and confirm the panel is normal before the permit is closed.

Why does the galley use heat detectors instead of smoke detectors?

Normal cooking produces smoke and steam that would set off smoke detectors constantly. Heat detectors respond to a temperature threshold or rapid rise instead, so they still detect a real fire with far fewer nuisance alarms.

What should the OOW do when a fire alarm sounds on the bridge?

Acknowledge it, note the zone or detector and send someone to investigate immediately. Inform the Master and, at night on UMS ships, the duty engineer, following the ship's procedure. Reset only after the cause is confirmed, and log the event.

How often must fire detectors be tested?

Intervals are set by your company's planned maintenance system. These usually follow IMO guidance in MSC.1/Circ.1432 and the maker's manual, with flag or class additions where they apply. Typically panel indicators are checked weekly and detectors are tested on a rotating periodic basis so every device is covered over the cycle.

What does an earth fault alarm on the fire panel mean?

Part of the detection wiring has leaked to earth, often from water in a junction box or damaged cable insulation. The system may still work, but reliability is reduced and a second fault could disable a section. Report it, raise a defect and trace it as soon as possible.

Test yourself

A fire alarm on the bridge panel is not acknowledged. What happens after two minutes under the FSS Code?

FSS Code Chapter 9 requires automatic escalation to these areas if the alarm receives no attention within two minutes. The detection panel does not release a total-flooding CO2 system; that requires deliberate manual release after evacuation of the space.

Which detector type is most appropriate for a ship's galley?

Cooking smoke and steam cause constant false alarms from smoke detectors, so galleys normally use heat detectors (design practice; detector suitability per FSS Code Ch 9).

The fire panel shows a fault alarm, not a fire alarm. What does this indicate?

Fault alarms indicate conditions such as an open circuit, short circuit, earth fault or power loss that can leave a section unprotected (FSS Code Ch 9; SOLAS II-2 Reg 14 on operational readiness).

On a cargo hold sample extraction system, a three-way valve has been left in the CO2 discharge position. What is the consequence?

Where sampling and CO2 lines are shared, the valve position decides the function. In the discharge position that hold is not monitored (FSS Code Ch 10).

What is the correct way to function-test a smoke detector?

Testing must use proper test equipment and be coordinated and recorded under the PMS (SOLAS II-2 Reg 14; MSC.1/Circ.1432). Naked flames and shorting the base are unsafe and do not prove the sensor works.
