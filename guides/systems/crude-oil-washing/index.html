Crude Oil Washing (COW): A Practical Guide for Tanker Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

Crude oil washing (COW) cleans crude oil cargo tanks during discharge by spraying part of the cargo back into the tanks through fixed washing machines. The tanks must be inerted throughout. COW is required by MARPOL Annex I for crude oil tankers of 20,000 dwt and above. It reduces sludge and remaining on board (ROB) quantities, improves cargo outturn and cuts the oily residue that would otherwise end up in slops.

What crude oil washing is and why we do it

Crude oil washing means cleaning cargo tanks with the cargo itself instead of water. While the ship discharges, a portion of the cargo pump discharge is diverted into a dedicated COW line. It is then sprayed at high velocity through fixed tank washing machines.

Crude oil is a good solvent for the waxy and asphaltic deposits that settle on tank bottoms and structure. The washings mix with the cargo and are pumped ashore with it.

Why it matters:

  • Pollution prevention. Less residue stays on board, so less oil ends up in slops and less oily water is generated when tanks are later water washed or ballasted.
  • Better outturn. Cargo that would have stayed as clingage and sludge is delivered to the receiver. This keeps ROB disputes down.
  • Sludge control. Regular COW stops sludge building up year on year. Uncontrolled sludge reduces cargo capacity and makes tank entry and repairs harder.
  • Ballast readiness. Cargo tanks that may take ballast on the ballast passage have to be crude washed first.

COW is a crude oil tanker operation only. It is not used on product or chemical tankers, and not every crude is a suitable wash medium. The ship's approved manual lists the crudes that may be used and any that may not.

Main components of a COW system

Layouts vary by builder, but these are the main parts you will find on most crude carriers:

Component Function
Cargo pumps Supply the wash medium, usually by bleeding off part of the discharge
COW main and branch lines Permanent steel pipework on deck, dedicated to tank washing and kept independent of the fire main
Isolating valves Isolate each tank washing machine or group of machines
Tank washing machines Fixed deck-mounted or submerged machines with one or two nozzles. Most are programmable for nozzle elevation arc and rotation
Drive units Turn the nozzle. They may be driven by the wash medium itself or be separate (often portable) drive units shifted from machine to machine
Stripping system Stripping pump or eductors that keep the tank bottom drained during washing
Inert gas system Keeps every tank under wash inerted, with positive pressure in the IG main
Gauging and oxygen measurement Closed ullage gauging, fixed or portable O2 analysers, and arrangements for checking tank bottoms after washing
Pressure gauges Show pressure at the pump discharge and on the COW line

If the ship also has a water washing heater, it must be isolated from the COW line so that crude can never be routed through it. Washing machines are fitted so that their combined jets reach the tank surfaces that matter. The "shadow areas" behind structure that the jets cannot hit are identified in the manual.

How COW is carried out, step by step

The ship-specific procedure is set out in the Crude Oil Washing Operations and Equipment Manual. This manual is approved by the flag Administration and must be followed. A typical sequence looks like this:

Before arrival

  1. Draw up the COW plan with the discharge plan. It should cover which tanks will be washed, in what order, single-stage or multi-stage, and which tanks are being washed for sludge control or for ballast.
  2. Pressure test the COW lines to working pressure and check for leaks before each discharge in which COW is planned, as required by the IMO COW specifications and the ship's manual.
  3. Check the machines, drive units, valves and gauges. Confirm that communications between deck and the cargo control room work.
  4. Agree COW with the terminal. It forms part of the ship/shore safety checklist exchange, and the terminal must accept it before you start.

Before washing each tank

  1. Confirm the IG plant is working and delivering gas within limits. Confirm the IG main is under positive pressure.
  2. Measure the oxygen content in the tank to be washed. It must not exceed 8% by volume.
  3. Make sure the wash medium is not water-contaminated. The source tank should have been dewatered as the manual requires, because crude/water mixtures increase the static electricity hazard.

During washing

  1. Top wash. This is done in the upper part of the tank as the ullage increases, often while the tank is still being discharged.
  2. Bottom wash. This is done when the tank is nearly empty. The stripping system must keep the bottom drained so the jets hit the deposits and not a pool of oil.
  3. Monitor nozzle movement, line pressure, O2 levels, IG pressure and tank ullages. Watch the deck for leaks.
  4. Strip the tank thoroughly. Wash medium and residues go ashore with the cargo.

After washing

  1. Check the tank bottom is drained, by sounding or by the method the manual specifies, at more than one point where possible.
  2. Drain the COW lines back to a cargo or slop tank before departure.
  3. Record the operation in Oil Record Book Part II and keep the COW records the manual requires.

Routine checks and maintenance

COW machines spend most of their life idle in a corrosive tank atmosphere. Planned maintenance is what keeps them working on the day you need them.

  • Machine function checks: turn each machine through its programme between discharges, as your planned maintenance system requires. Confirm free rotation and correct elevation limits.
  • Gland and seal inspection: deck-mounted machines leak at the gland if seals are worn. Renew them as per the maker's manual.
  • Drive units: clean, lubricate and test them. With portable units, check the couplings and the drive dogs on each machine.
  • Nozzles: check for wear and blockage when tanks are gas-free and entry is permitted under the enclosed space procedure.
  • Pipework and valves: pressure test, check supports and expansion arrangements, and confirm the isolating valves hold.
  • Pressure gauges: calibrate them or check them against a master gauge.
  • O2 analysers: calibrate fixed and portable instruments before use, as the maker's instructions require.
  • Stripping system: keep eductors and stripping pumps in condition. Poor stripping makes COW ineffective.

Keep records. Vetting inspectors and port State control officers commonly ask for evidence of COW machine maintenance and line pressure tests.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault Likely symptom Usual cause
Machine not rotating Position indicator stationary, no audible stepping Drive unit failure, seized gear, blocked nozzle, debris in machine
Low wash pressure Line pressure below the manual's figure, poor jet throw Too many machines open at once, pump output too low, valve not fully open
Poor cleaning result High ROB, sludge in shadow areas, high readings on bottom soundings Inadequate stripping, wrong programme, wash medium not suitable, low pressure
Gland or flange leak Oil on deck at the machine or line Worn seals, missed pressure test, gasket failure
Tank O2 rising Analyser reading climbing towards the limit IG plant fault, low IG main pressure, leaking ullage or vapour openings
Pooling on tank bottom Stripping suction losing, ullage not falling Stripping pump or eductor problem, trim not suitable for draining

If the oxygen limit is exceeded or IG main pressure is lost, stop washing at once and do not restart until the atmosphere is restored. The same applies to any significant leak: stop, isolate and report.

Safety precautions

COW involves high-pressure crude being sprayed inside a tank full of hydrocarbon vapour. The inert atmosphere is what makes it safe.

  • Never wash a tank that is not inerted within the oxygen limit. Never continue if the IG supply fails.
  • Avoid water in the wash medium. Washing with crude/water mixtures generates more static charge.
  • Closed operations. Use closed gauging and sampling. Do not open ullage ports or hatches on tanks being washed.
  • Watch for leaks on deck. A burst COW line puts crude onto the deck, which is both a fire and a pollution risk. Keep a deck watch during washing, as the manual and company SMS require.
  • Vapour emission. Washing generates vapour. Follow terminal and SMS requirements on venting and pressure in the IG main.
  • Qualified person in charge. The manual and the specifications require COW to be supervised by a suitably experienced officer. Crew involved must be trained, which is part of the oil tanker training under STCW.
  • Terminal restrictions. Some terminals limit or prohibit COW, or set their own conditions. These must be complied with.

Regulations in brief

  • MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 33: requires crude oil tankers of 20,000 tonnes deadweight and above, of the age categories defined in the regulation, to be fitted with a COW system. It also requires the system to meet IMO's specifications.
  • MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 35: covers COW operations. These include the approved Operations and Equipment Manual and the requirement for tanks to be crude washed before they take ballast.
  • MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 36: requires COW operations to be recorded in Oil Record Book Part II.
  • IMO Revised Specifications for the design, operation and control of COW systems (Resolution A.446(XI) as amended): set out the detailed technical and operational requirements behind the manual.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2 and FSS Code Chapter 15: cover the inert gas system. The FSS Code requires the plant to deliver gas with an oxygen content not exceeding 5% by volume, and to keep cargo tank atmospheres at no more than 8%.

Flag Administrations approve the COW manual. Check your own manual for ship-specific figures such as wash pressures, programmes and stripping requirements.

Oral exam style questions

Q: Why must tanks be inerted for COW? High-velocity jets can generate static charges in the tank. An oxygen content of 8% or less by volume means the atmosphere cannot support combustion even if there is a discharge.

Q: What is the difference between top and bottom washing? The top wash covers the upper structure as the tank empties. The bottom wash is done near the end of discharge, with the stripping system keeping the bottom drained.

Q: Where would you find the COW procedures for your ship? In the Crude Oil Washing Operations and Equipment Manual, which is approved by the Administration.

Q: What do you check before starting to wash a tank? - IG plant running and the IG main under positive pressure - Tank O2 within the limit - COW lines tested and tight - Terminal agreement in place - Wash medium dewatered - Stripping system ready - Communications working

Q: Where is COW recorded? In Oil Record Book Part II and in the records required by the manual.

Q: What do you do if O2 in a tank being washed rises above the limit? Stop washing that tank immediately. Find and correct the cause, then restore the atmosphere before resuming.

Key points
  • COW cleans crude tanks during discharge using the cargo itself as the wash medium.
  • MARPOL Annex I requires COW systems on crude oil tankers of 20,000 dwt and above.
  • Tanks must be inerted, with O2 at or below 8% by volume and the IG main at positive pressure, before and during washing.
  • Effective stripping is essential, especially during the bottom wash.
  • The Administration-approved COW Operations and Equipment Manual is the governing ship-specific document.
  • Water-contaminated crude must not be used as wash medium because of the static hazard.
  • COW is recorded in Oil Record Book Part II.

Frequently asked questions

Is crude oil washing mandatory on all tankers?

No. MARPOL Annex I requires COW on crude oil tankers of 20,000 tonnes deadweight and above, within the categories defined in the regulation. Product and chemical tankers do not crude wash. Smaller crude tankers may have COW systems, but the regulation does not require them.

Why is COW done with inert gas in the tanks?

Washing at high velocity can create static electricity inside the tank. Keeping the oxygen content at or below 8% by volume means the tank atmosphere cannot ignite even if a discharge occurs.

Can any crude oil be used for COW?

No. Some crudes are not effective as a wash medium. The approved COW manual identifies which cargoes are suitable. Any water must also be removed from the wash medium first, as the manual describes.

Does the terminal need to agree to COW?

Yes. COW is agreed with the terminal before discharge as part of the ship/shore safety checks. Some terminals impose their own restrictions or do not permit it.

Which training covers COW for officers?

COW is part of the advanced training for oil tanker cargo operations under STCW. The person in charge of COW must also meet the experience requirements set out in the ship's approved manual and IMO's COW specifications.

Test yourself

What is the maximum oxygen content permitted in a cargo tank during crude oil washing?

Tank atmosphere must not exceed 8% O2 by volume. The 5% figure is the IG plant delivery limit, not the tank limit. Reference: FSS Code Chapter 15; IMO COW specifications.

Which document gives the ship-specific procedures for crude oil washing?

MARPOL Annex I requires an Administration-approved COW Operations and Equipment Manual. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 35.

Why must the stripping system keep the tank bottom drained during the bottom wash?

A pool on the bottom absorbs the jet energy and the sludge is not removed, so washing is ineffective. Reference: IMO COW specifications (Res. A.446(XI) as amended).

Where is crude oil washing recorded on board?

Part II covers cargo and ballast operations, including COW. Part I is for machinery space operations. Reference: MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 36.

During COW the IG main pressure is lost. What should be done?

Washing must stop if the IG main loses positive pressure or O2 exceeds the limit. Opening tanks lets in air. Reference: IMO COW specifications; SOLAS Chapter II-2.
