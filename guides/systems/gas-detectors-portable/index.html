Portable Gas Detectors on Merchant Ships

Quick answer

A portable gas detector measures oxygen, flammable gas or vapour, and toxic gases such as hydrogen sulphide and carbon monoxide, and crew use it before and during enclosed space entry, hot work and cargo operations. SOLAS requires ships to carry suitable atmosphere testing instruments, and tankers and gas carriers carry additional instruments under their own codes. A reading is only as good as the sensor type, the bump test, the calibration and the sampling technique behind it.

What portable gas detectors are for and what the rules require

Many enclosed space casualties occur in atmospheres that look and smell normal. The portable gas detector is the crew's main way of finding out what the atmosphere actually contains. It answers three questions:

  • Is there enough oxygen? Too little causes collapse without warning. Too much increases fire risk.
  • Is there flammable gas or vapour? This is usually shown as a percentage of the lower flammable (explosive) limit, % LFL or % LEL.
  • Are toxic gases present? The usual ones are hydrogen sulphide (H2S) and carbon monoxide (CO). Cargo-specific vapours such as benzene are added where relevant.

The regulatory picture, at chapter level:

  • SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 requires ships to which SOLAS Chapter I applies to carry appropriate portable atmosphere testing instruments. They must be able to measure at least oxygen, flammable gases or vapours, H2S and CO before entry into enclosed spaces. IMO guidance on selecting these instruments is in MSC.1/Circ.1477.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2, Regulation 4 requires tankers to carry portable instruments for measuring oxygen and flammable vapour concentrations, with spares and a means of calibration.
  • FSS Code Chapter 15 covers inert gas systems. Measuring oxygen and hydrocarbon in inerted tanks needs instruments that work in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere.
  • IBC Code Chapter 13 and IGC Code Chapter 13 cover vapour and gas detection on chemical tankers and gas carriers. This includes detection of toxic cargo vapours.
  • SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 3 requires suitable gas and oxygen measuring instruments when carrying solid bulk cargoes that can give off toxic or flammable gases or deplete oxygen.

The exact instruments carried, the alarm set points and the calibration intervals are set by the flag administration, the manufacturer and your company SMS. Always check your own ship's procedures.

Main components and sensor types

A typical multigas detector contains the following parts:

  • Housing certified intrinsically safe for hazardous areas. Check the marking: IECEx, ATEX or another scheme depending on the maker and flag.
  • Sensors, usually three to five, each for a specific gas.
  • Sampling system. This is either diffusion (gas reaches the sensors on its own) or a pump, internal or clip-on, that draws a sample through a hose and probe.
  • Filters and water trap to keep dust and liquid out of the sensors.
  • Display and alarms: audible, flashing light and vibration, with low, high, short-term (STEL) and time-weighted average (TWA) alarms where fitted.
  • Battery and charger, plus an event and data log on most modern units.

Common sensor technologies:

Sensor Measures Key limitation
Electrochemical (galvanic) O2 Consumable cell with limited life. Affected by temperature and pressure changes
Catalytic bead (pellistor) Flammable gas, % LFL Needs oxygen to work. Reads falsely low in inerted or oxygen-deficient atmospheres. Can be poisoned
Infrared (NDIR) Hydrocarbons, often % volume Works in inert gas. Does not detect hydrogen
Electrochemical (toxic) H2S, CO and others Cross-sensitivity. For example, CO cells can respond to hydrogen
Photo-ionisation (PID) Volatile organic compounds Needs correction factors for specific chemicals
Detector tubes with hand pump Specific gases such as benzene or fumigants Single use. Check expiry date and the correct number of pump strokes

On tankers you will also meet the tankscope, a hydrocarbon meter reading % volume that works without oxygen (modern units are typically infrared), used in inerted tanks. The explosimeter or LEL meter is used for gas-freeing and entry checks where air is present. Mixing these up is a classic mistake and a classic oral exam question.

How it works: taking a reading step by step

  1. Pre-use check. Check battery charge, calibration due date, bump test record, and that the filters and hose are clean and undamaged.
  2. Switch on in fresh air. Let the instrument complete its self-test and sensor warm-up.
  3. Zero (fresh-air set-up) in known clean air. Do this on open deck, upwind, away from vents, exhausts and cargo openings. The instrument then sets that air as zero for flammable and toxic gases and, on most models, as 20.9% oxygen. Zeroing in contaminated air hides the very gas you are looking for.
  4. Bump test. Apply known test gas and confirm every sensor responds and every alarm activates, as the manufacturer and SMS require.
  5. Leak-check the sampling line. Block the hose inlet. The pump should go into flow-fault alarm. If it does not, the line is leaking and will draw in outside air.
  6. Sample from outside the space. Lower the hose and probe through the opening. Do not put your head in.
  7. Wait for the sample to arrive. Allow the hose transit time stated by the manufacturer for the length of hose used, then wait for a steady reading.
  8. Test at several levels. Gases stratify. H2S and many cargo vapours are heavier than air and collect low down. Methane and hydrogen rise. Test top, middle and bottom, and remote corners where you can reach.
  9. Record the readings on the permit with time, location and the name of the tester.
  10. Monitor continuously during entry. Use a personal detector worn by the entrants and/or continuous monitoring from the entrance, as your SMS requires.

The IMO recommendations for entering enclosed spaces call for a steady oxygen reading of 21% by volume and flammable gas not more than 1% LFL before entry without breathing apparatus. Toxic gases must be well below their occupational exposure limits. Your company may set its own figures. Follow the permit.

Worked scenario: testing a ballast tank before entry

Situation: On a bulk carrier, the No. 3 port topside ballast tank has been emptied and ventilated for inspection. The chief officer has issued an enclosed space entry permit. You are the officer testing the atmosphere.

  • Fresh-air zero: You zero on the hatch cover, upwind. O2 shows 20.9%, LEL 0, H2S 0, CO 0. The bump test passes.
  • Top of manhole: O2 20.9%, all others 0.
  • Hose lowered to mid-depth: O2 20.6%. You wait. It holds at 20.6%.
  • Hose to the bottom, aft end: O2 19.8%, H2S 2 ppm.

What it means: Oxygen is being used up somewhere low in the tank, most likely by corrosion. Some H2S is present, possibly from decaying sediment. The top reading alone would have passed the tank.

Action: Do not sign the permit as safe. Report to the chief officer. Ventilate further, positioning ducting so air reaches the bottom and far corners. Retest at all levels after a suitable interval. Entry proceeds only when all levels meet the permit criteria, with continuous monitoring by the entrants.

Lesson: One reading at the manhole proves nothing about the bottom of the tank.

Routine checks, bump testing and calibration

  • Bump test (functional check). Expose the instrument to a known gas to confirm the sensors respond and the alarms work. No adjustment is made. Many manufacturers recommend one before each day's use. Follow the manual and SMS.
  • Calibration. Adjust the instrument so it reads correctly against a certified calibration gas. Do this if the bump test fails and at the interval set by the manufacturer or company. Some ships calibrate on board. Others send instruments ashore to an approved service agent.
  • Calibration gas. Check the cylinder certificate, contents, concentration and expiry date. Out-of-date gas makes the whole exercise meaningless. Note which gas the LEL sensor was calibrated on. Other hydrocarbons give a different response, and the manufacturer provides correction factors.
  • Records. Log every bump test, calibration, sensor replacement and fault. Planned maintenance under the ISM Code, and inspectors such as port State control and vetting, will look for these records.
  • Housekeeping. Charge batteries as the manufacturer instructs. Replace filters and water traps. Inspect hoses for cracks and kinks. Store instruments away from silicone sprays and solvents.
  • Spares. Keep spare sensors, filters, hoses and test gas as your SMS requires. Tankers in particular must carry adequate spares.

Safety precautions

  • Never use an instrument with an overdue calibration, a failed bump test or a damaged housing.
  • Use only intrinsically safe instruments in hazardous areas. Do not open battery compartments or charge in those areas.
  • Choose the right sensor for the atmosphere. Use an LEL meter where air is present and an infrared % volume meter in inerted tanks.
  • Remember what the instrument cannot see. A standard four-gas unit will not detect benzene, fumigants or many cargo vapours. Use a PID or detector tubes where the cargo or SDS calls for them.
  • Atmospheres change. Cargo residues, sludge, scale and pipelines can release gas during work. Ventilation can stop. Monitor continuously.
  • An alarm during entry means everyone gets out at once. Investigate afterwards, not during.
  • The person testing must be trained and competent on that model. Familiarisation should cover every type of instrument on board.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Which gases must a SOLAS ship's portable instrument measure before enclosed space entry? Oxygen, flammable gases or vapours, H2S and CO, under SOLAS XI-1/7.
  • Why can you not use an explosimeter in an inerted cargo tank? The catalytic sensor needs oxygen to burn the gas on the bead. In inert gas it under-reads, so you use an infrared % volume meter.
  • What is the difference between a bump test and calibration? A bump test checks the response and alarms without adjustment. Calibration adjusts the readings against a certified gas.
  • Where do you zero the instrument, and why? In known clean fresh air. Zeroing in contaminated air makes the instrument subtract the hazard.
  • Why test at different levels? Gases stratify according to their density.
  • What poisons a catalytic sensor? Silicones, lead compounds, sulphur compounds and some halogenated substances.
  • Your four-gas meter reads clear in a chemical tanker tank. Can you enter? Not on that reading alone. Check the cargo's previous contents and test for its specific vapours with a PID or detector tubes as the permit requires.
Key points
  • SOLAS XI-1/7 requires portable instruments able to measure oxygen, flammable gas or vapour, H2S and CO before enclosed space entry.
  • Catalytic LEL sensors need oxygen and under-read in inerted tanks, where you must use an infrared % volume meter.
  • Zero only in clean fresh air, and bump test before use as the manufacturer and SMS require.
  • Sample from outside the space, allow hose transit time, and test top, middle and bottom.
  • Standard multigas units miss many cargo vapours and fumigants, so use a PID or detector tubes where required.
  • Calibration gas, intervals and records are checked by inspectors, so keep them in date and logged.
  • Any alarm during entry means immediate evacuation, with the investigation done afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a bump test and a calibration on a gas detector?

A bump test exposes the detector to a known gas to prove that the sensors respond and the alarms activate. Nothing is adjusted. Calibration adjusts the instrument's readings to match a certified calibration gas. If a bump test fails, the instrument must be calibrated or repaired before use.

How often does a portable gas detector need calibrating on a ship?

There is no single international figure. The interval comes from the manufacturer's instructions and your company SMS. Some ships calibrate on board with certified gas, while others send instruments ashore to an approved service agent. Whatever the interval, record every calibration and keep the certificate.

Can I use an LEL meter to check an inerted cargo tank?

No. A catalytic LEL sensor needs oxygen to work, so in an inert atmosphere it reads falsely low. Use an infrared hydrocarbon meter that reads % volume, together with an oxygen analyser suitable for inert gas.

Which gases must the ship's portable gas detector measure?

Under SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7, the instruments must measure at least oxygen, flammable gases or vapours, hydrogen sulphide and carbon monoxide before enclosed space entry. Tankers, gas carriers and ships carrying certain bulk cargoes need additional instruments under their own rules. Your cargo may require testing for other specific substances too.

Do I need to carry a personal gas detector inside an enclosed space?

The IMO recommendations call for the atmosphere to be monitored while people are inside, because conditions can change during the work. Most company SMSs meet this by requiring entrants to wear a personal multigas detector, sometimes in addition to monitoring from the entrance. Check your permit conditions.

Test yourself

Why is a catalytic (pellistor) LEL sensor unsuitable for measuring hydrocarbon gas in an inerted cargo tank?

Catalytic sensors oxidise the gas on a heated bead, which needs oxygen. Inerted tanks need an instrument designed to read hydrocarbon % volume in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, such as an infrared meter.

When testing a tank atmosphere before entry, where should samples be taken?

Gases stratify by density, with H2S settling low and methane rising. The IMO enclosed space entry recommendations call for testing at different levels.

What does a bump test prove?

A bump test is a functional check only. Calibration is the adjustment process, carried out per the manufacturer's instructions and the company SMS.

Where should a portable gas detector be zeroed before testing a space?

Zeroing sets the clean-air baseline. Zeroing in contaminated air makes the instrument subtract the gas present and hides the hazard.

SOLAS XI-1/7 requires portable atmosphere testing instruments capable of measuring which gases as a minimum?

SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 7 lists oxygen, flammable gases or vapours, H2S and CO as the minimum. Other gases depend on cargo and ship type.
