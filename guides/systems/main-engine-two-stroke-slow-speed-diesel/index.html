Main Engine (Two-Stroke Slow-Speed Diesel): A Practical Guide

Quick answer

Most large merchant ships are driven by a two-stroke, slow-speed crosshead diesel coupled directly to the propeller shaft. Most modern engines use uniflow scavenging. All have a separate cylinder lubrication system and a crosshead that keeps combustion products out of the crankcase. Engineers are expected to know its parts, its cycle, its watchkeeping checks and the emergencies it can produce: scavenge fires, crankcase explosions and starting air line explosions.

Purpose and why it is built this way

The main engine (ME) turns the propeller. On most bulk carriers, tankers and container ships it is a large two-stroke crosshead diesel coupled directly to the shaft. There is usually no reduction gearbox, so engine speed equals propeller speed. The design favours low revolutions because a large, slow-turning propeller is more efficient.

The engine is usually directly reversible. To go astern, the engine itself is started and run in the opposite direction. On ships with a controllable pitch propeller, the engine runs in one direction and the pitch is reversed instead.

Why a crosshead design?

  • The piston rod and crosshead carry the side thrust, not the piston skirt and liner.
  • The piston rod stuffing box separates the scavenge space from the crankcase. Combustion residue and cylinder oil stay out of the system oil. This lets the engine burn residual fuels and use a dedicated cylinder oil.
  • The engine can use a long stroke, which suits low speed and good fuel efficiency.

Two designers dominate the fleet: MAN B&W and WinGD (formerly Wärtsilä/Sulzer). Mitsubishi UEC engines are also in service. Older and licence-built engines are still at sea in large numbers. Always work to the engine maker's instruction manual and your company SMS. Limits and procedures differ between engine types.

Main components

Structure - Bedplate – carries the main bearings and forms the bottom of the crankcase. - A-frames / column – sit on the bedplate and carry the crosshead guides. - Cylinder frame (entablature) – houses the liners and forms the scavenge air space. - Tie bolts – long bolts that clamp bedplate, frames and cylinder frame together. They keep the cast parts in compression under firing loads.

Running gear - Crankshaft – usually semi-built. Each throw (crankpin and webs) is forged or cast in one piece, and the main journals are shrunk into the webs. - Connecting rod, crosshead and guide shoes – the crosshead bearing is the most heavily loaded bearing in the engine. - Piston rod and stuffing box – the box scrapes off oil and seals the crankcase from the scavenge space. - Piston – cooled by oil or water depending on the design. Fitted with compression rings. - Thrust bearing – passes propeller thrust into the hull.

Combustion and gas exchange - Cylinder liner – has scavenge ports at the bottom and quills for cylinder oil injection. - Cylinder cover – carries the fuel valves, starting air valve, safety valve and indicator cock. - Exhaust valve – a single central valve, usually opened hydraulically and closed by an air spring. - Turbocharger(s), scavenge air cooler and water mist catcher. - Auxiliary blowers – supply scavenge air at low load, when the turbocharger cannot supply enough.

Fuel and control - Camshaft engines – cam-driven fuel pumps and exhaust valve actuators. - Electronically controlled engines (MAN ME, WinGD RT-flex/X) – no camshaft. Hydraulic or common-rail systems time injection and exhaust valve movement. - Governor / engine control system, safety system (shutdowns and slowdowns) and alarm system. - Starting air system – a main starting valve, a distributor (or electronic control) and a starting valve in each cylinder cover.

Lubrication - System oil – feeds the bearings, the crosshead and often piston cooling. It is circulated from the sump tank and cleaned by purifiers. - Cylinder oil – a separate once-through system. Its base number (BN) is chosen to suit the fuel sulphur content.

How it works, step by step

A two-stroke engine completes its cycle in one revolution: one compression stroke and one power stroke. Most modern engines use uniflow scavenging. Air enters through the liner ports at the bottom and exhaust leaves through the valve at the top.

  1. Compression – the piston rises and covers the scavenge ports. The exhaust valve closes, and the trapped air is compressed until it is hot enough to ignite fuel.
  2. Injection and combustion – near top dead centre, fuel is injected through the fuel valves. It ignites in the hot air, and pressure rises sharply.
  3. Expansion – gas pressure drives the piston down. Through the crosshead and connecting rod, this turns the crankshaft.
  4. Exhaust – before the piston uncovers the ports, the exhaust valve opens. Hot gas blows down to the turbocharger.
  5. Scavenging – the piston uncovers the ports. Pressurised air from the turbocharger and cooler sweeps upward, pushing out the remaining exhaust and refilling the cylinder.
  6. The piston rises, the ports close, the exhaust valve closes, and the cycle repeats.

Starting and reversing (camshaft engine, simplified):

  1. Check the turning gear is disengaged and the interlock is clear.
  2. Starting air is admitted. The distributor opens each cylinder's starting valve in firing order, during part of the expansion stroke.
  3. Once the engine reaches firing speed, air is cut off and fuel is admitted.
  4. To go astern, the reversing mechanism shifts the fuel cam and air distributor timing (or the control system re-times them electronically). The engine is then started in the other direction.

Watchkeeping checks and routine maintenance

Typical rounds checks (follow your engine's alarm and shutdown settings and logbook format):

  • Exhaust temperatures after each cylinder and before/after the turbocharger. Look for deviation between cylinders, not just absolute values.
  • Scavenge air pressure and temperature. Pressure drop across the air cooler.
  • Pressures and temperatures of system oil, cooling water and piston coolant. Check the flow indicators.
  • Cylinder lubricator operation and feed rate. Check the tank level against consumption.
  • Fuel temperature or viscosity at the engine inlet. Check fuel leak-off pipes for leakage alarms.
  • Turbocharger lube oil, vibration and unusual noise.
  • Scavenge space drains – open them as per routine. Report heavy oily or sludgy discharge, or any water.
  • Stuffing box drain quantity and condition.
  • Oil mist detector (OMD) or bearing temperature monitoring readings.
  • Leaks, loose pipes, unusual knocks or vibration.

Periodic and planned work (intervals are set by the maker and the planned maintenance system):

  • Scavenge port inspection through the ports, with the engine stopped. Check the piston rings (free, broken, collapsed), the liner surface, and deposits in the scavenge space.
  • Crankcase inspection – check the bearings, crosshead and guides. Look for white metal in the sump, bolt locking and oil flow.
  • Indicator / pressure readings to check power balance and compression.
  • Overhaul of fuel valves and exhaust valves.
  • Piston overhaul and liner calibration to track wear.
  • Turbocharger washing (turbine and compressor side, by the approved method), filter cleaning and air cooler cleaning.
  • Cylinder oil drain analysis (residual BN and iron content) to optimise the feed rate.
  • System oil analysis by the shore laboratory.
  • Crankshaft deflection readings, taken under the conditions the maker specifies.

Planned maintenance of critical equipment is an ISM requirement. Keep the records honest, because Port State Control and class surveyors look at them.

Safety precautions and emergency actions

Before any work on the engine

  • Get permission from the bridge.
  • Engage the turning gear and confirm the start interlock is effective.
  • Shut the main starting air valve and vent the starting air line.
  • Apply lock-out/tag-out to the control positions, the auxiliary blowers and the relevant pumps, as per the permit to work.
  • Treat the crankcase and scavenge space as enclosed spaces: ventilate, test the atmosphere and follow the company procedure.
  • Allow the engine to cool before opening the crankcase after an OMD alarm or a hot spot. Fresh air entering hot oil mist is a classic way to trigger an explosion. The waiting time is in the maker's manual or the SMS.

Scavenge fire – general sequence (your engine's procedure takes precedence)

  1. Inform the bridge and the chief engineer, and reduce speed.
  2. Cut fuel to the affected unit and increase its cylinder lubrication. Many small fires burn out this way.
  3. Close the scavenge drains to deny air to the fire. Keep personnel clear of the scavenge doors.
  4. If the fire spreads or does not die out, stop the engine and engage the turning gear. Use the fixed scavenge fire extinguishing system (CO2, steam or water mist, depending on the fit).
  5. Do not open the scavenge space until it has cooled. Then inspect the liner, rings, stuffing box and the space itself before restarting.

Crankcase oil mist alarm

  1. Slow down immediately and inform the bridge.
  2. Keep everyone away from the crankcase relief doors.
  3. Stop the engine when the bridge agrees it is safe to do so. If the maker advises, engage the turning gear and keep the engine turning, with lube oil circulating, to prevent seizure while it cools.
  4. Do not open the crankcase until it has cooled. Then find the hot spot before restarting.

Regulations that apply

Keep this to what you can quote confidently in an oral exam:

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C (Machinery installations) covers:
    • means of going astern
    • machinery controls
    • starting arrangements
    • crankcase explosion relief devices on engines above certain size limits
    • protection against overspeed and other hazards
  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part E adds requirements for periodically unattended machinery spaces (UMS): alarms, safety systems and crankcase protection such as oil mist detection or bearing temperature monitoring.
  • SOLAS Chapter II-2 covers fire safety. This includes protection of hot surfaces, oil fuel arrangements and jacketing of high-pressure fuel delivery lines.
  • MARPOL Annex VI:
    • Regulation 13 covers NOx emission tiers. Each engine carries an EIAPP certificate and a Technical File. The NOx Technical Code 2008 governs certification and onboard verification. Altering NOx-critical components or settings outside the Technical File can breach compliance.
    • Regulation 14 covers fuel sulphur limits, which is one reason cylinder oil BN must match the fuel.
  • Classification society rules cover the detailed design, survey cycles (including continuous machinery survey) and repairs. These vary by class society, so follow your ship's class requirements.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Why does a crosshead engine need a separate cylinder oil? The stuffing box isolates the crankcase. Cylinder oil can therefore be formulated, with a suitable BN, to neutralise acids from the fuel sulphur. The system oil stays clean for the bearings.

What is the purpose of tie bolts? They hold the structure in compression so the cast parts are not subjected to tensile firing loads.

Why does the starting air valve open only after TDC? Air must push the piston down during the expansion stroke. Air admitted during compression would oppose the engine's rotation.

How is a starting air line explosion prevented? Through several measures:

  • non-return valves and relief valves or bursting caps in the line
  • flame arresters where fitted
  • draining the oil and water from the air system
  • checking for a hot starting air pipe after each start

Why must you not open the crankcase immediately after an oil mist alarm? Air entering a space full of oil mist with a hot spot can cause a secondary explosion, which is worse than the first.

What does a rising exhaust temperature on one unit tell you? Compare it with the scavenge temperature, the indicator readings and the fuel pump index. Then suspect the fuel valve, the exhaust valve or the rings, in that order of ease to check.

What is surging and how do you stop it? Surging is a breakdown of airflow through the compressor. The immediate action is to reduce load. The cure is to clean the turbocharger, air filter and cooler, and to balance the cylinder loads.

Why do we take crankshaft deflections? To detect misalignment from main bearing wear or hull deflection before it cracks the crankshaft.

Key points
  • Large merchant ships mostly use two-stroke crosshead diesels coupled directly to the propeller, usually without a gearbox.
  • The piston rod stuffing box separates the scavenge space from the crankcase, allowing a separate cylinder oil.
  • In modern uniflow-scavenged engines, air enters through the liner ports and exhaust leaves through a single valve in the cover.
  • Scavenge drains, exhaust temperature deviation and OMD readings are your early warning of trouble.
  • Never open a crankcase straight after an oil mist alarm, and keep clear of the relief doors.
  • Engage the turning gear, shut and vent starting air, and lock out before any work on the engine.
  • Annex VI NOx compliance depends on keeping the engine to its EIAPP Technical File.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a two-stroke and a four-stroke marine engine?

A two-stroke completes its cycle in one revolution and is typically slow-speed, crosshead-type and coupled directly to the propeller. A four-stroke needs two revolutions per cycle and runs faster. On merchant ships, four-strokes are usually used as generators, or for propulsion through a gearbox on smaller vessels.

What causes a scavenge fire?

Combustible oil and carbon build up in the scavenge space, mainly from excess cylinder oil, stuffing box leakage and ring blow-by. Hot gas blowing past the rings, or poor combustion, then ignites it. Regular drain checks, port inspections, correct cylinder oil feed rate and clean scavenge spaces prevent most fires.

Why is the cylinder oil BN important?

Sulphur in the fuel forms acids that attack the liner, which is known as cold corrosion. The alkaline reserve (BN) of the cylinder oil neutralises them. The BN and feed rate must suit the fuel actually being burned; too much or too little can both cause problems. Follow the engine maker's and oil supplier's guidance and use drain oil analysis.

What is the difference between a camshaft engine and an electronically controlled engine?

On camshaft engines, mechanical cams time the fuel pumps and exhaust valves. Electronically controlled engines replace the camshaft with hydraulic or common-rail systems timed by the control system. This allows injection and valve timing to be adjusted across the load range. Watchkeeping principles are similar, but troubleshooting relies more on the control system.

Do I need a special certificate to operate the main engine?

Operating and maintaining the main propulsion plant falls within the engineering CoC competences under STCW, Chapter III. Training on specific engine types is set by the company and sometimes by the flag administration. Check your company's familiarisation requirements when joining a new engine type.

Test yourself

What is the main function of the piston rod stuffing box on a crosshead engine?

The stuffing box seals the piston rod where it passes from the scavenge space into the crankcase, keeping combustion residue out of the system oil. Ref: engine maker's manual; general marine engineering knowledge.

During which part of the cycle does the starting air valve admit air to a cylinder?

Air must push the piston down after TDC; admission before TDC would oppose rotation. Ref: starting arrangements under SOLAS Chapter II-1, Part C; engine maker's manual.

After a crankcase oil mist detector alarm, what is the correct immediate action?

Slowing down reduces heat input, and the relief doors are where an explosion vents. The crankcase must not be opened until it has cooled. Ref: SOLAS Chapter II-1, Parts C and E; maker's emergency procedures.

Under MARPOL Annex VI, which document must stay consistent with the engine's NOx-critical components and settings?

NOx compliance is certified through the EIAPP certificate and verified against the Technical File. Ref: MARPOL Annex VI, Regulation 13; NOx Technical Code 2008.

What is the first immediate action for a minor scavenge fire at one unit?

Most small fires burn out once the fuel is cut and extra oil protects the liner. Opening the doors or drains admits air, and the fixed system is used if the fire persists. Ref: engine maker's scavenge fire procedure; company SMS.
