Accommodation ladder: components, operation, maintenance and safety

Quick answer

The accommodation ladder is the ship's permanently fitted means of access down the ship's side, hinged at an upper platform and hung from a winch wire at its lower end. SOLAS regulation II-1/3-9 requires it to be inspected and maintained to IMO guidelines (MSC.1/Circ.1331), and on ships built since 2010 also constructed and installed to them. It must be tended throughout the port stay, because a poorly rigged or neglected ladder is a classic cause of falls and fatal accidents.

What the accommodation ladder is and why it matters

The accommodation ladder is an inclined stairway mounted along the ship's side. It is used to board and leave the ship alongside a quay, at anchor from a launch, or in combination with the pilot ladder. It is different from a gangway, which is a portable walkway landed more or less at right angles between ship and shore.

Everyone uses it: crew going ashore, surveyors, agents, pilots, stevedores, port state control inspectors. Any defect therefore puts people at risk who are not part of the crew.

Falls from accommodation ladders, and ladders collapsing during hoisting or testing, have killed seafarers and visitors. The usual causes are a parted hoisting wire, a failed sheave or pin, or a ladder left untended as the tide or draught changed. Treat it as lifting equipment with people on it, not as a fixture.

Main components

Designs vary by maker. Most ladders are steel or aluminium and share these parts:

Component Function
Upper (turntable) platform Pivot point at deck level. It swings the ladder out from its stowed position and lets it hinge up and down.
Ladder flight(s) Stringers with steps, usually self-levelling or curved treads, so the footing stays usable over the working angle. Some ladders have two flights.
Lower platform Landing at the bottom. It usually has a wheel or roller so it can rest on and travel along the quay.
Handrails and stanchions Rigid rails or stanchions with taut handropes, on both sides.
Davit or suspension arm Carries the hoisting wire over the ladder.
Hoisting winch Electric, hydraulic or pneumatic. It has a brake and normally limit switches.
Hoisting wire, sheaves and blocks Hold the ladder's weight at the lower end.
Securing devices Lashings, pins or hooks that hold the ladder in its stowed and rigged positions.
Safety net Rigged under the ladder where there is a risk of falling between ship and quay.
Lighting and lifebuoy Illumination at night, and a lifebuoy with self-activating light and buoyant line ready at the head of the ladder.

Under the IMO guidelines each ladder is marked with a plate. The plate shows the maker, model and year of manufacture, the maximum permitted design angle(s) of inclination, and the maximum safe loading (number of persons and/or load). Know where the marking is on your ship. An inspector will ask.

Rigging and lowering: step by step

Follow the maker's manual and your company SMS. A typical sequence is:

  1. Brief and prepare. Assign a competent person to operate the winch and a second person on deck to watch the ladder. Wear PPE. Anyone leaning outboard or working overside wears a harness and follows the working-overside procedure.
  2. Check before use. Look over the wire, sheaves, winch brake, securing pins and lashings. Look for anything loose on the ladder.
  3. Release the stowage securing. Swing the ladder out on the turntable. Make sure nobody is between the ladder and fixed structure.
  4. Lower slowly and under control. Use the method in the maker's manual, and never let the ladder run free. No one should be on the ladder while it is hoisted or lowered unless the maker and the SMS specifically allow it.
  5. Set the angle. Keep within the marked maximum. Alongside, land the lower platform roller clear of bollards, rail tracks, cargo gear and quay edges where it could jam.
  6. Fit stanchions and handropes. Tension the handropes so they cannot be pulled outboard.
  7. Rig the safety net, lighting and lifebuoy. Check the top and bottom landings are clear, non-slip and well lit.
  8. Inform the gangway or security watch. Note the time rigged where the ship records it.

At anchor the ladder is lowered to a height suitable for boat transfer. The lower platform should be horizontal. In a swell, raise it so it cannot be struck by the launch or dipped into the sea.

Tending the ladder alongside

A rigged ladder is never "set and forget". The ship moves relative to the quay through:

  • tidal range
  • cargo and ballast operations changing the draught
  • ranging along the berth
  • passing traffic and swell

Worked scenario. A bulk carrier is loading on a falling tide. The ship sinks with the cargo and drops with the tide, so the ladder's lower end is pushed upward against the quay.

  • If the wire and lower platform are not adjusted, the ladder flattens. It may even go below horizontal, with the lower end forced upward against the hinge and wire.
  • The roller can be forced into a quay fitting.
  • At worst the ladder is overloaded, twisted or lifted off its pivot.

The opposite happens when discharging on a rising tide. The ship rises and the ladder steepens beyond its marked angle. It can also lift the lower platform clear of the quay, leaving the ladder hanging at an angle nobody can safely use.

The watch must check the ladder at regular intervals and adjust it with the winch. If it cannot be kept within safe limits, stop access and tell the duty officer.

Good gangway-watch habits:

  • Check the angle, the roller position and the handrope tension.
  • Check the net position at every round.
  • Clear oil, ice, grain dust or cargo residue from treads.
  • Control access as the ship security plan requires.
  • Keep the ladder clear while cranes, grabs or slings are working overhead.

Inspection, maintenance and testing

SOLAS II-1/3-9 requires means of embarkation and disembarkation to be inspected and maintained to the IMO guidelines (MSC.1/Circ.1331). Records must be kept on board. In outline:

Routine checks by ship's staff (frequency set by the SMS and the maker):

  • Wire: check for broken wires, corrosion, kinks, bird-caging, flattening and loss of lubrication. Pay special attention where it passes over sheaves and at the end fittings.
  • Sheaves: they must turn freely, with no grooves worn to the wire's shape and pins greased.
  • Winch: check the brake holding, limit switches stopping the ladder before over-hoisting, controls returning to neutral, and hydraulic or air leaks.
  • Structure: look for cracked welds, corroded stringers and steps, and loose or bent treads.
  • Fittings: check for worn hinge pins and bushes, a seized roller, and damaged stanchions.
  • Securing gear: stowage lashings and pins must be in good order. A ladder breaking loose at sea is a serious hazard.
  • Safety items: the safety net and lifebuoy must be present and in good condition.

Hoisting wires. These are to be maintained as for lifeboat falls under SOLAS III/20.4. That means periodic inspection and renewal when deteriorated, or at intervals of not more than five years, whichever is earlier.

Thorough examination and load testing. The guidelines provide for:

  • a thorough examination at annual surveys
  • an operational test with the specified maximum operational load at intervals not exceeding five years

Who carries these out and how they are documented depends on the flag administration, the classification society and the SMS. Check your ship's survey records.

Aluminium ladders need watching for galvanic corrosion where they are in contact with steel fittings, and for fatigue cracking at welds.

Common faults and their symptoms

Fault What you notice
Hoisting wire deteriorating Broken wires, rust, dry wire, kinks or flattened sections near sheaves
Seized sheave Wire scrubbing, a flat spot on the sheave, squealing, uneven wire wear
Winch brake slipping Ladder creeps down when stopped; a burnt smell after use
Limit switch failed Ladder over-hoists into the davit, risking wire overload
Hinge pin or bush worn Ladder rattles, sags to one side, or moves unevenly on the turntable
Roller seized Lower platform drags or jumps along the quay instead of rolling
Corroded or cracked structure Flaking paint at weld toes, rust streaks, flexing steps
Slack handropes Ropes can be pushed outboard, giving no real support
Hydraulic leaks Oil on deck, slow or jerky movement

Any defect affecting the wire, the brake, a pin or the structure takes the ladder out of service until it is repaired. Provide alternative safe access and report it through the SMS. Do not "just use it carefully".

Regulatory framework

  • SOLAS Chapter II-1, regulation 3-9 covers means of embarkation on and disembarkation from ships. It requires:
    • construction and installation based on IMO guidelines
    • inspection and maintenance to those guidelines
    • wire maintenance by reference to SOLAS III/20.4

Which construction requirements apply depends on the ship's build date and when the equipment was installed. Inspection and maintenance apply in service. - MSC.1/Circ.1331 contains the IMO guidelines for construction, installation, maintenance and inspection/survey of accommodation ladders and gangways. - SOLAS Chapter V, regulation 23 covers the pilot transfer arrangements. It requires an accommodation ladder combined with the pilot ladder, or equivalent safe means, where the distance from sea level to the point of access is more than 9 m. Resolution A.1045(27) gives the detail. - Amendments to SOLAS V/23 and new performance standards for pilot transfer arrangements (MSC.548(107)) apply from 1 January 2028. - ISPS Code governs access control at the ladder head as set out in the ship security plan.

Flag states, port authorities and terminals may add their own requirements, for example on safety nets or shore-side gangways. Always check local rules and your SMS.

Oral exam: questions examiners ask

Q: Which regulation governs the accommodation ladder? SOLAS II-1/3-9, with the guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1331. Add SOLAS V/23 if it is used in a pilot combination arrangement.

Q: How often is the hoisting wire renewed? When deteriorated, or at intervals of not more than five years, whichever is earlier. This is by reference to SOLAS III/20.4.

Q: What markings are on the ladder? A plate with:

  • the maker, model and year of manufacture
  • the maximum permitted design angle(s) of inclination
  • the maximum safe loading (number of persons and/or load)

Q: What do you do as OOW if the ladder angle exceeds its marked limit during cargo work? 1. Stop access. 2. Adjust with the winch, or re-position the ladder. 3. Provide alternative safe access if it cannot be corrected. 4. Inform the chief officer and record it.

Q: What safety equipment goes with a rigged ladder? A safety net where needed, adequate lighting, and a lifebuoy with self-activating light and buoyant line at the head of the ladder.

Q: Can a person stand on the ladder while it is hoisted? Not unless the maker and the SMS specifically permit it. The wire and winch are the single point of failure.

Q: How do you rig the accommodation ladder for a pilot combination? Rig it as required by SOLAS V/23 and A.1045(27), noting the amended requirements that apply from 1 January 2028:

  • leading aft and resting firmly against the ship's side
  • lower platform horizontal
  • pilot ladder secured as specified
  • the whole arrangement checked by a responsible officer
Key points
  • The accommodation ladder is regulated by SOLAS II-1/3-9 and the IMO guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1331.
  • Hoisting wires are renewed when deteriorated or at intervals of not more than five years, whichever is earlier (via SOLAS III/20.4).
  • Each ladder is marked with its maximum permitted design angle(s) and maximum safe loading (number of persons and/or load); never exceed either.
  • A rigged ladder must be tended continuously for tide, draught and ranging changes.
  • Safety net where needed, good lighting and a ready lifebuoy are part of a properly rigged ladder.
  • Defects in wire, brake, pins or structure take the ladder out of service until repaired.
  • Above 9 m from sea level to access point, pilot transfer needs a combination arrangement under SOLAS V/23.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an accommodation ladder and a gangway?

An accommodation ladder is fitted to the ship's side, hinged at an upper platform and runs along the hull, usually leading aft. A gangway is a portable walkway placed between ship and shore, generally at a shallower angle. Both are covered by SOLAS II-1/3-9 and MSC.1/Circ.1331.

How often must the accommodation ladder wire be changed?

SOLAS II-1/3-9 links the wire to the lifeboat falls requirement in SOLAS III/20.4. The wire is renewed when it has deteriorated or at intervals of not more than five years, whichever comes first. Your SMS may require more frequent renewal.

Is a safety net mandatory under the accommodation ladder?

The IMO guidelines call for a safety net where there is a risk of a person falling from the ladder. Many port and terminal rules, and most company SMS, require one whenever the ladder is rigged alongside. Check local regulations and your SMS.

Who is responsible for the accommodation ladder in port?

The chief officer normally has overall responsibility, and the officer of the watch ensures it is safely rigged and tended. The gangway watch carries out the routine checks and adjustments. Exact responsibilities are set out in your company SMS.

What does port state control check on the accommodation ladder?

Inspectors typically look at the overall condition, wire and fittings, the safety net, lighting and the lifebuoy at the ladder head. They also check the angle and markings and the inspection and maintenance records required by SOLAS II-1/3-9. A visibly unsafe ladder can lead to a deficiency being raised.

Test yourself

Which SOLAS regulation deals with means of embarkation on and disembarkation from ships, including accommodation ladders?

SOLAS II-1/3-9 covers means of embarkation and disembarkation. V/23 covers pilot transfer and III/20 covers LSA maintenance. Ref: SOLAS II-1/3-9.

Under SOLAS V/23, above what distance from sea level to the point of access is a combination arrangement (or equivalent) required for pilot transfer?

Where the distance from sea level to the point of access exceeds 9 m, a pilot ladder alone is not sufficient. Ref: SOLAS V/23.

What is the maximum interval for renewing the accommodation ladder hoisting wire?

SOLAS II-1/3-9 applies the falls maintenance rule of III/20.4: renew when deteriorated or within five years. Ref: SOLAS II-1/3-9 and III/20.4.

What information must be marked on the accommodation ladder under the IMO guidelines?

The guidelines require a plate showing the maker, model and year, the maximum permitted design angle(s) of inclination and the maximum safe loading (number of persons and/or load). Ref: MSC.1/Circ.1331.

During cargo operations the ladder angle exceeds its marked maximum. What should the OOW do?

The ladder must not be used beyond its marked limits and must be kept safe throughout the port stay. Ref: SOLAS II-1/3-9 and MSC.1/Circ.1331.
