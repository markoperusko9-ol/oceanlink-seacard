Immersion suits and lifejackets: a practical guide for crew and cadets

Quick answer

Lifejackets keep you afloat and turn you face up. Immersion suits protect you from cold shock and hypothermia long enough to be rescued. SOLAS Chapter III sets which ships must carry them and how they are maintained, and the LSA Code Chapter II sets their performance standards. On most ocean-going cargo ships you will have both in your cabin, and you are expected to inspect, don and use them correctly without help.

Why both exist: drowning and cold water

A person in the water faces two separate threats, and each appliance deals mainly with one of them.

  • Lifejacket: keeps your head clear of the water. Its job is to turn an exhausted or unconscious person onto their back with the mouth clear, so you do not drown before rescue arrives.
  • Immersion suit: reduces heat loss. In cold water, cold shock can kill in the first minutes. Swim failure and hypothermia follow. A suit greatly slows all three.

SOLAS Chapter III requires a lifejacket for every person on board, plus extra lifejackets for persons on watch and for remote survival craft stations. On cargo ships, an immersion suit of appropriate size is required for every person on board. The Administration may exempt certain ships constantly engaged on warm-climate voyages. Persons assigned to crew the rescue boat or a marine evacuation system party must be provided with an immersion suit or anti-exposure suit of appropriate size on all ship types. This does not apply if the ship is constantly engaged in warm climates where the Administration considers thermal protection unnecessary. Passenger ships have their own rules, including child and infant lifejackets, and those details depend on the voyage and the flag. Always check the ship's safety equipment record and your muster list for what applies on board.

Main components

Lifejacket (solid foam or inflatable)

  • Buoyancy element: closed-cell foam, or on inflatables at least two separate inflation chambers.
  • Body straps and fastenings, plus a crotch strap on many designs.
  • Lifejacket light: either water-activated or manually switched.
  • Whistle, firmly attached by a cord.
  • Retro-reflective tape.
  • Lifting loop or becket so rescuers can pull you out of the water.
  • Inflatables also have:
    • a CO2 cylinder
    • an automatic firing head (water-sensitive element)
    • a manual pull toggle
    • an oral inflation tube
    • a service-status indicator

Immersion suit

  • One-piece suit covering the whole body except the face. Hands are covered by gloves permanently attached to the suit.
  • Waterproof main zip, usually diagonal or vertical, with a flap.
  • Face seal or hood with an adjustable closure.
  • Feet with integral boots or soles.
  • Some suits have inherent buoyancy and their own light and whistle. Others are designed to be worn with a lifejacket over them. The maker's instructions and markings tell you which type you have.
  • Buddy line on many models, and a lifting loop.
  • Stowage bag marked with the size range.

Thermal protective aid (TPA)

This is a waterproof bag-type garment carried in survival craft and rescue boats. It is for people already out of the water who are not wearing an immersion suit. It is not a substitute for a suit in the water.

How they perform: the LSA Code standards in plain terms

The LSA Code Chapter II sets the standards. The main points, paraphrased:

Item Lifejacket Immersion suit
Donning At least 75% of people unfamiliar with it can don it correctly within 1 minute without help or demonstration. After a demonstration, everyone can do so within 1 minute. Unpacked and donned without help within 2 minutes, together with any lifejacket required.
Jump Wearer can jump from at least 4.5 m without injury or dislodging the jacket. Same 4.5 m jump requirement.
Flotation Turns an unconscious person face up with the mouth clear, to a standard set against a reference test device. Allows the wearer to turn face up and to swim a short distance and board survival craft.
Thermal Not applicable. Insulated suits must limit the fall in body core temperature to 2°C over 6 hours in calm water at 0–2°C.
Fire Withstands brief exposure to fire without burning or continuing to melt. Withstands brief exposure to fire without burning or continuing to melt.

Lifejacket lights must give a luminous intensity of at least 0.75 cd for at least 8 hours. If flashing, the rate is set by the Code.

Two practical points follow from these standards:

  • Size matters. An oversized suit floods, traps air in the legs and can turn you feet-up. Wear your correct size.
  • Suits are approved together with a specific lifejacket or none. Mixing types can defeat the self-righting function.

Step by step: donning and entering the water

Immersion suit (typical sequence; follow the maker's instructions on the bag)

  1. Take off shoes with sharp edges, and anything that could tear the suit. Keep warm clothing on, including a hat if possible.
  2. Lay the suit out and open the zip fully.
  3. Sit down and put both legs in first.
  4. Put in your weaker arm, then pull the hood over your head.
  5. Put in your stronger arm.
  6. Close the zip completely, pulling it to the top in one firm movement. Secure the flap.
  7. Adjust the face seal or hood.
  8. If the suit requires a lifejacket, don it over the suit and fasten all straps.
  9. Check the light, whistle and buddy line.
  10. Check your partner's suit, and have them check yours.

Entering the water (only if you cannot board survival craft dry)

  1. Choose the lowest point you can find, and check the water below is clear of debris and craft.
  2. Hold the lifejacket down firmly with your arms crossed over your chest.
  3. Block your nose and mouth with one hand.
  4. Keep your feet together, look straight ahead, and step off feet first.

With an inflatable lifejacket, do not inflate it before a high jump. Inflate it once in the water, or let the automatic head fire.

Worked scenario. A seaman jumps into 4°C water wearing only an inflatable lifejacket over a boiler suit. The jacket inflates, so he will not drown immediately. However, cold shock and rapid cooling put him at serious risk well before a rescue boat arrives. The same seaman in a correctly sized insulated suit with its lifejacket has hours of protection. This is why every person on a cargo ship must have a suit unless the Administration has granted a warm-climate exemption.

Routine checks and maintenance

SOLAS Chapter III requires a monthly inspection of life-saving appliances against a checklist. IMO guidance (MSC/Circ.1047) gives a checklist for immersion suits and anti-exposure suits. Your company SMS will have its own form.

Monthly / routine checks

  • Zip. Runs freely along its full length. Lubricate with the lubricant specified by the manufacturer, not with whatever grease is to hand.
  • Suit fabric, seams, face seal and gloves. No tears, cracking, delamination or mould.
  • Light. Present and working, and the battery expiry date is valid.
  • Whistle. Present and attached.
  • Reflective tape. Intact.
  • Bag. Correct size marked, and the suit is folded and stowed as the maker specifies.
  • Lifejacket straps and buckles. Undamaged. Foam is not crushed or cracked.
  • Inflatable lifejackets:
    • CO2 cylinder screwed in hand-tight and not pierced
    • firing head within its expiry date
    • status indicator showing serviceable
    • bladder not chafed

Periodic servicing and testing

  • Inflatable lifejackets. Must be serviced at an approved service station at intervals not exceeding 12 months. The Administration may allow a limited extension in specific cases.
  • Immersion suit seams and closures. MSC/Circ.1114 recommends an air-pressure test of each suit at intervals not exceeding 3 years, and more frequently for suits over 10 years old. Confirm what your flag and company apply.

Record every check. A port State control officer will often pick a random cabin suit and lifejacket, and an unlit light or a seized zip is an easy deficiency.

Common faults and what they look like

  • Seized or stiff zip. Caused by salt and corrosion. It shows during drills when crew cannot close the suit. Clean it and lubricate as the maker specifies. If it still binds, land the suit for repair or replacement.
  • Perished face seal or glove joints. Cracks appear when the suit is flexed, often on suits stored in hot cabins or sunlight. The suit will flood in the water, so treat it as unserviceable.
  • Wrong size in the cabin. Common after crew changes. The fix is a muster-list review on joining, matching suit size to each person.
  • Expired or dead lights. On water-activated lights, test with the test button if fitted. Never test by dunking unless the maker allows it.
  • Inflatable fired or tampered with. Signs are a red or missing indicator, a loose or empty cylinder, or an expired auto head. Take it out of service and replace it from spares.
  • Hidden damage from improper stowage. Lifejackets used as cushions, or suits crushed under gear. This can mean compressed foam and chafed bladders.
  • Missing items. Whistles and buddy lines go missing, often "borrowed". Replace them before the next drill.

Safety precautions

  • Know where your suit and lifejacket are, where the spare lifejackets at your watch station are, and how to get to them in darkness.
  • Never use a lifejacket as a pillow or cushion, or a suit bag as a seat.
  • Do not wear an immersion suit for long periods in a hot engine room or on deck. Heat stress is a real risk during long abandon ship preparations in tropical conditions.
  • Do not mix suits and lifejackets between designs unless the maker states they are compatible.
  • Rescue boat crew should don suits before launching, not after the boat is in the water.
  • In the water, use the HELP position (knees up, arms crossed) if alone, or huddle with others. Stay with the group, and use the whistle and light to attract attention.
  • Abandon ship drills and on-board training under SOLAS Chapter III include checking that lifejackets are correctly donned and instruction in the use of immersion suits. Treat these drills as real practice.

Oral exam: questions examiners actually ask

  • "Who on your last ship had to have an immersion suit, and why?" Explain the cargo-ship requirement for everyone on board, any warm-climate exemption, and the rescue boat crew requirement.
  • "Talk me through donning a suit." Give the sequence, the 2-minute standard, and the point about checking a buddy.
  • "What do you check monthly on an inflatable lifejacket?" Cover the cylinder, auto head expiry, indicator, bladder, light and whistle. Add the annual shore servicing.
  • "How do you jump into the water?" Lowest point, clear below, hold the jacket down, cover nose and mouth, feet first, look ahead.
  • "What does the light have to do?" At least 0.75 cd for at least 8 hours.
  • "A crew member has a suit two sizes too big. So what?" Explain the flooding risk, the feet-up flotation risk and the loss of thermal protection. Then say what you would do about it.

Answer with the purpose first, then the standard, then what you personally check. Examiners want to hear that you have actually opened the bag and looked.

Key points
  • Lifejackets prevent drowning; immersion suits prevent cold shock and hypothermia. You need both in cold water.
  • Cargo ships carry an appropriately sized immersion suit for every person on board, subject to any warm-climate exemption granted by the Administration.
  • Immersion suits must be donned unaided within 2 minutes, and you must be able to jump from 4.5 m wearing one.
  • Lifejacket lights must give at least 0.75 cd for at least 8 hours.
  • Inflatable lifejackets are shore-serviced at intervals not exceeding 12 months.
  • Monthly LSA inspections cover zips, seals, lights, whistles and inflatable components, and must be recorded.
  • Wrong size, a seized zip or a perished seal makes an immersion suit unserviceable.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to wear a lifejacket over my immersion suit?

It depends on the suit. Some suits have built-in buoyancy and their own light and whistle, so they are approved for use without a lifejacket. Others are approved only together with a specified lifejacket. Check the markings and the maker's instructions on board.

How often are immersion suits pressure tested?

MSC/Circ.1114 recommends an air-pressure test of each suit at intervals not exceeding 3 years, and more frequently for suits over 10 years old. Confirm what your flag and company apply by checking the ship's maintenance plan.

Can an inflatable lifejacket be serviced on board?

Monthly checks are done on board. The required periodic servicing, at intervals not exceeding 12 months, must be done at an approved service station. Crew should not dismantle or rearm the automatic firing heads except as the maker and company procedures permit.

Is an immersion suit required on ships trading only in the tropics?

SOLAS allows the Administration to exempt cargo ships constantly engaged on voyages in warm climates from the all-persons immersion suit requirement. The separate requirement for rescue boat crew and MES parties (immersion suit or anti-exposure suit) can also be waived if the Administration considers thermal protection unnecessary in that trade. Whether any exemption applies is shown in the ship's certificates and records.

What should I do if my suit is the wrong size?

Report it to the safety officer or the officer responsible for LSA straight away. An oversized suit can flood and float you feet-up, and an undersized one may tear or be impossible to close. A correctly sized replacement should be issued from spares or ordered.

Test yourself

What is the maximum normal interval for shore servicing of inflatable lifejackets under SOLAS?

Inflatable lifejackets must be serviced at intervals not exceeding 12 months, with limited extension possible at the Administration's discretion. SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20.

On a cargo ship with no warm-climate exemption, who must be provided with an immersion suit?

Cargo ships carry an immersion suit of appropriate size for every person on board, subject to any warm-climate exemption. SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 32.

What is the minimum performance of a lifejacket light?

The LSA Code requires lifejacket lights to give at least 0.75 cd for a period of at least 8 hours. LSA Code, Chapter II.

Which is the correct technique when you must jump into the sea wearing a lifejacket?

Holding the jacket down and entering feet first from the lowest practicable height prevents neck injury and keeps the jacket in place. LSA Code, Chapter II (4.5 m jump standard); PST training.

An immersion suit zip is stiff during the monthly inspection. What is the correct action?

Zips must be maintained as the manufacturer specifies, and an unserviceable suit must be replaced. MSC/Circ.1047; SOLAS Chapter III, Regulation 20.
