Autopilot on Merchant Ships: How It Works, Checks, Faults and Exam Questions

Quick answer

A ship's autopilot, formally a heading or track control system, keeps the ship on a set heading or track by sending rudder orders to the steering gear based on gyro heading and rudder feedback. SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 24 sets the rules for its use: manual steering must be available immediately in hazardous situations, a qualified helmsperson must be available without delay, changeover must be done by or under the supervision of the responsible officer, and manual steering must be tested after prolonged autopilot use and before areas needing special caution.

What the autopilot does and why it matters

The autopilot does the helmsman's job on a steady course. It compares the heading you want with the heading the ship actually has, then orders rudder to close the gap. It does this continuously, all the way from open-sea passages to long coastal legs.

IMO distinguishes two types:

  • Heading control system (HCS): holds a set heading. It does not know about set and drift. If the current pushes you sideways, you stay on heading but move off track.
  • Track control system (TCS): usually integrated with ECDIS. It steers to hold a planned track and can make pre-planned course alterations at waypoints. It corrects for cross-track error, not just heading.

Under SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19, ships of 10,000 gross tonnage and upwards must carry a heading or track control system (V/19.2.8.1). Your flag administration's survey checks it. The practical point for the watch officer is this. The autopilot reduces fatigue and saves fuel by steering tighter than most helmsmen. It does not keep a look-out, and it does not take collision avoidance decisions.

Main components

Makers' designs differ, so learn your own ship's manual. The building blocks are broadly the same everywhere.

Component What it does
Heading sensor Usually the gyrocompass. Some systems can select a transmitting magnetic compass or a GNSS compass as an alternative source.
Control unit (bridge console) Where you set the heading, select the mode and adjust parameters. Alarms are shown here.
Steering mode selector Switches between hand (follow-up), non-follow-up (NFU), auto and, where fitted, track mode.
Controller / processor Calculates the rudder order from heading error, rate of turn and settings. Modern units are often adaptive.
Rudder angle feedback unit Mounted at the rudder stock. Tells the controller what the rudder is actually doing.
Interface to steering gear Sends the rudder order to the steering gear control system, for example by energising solenoid valves or controlling the pump.
Speed input From the log or GNSS. Adaptive systems use it to adjust rudder response with speed.
Position and route input In track mode, from ECDIS and the position sensors.
Alarms Off-heading, sensor failure, power failure and, depending on the system, others such as heading monitor discrepancy.

How it works, step by step

Take a typical heading-mode cycle:

  1. Set heading. The OOW sets the heading on the control unit, say 075°.
  2. Measure. The gyro reports the actual heading, say 072°. The heading error is 3° to port of the set course.
  3. Calculate. The controller works out a rudder order. Most systems combine three actions:
    • Rudder (proportional): rudder in proportion to the heading error. Bigger error, more rudder.
    • Counter rudder (derivative): reacts to the rate of turn. It eases or reverses rudder as the ship swings back, so she does not overshoot.
    • Permanent helm (integral): builds up a steady rudder offset to cancel constant forces such as beam wind or propeller effect.
  4. Filter. The weather (yaw/dead band) setting tells the controller to ignore small, rapid yaws caused by sea. Without it, the rudder chases every wave, wearing out the steering gear and adding drag.
  5. Order rudder. The order goes to the steering gear. The rudder limit caps how much rudder the autopilot may use.
  6. Feedback. The rudder angle feedback unit confirms the rudder reached the ordered angle. The controller then repeats the cycle continuously.

For course alterations, most units let you choose a set rate of turn or radius. In track mode, the ECDIS supplies the track and the waypoints. The autopilot then steers to keep cross-track error inside limits and alerts you before each wheel-over.

Adaptive autopilots tune rudder and counter rudder themselves from speed, loading and observed response. You often only choose an operating mode such as economy or precise. They still need correct inputs. A wrong speed input or a failed sensor will degrade their steering just like a manual unit.

Routine checks and good practice on watch

Exact intervals come from your company SMS and the bridge procedures. Typical good practice:

Taking over the watch - Confirm the steering mode, set heading, heading source and which steering pumps are running. - Compare the gyro with the magnetic compass and the repeaters, and note the gyro error. - Check the off-heading alarm limit is set and the alarm is not silenced or disabled. - Look at the rudder angle indicator. Constant large rudder or rapid hunting is a sign something needs attention.

During the watch - Record compass comparisons as your SMS requires. - Adjust weather and rudder settings when conditions or loading change, on non-adaptive units. - In track mode, watch cross-track error and confirm the route in ECDIS is the checked, approved one.

Manual steering tests (SOLAS V/24) - Test manual steering after prolonged use of the autopilot. - Test it before entering areas where navigation demands special caution. - In practice: change over to hand steering, apply rudder both ways and confirm the rudder follows. Then return to the required mode and log it.

Engineering side - Maintenance of feedback units, steering gear solenoids and wiring is done to the maker's instructions and the planned maintenance system. - The pre-departure steering gear checks and the emergency steering drills required under SOLAS V/26 are separate from the autopilot. They are often done together, though, and the mode selector and changeover are exercised as part of them.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely causes First actions
Ship swings away steadily, rudder applied the wrong way or hard over Gyro failure or wrong heading source, rudder feedback fault Hand or NFU steering immediately, check gyro against magnetic
Ship oscillates (hunts) either side of course Rudder gain too high, counter rudder too low, weather setting too tight Reduce rudder, increase counter rudder, widen weather setting
Sluggish, wanders a long way off course Rudder gain too low, rudder limit too small, weather setting too wide Increase rudder or rudder limit, check loading and speed setting
Constant heading offset, slow to settle Permanent helm not built up, strong steady wind or current Allow time, check permanent helm setting
Rudder moves constantly in a seaway Weather setting too low Increase weather setting
Rudder does not move at all Power or fuse failure, steering pump stopped, solenoid fault, mode switch not in auto Change to alternative steering mode or pump, call master and engine room
Sudden heading jump on repeaters and autopilot Gyro fault or power interruption Hand steering by magnetic compass, investigate gyro
Alarm on heading monitor or sensor Disagreement between heading sources Identify the faulty source before trusting either

The key point: an autopilot fault is usually a heading sensor fault, a feedback fault or a settings problem. The response is always the same. Take manual control first, then diagnose.

Safety precautions and SOLAS rules on use

SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 24 sets the core rules. In paraphrase:

  • In high traffic density, restricted visibility and other hazardous navigational situations, you must be able to establish manual steering immediately.
  • In those same situations, the officer in charge of the navigational watch must have the services of a qualified helmsperson available without delay, ready at all times to take over steering (V/24.2).
  • Changeover between automatic and manual must be made by, or under the supervision of, the responsible officer.
  • Manual steering must be tested after prolonged autopilot use and before entering areas where navigation demands special caution.

Regulation 25 adds a related point. In areas where navigation demands special caution, more than one steering gear power unit must be running if the units can operate simultaneously.

Practical precautions:

  • Helmsman availability. SOLAS V/24 requires a qualified helmsperson to be available without delay in pilotage waters, traffic or poor visibility. Many masters go further and require hand steering in pilotage waters.
  • Know the changeover. Every OOW and helmsman should be able to switch from auto to hand to NFU without looking for the switch. Know whether your system has an override tiller that takes control instantly.
  • Collision avoidance. Altering by autopilot is acceptable when it gives a timely, substantial and readily apparent alteration, as the COLREG require. Check that the rudder limit or rate-of-turn setting does not make the turn too slow.
  • Off-heading alarm. Never disable it to stop nuisance alarms. Fix the settings instead.
  • Track mode. Track mode is only as good as the route and the position input. Monitor the position independently.
  • Fatigue. The autopilot plus the BNWAS do not replace the look-out required by COLREG Rule 5.

Worked scenario: gyro failure in auto

Night, coastal passage, auto heading mode, traffic about. The heading display suddenly jumps, and the ship starts a steady swing to starboard while the rudder indicator shows starboard helm building, although the visual references show she was on course.

  1. Take control. Switch to hand (follow-up) steering, which does not depend on the gyro, or NFU if follow-up steering is also affected. Steer by the magnetic compass and visual references.
  2. Assess the traffic. Check the radar picture. Remember that radar and ARPA stabilised by the faulty gyro may now show wrong target vectors.
  3. Call for help. Call the helmsman to the wheel if he is not already there, and inform the master.
  4. Identify the fault. Check the gyro status and alarms. If a second gyro or other heading source is fitted, change over once it agrees with the magnetic compass.
  5. Return to auto only when safe. Resume auto only on a verified heading source, with the master's agreement where standing orders require it.
  6. Record and report. Log the times and actions. Raise a defect report for the ETO or engineers under the SMS.

The examiner wants to hear manual control first, then traffic, then diagnosis.

Oral exam style questions

  • Explain rudder, counter rudder and weather settings and how you would adjust them in heavy weather. Widen the weather setting so the rudder ignores wave-induced yaw, and adjust rudder and counter rudder for the loaded or ballast condition.
  • What does SOLAS V/24 require? Manual steering available immediately in hazardous situations, a qualified helmsperson available without delay in those situations, changeover by or under supervision of the responsible officer, and manual steering tested after prolonged autopilot use and before areas needing special caution.
  • Difference between a heading control system and a track control system? An HCS holds a heading and ignores drift. A TCS holds a ground track using position input and corrects cross-track error.
  • What is NFU steering and when do you use it? Non-follow-up steering moves the rudder only while the tiller or button is held. It needs no rudder feedback loop, so it is a fallback when follow-up steering fails.
  • The ship is hunting badly in calm water. What do you check? Check for rudder gain set too high or counter rudder set too low, and check the speed input on adaptive systems.
  • Which inputs does the autopilot depend on? Heading, rudder feedback, power, and speed and position where used. Loss of any of these is a reason to go to manual steering.
Key points
  • The autopilot is a heading or track control system that orders rudder to hold a set heading or planned track.
  • Rudder, counter rudder, permanent helm and weather settings correspond to proportional, derivative, integral action and yaw filtering.
  • SOLAS V/24: manual steering must be available immediately in hazardous situations, and changeover is by or under supervision of the responsible officer.
  • Test manual steering after prolonged autopilot use and before areas where navigation demands special caution.
  • Most autopilot faults trace to the heading sensor, rudder feedback or wrong settings.
  • On any steering abnormality, take manual control first, then deal with traffic, then diagnose.
  • The autopilot never replaces a proper look-out or the watch officer's judgement.

Frequently asked questions

Can a ship use autopilot in restricted visibility?

SOLAS does not ban it, but V/24 requires that manual steering can be established immediately in restricted visibility, heavy traffic and other hazardous situations. V/24 also requires that, in these situations, a qualified helmsperson is available without delay and ready at all times to take over steering. Many masters' standing orders go further and require hand steering. Follow your master's orders and company SMS.

How often should manual steering be tested when using the autopilot?

SOLAS V/24 requires a test after prolonged use of the autopilot and before entering areas where navigation demands special caution. It does not give a fixed number of hours. Many companies set a routine, such as once a watch, in their SMS, so check your own procedures.

What is the difference between follow-up and non-follow-up steering?

In follow-up steering, the wheel position is a rudder order and the system drives the rudder to that angle using feedback. In non-follow-up steering, the rudder moves only while you hold the tiller or button and stays put when you release it. NFU is the usual fallback if follow-up control or feedback fails.

Why does the ship keep oscillating around the set course on autopilot?

The usual causes are rudder gain set too high, counter rudder set too low, or a weather setting that is too tight for the sea state. On adaptive units, check the speed input and loading mode. If adjustments do not cure it, suspect the rudder feedback unit or the heading sensor and report it.

Who can switch the steering from auto to manual?

SOLAS V/24 requires changeover to be made by, or under the supervision of, the responsible officer. A helmsman may physically operate the selector, but only on the OOW's order and under his supervision. The changeover should be confirmed and, where the SMS requires, logged.

Test yourself

Under SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 24, when must manual steering be tested?

Regulation V/24 requires manual steering to be tested after prolonged autopilot use and before areas needing special caution. The 12-hour pre-departure checks and the three-monthly drills come under V/26 and are separate requirements. (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulations 24 and 26)

Which autopilot function builds up a steady rudder offset to counter a constant beam wind?

Permanent helm, the integral term, accumulates a steady offset against constant external forces. Counter rudder reacts to rate of turn, and the weather setting only filters out wave-induced yaw. (Maker's manual; heading control system principles)

In calm water the ship hunts badly from side to side with large overshoots on autopilot. What is the most likely settings cause?

Too much proportional rudder, or too little counter rudder to check the swing, causes overshoot and oscillation. A wide weather setting or a small rudder limit makes steering sluggish rather than oscillating. (Heading control system principles; maker's manual)

According to SOLAS V/24, who must carry out or supervise the changeover from automatic to manual steering?

Changeover between automatic and manual steering must be made by, or under the supervision of, the responsible officer. (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 24)

What is the purpose of the autopilot off-heading alarm?

The off-heading alarm compares actual and set heading and alerts the OOW when the difference exceeds the set limit. Sensor and pump failures have their own alarms. (IMO performance standards for heading control systems, MSC.64(67) Annex 3)
