Hatch Covers on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Crew and Cadets

Quick answer

Hatch covers close the cargo hatchways and keep the holds weathertight. They do this by pressing a rubber packing against a steel compression bar on the coaming, held down by cleats and wedges. Keeping them tight depends on looking after the packing, compression bars, cleats, drains and running gear, and testing them properly, preferably by ultrasonic testing. Poorly maintained covers lead to wet-damage claims, detentions and, in the worst cases, flooded forward holds.

What hatch covers do and the main types

A hatch cover has three jobs:

  • Keep water out of the cargo hold. Covers are weathertight, not watertight, so they resist water from weather and seas but are not designed for sustained head pressure.
  • Carry loads, such as green seas and, on many ships, deck cargo like containers or timber.
  • Keep the hull intact. On bulk carriers in particular, the forward hatch covers keep the holds weathertight, which preserves the ship's reserve buoyancy. If one fails in heavy weather, the hold can flood progressively.

The common types you will meet:

Type Typical ships How it moves
Folding (hydraulic or wire-pulled) General cargo, multipurpose, some bulkers Hinged panels fold up in pairs and stow at the hatch end
Side-rolling Bulk carriers Panels are jacked up and roll sideways on rails
End-rolling / roll-stowing Older general cargo, some bulkers Panels roll to the hatch end and tip up to stow
Lift-away / pontoon Container ships, multipurpose Lifted off by shore or ship's crane
Piggy-back / stacking Multipurpose, heavy-lift One panel is lifted and moved over another
Tween-deck covers General cargo, multipurpose Pontoons or folding panels inside the hold

Some older general cargo ships still use portable beams with tarpaulins and battening arrangements. The principle is the same, but tightness depends on the tarpaulins and wedges rather than on a gasket.

Main components

Know these by name. Surveyors and examiners will expect you to.

  • Hatch coaming. The raised steel frame around the hatchway. Its top carries the compression bar, rails and stoppers.
  • Compression bar. A flat steel bar on the coaming top that the rubber packing seals against. It must be straight, smooth and free of corrosion and damage.
  • Rubber packing (gasket) and packing channel. The rubber is held in a steel channel on the underside of the panel edges and cross-joints. The seal comes from compressing the rubber onto the compression bar.
  • Bearing pads. Steel-to-steel contact points between panel and coaming. They carry the load and limit how far the rubber is squeezed. Without them, the rubber would be crushed.
  • Cleats. Manual or quick-acting fasteners around the periphery that hold the panel down. They are fitted with rubber washers to keep some flexibility.
  • Cross-joint wedges and seals. Where two panels meet, these keep the joint tight.
  • Locators and stoppers. These stop panels shifting when the ship works in a seaway.
  • Wheels, rails and lifting jacks. Found on rolling covers. Panels are jacked up off their seating onto the wheels before moving.
  • Hydraulic cylinders, power pack, hoses and valves. Found on hydraulic types. Wire-pulled types use a winch, wires and sheaves instead.
  • Drain channels and non-return drain valves. Any water that gets past the outer seal is led to drains on the coaming. The non-return valves stop seawater coming back in.
  • Locking pins, hooks or safety chains. These hold panels mechanically in the open position.

Opening and closing: step by step

The exact sequence depends on the maker's manual and the company procedure. Always follow those. A typical hydraulic folding or side-rolling operation goes like this.

Before opening

  1. Get permission from the duty officer. Confirm the hold is ready and that nobody is on or around the covers.
  2. Check that nothing is stowed on the panels or rails, such as lashing gear, hoses or ropes.
  3. Check the ship is reasonably upright. A heel or trim beyond the maker's limit can make panels run away or jam.
  4. Start the hydraulic power pack and check the oil level and that no alarms are showing.

Opening

  1. Release all cleats and cross-joint wedges. Walk the full perimeter and check them. A single cleat left on can bend a panel or burst a hose.
  2. For rolling types, raise the panels on the lifting jacks.
  3. Move the panels steadily with a lookout posted, keeping clear of the hinge and fold areas.
  4. Fit the locking pins, hooks or chains in the open position before anyone works near the stowed panels.

Closing is the same in reverse, with extra checks:

  • Clear cargo residue off the coaming top, compression bar and drain channels first. Grain, coal or ore left under the packing will stop it sealing.
  • Lower the panels fully onto the bearing pads.
  • Secure all cleats and wedges evenly. Over-tightening crushes the rubber, and under-tightening leaks.
  • Check the drain valves are clear and free.
  • Make a log entry that the covers are closed and secured before sailing. Many companies use a pre-departure checklist for this.

Routine checks and maintenance

Hatch covers are normally included in the ship's planned maintenance system, which supports the maintenance requirements of ISM Code Section 10. Typical items include:

  • Rubber packing. Look for permanent set (a groove deeper than the maker allows), hardening, cracking, tears, paint on the rubber, and missing sections. Renew packing in lengths, not short patches, wherever possible. Never paint the rubber.
  • Compression bars. Check for wear, wastage, distortion and damage from cargo grabs. Repair to the maker's or class guidance.
  • Cleats. Check the adjustment, the condition of the rubber washers, missing or seized cleats and bent rods. Grease the threads.
  • Bearing pads and locators. Look for wear or missing pads. If the pads are worn, the rubber gets over-compressed.
  • Wheels, rails, jacks and hinges. Grease them, check for flat spots on wheels, and check rails for damage and debris.
  • Hydraulic system. Check for leaking hoses and cylinders, the condition and level of the oil, filters, and that hose dates are within the company renewal interval.
  • Wire-pulled systems. Check wires, sheaves, chains and winch brakes.
  • Drain valves. Check they are free and the non-return balls or flaps are working. Blocked drains are a common contributor to wet cargo.
  • Coamings and panel tops. Look for corrosion, cracks and buckling, especially on forward hatches.

Temporary sealing tape over cross-joints may be required by charterers for some cargoes. It is an extra measure only. It does not cure worn packing or badly adjusted cleats.

Testing weathertightness

There are three common methods, and they tell you different things.

  • Chalk test. Chalk is rubbed on the compression bar and the cover is closed and opened again. The chalk line on the rubber shows contact only. It does not prove the cover is weathertight, because it says nothing about how hard the rubber is compressed. It is useful for finding misalignment.
  • Hose test. Water is sprayed at the joints from outside while someone inside the hold looks for leaks. The class or company sets the nozzle and distance. It cannot be done with water-sensitive cargo in the hold, and the observer inside the hold is entering a space that may be an enclosed space.
  • Ultrasonic test. A transmitter is placed in the closed hold, and a receiver is moved along every joint outside. Readings are compared with the open-hatch value, and the acceptance criteria come from the equipment maker, class or charterer. It can be done with cargo loaded, it gives a recorded result, and it is the method most surveyors and charterers prefer. The operator should be competent and trained in the method.

Keep the test records. They are evidence in any wet-damage claim. That claim will often turn on whether the water was seawater ingress or condensation (sweat).

Common faults and symptoms

Symptom Likely cause
Water staining below a joint, wet cargo near the coaming Worn or set packing, damaged compression bar, blocked drain
Ultrasonic reading high along one side Misaligned panel, worn bearing pads, cleats slack
Packing badly cut or flattened Over-tightened cleats, missing bearing pads, cargo residue left on the coaming
Panel will not lift or travels unevenly Low hydraulic pressure, jack fault, wheel seized, a cleat left secured
Panels jerk or creep when stopped Air in the hydraulic system, internal leakage in a cylinder or valve
Oil on deck or panels Hose or cylinder seal failure (also a pollution risk)
Cracks at coaming corners or brackets Fatigue, heavy-weather loading, grab damage
Panels shifting at sea, noise in a seaway Worn locators or stoppers

Report defects, record them in the PMS, and inform the master before sailing if weathertightness is in doubt.

Safety precautions

Hatch covers move heavy steel panels near open drops. Most accidents involve crushing, falls or hydraulic failure.

  • Never stand on, under or between moving panels, near the fold or hinge, or in line with the rails.
  • Fit the mechanical locking devices before working near open panels. Do not rely on hydraulic pressure alone.
  • Use one operator and a clear signal system. The operator must be able to see the whole cover or have a lookout who can.
  • Fence or guard open hatchways. Use fall protection when working on coaming tops or inside the hold edge.
  • Treat cargo holds as possible enclosed spaces. Some cargoes deplete oxygen or give off toxic gas, including coal, timber, scrap and steel products, and holds may also have been fumigated. Follow the enclosed space entry and permit procedure before anyone goes in, including for a hose test.
  • Depressurise the hydraulics and isolate the power before maintenance on cylinders or hoses (lock-out tag-out).
  • In heavy weather, recheck cleats and lashings on deck cargo before conditions deteriorate, not after. Do not send crew onto the hatches in green seas.

Regulations and oral exam questions

Where the rules sit (paraphrased)

  • Load Line Convention 1966, Annex I, Chapter II (conditions of assignment) covers hatchway coamings and the closure of hatchways by covers. Hatch covers are examined at load line surveys, including the annual survey.
  • SOLAS Chapter XII contains additional safety measures for bulk carriers, several of which concern hold flooding.
  • Enhanced survey programme (ESP Code) requirements for bulk carriers, made mandatory under SOLAS Chapter XI-1, include examination of hatch covers and coamings.
  • Classification society rules set the strength, testing and repair standards. These vary by class.
  • Port State Control inspectors commonly check the condition of hatch covers, cleats and drains.

Typical oral questions

  1. How does a hatch cover achieve weathertightness? Rubber packing is compressed onto the compression bar. Bearing pads give steel-to-steel contact and limit the compression. Cleats hold the panel down, and drains with non-return valves carry away any water that gets past.
  2. Does a good chalk test prove the cover is tight? No. It only shows contact. Ultrasonic or hose testing is needed to show tightness.
  3. Why are bearing pads important? They carry the load and stop the rubber being crushed. If they wear, the packing takes a permanent set.
  4. What will you check before closing after discharge? That the coaming top, compression bar and drains are clean, the packing is in good order, the drain valves are free, and all cleats and wedges are secured evenly.
  5. Why are forward hatch covers critical on bulk carriers? They take the heaviest green-sea loads. If one fails, the hold can flood and the ship can be lost.
  6. What do you do if a cover will not close properly before sailing? Stop, inform the master, find the cause, and record it. Do not sail with a hatch you cannot make weathertight without the master's decision and, where needed, class involvement.
Key points
  • Hatch covers are weathertight closures: the rubber packing is compressed onto a steel compression bar.
  • Bearing pads limit how far the packing is squeezed. If they wear, the rubber gets crushed and leaks.
  • A chalk test shows contact only, while ultrasonic testing is the preferred way to prove tightness.
  • Clean coaming tops and keep drain non-return valves free before every closing.
  • Engage the mechanical locking devices on open panels and never rely on hydraulic pressure alone.
  • Treat cargo holds as possible enclosed spaces before entry, including entry for testing.
  • Hatch cover requirements sit mainly in the Load Line Convention and class rules, with extra measures for bulk carriers in SOLAS Chapter XII.

Frequently asked questions

Are hatch covers watertight or weathertight?

They are weathertight. That means they stop water getting in from weather and seas under normal conditions at sea, but they are not designed to hold out water under continuous pressure the way a watertight bulkhead does. That is why drains with non-return valves are fitted at the coaming.

What is the difference between the chalk test and the ultrasonic test?

The chalk test only shows whether the compression bar is touching the rubber packing along its length. The ultrasonic test checks for actual leak paths by placing a transmitter in the hold and scanning every joint from outside. The ultrasonic test can be done with cargo loaded, so surveyors and charterers usually prefer it.

How often should hatch cover rubber packing be renewed?

There is no single fixed interval. Renewal depends on condition, especially permanent set, hardening and damage, judged against the maker's limits. The company's planned maintenance system and class guidance decide when it is due. Renew in proper lengths rather than short patches, and never paint the rubber.

Why does cargo get wet even when the hatch covers are closed?

Common causes are worn or set packing, a damaged compression bar, slack or misadjusted cleats, worn bearing pads and blocked drain valves. Condensation (sweat) can also wet cargo with no leak at all. That is why test records and ventilation records matter when a claim is investigated.

Can sealing tape replace hatch cover maintenance?

No. Charterers may ask for tape on cross-joints for sensitive cargoes, but it is only an extra barrier. It does not make up for defective packing, compression bars or cleats, and surveyors will still expect the cover itself to be in good order.

Test yourself

What creates the weathertight seal on a modern steel hatch cover?

The seal comes from the rubber packing being compressed against the compression bar. Bearing pads give steel-to-steel contact only to carry the load and limit compression (Load Line Convention 1966, Annex I, Chapter II; class rules).

A chalk test shows a continuous chalk line on all the packing. What does this prove?

A chalk test indicates contact only. It does not prove the cover is tight, so ultrasonic or hose testing is needed for that (class and maker's guidance).

Before crew work near folding hatch panels in the open position, what must be done?

Hydraulic systems can lose pressure or leak, so open panels must be secured mechanically. This is set out in the maker's manual and the company SMS under the ISM Code.

What is the purpose of the non-return valves on hatch coaming drains?

The drains carry away water that gets past the outer seal, and the non-return valves stop backflow when seas break on deck (class rules and maker's manual).

Which instrument sets the conditions of assignment covering hatchway coamings and their closures?

Hatchways and their closing arrangements are among the conditions of assignment of freeboard in the Load Line Convention 1966, Annex I, Chapter II.
