Echo Sounder on Merchant Ships: A Practical Guide for Officers and Cadets

Quick answer

An echo sounder measures the depth of water by sending a sound pulse from a hull-mounted transducer and timing the echo from the seabed. Depth equals half the round-trip time multiplied by the speed of sound in water. SOLAS Chapter V requires one on ships of 300 GT and upwards and on all passenger ships. Its reading is only as good as its offset setting, sound speed value and transducer condition, so cross-check it against the chart.

What the echo sounder is for

The echo sounder is the only bridge instrument that measures the water actually under the ship. GNSS tells you where you are. ECDIS tells you what the chart says. The echo sounder tells you what is really there.

On a merchant ship it is used for:

  • Under-keel clearance (UKC) monitoring in pilotage waters, channels, port approaches and shallow passages.
  • Position checking by comparing soundings with charted depths, which is useful where contour lines are distinct.
  • Anchoring, to confirm depth at the planned anchor position before letting go.
  • Early warning of grounding through the shallow-depth alarm.
  • Evidence, because depth data is fed to the VDR and is often logged in the deck log during pilotage, as your company SMS requires.

Do not confuse it with the speed log. A Doppler log also uses acoustic pulses, but it measures speed, not depth. Some multi-function units do both, but they are separate functions with separate settings.

Main components

A typical installation has these parts:

Component Function
Transmitter Generates a short electrical pulse at the operating frequency
Transducer Converts the electrical pulse to sound and the returning echo back to an electrical signal. Usually piezoelectric ceramic; older sets used magnetostrictive types
Transducer housing / tank Mounted in the bottom plating, often well forward. May sit in a small cofferdam or tank, and some have a gate valve so the transducer can be withdrawn afloat
Junction box and cable Connects the transducer to the bridge unit. A common source of faults
Receiver / amplifier Amplifies the weak echo and applies gain and time-varied gain (TVG), which increases gain with elapsed time to compensate for signal loss with depth and reduces strong near-surface clutter
Processor and display Calculates depth and shows a digital value plus a scrolling depth profile. Older sets used paper recorders
Alarm Shallow-depth alarm, set by the officer of the watch (OOW)
Interfaces Digital output to ECDIS, VDR, conning display and sometimes the integrated bridge system

Many ships have two transducers, forward and aft, with a selector. Some deep-sea units are dual-frequency. A lower frequency gives more range and a wider beam. A higher frequency gives better resolution in shallow water.

How it works, step by step

  1. Transmit. The transmitter sends a short burst of electrical energy to the transducer.
  2. Pulse goes down. The transducer face vibrates and sends a cone-shaped beam of sound vertically down through the water.
  3. Reflection. The pulse hits the seabed, and part of the energy reflects back up. Hard bottoms such as rock and sand reflect strongly. Soft mud gives a weaker, fuzzier echo.
  4. Receive. The same transducer, now acting as a receiver, picks up the echo and converts it into an electrical signal.
  5. Time and calculate. The processor measures the time between transmission and the echo and applies: depth = (speed of sound × time) ÷ 2. It divides by two because the sound travelled down and back.
  6. Apply offset. The raw figure is depth below the transducer. The set then applies the offset you have entered to show depth below the keel or below the surface.
  7. Display and output. The depth is shown, recorded, compared with the alarm setting, and sent to other equipment.

Speed of sound. Sets are calibrated with a nominal value, commonly around 1,500 m/s for seawater. The real speed changes with temperature, salinity and pressure, so readings carry a small error. That error is usually small at merchant-ship depths, but it is one reason the echo sounder is never treated as exact.

Worked example. The echo returns 0.04 s after transmission. Taking 1,500 m/s, depth below transducer = 1,500 × 0.04 ÷ 2 = 30 m. If the transducer sits 0.5 m above the keel line and the display is set to read below the keel, it should show 29.5 m.

Getting the datum right: transducer, keel or surface

The most common way to misread an echo sounder is to forget what it is measuring from. Check which datum the display is set to:

  • Below transducer. This is the raw measurement.
  • Below keel. Raw depth minus the transducer-to-keel offset. This is the one that matters for UKC.
  • Below surface. Raw depth plus the depth of the transducer below the waterline (draught at the transducer position minus the transducer's height above the keel). Use it to compare with charted depth after allowing for tide.

The below-surface setting depends on the draught entered. If the ship loads or discharges and nobody updates the draught, the reading is wrong by the change in draught. Make the offset check part of the departure and arrival checklists.

Transducer position matters too. A transducer near the bow measures clearance at the bow. With trim by the stern, or in shallow water where squat changes trim, the least clearance may be aft. Use the aft transducer if one is fitted. Otherwise, apply the trim correction yourself.

Routine checks and maintenance

Bridge checks (OOW):

  • Switch on and test well before entering shallow or pilotage waters. Record this in the pre-arrival checklist.
  • Confirm the range, gain, datum, offset and draught settings, and set the shallow alarm to a value from the passage plan.
  • Compare the reading with charted depth corrected for tide at a known position. A consistent difference points to an offset or calibration error.
  • Check that depth is reaching ECDIS and the VDR without an interface alarm.
  • Keep a hand lead line available as an independent backup, if your vessel carries one.

Technical and dry-dock items:

  • Transducer face. Inspect and clean marine growth in dry dock. Never coat the face with ordinary paint or antifouling. Use only what the manufacturer allows, because the wrong coating can weaken or kill the signal. Mark it off before painting starts.
  • Do not transmit in air. Many manufacturers warn that energising the transducer out of water can damage it. Isolate the set while the ship is in dry dock.
  • Transducer tank or cofferdam. Check for water ingress, corrosion and the condition of the cable gland.
  • Gate valve (where fitted). Follow the maker's procedure only. A wrong sequence means seawater entering the hull.
  • Junction box and cable. Look for moisture, corrosion and chafe.

Maintenance intervals and records belong in your planned maintenance system and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Common faults and their symptoms

Symptom Likely cause What to do
Reading jumps or drops out while going astern, in heavy weather or when thrusters run Aeration: air bubbles under the transducer block the pulse Expect it. Rely on the chart and other means until the water clears
Weak or no echo in normal depths Fouled or painted transducer face, too little gain, damaged cable, wet junction box Adjust gain, then investigate the hardware
Steady error against chart at all depths Wrong offset or draught entered, wrong datum selected Re-enter the settings and verify again
Two bottom traces, the second at twice the depth Multiple echo: the pulse bounced seabed–hull–seabed, mainly in shallow water with a hard bottom Read the first trace. Reduce gain
Shallow trace that comes and goes in deep water Deep scattering layer (plankton), fish shoals, thermoclines Recognise it as a false bottom. Compare with chart depth
Shoal reading on a steep slope Side echo from the nearest part of a sloping bottom within the beam Treat it as real until proven otherwise
Erratic or noisy trace Interference from another sounder, electrical noise, poor earthing Check whether another sounder is running. Report to the ETO
False shallow trace when the selected range is shallower than the actual depth Second-trace echo: the echo from the previous pulse arrives after the next pulse is transmitted because the true depth exceeds the selected range Select a deeper range scale. A true shoal stays at the same depth when the range changes; a second-trace echo shifts

Rule of thumb: if the echo sounder shows less water than expected, treat it as true and slow down until you have checked. Assume equipment error only after you have proven it.

Regulation and safety precautions

Carriage. SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires ships of 300 GT and upwards, and all passenger ships regardless of size, to have an echo-sounding device or other electronic means to measure and display the available depth of water. The equipment must meet IMO performance standards. For newer installations these are in Resolution MSC.74(69), Annex 4, as amended by MSC.224(82), which cover range, display, depth recording and the shallow alarm. Your flag state may add requirements, and the type-approval certificate and equipment records will show the standard your set meets.

VDR. Depth information is among the data recorded by the VDR under SOLAS Chapter V. A dead echo sounder can therefore also mean a VDR input alarm.

Defects. A defective echo sounder must be reported to the master. It is often noted for port state control or class, depending on flag. How long you may sail with it and what you must record follow flag-state and company SMS procedures.

Safety precautions:

  • If the transducer is in a tank or cofferdam, accessing it may be an enclosed space entry. Use a permit, gas test and rescue arrangements under your SMS.
  • Treat any hull penetration work as watertight-integrity work, under a permit and with the master's knowledge.
  • Isolate electrically (lock-out tag-out) before opening the junction box or transceiver.
  • Do not let the echo sounder lull you. It shows depth under one point of the hull, not ahead. It warns you only once the shoal is already beneath you.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Q: How does an echo sounder measure depth? It times a sound pulse from the transducer to the seabed and back. Depth equals the speed of sound multiplied by the time, divided by two. The set then applies the offset to give depth below keel or surface.

Q: Why might the reading differ from the charted depth? Possible reasons are tide, a wrong draught or offset, the sound-speed error, a chart that is out of date or poorly surveyed, squat and trim, and false echoes.

Q: What is a multiple echo and how do you recognise it? It is a second trace at roughly twice the true depth, caused by the pulse bouncing between seabed and hull. It is common in shallow water over a hard bottom. Read the first trace.

Q: Why does the echo sounder fail when going astern? Propeller wash pushes aerated water under the transducer. The bubbles absorb and scatter the pulse.

Q: What precautions apply to the transducer in dry dock? Do not paint or antifoul the face except as the manufacturer allows, clean off growth, and do not transmit out of water. Also inspect the tank, the gland and the cable.

Q: Your transducer is forward and the ship is trimmed by the stern in a channel. Is the displayed UKC your minimum? No. The deepest point is aft, so correct for trim or use the aft transducer. Squat in shallow water can change the trim further.

Key points
  • Depth = speed of sound × echo time ÷ 2, measured from the transducer face.
  • Always confirm the datum (transducer, keel or surface) and update draught and offset after cargo work.
  • SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 requires an echo sounder on ships of 300 GT and upwards and on all passenger ships.
  • Aeration, the deep scattering layer, multiple echoes and side echoes are the classic false readings.
  • Never paint the transducer face or transmit it out of water in dry dock unless the manufacturer allows it.
  • The echo sounder shows depth under one point of the hull, not ahead, so it confirms the passage plan but does not replace it.
  • Treat a shoaler-than-expected reading as real until proven otherwise.

Frequently asked questions

Is an echo sounder mandatory on all merchant ships?

SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 requires an echo-sounding device, or other electronic means to measure the available depth, on ships of 300 GT and upwards and on all passenger ships irrespective of size. Smaller non-passenger ships fall under flag-state rules. Check your flag administration's requirements.

What speed of sound does a ship's echo sounder use?

Most sets use a nominal calibration value of around 1,500 m/s for seawater. The actual speed varies with temperature, salinity and pressure, so there is always a small error. For merchant-ship depths this is usually minor compared with offset or draught errors.

Why does my echo sounder show a second bottom line?

It is usually a multiple echo. The pulse has bounced from the seabed to the hull and back down again, producing a trace at about twice the true depth. It is most common in shallow water over a hard bottom. Read the shallower trace and reduce the gain.

Does the echo sounder show depth below the keel or below the surface?

It can show either, or depth below the transducer, depending on how it is set. The below-keel and below-surface readings rely on the offset and draught entered. Confirm the datum on the display and update the draught whenever it changes.

Can I paint over the echo sounder transducer in dry dock?

Not with ordinary hull paint or antifouling. A coating on the face can attenuate the signal badly. Follow the manufacturer's instructions, which may permit a specific coating or require the face to be left clean. Mark the transducer so the paint gang avoids it.

Test yourself

An echo returns 0.06 s after transmission. Using 1,500 m/s, what is the depth below the transducer?

Depth = 1,500 × 0.06 ÷ 2 = 45 m. The division by two allows for the pulse's round trip (basic echo-sounding principle).

After loading, the echo sounder set to 'below surface' disagrees with the tide-corrected chart depth by roughly the change in draught. Most likely cause?

The below-surface reading adds the transducer's depth below the waterline, worked out from the entered draught, so a stale draught produces a steady error equal to the draught change (manufacturer's operating principle).

While going astern on the berth, the depth readings become erratic and drop out. Why?

Air bubbles from propeller or thruster wash absorb and scatter the acoustic pulse, a recognised cause of echo loss.

SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 requires an echo-sounding device on which ships?

SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 19 sets the threshold at 300 GT and upwards, plus all passenger ships regardless of size.

In deep water a faint trace appears at a few hundred metres, then fades, while the chart shows several thousand metres. Most likely explanation?

The deep scattering layer of marine organisms gives a false mid-water trace that varies over time. It is a known echo sounder limitation.
