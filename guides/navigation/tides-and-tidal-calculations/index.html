Tides and Tidal Calculations: Principles, Method and Worked Example

Quick answer

A tidal calculation finds the height of tide above chart datum at a given time, or the time when a required height is reached. From that you can work out actual depth, under keel clearance or air draught clearance. You take high and low water from the tide tables, correct them for a secondary port if needed, then interpolate with the tidal curve, or with the cosine method or rule of twelfths where the tide is regular. Examiners expect you to know the datums, the method, and where the predictions can go wrong.

The datums and terms you must have cold

Every tidal problem is really a datum problem. If you get the reference levels wrong, the arithmetic does not matter.

  • Chart datum (CD): the level to which charted depths and tidal predictions are referred. On Admiralty charts it is generally Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT) or close to it. Other hydrographic offices and older surveys may use different datums. The title block or source notes tell you which.
  • Height of tide: the vertical distance from chart datum to the sea surface at a given moment. It can be negative, meaning the water is below chart datum.
  • Charted depth: depth below chart datum. Actual depth = charted depth + height of tide.
  • Drying height: height above chart datum of ground that uncovers (underlined figures on paper charts). Depth over it = height of tide − drying height.
  • LAT / HAT: the lowest and highest levels that can be predicted to occur under average meteorological conditions and any combination of astronomical conditions.
  • MHWS, MLWS, MHWN, MLWN: mean high and low water springs and neaps. Use them to judge whether a day is nearer springs or neaps.
  • Range: the difference between successive HW and LW heights.
  • Duration: the time from LW to HW (or HW to LW).

For heights above the water, Admiralty charts normally give elevations of lights above MHWS (or MHHW where appropriate) and vertical clearances under bridges and cables above HAT. Always confirm against the chart notes, especially on foreign charts and ENCs.

Why the tide behaves as it does

The tide-raising forces of the moon and sun produce the astronomical tide.

  • When sun and moon are in line (new and full moon), their effects combine to give spring tides with a large range.
  • When they are at right angles (first and last quarter), you get neap tides with a small range.

In many places the largest springs arrive a day or two after new or full moon. This lag is known as the age of the tide.

Tides are broadly:

  • Semi-diurnal: two highs and two lows a day of roughly similar height.
  • Diurnal: one high and one low a day.
  • Mixed: two highs and two lows with a marked difference between them (diurnal inequality).

Local geography distorts the simple shape. You will meet double high waters, long stands and asymmetric rise and fall. This is why the tide tables provide a curve for each standard port rather than expecting you to assume a sine wave.

Weather also changes the actual level:

  • High pressure depresses the sea surface and low pressure raises it, by roughly 1 cm for each hPa of departure from average.
  • Strong onshore winds and storm surges raise the level. Offshore winds lower it.

Predictions do not include any of this.

Step-by-step: standard and secondary ports

Admiralty Tide Tables are arranged in three parts:

  • Part I gives daily HW/LW predictions and tidal curves for standard ports.
  • Part II gives time and height differences for secondary ports.
  • Part III gives harmonic constants for use with the simplified harmonic method.

Read the introductory instructions in the edition on board. They explain the detail of seasonal corrections and exceptions.

Standard port, height at a given time:

  1. Extract the HW and LW times and heights either side of the required time. Check the time zone of the tables against ship's time and apply any daylight-saving correction.
  2. Work out the range and compare it with the mean spring and neap ranges printed on the curve diagram. Decide whether to use the spring curve, the neap curve, or to interpolate between them.
  3. On the curve diagram, mark the HW height on the top scale and the LW height on the bottom scale, and join them.
  4. Enter the time scale with the interval from HW and go vertically to the curve. Go horizontally to the line joining the HW and LW heights, then vertically to the height scale and read the height of tide. Alternatively, read the factor from the factor scale and multiply it by the range.

Secondary port:

  1. Extract the standard port HW and LW for the day.
  2. From Part II, interpolate the time differences according to the standard port HW/LW time. For example, suppose HW differences are +0035 when standard port HW is at 0000/1200 and +0045 at 0600/1800. A standard port HW at 0300 then gives +0040.
  3. Interpolate the height differences according to the standard port height, again between the tabulated values.
  4. Apply seasonal corrections to mean level if the tables tell you to.
  5. Use the standard port curve with the secondary port times and heights, unless the tables direct otherwise.

On board, digital tidal software does much of this for you. You are still accountable for checking the port, date, time zone and datum it has used.

Quick methods: rule of twelfths and cosine method

Where the tide is regular and roughly sinusoidal, two shortcuts are acceptable for planning and cross-checking.

Rule of twelfths assumes a duration of about 6 hours. The tide rises (or falls) by these fractions of the range in successive hours:

Hour 1st 2nd 3rd 4th 5th 6th
Fraction of range 1/12 2/12 3/12 3/12 2/12 1/12

Cosine method treats the curve as a half cosine wave. With duration D, measuring time t from LW on a rising tide:

  • Height = LW + Range × (1 − cos(180° × t / D)) / 2

Measuring time t from HW on a falling tide:

  • Height = LW + Range × (1 + cos(180° × t / D)) / 2

Both methods fail where the tide is not sinusoidal, for example where there are double high waters, long stands or shallow-water distortion. In those places, use the published curve or a harmonic prediction.

The rule of twelfths also handles durations far from 6 hours badly, because it assumes about 6 hours. The cosine method uses the actual duration, but it still assumes a symmetrical curve.

In the exam, use the method the question asks for and state your assumption.

Worked example: tidal window over a bar

Illustrative figures only, not a real port.

Data (standard port, local time):

  • LW 0410 at 1.0 m
  • HW 1010 at 5.2 m
  • Following LW 1625 at 1.0 m
  • Least charted depth on the bar: 7.5 m
  • Static draught: 9.8 m
  • Required UKC under company policy: 1.0 m. Squat is assumed to be already allowed for in this figure.

Step 1: required height of tide

  • Required depth = 9.8 + 1.0 = 10.8 m
  • Required height of tide = 10.8 − 7.5 = 3.3 m
  • Rise needed above LW = 3.3 − 1.0 = 2.3 m. Range = 4.2 m, so factor = 2.3 / 4.2 = 0.548

Step 2: earliest time on the flood (cosine method, D = 6 h 00 m)

  • (1 − cos θ)/2 = 0.548, so cos θ = −0.095 and θ = 95.5°
  • t = 95.5/180 × 6 h = 3.18 h = 3 h 11 m after LW, so 0721

The rule of twelfths gives the same answer:

  • After 3 hours the tide has risen 6/12 × 4.2 = 2.1 m.
  • The 4th hour adds 1.05 m.
  • The remaining 0.2 m takes about 11 minutes, which again gives 0721.

Step 3: latest time on the ebb (D = 6 h 15 m)

  • (1 + cos φ)/2 = 0.548, so cos φ = 0.095 and φ = 84.5°
  • t = 84.5/180 × 6.25 h = 2.93 h = 2 h 56 m after HW, so 1306

Result: the predicted window is 0721–1306. Now apply judgement:

  • If the barometer reads 1030 hPa, expect the water to be roughly 0.1–0.2 m lower than predicted. This narrows the window.
  • Allow for ETA uncertainty. Plan to cross well inside the window rather than at its edges.
  • If the published curve for the port is not symmetrical, rework the problem with the curve. The real times can differ noticeably from the cosine result.

Same day, drying bank: a shoal with a drying height of 1.5 m has 3.3 − 1.5 = 1.8 m of water over it at 0721.

Air draught: take a bridge with a charted clearance of 40.0 m above HAT, where HAT is 5.8 m. At 0721 the clearance is 40.0 + (5.8 − 3.3) = 42.5 m. Clearance under a bridge is least at high water, so check it at HW, not only at your transit time.

Common mistakes that fail candidates and ground ships

  • Mixing datums. Using a tide table referred to one datum with a chart on another. This is more common with foreign charts, ENCs and port-supplied data.
  • Time zone errors. The tables are in standard time but the ship is on daylight saving or a different zone. Apply the correction once, and only once.
  • Wrong curve. Using the spring curve on a neap day without checking the range, or interpolating the wrong way.
  • Secondary port shortcuts. Taking the nearest tabulated difference instead of interpolating, or forgetting the seasonal correction.
  • Rule of twelfths everywhere. Applying it in ports with double HWs or long stands.
  • Ignoring weather. Predictions assume average conditions. A high barometer plus an offshore wind can take away tens of centimetres.
  • Forgetting the other vertical limits. Charted bridge clearances are normally given above HAT and light elevations above MHWS, not directly above chart datum. Convert using the height of tide before comparing with your air draught.
  • ECDIS complacency. The safety contour and safety depth do not automatically account for the height of tide. Deciding whether and how to include tide is a passage-planning decision, set out in your company SMS.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

Expect the examiner to move from definitions to judgement. Practise saying these answers aloud.

  • What is chart datum and why is it set so low? It is the reference for depths and tidal heights. It is set low (often LAT) so that the actual depth is rarely less than charted.
  • Can the height of tide be negative? When? Yes. It happens when the water falls below chart datum, for example with exceptional astronomical tides, high pressure or offshore winds.
  • Why do springs occur, and why after new or full moon rather than on the day? Sun and moon are aligned and their effects combine. The lag of a day or two is the age of the tide.
  • How do you find the tide at a secondary port? Interpolate the time and height differences, apply seasonal corrections as instructed, then use the standard port curve.
  • When would you not use the rule of twelfths? Where the tide is not sinusoidal or the duration is far from 6 hours.
  • From what level are bridge clearances and light elevations measured on your chart? On Admiralty charts, normally HAT for clearances and MHWS for light elevations. Always confirm in the chart notes.
  • What is the effect of a 1030 hPa barometer on predicted heights? The water will be lower than predicted, by roughly 1 cm per hPa above average.
  • You have a tidal window. What else goes into the UKC? Squat, heel, wave response, survey accuracy and the company UKC policy.

The exact format and depth of questioning varies between administrations and examiners. Use your own administration's syllabus and the guidance from your training institute.

Key points
  • Actual depth = charted depth + height of tide; depth over a drying bank = height of tide − drying height.
  • Check that the chart and the tide tables use the same datum before any calculation.
  • Secondary ports: interpolate the time and height differences, then use the standard port curve.
  • The rule of twelfths and the cosine method only suit regular, roughly sinusoidal tides.
  • Predictions ignore weather; high pressure and offshore winds lower the actual level.
  • Bridge clearances are normally given above HAT and light elevations above MHWS, not directly above chart datum.
  • Plan to transit well inside a tidal window, not at its edges.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between height of tide and depth of water?

Height of tide is the level of the sea above chart datum at a given time. Depth of water is the charted depth plus that height of tide. Depth is what matters for under keel clearance.

How do I calculate the tide at a secondary port?

Take HW and LW at the associated standard port. Interpolate the secondary port time and height differences from the tide tables, and apply any seasonal correction as the instructions direct. Then use the standard port tidal curve with the corrected secondary port times and heights.

Is the rule of twelfths accurate enough for real navigation?

It gives a reasonable estimate where the tide is regular and the duration is about six hours. It is unreliable in places with double high waters, long stands or distorted curves. For critical UKC decisions, use the published curve or tidal prediction software and add a margin.

Why is the actual tide sometimes lower than the tide tables predict?

Predictions are astronomical and assume average weather. High atmospheric pressure, offshore winds and other meteorological effects can lower the sea level. Low pressure, onshore winds and surges can raise it.

How do I find clearance under a bridge using the tide?

Find the datum of the charted vertical clearance, which is normally HAT on Admiralty charts. Add the difference between HAT and the actual height of tide to the charted clearance. Compare the result with your air draught plus your required safety margin, and check it at the highest water during your passage.

Test yourself

Charted depth is 6.2 m and the height of tide is 2.4 m. What is the actual depth?

Actual depth equals charted depth plus height of tide, so 6.2 + 2.4 = 8.6 m. The height of tide is added to a charted depth, not subtracted from it (Admiralty Tide Tables, introductory notes).

A shoal has a drying height of 1.2 m and the height of tide is 3.0 m. What depth is over the shoal?

Drying heights are above chart datum, so depth = height of tide − drying height = 3.0 − 1.2 = 1.8 m (Admiralty Tide Tables, introductory notes).

When are spring tides normally experienced?

Springs follow the alignment of sun and moon, and the largest range often lags by a day or two (the age of the tide). The quarters give neaps (Admiralty Tide Tables, introductory notes).

On Admiralty charts, vertical clearances under bridges are normally referred to which level?

Clearances are normally given above HAT so that the charted figure is close to the least clearance. Confirm this in the notes for each chart (NP5011, Symbols and Abbreviations used on Admiralty Charts).

Where is the rule of twelfths least reliable?

The rule assumes a sinusoidal tide of about six hours' duration. Double high waters and stands break that assumption, so use the published tidal curve (Admiralty Tide Tables, Part I curves).
