Tidal Streams and Set and Drift: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Set is the direction towards which a tidal stream or current flows, in degrees true. Drift is the distance the vessel is carried in a stated time, and rate is that drift expressed in knots. You get tidal stream data from chart tidal diamonds, tidal stream atlases and sailing directions, adjust the rate between springs and neaps for the day's range, and then use a vector triangle to find a course to steer, an estimated position, or the set and drift actually experienced.

Set, drift and rate: get the words right first

Examiners pick up loose terminology straight away, so fix these definitions before anything else:

  • Set: the direction towards which the water is moving, in degrees true. A stream setting 270° flows towards the west. This is the opposite convention to wind, which is named for the direction it blows from.
  • Drift: the distance the vessel is carried by the stream over a stated period, in nautical miles.
  • Rate: drift per hour, in knots. A rate of 1.5 kn over 2 hours gives a drift of 3 nm.
  • Tidal stream: horizontal movement of water caused by the tide-raising forces. It is periodic and linked to the times of high water.
  • Current: non-tidal movement of water caused by wind, density differences or ocean circulation. In everyday bridge language, people often use "current" for both.
  • Leeway: the angle between the heading and the water track, caused by wind. It is not set. Keep it separate in your construction.

In open water, tidal streams are often rotary, meaning the direction swings round the compass through the tidal cycle. In channels and estuaries they are usually rectilinear, meaning they flood one way, ebb the other way, and pass through slack water in between. Slack water and the turn of the stream do not necessarily coincide with local high or low water. In many estuaries and straits the stream keeps running for a considerable time after HW. Always check the published data rather than assuming.

Where tidal stream data comes from

Tidal diamonds on charts. A lettered diamond on a paper chart refers to a table on the same chart. For each hour from about six hours before to six hours after HW, the table gives:

  • the direction of the stream in degrees true;
  • the rate at springs and at neaps in knots.

The times are referred to HW at the standard port named in the table heading. They are not referred to the nearest secondary port. Each tabulated value is normally treated as representative of the period from half an hour before to half an hour after that hour.

Tidal stream atlases. These are published for areas with significant streams. They show arrows for each hour relative to HW at a reference port. Each arrow carries paired figures giving the neap and spring rate in tenths of a knot, so "08,15" means 0.8 kn at neaps and 1.5 kn at springs. Most atlases include a computation-of-rates diagram for interpolating between the two.

Sailing Directions and tidal stream tables. These give descriptive information and predictions for straits, harbour approaches and races. They are often more detailed than the chart.

Ocean currents. Routeing charts and pilot charts give mean current directions and rates for each month. Treat these as averages, not predictions.

ECDIS. Some ECDIS can display tidal stream information if suitable data is loaded, but capability and data coverage vary between makers and installations. Check what your system actually shows. Do not assume an absence of arrows means an absence of stream.

Step-by-step: finding the stream for a given time

  1. Find the time and height of HW at the standard port referenced by the diamond or atlas, using the tide tables. Work in one time zone throughout.
  2. Work out how many hours before or after that HW your planned time falls. Round to the nearest whole hour, or interpolate between hours if the stream is changing fast.
  3. Read the direction and the spring and neap rates for that hour.
  4. Find the day's range at the standard port: HW height minus LW height.
  5. Compare the day's range with the mean spring and mean neap ranges for that port. Interpolate the rate, either with the atlas diagram or by simple proportion:

Rate = neap rate + (spring rate − neap rate) × (today's range − mean neap range) ÷ (mean spring range − mean neap range)

Example (hypothetical port). The diamond shows 245°T, springs 2.4 kn, neaps 1.2 kn. Mean spring range is 5.0 m, mean neap range is 2.0 m, and today's range is 3.8 m.

  • Fraction of the way to springs = (3.8 − 2.0) ÷ (5.0 − 2.0) = 0.6
  • Rate = 1.2 + (1.2 × 0.6) = 1.9 kn setting 245°T

If today's range is larger than mean springs, the stream may well be stronger than the spring figure. Treat any extrapolation with caution, and allow a margin in confined waters.

For a passage spanning several hours, repeat the lookup for each hour of the passage. You can either combine the hourly vectors or plot each hour separately.

Worked example 1: finding set and drift from a fix

Situation. At 0800 you have a good fix. You steer 045°T at 10 kn through the water, there is no appreciable wind, and the log is reliable. At 1000 you take another fix.

Step 1: plot the DR. From the 0800 fix, lay off 045°T for 2 h × 10 kn = 20 nm. This is your 1000 DR (water) position.

Step 2: plot the 1000 fix. In this example, the fix lies 3.0 nm from the DR in a direction of 135°T.

Step 3: measure from DR to fix. Always measure in this order, never fix to DR.

  • Set = 135°T
  • Drift = 3.0 nm in 2 hours
  • Rate = 3.0 ÷ 2 = 1.5 kn

Step 4: interpret. The line from DR to fix contains everything you did not account for, including stream, leeway, log error, gyro error and steering error. Calling it all "set and drift" is normal practice. But if the figure disagrees badly with the predicted stream, check the gyro error and the log before blaming the tide.

The same principle applies to the ground track. Joining the 0800 fix to the 1000 fix gives your course and speed made good. Here that is about 053.5°T, 20.2 nm in 2 hours, or about 10.1 kn. You can check this by resolving the two vectors.

Worked example 2: course to steer and the estimated position

Course to steer (CTS). You want to make good 090°T. Speed through the water is 10 kn. The predicted stream is setting 000°T at 2 kn, which pushes you directly off track to the north.

On the chart:

  1. From your start point A, draw the intended track 090°T.
  2. From A, lay off the stream vector: 000°T, 2 nm for one hour. Call its end B.
  3. From B, swing an arc of 10 nm (one hour of speed through water) to cut the track line at C.
  4. Measure B to C. That is your course to steer. A to C is your speed made good.

By calculation, the stream is at right angles to the track:

  • sin α = 2 ÷ 10 = 0.2, so α ≈ 11.5°
  • CTS ≈ 090° + 11.5° = 101.5°T, steer 102°T (add leeway separately if there is wind)
  • Speed over ground = 10 × cos 11.5° ≈ 9.8 kn
  • A 20 nm leg takes about 20 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 2.04 h, or about 2 h 02 min

The golden rule is that every side of the triangle must represent the same time interval. If the leg takes 2 hours, use 4 nm of stream with 20 nm of water distance, or work in one-hour units throughout.

Estimated position (EP). EP is the reverse operation.

  1. From the last fix, lay off the water track: heading corrected for leeway, and distance by log.
  2. From the end of that line, lay off set and drift for the same period.
  3. The end point is the EP. Mark it with the standard EP symbol and the time.

An EP is only as good as the stream prediction. Confirm it with a fix as soon as practicable.

Common mistakes on the bridge and in the exam

  • Reversing the set. Plotting the stream as coming from 135° instead of going towards it. This puts your EP on the wrong side of the track.
  • Using rate as drift. A 1.5 kn stream over 40 minutes is a 1.0 nm drift, not 1.5 nm.
  • Using the wrong HW. Tidal diamonds and atlases use HW at the stated standard port. Using a nearby secondary port's HW shifts every hourly value.
  • Using spring rates every day. Over-correcting at neaps can be as dangerous as under-correcting at springs, especially near shoals.
  • Mixing time intervals in the triangle. For example, using one hour of stream with two hours of ship's speed.
  • Measuring fix to DR. Set is measured from the DR or EP towards the fix.
  • Confusing COG with heading. GNSS gives course and speed over ground. The ship's head can be well off COG in a cross stream. Lookouts and pilots need to know where the bow is actually pointing.
  • Forgetting the stream changes. A CTS worked for one hour is wrong in the next if the stream has turned. Re-plan at each hourly change in narrow waters.
  • Ignoring the stream at slow speed. At manoeuvring speeds, near berths and at anchor, a 2 kn stream can dominate the ship's movement.

Oral exam: questions you should be ready for

Q: Define set, drift and rate.
Set is the direction towards which the stream flows, in degrees true. Drift is the distance carried in a stated time. Rate is drift per hour, in knots.

Q: Your fix is consistently to the south of your DR. What do you do?
Work out the set and drift and compare them with the predicted stream. Check the gyro error by azimuth, transit or other means, and check the log. Then apply a corrected course to steer and monitor with closer fix intervals, keeping clear of dangers on the side you are being set towards.

Q: How do you find the rate for a day between springs and neaps?
Compare the day's range at the standard port with the mean spring and neap ranges. Then interpolate, either by proportion or with the atlas computation-of-rates diagram.

Q: Is slack water at HW?
Not necessarily. The turn of the stream can differ significantly from local HW or LW, so check the diamonds, the atlas or the Sailing Directions.

Q: In a strong stream, should you use ground- or sea-stabilised radar for collision avoidance?
Use sea-stabilised to judge the other vessel's aspect and heading through the water. Ground-stabilisation shows tracks over ground, which is useful for navigation but can mislead you about aspect when a stream is running. Follow your company procedures and understand which mode your display is in.

Q: How does the stream affect your passage plan?
It affects ETAs, the timing of tidal gates and narrow passages, under-keel clearance windows, the courses to steer on each leg, and the margins of safety off dangers lying on the down-stream side.

Key points
  • Set is the direction the stream flows towards, in degrees true; wind is named for where it comes from.
  • Drift is distance carried in a stated time; rate is that drift per hour in knots.
  • Tidal diamond and atlas times refer to HW at the named standard port, not the nearest secondary port.
  • Interpolate the rate between neap and spring values using the day's range at the standard port.
  • Every side of a course-to-steer or EP triangle must cover the same time interval.
  • Measure set and drift from the DR or EP towards the fix, and suspect gyro or log error if it disagrees badly with prediction.
  • In a cross stream, heading and COG differ, and when you steer to hold a track, speed over ground is less than speed through the water.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between set and drift?

Set is the direction towards which the current or tidal stream flows, in degrees true. Drift is how far the vessel has been carried in a given time, in nautical miles. Rate is drift per hour, in knots.

How do I read a tidal diamond on a chart?

Find the table for that diamond's letter on the same chart. Note the standard port named in it and work out how many hours you are before or after HW there. Read the direction and the spring and neap rates for that hour, then interpolate the rate for the day's range.

Is the set given in true or magnetic?

Tidal stream directions on charts and in atlases are given in degrees true. Plot them directly on the chart without applying variation or deviation.

Why is my fix different from my DR even with the tide applied?

The difference between DR and fix includes not just the stream but also leeway, gyro error, log error and steering error. The predicted stream may also differ from the actual one because of wind or local effects. Check the gyro and the log before assuming the tidal data is wrong.

Does a current change my speed over ground?

Yes. A following stream increases speed over ground and a head stream reduces it. When you steer into a cross stream to hold a track, your speed over ground is less than your speed through the water. This must be reflected in ETAs and tidal-window planning.

Test yourself

A tidal stream is described as setting 270° at 2 knots. Which way is the water moving?

Set is the true direction towards which the stream flows, the opposite convention to wind direction. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-II/1 (position determination and use of tidal stream data).

What does 'drift' mean in set and drift?

Drift is a distance; rate is speed, leeway is the wind effect, and set is a direction. Reference: competence in position determination under STCW Code, Table A-II/1.

A diamond gives 2.0 kn at springs and 1.0 kn at neaps. Mean spring range is 4.0 m, mean neap range is 2.0 m, and today's range is 3.0 m. What is the interpolated rate?

Today's range is halfway between neap and spring ranges, so the rate is halfway between 1.0 and 2.0 kn. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-II/1 (use of tidal data).

The hourly times in a chart's tidal stream table are referred to HW at which port?

Tidal stream tables and atlases are referenced to HW at the stated standard port; using a nearby secondary port shifts every value. Reference: the chart's tidal stream table heading / tidal stream atlas introduction; STCW Code, Table A-II/1 (use of tidal stream data).

A vessel steers into a stream running at right angles to her intended track so as to hold that track. Her speed over ground will be:

Part of the ship's speed through the water is used to cancel the cross stream, so speed over ground equals speed through the water times the cosine of the correction angle. Reference: STCW Code, Table A-II/1.
