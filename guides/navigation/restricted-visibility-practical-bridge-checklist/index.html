Restricted Visibility: Practical Bridge Checklist for Deck Officers

Quick answer

When visibility drops, call the master, put the engines on immediate notice, reduce to a safe speed, start fog signals, show navigation lights, double the lookout and use radar properly. For any radar-only target, plot it, decide whether close quarters or risk of collision is developing, and act early under Rule 19(d). Stand-on and give-way do not apply to vessels that cannot see each other.

What counts as restricted visibility and when Rule 19 applies

Rule 3 defines restricted visibility as any condition in which visibility is reduced by fog, mist, falling snow, heavy rainstorms, sandstorms or similar causes. No visibility figure appears anywhere in COLREG. The trigger in your master's standing orders or company SMS is often a set distance, so learn the one on your ship. The Rules themselves only ask whether you can see the other vessel.

Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another when navigating in or near an area of restricted visibility. Two points get candidates into trouble:

  • 'Near' counts. If you are in clear weather beside a fog bank, a vessel inside it cannot see you. Rule 19 applies, and so do the Rule 35 sound signals.
  • No stand-on or give-way. Rules 11 to 18 (Section II) apply only to vessels in sight of one another. Once you can see each other visually, Section II applies again from that moment.

Rules 4 to 10 (Section I) still apply in any visibility. Rule 19(c) tells you to have due regard to the prevailing conditions when you comply with them. Look-out, safe speed, risk of collision and action to avoid collision all carry more weight in fog.

The checklist: first actions when visibility drops

Section A-VIII/2 of the STCW Code requires the OOW to comply with COLREG first. It singles out sound signals, safe speed and engines ready for immediate manoeuvre. It also requires you to inform the master, post a proper look-out, exhibit navigation lights and operate the radar.

A practical sequence that works on most ships is below. Your company's checklist takes precedence.

  1. Call the master. STCW requires you to notify the master immediately if restricted visibility is encountered or expected. Do not wait until you are in it.
  2. Engine room. Tell the engineer of the watch that the engines must be ready for immediate manoeuvre. If the machinery space is unmanned, follow the SMS procedure to man it. Note the time.
  3. Safe speed. Reduce to a speed that suits the visibility, the traffic density and your stopping distance. Do not just leave the telegraph where it was.
  4. Fog signals. Start the Rule 35 signal and check that the whistle actually sounds. In cold weather, check the whistle heater if fitted.
  5. Navigation lights. Switch them on. Rule 20 requires lights by day in restricted visibility.
  6. Look-out. Post an additional look-out positioned to see and hear. A bridge wing with the door open is often better than inside the wheelhouse. Stop noisy deck work if it masks sound signals.
  7. Steering. Change to manual steering with a helmsman if the master's orders or the SMS require it.
  8. Radar. Run both radars, ideally X-band and S-band. Use one on a longer range for early detection and one on a shorter range for close targets. Tune clutter controls carefully and start ARPA acquisition on all relevant targets.
  9. Position monitoring. Fix more often. Use parallel indexing and the echo sounder near the coast. Cross-check GNSS against radar.
  10. Watertight doors and openings. Close them per the SMS.
  11. Communications. Keep the VHF watch. Treat VHF agreements to manoeuvre with caution, because you may be talking to the wrong ship.
  12. Log. Record the time, the visibility and every precaution taken.

Also consider whether to anchor, delay the passage, or change the route away from dense traffic.

Conduct under Rule 19: the logic behind the actions

Safe speed and engines ready (Rule 19(b)). Rule 6 lists the factors, including radar factors for ships fitted with radar:

  • small craft and ice may not be detected
  • sea and rain clutter affect detection
  • the range scale in use limits what you see

You may hear the rule of thumb 'be able to stop in half the visible distance'. It comes from case law and is not COLREG text. Know that it exists, but argue from Rule 6 in an exam.

Radar-only targets (Rule 19(d)). If you detect a vessel by radar alone, decide whether a close-quarters situation is developing and/or risk of collision exists. If so, take avoiding action in ample time. If that action is an alteration of course, avoid, so far as possible:

  • an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken
  • an alteration towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam

Fog signal heard forward of the beam (Rule 19(e)). This applies unless you have determined that risk of collision does not exist. It also applies if you cannot avoid a close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam. In either case:

  • reduce to the minimum speed at which you can keep your course
  • take all way off if necessary
  • navigate with extreme caution until the danger is over

Supporting rules.

  • Rule 7(b) requires proper use of radar, including long-range scanning and systematic observation.
  • Rule 7(c) forbids assumptions based on scanty information, especially scanty radar information.
  • Rule 8 requires action that is large enough to be readily apparent on another ship's radar. Avoid a series of small alterations.

Worked example: radar target fine on the starboard bow

Situation. Own ship is steering 000° at 10 knots in fog. Visibility is about 0.5 nm. The master is on the bridge. One target is acquired:

Time Bearing (true) Range
0600 030° 8.0 nm
0606 030° 7.0 nm
0612 030° 6.0 nm

Step 1: Assess.

  • The bearing is steady and the range is closing 1 nm every 6 minutes, so the relative speed is 10 knots.
  • The relative track runs straight down the bearing line, so the CPA is effectively zero.
  • The TCPA from 0612 is 6 ÷ 10 h = 36 minutes, so CPA is at about 0648.
  • Risk of collision exists.

Step 2: Find the target's true motion.

  • Own ship moved 2.0 nm north in 12 minutes.
  • The target's relative motion was 2.0 nm towards 210°.
  • Adding these vectors gives the target's true track: about 285° at roughly 5 knots.
  • The target is crossing from starboard to port, forward of our beam. We are not overtaking it.

Step 3: Choose the action.

  • Rule 19(d)(i) says avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam.
  • A bold alteration to starboard takes us under the target's stern.
  • Before turning, check sea room and other targets.
  • Alter to 060° and keep 10 knots, or reduce speed as well if sea room is tight.

Step 4: Predict and verify. Ignoring turning time:

  • Relative velocity becomes about 14 knots towards roughly 255°.
  • The predicted CPA is about 4.2 nm, at roughly 18 minutes after the alteration.
  • The target should now sit on our port bow, with its bearing drawing steadily left.
  • Monitor until it is past and clear.

Step 5: Watch for changes. If the bearing stops changing, the target may have altered too. Re-plot immediately. If at any point you hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam, Rule 19(e) applies until you have determined there is no risk of collision.

Step 6: Resume and log. Return to your track only when the target is past and clear. Log every action.

Sound signals you must know without thinking (Rule 35)

Rule 32 defines the blasts:

  • a short blast lasts about 1 second
  • a prolonged blast lasts 4 to 6 seconds

Rule 35 signals are sounded in or near restricted visibility, by day or night.

Vessel Signal Interval
Power-driven, making way 1 prolonged not more than 2 min
Power-driven, underway but stopped 2 prolonged, about 2 s apart not more than 2 min
NUC, RAM, CBD, sailing, fishing, towing or pushing 1 prolonged + 2 short not more than 2 min
Manned vessel being towed (last of tow if more than one) 1 prolonged + 3 short, immediately after the tug's signal not more than 2 min
At anchor rapid bell for about 5 s; at 100 m or more, bell forward then gong aft not more than 1 min
Aground anchor signal plus 3 separate distinct strokes on the bell before and after not more than 1 min

Notes on the table:

  • A RAM vessel at anchor while carrying out her work sounds the RAM signal. So does a fishing vessel at anchor while fishing.
  • An anchored vessel may also sound short, prolonged, short to warn an approaching vessel.
  • A pilot vessel on duty may add an identity signal of four short blasts.
  • Vessels under 12 m are not obliged to sound these signals, but must make some other efficient sound signal at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.

Rule 34 manoeuvring signals, such as one short blast when altering to starboard, are for vessels in sight of one another. Do not sound them in fog.

Common mistakes on the bridge and in the exam room

  • Treating AIS as a radar plot. AIS data can be wrong, delayed or absent. Small craft often do not transmit at all. Use AIS to support radar, never to replace it.
  • Small, slow alterations. A 10° course change is unlikely to be readily apparent to another vessel observing by radar (Rule 8(b)). Make it bold, or slow down substantially.
  • Turning to port for a target on the starboard bow. This is the classic radar-assisted collision pattern.
  • Applying stand-on logic. There is no stand-on vessel in Rule 19. Both ships have a duty to act.
  • Trusting the direction of a fog signal. Sound behaves unpredictably in fog. You cannot rely on the direction it seems to come from, or on how loud it is.
  • Assuming the radar target is the vessel you can hear. It may be a different ship, or one you are not detecting.
  • One radar on a short range only. You lose early detection and the time to make a full plot.
  • Forgetting 'near'. Sound signals and Rule 19 apply when you are close to fog, not just inside it.
  • Using VHF to agree a manoeuvre with a ship you have not positively identified.
  • Calling the master late. STCW requires it when restricted visibility is expected, not only once you are in it.

Oral exam questions examiners actually ask

  • 'Visibility drops to 1 mile on your watch. What do you do?' Give the checklist in order: master, engines, safe speed, signals, lights, look-out, radar, then fixing and log. Then mention your company's specific requirements.
  • 'Target on the port bow, closing, steady bearing. Which way do you go?' For a target forward of the beam that is not being overtaken, avoid an alteration to port. A bold alteration to starboard, or a substantial speed reduction, is usual. Explain the CPA check you would make first.
  • 'Target on your starboard quarter, closing.' Avoid altering towards it, which here means avoid starboard. An alteration to port takes you away. Increasing speed may also be an option if you have searoom.
  • 'You hear a fog signal ahead and see nothing on radar.' Apply Rule 19(e): reduce to minimum steerage speed, take all way off if necessary, and proceed with extreme caution.
  • 'Do you have a stand-on vessel in fog?' No. Section II applies only when vessels are in sight of one another.
  • 'Sound signal for a vessel stopped in fog?' Two prolonged blasts about 2 seconds apart, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.
  • 'What is safe speed in fog?' There is no number. Argue from the Rule 6 factors, including the radar factors.
Key points
  • Rule 19 applies to vessels not in sight of one another, in or near restricted visibility; there is no stand-on or give-way.
  • Call the master as soon as restricted visibility is encountered or expected, and put the engines ready for immediate manoeuvre.
  • Sound Rule 35 signals, show navigation lights and post an extra look-out who can both see and hear.
  • For radar-only targets, plot systematically and act in ample time; do not decide on scanty information.
  • Avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam (unless overtaking it) and avoid altering towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam.
  • A fog signal heard forward of the beam means minimum steerage speed unless you have ruled out risk of collision.
  • Rule 34 manoeuvring signals are for vessels in sight; AIS and VHF support but never replace radar plotting.

Frequently asked questions

At what visibility distance does restricted visibility start under COLREG?

COLREG gives no distance. Rule 3 defines restricted visibility by its cause, such as fog, mist, snow or heavy rain. Many companies and masters set a trigger distance in the SMS or standing orders, so follow the one on your ship.

Why should I avoid altering to port for a radar target ahead in fog?

Rule 19(d) asks you to avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken. The concern is that the other ship may alter to starboard at the same time, which closes the gap. A bold starboard alteration or a reduction in speed is the usual choice.

Do overtaking and crossing rules apply in restricted visibility?

Not while the vessels cannot see each other. Rules 11 to 18 apply only to vessels in sight of one another, so Rule 19 governs until then. Once you are in visual sight, Section II applies again from that moment.

What fog signal does a power-driven vessel sound when stopped in the water?

Two prolonged blasts in succession, about 2 seconds apart, at intervals of not more than 2 minutes. When making way, the signal is one prolonged blast at intervals of not more than 2 minutes.

Can I agree a passing arrangement on VHF in fog instead of manoeuvring under Rule 19?

VHF does not change your obligations under the Rules. Several collisions have happened after an agreement with the wrong ship, or one that was misunderstood. If you use VHF, make sure the other vessel is positively identified, and still take Rule 19 action based on your own plot.

Test yourself

What sound signal does a power-driven vessel making way through the water give in restricted visibility?

Rule 35(a) prescribes one prolonged blast every 2 minutes or less for a power-driven vessel making way. Two prolonged blasts are for a vessel underway but stopped (Rule 35(b)).

In fog, a radar-only target is fine on your starboard bow with a steady bearing and closing range. You are not overtaking it. Which action should you avoid so far as possible?

Rule 19(d)(i) asks you to avoid an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken.

You hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam and have not determined that risk of collision does not exist. What does Rule 19(e) require?

Rule 19(e) requires reduction to bare steerage way, taking all way off if necessary, and extreme caution until the danger of collision is over.

When do the steering and sailing rules of Section II (Rules 11 to 18) apply?

Rule 11 limits Section II to vessels in sight of one another. Vessels not in sight in or near restricted visibility follow Rule 19.

A vessel 150 m long is at anchor in fog. What is her basic sound signal?

Rule 35(g) requires a vessel of 100 m or more at anchor to ring the bell forward and immediately afterwards sound the gong aft, at intervals of not more than 1 minute.
