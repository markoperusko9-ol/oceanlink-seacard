Azimuth and Amplitude Observations for Compass Error

Quick answer

An azimuth or amplitude gives you the true bearing of a celestial body. Compare it with the bearing taken by gyro or magnetic compass and you have the compass error. Use an amplitude when the body is on the celestial horizon at rising or setting. Use an azimuth at any other altitude, with the true bearing calculated from latitude, declination and LHA.

What they are and why you still do them

Every compass error check comes down to one comparison. You take a bearing with the compass, you find the true bearing by an independent method, and the difference is the error. Near the coast, transits and leading lines do this well. In open water, the sun or a star is often the only reliable true reference you have.

  • Azimuth: the true bearing of a celestial body at any altitude, calculated for the moment of observation.
  • Amplitude: the true bearing of a body at the instant its true altitude is zero, meaning its centre is on the celestial horizon. It is measured from the East point (rising) or the West point (setting), towards North or South.

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires ships to carry a means of correcting heading and bearings to true at all times. The STCW Code watchkeeping principles (Section A-VIII/2) expect the OOW to check compass error at least once a watch and, when possible, after a major alteration of course. Your company SMS may set additional requirements. These include a fixed frequency, a compass error book format, or checks before entering coastal waters, so follow your SMS. ECDIS and GNSS do not remove the duty. A gyro error feeds into radar, ARPA target vectors, the autopilot and ECDIS heading.

Naming compass error, deviation and variation

Get the naming wrong and a correct calculation still gives a wrong answer. Fix this first.

  • Compass error = True bearing − Compass bearing.
  • If true is greater than compass, the error is East. If true is less, the error is West.
  • The memory aid is "Compass least, error East; compass best, error West."
  • Gyro error is often described as high (gyro reads more than true, so the error is West) or low (gyro reads less than true, so the error is East).

For the magnetic compass, the total error is made up of variation and deviation:

  • Error = Variation + Deviation, with East counted as positive and West as negative.
  • Variation comes from the chart and is corrected for the year.
  • Deviation is whatever is left. It applies only to the ship's heading at the time of observation, so always record the heading alongside the result.

Deviation changes with heading. A deviation found on 090° says nothing reliable about 270°. That is why the deviation card exists, and why a fresh check after a large course change is good practice.

The amplitude: principle, formula and when to observe

The amplitude needs only latitude and declination. No time or LHA is required:

sin Amplitude = sin Declination ÷ cos Latitude

Naming works as follows:

  • The first letter is E for a rising body and W for a setting body.
  • The second letter is N or S, the same name as the declination.
  • So a sun setting with northerly declination gives an amplitude like W16°N. Convert it to a true bearing: 270° + 16° = 286°.

When to take the bearing. The formula assumes the body's centre is on the celestial horizon, not the visible one. Refraction lifts the body near the horizon by roughly half a degree. Dip from your height of eye also matters. For the sun, the result is that the centre reaches true altitude zero while the lower limb is still visibly above the horizon. This is commonly taught as about half a diameter, and the exact amount depends on refraction and height of eye. For the moon, horizontal parallax (about 57') exceeds refraction (about 34'), so at true altitude zero the centre is below the visible horizon. As a rough guide, take the bearing when the upper limb is about on the visible horizon.

If you take the bearing with the body sitting on the visible horizon, apply the correction for an amplitude observed on the visible horizon. Nautical tables such as Norie's and Bowditch include this correction, with rules for applying it. The correction is small in low latitudes and grows with latitude and declination.

Limits of the method:

  • If declination is equal to or greater than (90° − latitude) and of the same name, the body never sets.
  • If it is of contrary name, the body never rises.
  • Either way, no amplitude is possible.
  • In high latitudes the body rises at a shallow angle, so small timing errors move the bearing a lot. An azimuth at low altitude is usually the better choice there.

The azimuth: ABC method and the formula

An azimuth needs latitude, declination and LHA at the moment of the bearing. So you need the exact time, the GHA and declination from the Nautical Almanac, and your longitude:

LHA = GHA + East longitude (or − West longitude)

ABC tables (Norie's and similar):

  1. A = tan Lat ÷ tan LHA. Name it opposite to latitude, except when LHA lies between 90° and 270°. In that case name it the same as latitude.
  2. B = tan Dec ÷ sin LHA. Name it the same as declination.
  3. C = A ± B. Add if A and B have the same name, subtract if they differ. Name C after the larger.
  4. tan Azimuth = 1 ÷ (C × cos Lat).
  5. Name the azimuth with C's letter (N or S) first. Then add W if LHA is less than 180°, or E if LHA is more than 180°.

Direct formula (for a calculator):

tan Z = sin LHA ÷ (cos Lat × tan Dec − sin Lat × cos LHA)

Use these sign conventions:

  • Latitude is positive.
  • Declination is positive if it has the same name as latitude, and negative if contrary.
  • Z is measured from the elevated pole.
  • If the denominator is negative, Z = 180° − the arctan result.
  • Name Z towards W when LHA is less than 180°, and towards E when it is more.

Sight reduction tables and approved navigation software give the same answer. In the exam, show the working.

Practical points:

  • Choose a low body. A bearing taken with an azimuth circle on a high-altitude body is sensitive to any tilt of the instrument.
  • Avoid meridian passage at high altitude. Close to the meridian the bearing changes rapidly, so a timing error becomes a bearing error.
  • For Northern-hemisphere observers, the Nautical Almanac's Polaris tables include an azimuth for Polaris, which is a convenient night check.

Worked examples

Example 1: Amplitude of the setting sun (gyro and magnetic)

  • DR latitude 20°00'N, sun's declination 15°00'N.
  • sin Amp = sin 15° ÷ cos 20° = 0.2588 ÷ 0.9397 = 0.2754, so the amplitude is W16.0°N.
  • True bearing = 270° + 16.0° = 286.0°T.
  • Gyro bearing at the moment the centre was on the celestial horizon: 287.0°.
  • Gyro error = 286.0 − 287.0 = 1.0° West (gyro 1° high).
  • Standard magnetic compass bearing at the same time: 290.0°. Compass error = 286.0 − 290.0 = 4.0° West.
  • Variation from the chart, corrected for the year: 2° East.
  • Deviation = Error − Variation = (−4°) − (+2°) = 6° West, for the ship's heading at that time. Record the heading.

Example 2: Azimuth by ABC, afternoon sun

  • Latitude 30°00'N, declination 10°00'S, LHA 40° (sun west of the meridian).
  • A = tan 30° ÷ tan 40° = 0.5774 ÷ 0.8391 = 0.688 S. LHA is not between 90° and 270°, so A is named opposite to latitude.
  • B = tan 10° ÷ sin 40° = 0.1763 ÷ 0.6428 = 0.274 S. B takes the name of the declination.
  • C = 0.688 + 0.274 = 0.962 S. Same names, so add.
  • tan Az = 1 ÷ (0.962 × cos 30°) = 1 ÷ 0.833 = 1.200, so Az = S50.2°W. LHA is less than 180°, so it is named West.
  • True bearing = 180° + 50.2° = 230.2°T.
  • Gyro bearing observed: 229.5°. Gyro error = 230.2 − 229.5 = 0.7° East (gyro low).

The direct formula gives the same answer. The denominator is (0.866 × −0.176) − (0.5 × 0.766) = −0.536. tan Z = 0.643 ÷ −0.536, giving Z = 180° − 50.2° = N129.8°W, which is 230.2°T.

Common mistakes on the bridge and in the exam

  • Naming the error backwards. Write "True − Compass" every time until it is automatic.
  • Taking the amplitude on the visible horizon without the correction. The resulting error becomes noticeable in higher latitudes.
  • Wrong LHA. Common causes are adding West longitude, using a watch not set to UTC, or taking GHA from the wrong day. The azimuth is only as good as the time.
  • Misnaming A in the ABC method when LHA is between 90° and 270°, or forgetting that C takes the name of the larger value.
  • Applying variation to a gyro error. Variation and deviation belong to the magnetic compass only.
  • Quoting deviation without the heading. It is meaningless on its own.
  • Using an unsynchronised repeater. Compare the repeater with the master gyro before blaming the gyro.
  • Instrument problems. An azimuth circle that is not level, or a high body read through the mirror at a steep angle, will give a poor bearing.
  • Not acting on the result. If a gyro error is found, apply it on charts, radar and ECDIS. Inform the Master as your SMS requires, and record it in the compass error book or log.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Examiners at this level want short, correct answers and evidence that you actually do this at sea.

  • What is an amplitude? The true bearing of a body when its true altitude is zero, measured from the E or W point towards N or S.
  • Why not just take the bearing when the sun touches the horizon? Refraction and dip mean the centre is not then on the celestial horizon. Either observe when the lower limb is still above the horizon, or apply the visible-horizon correction from the tables.
  • Which is more accurate, azimuth or amplitude? Both are sound when done properly. The amplitude needs no time. The azimuth needs accurate time but can be taken at any convenient low altitude, and is preferred in high latitudes.
  • How often do you check compass error? At least once a watch and, where possible, after a major alteration of course, in line with STCW watchkeeping principles. Your company SMS may require more.
  • You find deviation has changed noticeably from the card. What do you do? Recheck it, confirm the heading and the variation, and inform the Master. Record the result, and consider whether a compass adjustment or a new deviation card is needed.
  • What else can you use to find gyro error? Transits, leading lines, a bearing of a charted object from a known position, and Polaris.
  • The gyro error has suddenly jumped. What could cause this? Possible causes include a gyro fault, power or alarm issues, a wrong speed or latitude input to the gyro correction, or a repeater out of step. Check the master compass against the magnetic compass and report.
Key points
  • Compass error = True bearing − Compass bearing: compass least, error East; compass best, error West.
  • Amplitude: sin Amp = sin Dec ÷ cos Lat, named E/W for rising/setting and N/S after the declination.
  • Take a sun amplitude when its centre is on the celestial horizon, with the lower limb still visibly above the horizon, or apply the visible-horizon correction.
  • An azimuth needs accurate UTC and LHA; ABC tables or the tan Z formula give the true bearing.
  • Deviation = Error − Variation, and it is valid only for the heading at the time of observation.
  • Check error at least once a watch and after major course changes, plus whatever your SMS requires.
  • Prefer low-altitude bodies, avoid high bodies near meridian passage, and use azimuths in high latitudes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between azimuth and amplitude?

An azimuth is the true bearing of a celestial body at any altitude, calculated from latitude, declination and LHA. An amplitude is a special case: the bearing when the body's true altitude is zero, measured from East or West. An amplitude needs no time or LHA, only latitude and declination.

When exactly should I take the bearing for a sun amplitude?

Take it when the sun's centre is on the celestial horizon. Because of refraction and dip, this happens while the lower limb is still a little above the visible horizon, commonly taught as about half a diameter. If you take it with the sun on the visible horizon, apply the visible-horizon correction from your nautical tables.

How do I know if gyro error is high or low?

Subtract the gyro bearing from the true bearing. If the gyro reads more than true, it is high and the error is West. If it reads less than true, it is low and the error is East.

How often must compass error be checked at sea?

The STCW Code watchkeeping principles expect a check at least once a watch and, when possible, after a major alteration of course. Many company SMS procedures add their own requirements, such as recording format or extra checks, so follow your ship's procedures.

Can I use a star or planet for an amplitude or azimuth?

Yes. Any body with a known declination works for an azimuth, and Polaris has its own azimuth table in the Nautical Almanac. Star and planet amplitudes are possible in principle, but faint bodies are hard to see right at the horizon. In practice the sun is the usual choice for amplitudes.

Test yourself

Which formula gives the amplitude of a body?

The amplitude follows from the spherical triangle with zero true altitude: sin Amp = sin Dec × sec Lat. This is a standard result in nautical tables such as Norie's and Bowditch.

True bearing by azimuth is 112.0° and the gyro bearing is 113.5°. What is the gyro error?

Error = True − Gyro = 112.0 − 113.5 = −1.5°. The compass reads more than true, so the error is West and the gyro is high.

For a sun amplitude, the correct moment for the bearing is when:

The amplitude formula assumes a true altitude of zero. Refraction and dip mean this occurs with the lower limb still above the visible horizon; otherwise the visible-horizon correction from the tables must be applied.

In the ABC method, LHA is 300° and C is named N. How is the azimuth named?

The azimuth takes C's name first (N or S). It is then named E when LHA exceeds 180°, because the body is east of the meridian, and W when LHA is less than 180°.

Compass error is 3° East and variation is 5° West. What is the deviation?

Deviation = Error − Variation = (+3°) − (−5°) = +8°, which is 8° East. It is valid for the ship's heading at the time of observation.
