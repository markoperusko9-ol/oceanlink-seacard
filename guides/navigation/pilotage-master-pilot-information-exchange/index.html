Pilotage: The Master-Pilot Information Exchange

Quick answer

The master-pilot information exchange (MPX) is the structured briefing, held as soon as the pilot reaches the bridge, where the ship hands over its manoeuvring data and condition and the pilot explains the intended passage, tugs, traffic and local rules. Both sides then agree on one plan. Having a pilot aboard does not relieve the master or the OOW of their responsibility, so the bridge team must keep monitoring and challenge anything that does not match the agreed plan.

Why the exchange matters and where it comes from

Many pilotage incidents start with a bridge team and a pilot who have two different plans in their heads. The MPX exists to merge them into one before the ship commits to the channel.

The main references you should be able to name in an oral exam:

  • IMO Resolution A.960(23), the recommendations on training, certification and operational procedures for maritime pilots. Its annex on operational procedures covers the duties of the master, the bridge officers and the pilot, and the information they should exchange.
  • STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2, the watchkeeping principles. With a pilot aboard, the master and OOW keep their duties. The OOW must work closely with the pilot and keep an accurate check on position and movement. If in doubt about the pilot's actions or intentions, the OOW should ask the pilot to clarify. If the doubt remains, the OOW calls the master and takes any necessary action before the master arrives.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34, on safe navigation and voyage planning. IMO Resolution A.893(21) supports it with guidelines saying the plan runs berth to berth, including the pilotage waters.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 23, on pilot transfer arrangements. It deals with getting the pilot aboard safely, not with the briefing itself.
  • IMO Resolution A.601(15), on manoeuvring information. It covers the pilot card, the wheelhouse poster and the manoeuvring booklet.

Industry guidance, such as the ICS Bridge Procedures Guide, gives model checklists. Your company SMS will normally have its own MPX form, and that is the one you use and sign.

What the ship gives the pilot

The pilot card is the core document. Fill it in before the pilot boards, using actual figures for this call rather than last port's numbers. Typical content:

  • Draughts: forward, aft and midship, plus air draught.
  • Dimensions: length overall, beam, displacement and deadweight.
  • Propulsion:
    • engine type, whether fixed or controllable pitch propeller, and direction of turn;
    • speed table for each telegraph position, loaded and ballast;
    • time from full ahead to full astern, and astern power as a proportion of ahead;
    • any limit on the number of consecutive starts.
  • Steering: rudder type, maximum angle, hard-over to hard-over time, and number of steering motors in use.
  • Thrusters: bow and stern thrusters with their power, plus any limits such as speed above which the thruster is ineffective.
  • Anchors: both anchors ready, number of shackles on each cable.
  • Mooring: winches, lines and fairleads.
  • Defects and peculiarities: anything that affects manoeuvring, for example:
    • a slow-starting engine;
    • a weak thruster;
    • a gyro error;
    • an inoperative radar;
    • a known tendency to sheer in shallow water.
  • Bridge equipment status: radars, ECDIS, VHF and the gyro error.

Also tell the pilot about the wheelhouse poster and the manoeuvring booklet, where carried. These give turning circles, stopping distances and squat information. Point out where the main controls, indicators and the telegraph are.

Be honest about defects. A pilot who discovers halfway up the river that the bow thruster trips at 70% load has been set up to fail.

What the pilot gives the ship

The pilot brings local knowledge that your passage plan can only partly cover:

  • The pilotage plan:
    • intended track and courses;
    • wheel-over points and speeds by leg;
    • critical areas and any no-turning-back point.
  • Tidal information: height and stream at critical points and the time window.
  • Under keel clearance: expected UKC and any local UKC rules.
  • Tugs: number and bollard pull, where they make fast, whether ship's or tug's line, and the expected manoeuvre at the berth.
  • Berthing: side to, mooring line plan, and any restrictions at the berth.
  • Traffic and VTS: expected traffic, any passing or overtaking arrangements, reporting points and VHF channels.
  • Local regulations: special signals or speed limits.
  • Weather: local forecast, especially wind effect on high-sided ships.
  • Contingencies:
    • where the ship can anchor or abort;
    • what happens if the engine or steering fails;
    • what happens if a tug is not available.

Many ports now send a pilotage passage plan or information sheet in advance through the agent or VTS. Use it to build your own plan before arrival. The on-board exchange then confirms the plan instead of starting from scratch.

Step-by-step: running the exchange

  1. Before the pilot boards:
    • complete the pilot card and the company MPX checklist;
    • plot the pilotage passage in ECDIS or on the paper chart, using any pre-arrival information from the port;
    • test steering and engines as required by the SMS;
    • have the anchors cleared away and ready.
  2. Boarding: an officer meets the pilot at the ladder and escorts them to the bridge. The boarding arrangement must comply with SOLAS V/23.
  3. On the bridge, first minute:
    • give the pilot the immediate state: course, speed, engine and steering mode, and nearby traffic;
    • the ship stays in a safe position while the briefing happens, so do not rush into the channel.
  4. Exchange documents: hand over the pilot card and take the pilot's plan.
  5. Compare plans: go through track, speeds, UKC, tugs and abort points against your own plan. Resolve every difference before reaching the critical leg.
  6. Agree roles:
    • who gives helm orders;
    • who handles VHF with VTS and tugs;
    • the working language, normally English using IMO Standard Marine Communication Phrases;
    • that the pilot will brief the bridge team on any local-language radio exchanges.
  7. Agree monitoring: the OOW reports position, cross-track error and deviations from the plan at agreed intervals and at each wheel-over point.
  8. Record: log the time the exchange was completed. Have the master and pilot sign the MPX form if the SMS requires it.
  9. Keep exchanging: the MPX continues as conditions change, for example a tug delayed, traffic altered or the berth changed.

Worked example: a UKC disagreement found during the MPX

The ship. A bulk carrier arrives at even keel, draught 12.5 m.

The channel. The shallowest charted depth is 13.0 m. Predicted height of tide at the time of passing is 2.4 m.

The company rule. The SMS requires a minimum dynamic UKC in channels of 10% of static draught, which is 1.25 m. This is an assumed figure for the example; your company and port figures will differ.

Squat. The ship's squat table gives an estimated 0.6 m at 8 knots and 1.1 m at 11 knots.

The pilot's proposal. The pilot plans 11 knots through the shallow section to keep steerage against a strong cross-set.

Item 8 kn 11 kn
Charted depth + tide 15.4 m 15.4 m
Static draught 12.5 m 12.5 m
Static UKC 2.9 m 2.9 m
Estimated squat 0.6 m 1.1 m
Dynamic UKC 2.3 m 1.8 m

Both speeds meet the 1.25 m minimum, so the pilot's proposal is acceptable on these figures. Raising it was still the right call, because:

  • Timing matters. If the ship is 40 minutes late and the tide has fallen by 0.6 m, dynamic UKC at 11 knots drops to 1.2 m. That is below the company minimum.

Outcome of the exchange:

  • the master and pilot agree a latest time for passing the shallow section;
  • 11 knots is accepted only within that window;
  • the OOW is tasked with monitoring the echo sounder and the time against the window;
  • the master and pilot agree to anchor at the designated outer anchorage if the window is missed.

That is exactly what a good MPX produces: one plan, numerical limits and an agreed abort option.

Common mistakes

  • No exchange because "the pilot knows the port". Local knowledge is not knowledge of your ship's handling or defects.
  • Starting the exchange in the channel. The ship is already committed before the plans are compared.
  • Pilot card errors:
    • out-of-date draughts;
    • ballast speeds given for a loaded ship;
    • missing air draught when there are bridges or power cables on the route.
  • Passage plan stops at the pilot station. The plan should be berth to berth, with the pilotage waters at least outlined.
  • Silent bridge team. The OOW stops fixing the position once the pilot has the con. Helm orders are not repeated back and checked against the rudder indicator.
  • Language gaps. Pilot and tugs talk in the local language and the bridge team never gets a summary.
  • Hiding defects to avoid delay or a port state control (PSC) visit.
  • Ignoring doubt. The OOW sees the ship setting towards a buoy but says nothing. STCW expects the OOW to seek clarification, then call the master and take whatever action is necessary before the master arrives.
  • Assuming the pilot has taken responsibility. Under most legal regimes the master keeps command and responsibility for the ship. The exact legal position of the pilot varies by country and port law.

Oral exam questions with model answers

Q: Does the master hand over responsibility to the pilot? No. The pilot advises and usually has the con, but the master keeps overall responsibility and can intervene. The OOW's watchkeeping duties also continue under STCW A-VIII/2.

Q: You are OOW and believe the pilot's helm order will put the ship outside the channel. What do you do? 1. Tell the pilot immediately and ask for clarification. 2. If the doubt continues, call the master. 3. Take whatever action is necessary for the safety of the ship before the master arrives. 4. Record events in the log.

Q: What three documents give manoeuvring information, and under which resolution? The pilot card, the wheelhouse poster and the manoeuvring booklet, under IMO Resolution A.601(15).

Q: When should the exchange take place? As early as possible, ideally with information exchanged before arrival. In any case it should happen on the bridge before the ship enters the critical part of the pilotage, with time to resolve differences.

Q: What would you include as contingencies? - Anchoring positions. - Abort or turning points. - Actions on engine failure, steering failure, blackout or loss of a tug. - Who does what in each case.

Key points
  • The master-pilot exchange merges the ship's plan and the pilot's plan into one agreed plan before the critical part of the pilotage.
  • Fill in the pilot card before boarding with this call's actual draughts, speeds, defects and equipment status.
  • The pilot provides the track, speeds, tides, UKC, tugs, berthing plan, traffic, VTS requirements and local rules.
  • Agree roles, working language, monitoring reports and abort or anchoring options, not just the courses.
  • A pilot aboard does not relieve the master or OOW; the OOW keeps fixing the position and challenges any doubt.
  • Resolve disagreements with numbers, such as a UKC and speed check against a time window.
  • Use the MPX form in your company SMS; exact requirements vary by company, port and flag.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for the ship when the pilot is on board?

The master remains in command and retains responsibility for the safe navigation of the ship. The pilot advises on navigation and usually has the con. The precise legal liability of pilots differs between countries and ports, so check local rules in the port information.

What is a pilot card and who fills it in?

It is a summary of the ship's particulars, manoeuvring characteristics, equipment status and defects, given to the pilot on boarding. The ship fills it in, normally the OOW under the master's supervision, before the pilot arrives. The recommended format is given in IMO Resolution A.601(15).

Does the passage plan need to cover pilotage waters?

Yes. IMO voyage planning guidance (Resolution A.893(21)) expects planning berth to berth, including areas where a pilot will be aboard. The pilot's local plan is then compared against yours during the exchange.

What should the OOW do if the pilot is speaking to VTS or tugs in a local language?

Ask the pilot to brief the bridge team on what was agreed, ideally arranged at the start of the exchange. The bridge team needs to know about traffic arrangements and tug instructions in order to monitor the passage properly.

Can the master refuse a pilot's plan?

The master can disagree, intervene and, where safety requires, decline to proceed with a plan they consider unsafe. They should discuss it with the pilot first. Where pilotage is compulsory, any dispute is normally handled with the pilot, VTS or port authority under local procedures, and should be fully logged.

Test yourself

With a pilot conducting the ship, what is the OOW's position under STCW watchkeeping principles?

The presence of a pilot does not relieve the master or OOW of their duties; they cooperate closely and monitor position (STCW Code, Section A-VIII/2).

Which IMO resolution covers the pilot card, wheelhouse poster and manoeuvring booklet?

A.601(15) deals with provision and display of manoeuvring information; A.960(23) covers pilot training and procedures, A.893(21) voyage planning and A.1045(27) pilot transfer arrangements.

In the worked example, what is the dynamic UKC at 11 knots (draught 12.5 m, charted depth 13.0 m, tide 2.4 m, squat 1.1 m)?

13.0 + 2.4 - 12.5 = 2.9 m static UKC; minus 1.1 m squat gives 1.8 m. The figure of 2.9 m ignores squat, and 2.3 m uses the 8-knot squat.

The OOW doubts a pilot's intended helm order. What is the correct first step?

STCW A-VIII/2 expects the OOW to seek clarification from the pilot first, then notify the master if doubt persists and act if needed before the master arrives.

When should the master-pilot information exchange ideally take place?

IMO Resolution A.960(23), Annex 2, says each pilotage assignment should begin with an information exchange between master and pilot, continuing throughout the pilotage. Holding it early, before the critical part of the passage, gives time to resolve differences before the ship is committed.
