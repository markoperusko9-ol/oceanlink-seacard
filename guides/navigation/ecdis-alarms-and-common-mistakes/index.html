ECDIS Alarms and Common Mistakes

Quick answer

ECDIS warns you only against the safety values you set and the official ENC data loaded, so a wrong safety contour, a poorly chosen look-ahead or a muted alarm can let the ship run into danger while the screen looks fine. This guide covers the alarms required by the IMO performance standards, how to set them for your draft and squat, and the mistakes that keep showing up in groundings, audits and oral exams.

How ECDIS alerts work

ECDIS does not know where the danger is. It compares own ship's position and planned route against the ENC data, using the safety settings entered by the navigator. If the settings are wrong, the alerts are wrong too.

The IMO performance standards (Resolution MSC.232(82)) list the events that must produce either an alarm or an indication. An alarm draws attention with sound and a visual signal. An indication is visual information only.

Newer equipment built to the Bridge Alert Management (BAM) standard sorts alerts by priority:

  • Emergency alarm: immediate danger to life or the ship, requiring immediate action (not normally generated by ECDIS itself).
  • Alarm: needs immediate attention and action.
  • Warning: not immediately dangerous, but may become so.
  • Caution: for awareness only.

Older units may label things differently, so learn the alert list for your own type. That is part of the type-specific familiarisation required before you keep a watch on it.

Acknowledging is not the same as resolving. Acknowledging silences the alert and tells the system the OOW has seen it. It does not remove the cause. A safety contour alarm you acknowledge and then forget is still telling you the ship is heading for water you decided was unsafe.

The alerts that matter on watch

These are the main events the performance standards cover. Your equipment may present them under different names or priorities.

Event What triggers it Typical OOW response
Crossing safety contour Own ship will cross the safety contour within the look-ahead time or area you set Check the chart, the position and the heading at once. Alter course or reduce speed if needed.
Area with special conditions / prohibited area Look-ahead reaches a boundary the navigator selected for alerting Identify the area (for example a TSS, anchorage, cable area or restricted zone) and comply.
Deviation from route Cross-track distance exceeds the limit set for the leg Bring the ship back or re-plan. Find out why: set, a helm error or an autopilot problem.
Approach to critical point Wheel-over or waypoint reached within the time or distance set Get ready for the turn and confirm the next leg is clear.
Positioning system failure Position input lost Fix the position by another method at once and check which mode the ECDIS has fallen back to.
Different geodetic datum Position input is not on the chart datum (WGS 84) Correct the sensor datum setting. Do not trust the plotted position until it is fixed.
ECDIS malfunction / system test failure Internal fault Go to the backup arrangement and inform the master.

Common indications include:

  • the chart is displayed beyond its compilation scale (overscale);
  • a larger-scale ENC is available for the area;
  • non-official data or raster charts (RCDS mode) are in use;
  • the safety contour has been left at its default.

If the navigator does not specify a safety contour, the standards require ECDIS to default to 30 m.

Setting safety values: step by step

Do this during passage planning. Write the values in the passage plan, and re-check them whenever the draft changes or you enter different waters.

  1. Start from the maximum draft for the passage. Use the deepest point of the ship, not the mean draft.
  2. Add squat for the planned speed and expected depth. Use the ship's squat data or pilot card.
  3. Add the minimum UKC required by your company SMS. Policies differ between companies, and port or pilotage authorities may set their own limits.
  4. Decide whether to take credit for the tide. A conservative approach ignores height of tide for the contour setting. Tidal allowance is then handled by a separate UKC calculation for critical areas.
  5. Set the safety depth. This is usually the figure from steps 1–3. Spot soundings shallower than this value are highlighted.
  6. Set the safety contour to the same or a slightly larger value. ECDIS will then pick the next deeper contour actually present in the ENC if your exact value is not in the data.
  7. Set the shallow and deep contours if you use the four-shade display.
  8. Set the look-ahead, as a time or as a vector/sector length and width depending on the make. Make it long enough to stop or turn, but not so long that it raises constant nuisance alarms in coastal waters.
  9. Select which areas give an alert (special conditions, prohibited areas).
  10. Set the cross-track limit for each leg to suit the sea room on that leg.
  11. Run the route check at the planning stage and deal with every flagged item. Do not just clear them.

Worked example

This example is for illustration only. Your company UKC policy and the ship's own squat data take precedence.

Ship data

  • Maximum draft: 10.6 m
  • Estimated squat at 14 knots (from the ship's data): 0.6 m
  • Company minimum UKC for this coastal passage: 1.5 m
  • No tidal credit taken

Safety depth

10.6 + 0.6 + 1.5 = 12.7 m. Enter 12.7 m as the safety depth. Any spot sounding of 12.7 m or less is now displayed in black (deeper soundings in grey), provided soundings are switched on.

Safety contour

Enter 12.7 m. The ENCs for the area contain only the 10 m, 15 m and 20 m contours. ECDIS therefore uses the 15 m contour as the safety contour, and the display shows this.

What that means in practice

Water between 12.7 m and 15 m is shaded as unsafe even though the ship could transit it. In open water this costs nothing. In a narrow channel whose charted depth is 13–14 m, the route check and route monitoring will raise alarms along the whole leg. The correct response is not to lower the safety contour to stop the noise. Instead:

  • confirm the leg with a proper UKC calculation, including tide;
  • record the decision in the passage plan;
  • brief the bridge team that alarms are expected on that leg and what to watch for.

Look-ahead

The master sets 6 minutes for this passage. At 14 knots that is 14 × 6 ÷ 60 = 1.4 nm. The master has judged this sufficient for this ship's turning and stopping characteristics in moderate traffic. Reduce it in confined pilotage waters, as briefed, to avoid constant false alarms.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving the default 30 m safety contour. On a deep-draft ship this can be too shallow. On a small coaster it floods the screen with alarms. Either way, nobody consciously chose it.
  • Lowering the safety contour to stop alarms. This is the classic route to a grounding. The alarm was telling you something.
  • Not realising ECDIS uses the next deeper contour. The officer believes the contour is at 12.7 m while the screen is actually using 15 m or 20 m. They then misjudge the room available.
  • Watching in Base display or with soundings off. Spot soundings are not in the standard display. You must switch them on.
  • Ignoring SCAMIN and overscale. Objects can disappear when zoomed out. Zooming in beyond the compilation scale gives false confidence in accuracy.
  • Ignoring the zone of confidence (CATZOC). Old or poor-quality survey data may not support a small UKC.
  • Running in RCDS (raster) mode and expecting alarms. Raster charts do not trigger automatic chart-based alarms. The navigator must add manual danger lines or areas.
  • Rubber-stamping the route check. Clearing every flag without reading it, or using a check corridor narrower than the ship's real track spread.
  • Unsuitable look-ahead. Too short gives no time to react. Too long causes alarm fatigue, which leads to muting.
  • Muting audible alarms. Turning the volume to zero, or acknowledging by habit without looking.
  • Trusting one position source. Not cross-checking GNSS with radar ranges, visual bearings or a second receiver. Not noticing a datum or spoofing problem.
  • Out-of-date data. Not applying ENC updates, letting chart permits expire, or not checking that cells cover the whole route. This matters under SOLAS V/27.
  • No type-specific familiarity. Not knowing where the alert list, settings and fallback modes are on this particular ship's unit.

When an alarm sounds: OOW response

  1. Look before you acknowledge. Read the alert text and identify the object, area or leg involved.
  2. Verify the position independently: radar range and bearing, a visual fix, or the parallel index.
  3. Assess the danger. How far away is it, how much time do you have, and is the ship's heading and speed taking you toward it?
  4. Act. Alter course, reduce speed or take manual steering as required. Do not wait to finish the analysis if the danger is close.
  5. Acknowledge once you have the situation in hand.
  6. Inform the master whenever the standing or bridge orders require it, and always if in any doubt.
  7. Record the event and the action taken. Report any equipment fault through the SMS.

If the position source fails, switch to the backup position. Know whether your ECDIS has fallen back to dead reckoning, and treat the plotted position as an estimate until a fix confirms it.

Oral exam style questions

Q: What is the difference between safety depth and safety contour? Safety depth highlights individual spot soundings at or shallower than the set value. Safety contour is a line that divides safe from unsafe water. It drives the anti-grounding alarm and the route check.

Q: You set a safety contour of 12 m but the screen shows 15 m. Why? The ENC has no 12 m contour. ECDIS selects the next deeper contour available in the data.

Q: What happens if no safety contour is entered? The performance standards require a default of 30 m.

Q: Will ECDIS alarm for a shoal when displaying a raster chart? No. In RCDS mode there are no automatic chart-data alarms, so the navigator must add manual danger markings and keep up traditional monitoring.

Q: Your look-ahead is causing continuous alarms in a pilotage area. What do you do? Adjust it within the limits set by the master's orders, brief the team, and record the change. Do not mute alarms or change the safety contour to silence them.

Q: Name four alarms required by the ECDIS performance standards. Any four of: crossing safety contour, deviation from route, positioning system failure, approach to critical point, different geodetic datum, and ECDIS malfunction.

Key points
  • ECDIS only warns against the safety values you set and the official ENC data loaded.
  • Safety depth highlights soundings; safety contour drives the anti-grounding alarm and route check.
  • If your safety contour value is not in the ENC, ECDIS uses the next deeper contour; if none is set, it defaults to 30 m.
  • Never lower the safety contour or mute alarms to stop nuisance alerts; manage the leg through UKC calculation and briefing.
  • Raster (RCDS) mode gives no automatic chart-based alarms, so add manual dangers.
  • Acknowledging an alarm does not remove its cause: verify the position, assess, act, then acknowledge.
  • Look-ahead must give time to react without causing alarm fatigue; set it to the master's orders.

Frequently asked questions

What is the default safety contour in ECDIS?

If the navigator does not enter a value, the IMO performance standards require ECDIS to default to 30 m, and an indication should show that the default is in use. That figure suits no ship in particular, so always set a value based on your draft, squat and company UKC policy.

Why does my ECDIS show a different safety contour from the one I entered?

ENCs contain only certain depth contours. If your value is not one of them, ECDIS uses the next deeper contour available in the data. Check what the display is actually using, especially in shallow or restricted waters.

How long should the ECDIS look-ahead be?

There is no single figure. It depends on speed, ship handling characteristics, sea room and traffic, and is usually set by the master's standing orders or the company SMS. It must give enough time to take action without producing constant false alarms.

Does ECDIS give anti-grounding alarms on raster charts?

No. When operating in RCDS mode with raster navigational charts, ECDIS does not generate automatic alarms from chart data. The navigator must draw manual danger lines or areas and rely more heavily on traditional monitoring such as parallel indexing and visual fixes.

Is it acceptable to silence ECDIS alarms during pilotage?

Audible alarms should not be disabled to reduce noise. Look-ahead and cross-track limits may be adjusted for confined waters if the master's orders allow, with the change briefed and recorded. Whether any alarm configuration may be changed, and by whom, is set by your company SMS.

Test yourself

A navigator enters a safety contour of 12 m, but the ENC contains only 10 m, 15 m and 20 m contours. Which contour will ECDIS use?

Where the chosen value is not in the data, ECDIS uses the next deeper available contour. Ref: IMO Res. MSC.232(82), ECDIS performance standards.

What does the safety depth setting do on the ECDIS display?

Safety depth only highlights soundings. The anti-grounding alarm and route check work on the safety contour. Ref: IMO Res. MSC.232(82); IHO S-52.

The ECDIS is operating in RCDS mode on raster charts. How will it alert you to a charted shoal ahead?

Raster charts contain no machine-readable depth data, so no automatic chart-based alarms are generated. Ref: IMO Res. MSC.232(82), RCDS mode provisions.

Constant safety contour alarms occur on a leg through a dredged channel. What is the correct action?

Changing safety values or muting alarms to stop noise removes protection. The leg should be justified by calculation and briefing. Ref: MSC.1/Circ.1503 (as revised), ECDIS guidance for good practice.

The ECDIS displays a 'different geodetic datum' alarm. What does it mean?

ECDIS requires position input on WGS 84 and must alarm if the sensor is outputting a different datum. Ref: IMO Res. MSC.232(82), alarms and indications.
