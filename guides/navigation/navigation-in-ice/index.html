Navigation in Ice: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Navigating in ice means knowing your ship's ice capability, reading ice information correctly, assessing risk before entering ice, and handling the ship slowly and deliberately once you are in it. In polar waters, the Polar Code, the ship's Polar Waters Operational Manual (PWOM) and a risk method such as POLARIS govern what you may do. In all waters, SOLAS requires you to report dangerous ice, and at night to slow to moderate speed or keep well clear of reported ice.

Why ice is different: the principles

Ice does not obey the habits you learn in open water. Four principles sit underneath everything else:

  • Know your ship. Ice class, hull strength, propeller and rudder protection, sea chest arrangement and engine power decide where you can go. A ship that is not ice-strengthened has very little margin, even in light ice.
  • Avoid where you can. The best ice passage is often the one that goes round the ice. Thin-looking ice can hide old, hard floes. Avoidance is always the first option.
  • Speed kills hulls. Hull damage depends heavily on the speed at which you hit ice. Go slowly, especially at night, in poor visibility and when you cannot read the ice ahead.
  • Keep moving, but under control. A ship stopped in pressured ice can become beset and drift with the pack. Keep steady, moderate headway and follow leads rather than forcing through.

Ice also affects the engine room. Slush and brash can choke sea chests and cause overheating, so engineers need advance warning before you enter ice. Ice accretion on deck in freezing spray raises the centre of gravity and reduces GM, which turns a navigation problem into a stability problem.

Reading ice: terminology you must know

Ice charts and ice reports use the WMO sea-ice nomenclature. Examiners expect you to know the main terms and approximate thicknesses.

Concentration is given in tenths of the sea surface covered:

Term Concentration
Open water less than 1/10
Very open drift ice 1/10 to 3/10
Open drift ice 4/10 to 6/10
Close pack ice 7/10 to 8/10
Very close pack ice 9/10 to less than 10/10
Compact or consolidated ice 10/10

Stage of development (main sea-ice types):

  • New ice (frazil, grease, slush, shuga), then nilas, under 10 cm thick
  • Young ice (grey and grey-white), roughly 10 to 30 cm
  • First-year ice: thin (30 to 70 cm), medium (70 to 120 cm), thick (over 120 cm)
  • Old ice: second-year and multi-year ice, which has survived at least one summer melt and is much harder

Glacial ice comes from land: icebergs, bergy bits and growlers. Bergy bits and growlers are small, sit low in the water and are poor radar targets, especially in sea clutter or swell. They are often the real hazard, because the large berg nearby is easy to see while its broken fragments are not.

On ice charts, information is usually coded in the egg code, which gives total concentration, partial concentrations, stage of development and floe size. Practise decoding it before your exam. Under questioning, many candidates confuse stage of development with floe size.

The rules: SOLAS, the Polar Code and POLARIS

SOLAS Chapter V applies in all waters:

  • When the master meets dangerous ice, they must report it to ships nearby and to the competent authorities. The report gives the type of ice, its position, and the time and date (UTC) of observation.
  • Air temperatures below freezing with gale-force winds that cause severe ice accretion must also be reported. That report includes air and sea temperature and wind force and direction.
  • When ice is reported on or near the ship's track, at night the master must proceed at moderate speed or alter course to go well clear of the danger zone.
  • SOLAS Chapter V also provides for the International Ice Patrol service in the North Atlantic.

The Polar Code is made mandatory through SOLAS Chapter XIV and MARPOL. It applies in the defined Arctic and Antarctic polar waters. Key items for the bridge:

  • Polar Ship Certificate, which states the ship's category and operational limitations.
  • Category A ships are designed for at least medium first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions. Category B ships are designed for at least thin first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions. Category C ships are designed for open water or ice less severe than A and B.
  • Polar Waters Operational Manual (PWOM), which contains ship-specific procedures, limits and the method used to assess operational capability in ice.
  • Voyage planning must take account of ice information, routeing, areas of special significance, and the limits of SAR capability and charting.

POLARIS (Polar Operational Limit Assessment Risk Indexing System) is the IMO-accepted method, set out in MSC.1/Circ.1519, for assessing operational limits in ice. It uses Risk Index Values (RIVs) for each ice type, which vary with the ship's ice class. Your PWOM tells you whether your ship uses POLARIS or another accepted method.

Training under STCW Regulation V/4 depends on ship type and ice conditions:

  • Basic training is for masters, chief mates and OOWs.
  • Advanced training is for masters and chief mates.

For example, on tankers and passenger ships in open waters, masters, chief mates and OOWs need basic training. In other (more severe) ice, masters and chief mates need advanced training and OOWs need basic training. Your administration decides how certificates are issued.

Outside polar waters, local rules apply. Examples include the Baltic, the Gulf of St Lawrence and northern Chinese ports in winter. Coastal states may set ice-class and deadweight restrictions for icebreaker assistance. Check the coastal state's current notices. Do not rely on last season's figures.

Worked example: a POLARIS-style risk calculation

POLARIS produces a Risk Index Outcome (RIO). To get it, multiply the concentration of each ice type (in tenths) by that ice type's RIV for your ice class, then add the results:

RIO = (C1 × RIV1) + (C2 × RIV2) + …

The concentrations must total 10/10, and open water counts as a type with its own RIV.

Scenario. Your ship is approaching an area that the latest ice chart describes as:

  • 3/10 open water
  • 5/10 grey-white ice
  • 2/10 thin first-year ice (2nd stage)

Assume the RIVs for your ship's class are 3 for open water, 1 for grey-white and −1 for thin first-year (2nd stage). These numbers are illustrative only. Always take the real values from the RIV table in MSC.1/Circ.1519 for your actual ice class. In the real table, thin first-year ice is split into 1st and 2nd stage, each with its own RIV.

Ice type Concentration RIV Product
Open water 3 3 9
Grey-white 5 1 5
Thin first-year (2nd stage) 2 −1 −2
Total 10 RIO = 12

A RIO of zero or above means normal operation.

Now suppose the chart is updated. The new picture is:

  • 1/10 open water
  • 3/10 grey-white ice
  • 6/10 thin first-year ice (2nd stage)

(1 × 3) + (3 × 1) + (6 × −1) = 3 + 3 − 6 = 0

This RIO is still zero, so you are at the boundary. One more tenth of thin first-year ice (2nd stage) in place of grey-white would make it negative.

What a negative RIO means depends on the ice class:

  • Polar Class ships: a RIO between 0 and −10 means elevated operational risk, with additional measures such as reduced speed. A RIO below −10 means the operation is subject to special consideration.
  • Ships below PC7 and non-ice-strengthened ships: any negative RIO means special consideration.

Your PWOM sets out what "special consideration" means for your ship. Normally it means not proceeding independently: you reroute or wait. Icebreaker-escorted operation is assessed separately, using the ice conditions in the escort channel.

The lesson: the calculation is simple. The judgement lies in getting honest ice information and recalculating as conditions change.

Step by step: approaching, entering and transiting ice

Before reaching the ice

  1. Obtain the latest ice charts, ice service bulletins and NAVTEX or SafetyNET ice warnings. Note their issue times, because ice moves.
  2. Run the PWOM risk assessment (for example POLARIS) for the planned route and for alternative routes.
  3. Brief the engine room. Change to the ice or high sea chest where fitted, arrange recirculation or heating if available, and confirm power availability.
  4. Ballast so the propeller and rudder are well immersed and the bow is not trimmed by the head into ice, within stability and strength limits.
  5. Prepare searchlights, double the look-out, set radars for small-target detection, and test the whistle and steering, including emergency steering.
  6. Check ice accretion procedures and equipment, such as mallets and de-icing gear.

Entering the ice

  • Choose the entry point where concentration is lowest and the ice is weakest. Leads and areas of water sky (dark patches on low cloud above open water) can help.
  • Approach slowly and enter as near perpendicular to the ice edge as possible, so that the shoulders do not take a glancing blow.
  • Once in, build up to a moderate, controlled speed. Do not go faster than lets you stop or turn away from a hard floe you see late.

In the ice

  • Follow leads and pass between floes rather than into them. Small, frequent helm movements beat sharp or hard-over turns, because turning hard in ice can jam the ship and leave you beset.
  • Avoid going astern. The rudder and propeller are the most vulnerable parts. If you must go astern, use the rudder amidships and gentle power, and keep watch aft.
  • If the ship becomes beset, avoid prolonged full-power thrashing, which wastes fuel and risks machinery damage. Work the ship ahead and astern carefully, with ballast shifts if your procedures allow. If that fails, request icebreaker assistance.
  • Log ice conditions regularly and report dangerous ice as SOLAS requires.

Under icebreaker escort

  • Follow the icebreaker's instructions exactly. Keep the ordered distance and speed, and maintain a continuous VHF watch.
  • Be ready to stop instantly. Icebreakers can stop suddenly in heavy ice, and running into the icebreaker's stern is a classic accident.
  • Know the icebreaker signals in the International Code of Signals, and the local procedures in the coastal state's notices.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting radar for small ice. Growlers can disappear in clutter. Speed must allow for what you cannot see, as COLREG Rule 6 already requires.
  • Using an old ice chart. Ice drifts with wind and current. A chart from a day or two ago may not reflect the ice in front of you.
  • Treating all ice of one thickness as equal. Old ice inclusions in first-year ice are much harder than the surrounding ice.
  • Charging the ice edge. High-speed entry is the classic cause of bow and shoulder damage.
  • Forgetting the engine room. Blocked sea chests can lead to high jacket water temperatures and a blackout, at the worst possible moment.
  • Ignoring ice accretion. Topside ice reduces GM and can block vents, fire hydrants and lifesaving appliances.
  • Following too closely. Keep the ordered distance behind an icebreaker or another ship in convoy.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: You receive a report of icebergs 30 miles ahead on your track at night. What do you do? SOLAS requires moderate speed at night or an alteration to pass well clear of the danger zone. I would inform the master, reduce speed, double the look-out and set the radars for small targets. I would plot the reported position with an allowance for drift and, if practicable, alter course to keep well clear.

Q: What goes into an ice report? The type of ice, its position, and the UTC time and date of observation. For severe ice accretion, the report includes air and sea temperature and wind force and direction.

Q: Why are growlers dangerous? They are small, dense, low in the water and give poor radar returns, especially in swell or sea clutter. They are hard enough to damage a hull.

Q: How would you enter pack ice? Slowly, at the point of lowest concentration, as close to perpendicular to the edge as possible. Beforehand, I would brief the engine room and ballast to immerse the propeller.

Q: What is the PWOM? The Polar Waters Operational Manual required by the Polar Code. It holds the ship's capabilities, limits and procedures for operating in polar waters, including the method used to assess operational limits in ice.

Q: What is POLARIS? The IMO-accepted method in MSC.1/Circ.1519. It combines ice concentrations with ice-class-specific Risk Index Values to give a Risk Index Outcome. The outcome tells you whether operation is normal, elevated risk or subject to special consideration.

Key points
  • Avoidance is the first option: going round ice is usually better than forcing through it.
  • Ice concentration is given in tenths; know the WMO terms and the thickness of each stage of development.
  • SOLAS requires you to report dangerous ice, and to use moderate speed or alter well clear when ice is reported on your track at night.
  • In polar waters, the Polar Ship Certificate, the PWOM and a method such as POLARIS govern your operating limits.
  • Enter ice slowly, perpendicular to the edge, at the point of lowest concentration, and avoid going astern.
  • Growlers and bergy bits are poor radar targets, so speed must allow for ice you cannot see.
  • Brief the engine room before entering ice, and plan for sea chest blockage and topside ice accretion.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Polar Code apply to my ship if we only trade in the Baltic in winter?

No. The Polar Code applies in the defined Arctic and Antarctic polar waters, not in the Baltic. Baltic winter navigation is governed by the coastal states' own rules, such as ice-class and size requirements for icebreaker assistance. These rules are published in the coastal states' current notices and can change each season.

What is the difference between Polar Code Category A, B and C ships?

Category A ships are designed for at least medium first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions. Category B ships are designed for at least thin first-year ice, which may include old ice inclusions. Category C ships are designed for open water or ice less severe than A and B. The category is stated on the Polar Ship Certificate.

Do I need Polar Code training as a deck officer?

STCW Regulation V/4 requires basic training for masters, chief mates and OOWs, and advanced training for masters and chief mates, on certain ships in polar waters, depending on ship type and ice conditions. How the requirement is applied, and which courses are approved, is decided by your administration. Check with them and your company before joining a polar voyage.

Why should I avoid going astern in ice?

The propeller and rudder are the most vulnerable parts of the ship in ice. Going astern can drive ice into them and cause damage. If you must go astern, use the rudder amidships and modest power, and keep a watch aft.

Can I rely on radar to detect icebergs and growlers?

Radar usually detects large icebergs, but growlers and bergy bits sit low in the water and can be lost in sea clutter or swell. Back up the radar with a proper visual look-out and searchlights at night. Keep to a speed that allows for ice detected late.
