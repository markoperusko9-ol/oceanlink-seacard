CPA and TCPA explained

Quick answer

CPA (closest point of approach) is the minimum distance a target will come to your ship if both vessels hold course and speed. TCPA is the time remaining until that point. Both come from the target's relative motion, which you find from at least two timed radar observations, ARPA tracking or AIS. On their own they do not tell you who must give way.

What CPA and TCPA actually measure

CPA is the shortest distance between your ship and a target if neither alters course or speed. TCPA is the time from now until that closest approach.

Both values come from relative motion. This is the target's movement as seen from your ship, treating your own ship as fixed at the centre of the display. On a relative-motion radar picture the echo moves along a straight line, the relative track. CPA is the perpendicular distance from own ship to that line, extended ahead. TCPA is the distance along the line to that perpendicular point, divided by relative speed.

Three points examiners like to hear you state clearly:

  • CPA and TCPA are predictions. They assume both ships hold course and speed. Any alteration by either ship makes the old figures obsolete.
  • Because they come from relative motion, they do not depend on own ship's speed input or on the stabilisation mode. Errors in log speed or set and drift corrupt the target's true vector and aspect. They do not change the CPA itself.
  • CPA and TCPA tell you whether there is a close-quarters situation and how soon it will develop. They do not tell you who gives way. For that you need the target's true course, its aspect and the steering and sailing rules.

Where CPA fits in the COLREGs

CPA and TCPA have no definition in the COLREGs. They are the practical tools for meeting several rules:

  • Rule 7(b) requires proper use of radar, including long-range scanning and radar plotting or equivalent systematic observation of detected objects.
  • Rule 7(c) warns against assumptions based on scanty information, especially scanty radar information. One observation, or a CPA read off the screen seconds after acquisition, is scanty.
  • Rule 7(d) covers bearings. If the compass bearing does not change appreciably, risk of collision is deemed to exist. Risk may still exist when the bearing is changing, especially with a very large vessel, a tow or a vessel at close range. A steady bearing means the CPA is close to zero.
  • Rule 8(d) requires that avoiding action results in passing at a safe distance. Its effectiveness must be checked carefully until the other vessel is finally past and clear. In practice you check this by watching the new CPA develop.
  • Rule 19(d) applies in restricted visibility to a vessel detected by radar alone. You must determine whether a close-quarters situation is developing and/or risk of collision exists. That is a CPA and TCPA judgement.

The convention sets no minimum CPA. Your passing distance comes from the Master's standing orders, the company SMS and the circumstances: sea room, traffic density, visibility, size and type of the target, and weather. Do not quote a fixed figure as a rule in an oral exam. Say what your standing orders required and why.

Step-by-step: finding CPA and TCPA by manual plot

This is the plot taught for the radar and ARPA course and expected at the CoC oral. It works on a plotting sheet, a reflection plotter or the EBL/VRM.

  1. Plot the first position (O). Note the target's bearing, range and the time.
  2. Plot later positions at fixed intervals. Six-minute intervals make the arithmetic easy, because 6 minutes is one tenth of an hour. Use at least three plots to confirm the target is steady. The last plot is A.
  3. Draw the relative track from O through A and extend it past the centre of the plot.
  4. CPA: drop a perpendicular from own ship (the centre) to the extended line. Its length is the CPA. Its direction gives the bearing at CPA.
  5. Relative speed: take the distance O–A and divide it by the time between the plots.
  6. TCPA: take the distance from A to the CPA point and divide it by relative speed. Add the result to the time of A.
  7. True course and speed of target (needed for the rules): from O, lay off own ship's course reversed, for own ship's speed over the plotting interval. This gives point W. The vector W–A is the target's true course and distance run.

Using the EBL and VRM on the radar gives the same answer. Set the EBL offset (or parallel index line) along the relative track. The VRM touching it gives the CPA.

Worked example

Own ship: course 000° (T), speed 12 kn, open sea, good visibility.

Time Bearing (T) Range
1200 037° 10.0 nm
1212 045° 5.7 nm

Relative track. The two positions are about 4.4 nm apart, and the line between them runs about 207° (SSW). Relative speed is 4.4 nm in 12 minutes, which is about 22 kn.

CPA. Extend the line past own ship and drop a perpendicular from the centre. CPA is about 1.8 nm, bearing about 117°. That is just abaft the starboard beam.

TCPA. From the 1212 position to the CPA point is about 5.4 nm along the line. 5.4 ÷ 22 kn ≈ 0.25 h ≈ 15 minutes, so the CPA occurs at about 1227.

True motion of target. From the 1200 position, lay off own ship's course reversed (180°) for 2.4 nm, which is 12 kn for 12 minutes. Join that point to the 1212 position. The target's true course is about 232° and its speed about 12.6 kn.

What it means. The bearing is drawing aft, from 037° to 045°. Extended further, the relative track crosses astern of own ship, roughly 4 nm astern. The target is a power-driven vessel on the starboard bow, crossing. The target passes astern, so own ship would cross ahead of it.

The question is whether a 1.8 nm CPA just abaft the starboard beam is acceptable. First judge whether risk of collision exists (Rule 7).

  • If 1.8 nm meets the standing orders and the circumstances, and continued plotting confirms it, there may be no risk of collision and you keep plotting.
  • If risk of collision is deemed to exist, Rule 15 makes own ship the give-way vessel, which should avoid crossing ahead. Take early and substantial action under Rule 16, typically a bold alteration to starboard to pass astern of the target. Then confirm the new CPA once the track has settled.

Notice what a single bearing would have shown: a target on the starboard bow at 10 nm. Only the second timed observation revealed the relative track and the CPA.

ARPA and AIS CPA: strengths and traps

ARPA / target tracking does the plot continuously. IMO performance standards allow a settling period after acquisition. A trend is expected within about a minute, and full prediction accuracy within about three minutes of steady tracking. Until then, treat the vector and CPA as indicative only.

The settling period starts again after any manoeuvre by either ship. A turning target, or own ship turning, produces lag and misleading vectors.

Good practice:

  • Set CPA/TCPA alarm limits to suit the area, not left at whatever the last watch chose.
  • Use the trial manoeuvre to test a planned alteration before executing it.
  • Remember that lost targets and target swap in clutter or close quarters can silently corrupt the data.

AIS-derived CPA uses the target's broadcast GNSS position, COG and SOG. It is useful for identification and early warning, but:

  • Not every vessel carries AIS, and AIS can be switched off.
  • The data may be wrong: a GNSS fault, wrong COG/SOG, or incorrect static data such as antenna position.
  • Reporting intervals and latency vary.

Treat AIS as supplementary. Verify the CPA with radar tracking or plotting before basing collision-avoidance action on it.

Stabilisation. As stated above, CPA and TCPA do not depend on whether true vectors are sea- or ground-stabilised. True vectors do. Use sea-stabilised (through-the-water) vectors to judge aspect and the rules when there is a current. Ground-stabilised vectors show the target's ground track. In a strong tide or current that can give a false aspect and a wrong reading of the encounter.

Common mistakes

  • Taking CPA from one observation or immediately after acquisition. This is scanty information under Rule 7(c).
  • Ignoring TCPA. A 1 nm CPA in 40 minutes and a 1 nm CPA in 4 minutes are very different situations.
  • Trusting a slowly changing bearing at close range. With large ships or tows, the bearing can change while the hull still closes dangerously.
  • Using AIS CPA alone, especially for small craft or vessels without AIS.
  • Not re-checking after an alteration. Rule 8(d) requires checking until finally past and clear. Own ship's turn also upsets ARPA for a period.
  • Using ground-stabilised vectors to decide the encounter type in a strong current.
  • Small successive alterations. Each alteration only nudges the CPA, and the other vessel may never notice it. Rule 8(b) asks for alterations large enough to be readily apparent.
  • Ignoring where the CPA occurs. CPA gives the minimum distance, not whether the target passes ahead or astern of you or on which side. A 2 nm CPA with the target crossing ahead is very different from one with it passing astern. Check the bearing at CPA, and BCR/BCT (bow crossing range and time) where your ARPA shows them.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Practise answering these out loud, briefly and in your own words:

  • Define CPA and TCPA. On what assumption are they based? Both vessels maintaining course and speed.
  • Draw the plot for a target given two bearings and ranges. Find CPA, TCPA, true course and speed and aspect. Expect to do this on paper in front of the examiner.
  • Does an error in own ship's log speed affect the CPA? No, CPA comes from relative motion. It does affect the target's true course, speed and aspect.
  • Why might a bearing be changing yet risk of collision still exist? Rule 7(d)(ii): a large vessel, a tow or close range.
  • In fog you detect a target by radar alone, fine on the port bow, CPA 0.5 nm, TCPA 15 minutes. What do you do? Discuss Rule 19(d): avoid altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam (other than one being overtaken). Cover the alteration to starboard and/or speed reduction, informing the Master, and the sound signals.
  • How long after acquisition do you trust an ARPA vector? What happens when either vessel manoeuvres?
  • What CPA do you keep in open sea and in a TSS? Explain the standing orders and the factors, not a single number.
Key points
  • CPA is the closest distance a target will come if both ships hold course and speed. TCPA is the time until then.
  • Both are derived from relative motion, so log speed errors and stabilisation mode do not change them. They do change true vectors and aspect.
  • A single observation is scanty information. Plot at least two, preferably three, timed positions or let ARPA settle.
  • A steady compass bearing means CPA near zero, but risk can exist even with a changing bearing at close range or with large vessels.
  • CPA and TCPA show how close and how soon. The steering and sailing rules decide who gives way.
  • AIS CPA is supplementary. Verify it with radar before acting.
  • Minimum passing distance is set by the Master's standing orders and the circumstances, not by the COLREGs.

Frequently asked questions

What is a safe CPA at sea?

The COLREGs give no figure. Rule 8(d) only requires passing at a safe distance. Masters set minimum CPAs in their standing orders, often different for open sea, coastal waters and restricted visibility, and company SMS may set its own. Always follow your ship's standing orders and widen the margin when conditions are poor.

Why does ARPA show a negative TCPA?

On many displays a negative TCPA means the closest point of approach has already passed and the target is opening. Check your equipment's manual, as presentation varies between manufacturers.

Can I rely on the CPA shown by AIS?

Not on its own. AIS CPA depends on the other vessel's broadcast GNSS data, which can be wrong, delayed or absent. Use it for early warning and identification, but confirm with radar plotting or ARPA tracking before taking avoiding action.

How many radar plots do I need to find CPA?

Two timed positions define a relative track. At least three let you confirm that the target is not altering and that the track is steady. Plotting at regular intervals, such as every six minutes, keeps the speed and time arithmetic simple.

Does current or tide affect CPA and TCPA?

When both ships are in the same body of water, the current affects them equally, so the relative motion and the CPA are unchanged. What changes is the true vector you see. Ground-stabilised vectors show ground tracks, which can give a misleading aspect, so use sea-stabilised vectors for applying the rules.

Test yourself

Your speed log over-reads by 2 knots. What effect does this have on the ARPA-derived CPA of a target?

CPA comes from the target's observed relative motion. Own speed input only affects the conversion to true vectors (radar plotting principles; COLREG Rule 7(b) systematic observation).

A large container ship's bearing is changing slowly at 2 nm range. Which statement is correct?

COLREG Rule 7(d)(ii) states that risk may exist even with an appreciable bearing change, particularly with a very large vessel, a tow or at close range.

In a strong tidal stream, which ARPA vector mode should you use to judge the target's aspect for applying the steering rules?

Sea-stabilised vectors represent course through the water and so give the correct aspect. Ground-stabilised vectors include set and drift and can mislead (radar and ARPA training principles; COLREG Rule 7).

You acquire a target on ARPA and it immediately shows CPA 3 nm. What is the correct approach?

IMO radar performance standards allow time after acquisition for tracking to reach accuracy. Acting on early data risks breaching COLREG Rule 7(c) on scanty information.

After altering course to starboard for a give-way situation, what must you do?

COLREG Rule 8(d) requires the effectiveness of avoiding action to be carefully checked until the other vessel is finally past and clear.
