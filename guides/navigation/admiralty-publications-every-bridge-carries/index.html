Admiralty publications every bridge carries

Quick answer

SOLAS Chapter V requires every ship to carry nautical charts and publications (V/19.2.1.4), and Regulation 27 requires them to be adequate and up to date for the intended voyage. The flag state decides which publications are acceptable. On most deep-sea ships the core set is the Admiralty (UKHO) Sailing Directions, List of Lights and Fog Signals, List of Radio Signals, Tide Tables, Notices to Mariners, The Mariner's Handbook and Ocean Passages for the World, in paper or approved digital form. You need to know what each one is for, how to keep it corrected, and how to use it when the chart alone is not enough.

Why the bridge carries them: the legal basis

The requirement comes from SOLAS Chapter V:

  • Regulation 2 defines nautical charts and publications as material issued officially by, or on the authority of, a government, an authorised hydrographic office or another relevant government institution.
  • Regulation 19.2.1.4 requires ships to carry nautical charts and nautical publications to plan and display the route for the intended voyage, and to plot and monitor positions throughout it. An ECDIS may be accepted as meeting the chart element.
  • Regulation 27 requires that the charts and publications needed for the intended voyage are adequate and up to date.

Three practical consequences follow:

  • Admiralty is not mandatory as a brand. Publications from other national hydrographic offices can be acceptable. The flag administration decides what it accepts, and your company SMS will usually name the standard outfit.
  • "Adequate" depends on the voyage. A ship on a fixed Gulf–India trade needs different volumes from a tramp bulker fixed for South America. Ordering the right volumes is part of the appraisal stage of passage planning.
  • "Up to date" means latest edition plus all corrections. An old edition, or a current edition with missing corrections, is a deficiency. Port State Control checks this routinely.

Two items usually kept with them are IMO publications, not Admiralty: the International Code of Signals and IAMSAR Manual Volume III. SOLAS V/21 covers both. Candidates often lump them together, and examiners notice.

List of Radio Signals volumes you will use most:

  • Vol 1 (NP 281): maritime radio stations, including coast stations and medical advice services.
  • Vol 2 (NP 282): radio aids to navigation, satellite navigation systems, legal time and time signals.
  • Vol 3 (NP 283): maritime safety information services, including NAVTEX and weather broadcasts.
  • Vol 5 (NP 285): GMDSS.
  • Vol 6 (NP 286): pilot services, VTS and port operations. This is the volume you need for reporting points and VHF channels on arrival.

The flag state, company and trade decide the exact outfit. Treat the table as the typical core set, not a statutory list.

Keeping them corrected: step-by-step

Correction is normally the 2nd Officer's job, but every watchkeeper should be able to do it and check it.

  1. Confirm the edition. Check the Cumulative List, the catalogue or the UKHO website for the current edition of each volume. Order new editions in good time and remove superseded ones from the bridge.
  2. Work through each weekly Notice to Mariners in sequence. The weekly edition is divided into sections:
    • Section I: explanatory notes and indexes.
    • Section II: chart corrections.
    • Section III: reprints of radio navigational warnings.
    • Section IV: amendments to Sailing Directions.
    • Section V: amendments to the List of Lights and Fog Signals.
    • Section VI: amendments to the List of Radio Signals.
  3. Check the "previous update" reference. Each chart correction quotes the previous notice affecting that chart. If it does not match the last entry on your chart, you have missed a notice. Find it before going further.
  4. Amend the publication. Paste in the cut-out or make the pen amendment as instructed.
  5. Record the correction. Enter the notice number on the publication's record-of-amendments page and in the ship's chart and publication correction log, in whatever form the SMS requires.
  6. Track T&P notices separately. Temporary and Preliminary notices are pencilled on charts. Review them against the Annual Summary and the weekly notices that cancel them.
  7. Re-check routes when a correction lands on your planned track. A new light sector, a moved buoy or a revised VTS channel may change the passage plan, not just the book.

Never correct a publication from a navigational warning alone and then forget it. Warnings are temporary information. The NtM is the permanent correction.

Worked example: when will a light first be sighted?

This example shows why the List of Lights matters even when the chart shows the light.

Data

  • Light from the List of Lights: elevation 36 m, nominal range 20 M.
  • Your height of eye: 25 m.
  • Meteorological visibility from the forecast and your own observation: 10 nautical miles.

Step 1: Geographical range. Use the common approximation, distance ≈ 2.08 × (√height of eye + √elevation). It includes standard refraction. The List of Lights also contains a geographical range table.

  • √25 = 5
  • √36 = 6
  • 2.08 × (5 + 6) = 2.08 × 11 ≈ 22.9 nautical miles

Step 2: Luminous range. Nominal range is defined as the luminous range when meteorological visibility is 10 nautical miles. With visibility at 10 miles, luminous range = 20 M. In any other visibility, read luminous range from the luminous range diagram in the List of Lights.

Step 3: Compare. The light is first seen at the lesser of the two ranges.

  • Lesser of 22.9 and 20 = 20 nautical miles.
  • The light will not "rise" over the horizon at 22.9 miles. It will appear at about 20 miles, once you are within its luminous range.
  • A rising and dipping bearing only works for a fix when the geographical range is the limiting factor.

Step 4: Cross-check the characteristics. Compare the chart's light description with the List of Lights entry: character, period, sectors and remarks. If they differ, check the edition and correction status of both. Then use the more recently corrected source, and note the discrepancy for reporting.

If the visibility drops to, say, 5 miles, the luminous range falls. Go back into the diagram rather than assuming the nominal range still applies.

Digital publications and ECDIS

Many ships now carry some or all of these publications digitally. Examples are the ADMIRALTY Digital Publications suite (digital List of Lights, digital Radio Signals and TotalTide) and e-NP versions of Sailing Directions and other titles.

Points to remember:

  • Acceptance is a flag-state matter. Some administrations accept digital publications in place of paper, subject to conditions. Check the flag's guidance and your SMS, and keep any acceptance letter or circular the company provides.
  • Digital means updated, not automatically correct. Weekly updates still have to be downloaded and applied. Licences still have to be current. An expired licence or a missed update leaves you with an out-of-date publication, just as a missed paper correction would.
  • ECDIS does not replace publications. An ENC carries some light and navaid information, but not everything in Sailing Directions or the List of Radio Signals. Pilotage, VTS procedures and local regulations still come from the publications.
  • Have a backup plan. Know what you will use if the bridge PC holding the e-publications fails. Your SMS should say.

Common mistakes on board

  • Using a superseded edition that was never removed from the shelf. Mark withdrawn volumes clearly or land them.
  • Skipping the "previous update" check, so missed notices go unnoticed for months.
  • Correcting charts but not publications. Sections IV to VI of the weekly NtM get ignored.
  • Relying only on the chart for a light's details and missing a sector change or an unlit period that is described in the List of Lights.
  • Taking VTS channels from memory or an old port guide instead of the current List of Radio Signals Vol 6 or the port's official information.
  • Forgetting the Annual Summary. Its annual notices hold standing information on matters such as safety, regulations and warnings, and these do not appear on the chart.
  • Letting digital licences or update downloads lapse before a voyage into a new area.
  • Treating the publications list as a formality at appraisal, then finding mid-passage that the needed Sailing Directions volume is not on board.

Oral exam questions you should expect

Examiners at OOW and Chief Mate level commonly ask questions like these. Answer from the publications, not from memory of one ship.

  • "Which regulation requires you to carry charts and publications?" SOLAS V/19.2.1.4 covers carriage. V/27 requires them to be adequate and up to date for the intended voyage. V/2 defines what counts as an official chart or publication.
  • "Where will you find the pilot boarding position and VTS reporting points?" List of Radio Signals Vol 6 (NP 286). Sailing Directions add useful context.
  • "How do you know a correction has been missed?" The "previous update" reference in the NtM does not match the last correction recorded on the chart.
  • "Explain nominal, luminous and geographical range." Define each term, then explain that the light is seen at the lesser of luminous and geographical range.
  • "Where do you find tidal data for a secondary port?" Admiralty Tide Tables Part II, applied to the standard port predictions in Part I.
  • "What is in The Mariner's Handbook?" Use and reliability of charts, publications, navigation hazards, ice, meteorology and general guidance.
  • "What publications would you need for a voyage from X to Y?" Work through charts, Sailing Directions, Lights, Radio Signals, Tide Tables, Ocean Passages and the Annual Summary, in a logical order.
  • "Are digital publications acceptable?" If the flag state accepts them, provided they are kept updated and properly licensed, and there is a backup arrangement.
Key points
  • SOLAS V/19.2.1.4 requires carriage of charts and publications, and V/27 requires them to be adequate and up to date for the intended voyage. The flag state decides what is acceptable.
  • The core Admiralty set is Sailing Directions, List of Lights, List of Radio Signals, Tide Tables, NtMs, the Annual Summary, NP 100 and NP 136.
  • Weekly NtMs: Section II corrects charts, IV Sailing Directions, V the List of Lights, VI the List of Radio Signals.
  • Always check the "previous update" reference to catch missed corrections.
  • A light is first sighted at the lesser of its luminous and geographical range.
  • Digital publications still need current updates, valid licences and flag acceptance.
  • The International Code of Signals and IAMSAR Vol III are IMO publications required under SOLAS V/21, not Admiralty titles.

Frequently asked questions

Is it compulsory to carry Admiralty publications on a merchant ship?

SOLAS requires official nautical publications that are adequate and up to date, not a particular brand. Admiralty (UKHO) titles are the most widely used worldwide. Other national hydrographic office publications may be accepted, and the flag administration and company SMS decide the outfit.

Which publication gives VTS and port reporting procedures?

The Admiralty List of Radio Signals Volume 6 (NP 286) covers pilot services, vessel traffic services and port operations. Sailing Directions give supporting local information. Always use the latest corrected edition, and confirm with any official port information you receive.

How are Sailing Directions and the List of Lights corrected?

Through the weekly Notices to Mariners: Section IV for Sailing Directions, Section V for the List of Lights and Fog Signals, and Section VI for the List of Radio Signals. Each amendment is recorded in the publication's record of amendments and in the ship's correction log, as the SMS requires.

Can a ship use only digital Admiralty publications?

Many flag states accept digital publications in place of paper, but acceptance and conditions vary by administration. The publications must be kept updated and licensed. The ship should have a documented backup arrangement in case the system fails.

What is the difference between nominal range and luminous range of a light?

Luminous range is the maximum distance at which a light can be seen in the prevailing meteorological visibility. Nominal range is the luminous range when that visibility is 10 nautical miles. For other visibilities, use the luminous range diagram in the List of Lights.

Test yourself

Where would you primarily look for VHF reporting points and pilot boarding procedures for a port?

ALRS Vol 6 covers pilot services, VTS and port operations. It is kept current through Section VI of the weekly Notices to Mariners.

Which section of the weekly Admiralty Notices to Mariners contains amendments to the List of Lights and Fog Signals?

Section V amends the List of Lights. Section II corrects charts, IV amends Sailing Directions and VI amends Radio Signals.

Height of eye is 25 m, light elevation 36 m, nominal range 20 M and meteorological visibility 10 nm. At about what distance will the light first be seen?

Geographical range is about 2.08 × (5 + 6) ≈ 22.9 nm, and luminous range at 10 nm visibility equals the nominal range of 20 M. The lesser value, 20 nm, applies (List of Lights, range definitions).

Which SOLAS regulation requires charts and nautical publications to be adequate and up to date for the intended voyage?

V/19 covers carriage of navigational systems and equipment, including charts and publications under V/19.2.1.4, but the "adequate and up to date" requirement is V/27. V/21 covers the International Code of Signals and IAMSAR, and V/34 covers safe navigation and voyage planning.

In the Admiralty Tide Tables, where are time and height differences for secondary ports found?

Part II lists secondary ports with their differences, which are applied to the standard port predictions in Part I. Part III gives harmonic constants (Admiralty Tide Tables, explanatory notes).
