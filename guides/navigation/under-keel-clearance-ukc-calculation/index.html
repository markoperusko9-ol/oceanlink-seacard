Under keel clearance (UKC) calculation: method, worked example and oral questions

Quick answer

Under keel clearance is the depth of water you expect under the keel at a given place and time. You find it from charted depth plus predicted height of tide, minus the ship's deepest draught. A useful figure then deducts squat, heel, wave response, survey uncertainty and weather effects, and compares the result with the minimum set by your company SMS and any port or pilotage authority.

Static and dynamic UKC: what you are actually calculating

UKC is the vertical distance between the deepest point of the hull and the seabed. Static UKC treats the ship as stopped and upright. Dynamic UKC is what remains once the ship is moving, turning and responding to the sea.

The basic relationship:

  • Depth of water = charted depth (sounding below chart datum) + height of tide at the time of passage
  • Static UKC = depth of water − deepest static draught (corrected for water density)
  • Dynamic UKC = static UKC − squat − heel allowance − wave/swell allowance
  • Net (or effective) UKC = dynamic UKC − survey/chart uncertainty − allowances for weather effects on tide

Terms vary between companies. Some SMS use "gross" and "net" UKC, while others use "static" and "dynamic". Read your own Navigation Manual and use its definitions, because the minimum requirement is defined against one of these figures.

The minimum is never a single universal number. Company policies often express it as a fixed value, a percentage of draught, or both. They usually set different values for open sea, approaches, channels and berths. Port authorities and pilotage services may set their own minimums, which can be stricter.

SOLAS Chapter V requires the voyage to be planned. The IMO Guidelines for Voyage Planning (A.893(21)) require two things:

  • the ship's draught is considered at the appraisal stage
  • the minimum UKC in critical areas of restricted depth is set out in the detailed plan

In practice, the UKC calculation is part of the passage plan. It is not something you do when the pilot boards.

Inputs and where each one comes from

Input Source Watch out for
Charted depth Largest-scale chart or ENC, shoalest sounding on or near the track Depths are below chart datum, not below MSL
Height of tide Tide tables (standard and secondary ports) or approved tidal software Secondary port corrections; time zone of predictions
Draught Marks read on departure, loading computer, or calculated arrival draught Trim, hog/sag, water density, fuel and ballast consumed en route
Squat Pilot card/manoeuvring data, port guidance, or an approximation formula Speed through the water, not over the ground
Heel Wind, turning, or a list from cargo or ballast Fast ships and ships with a high VCG (e.g. container ships, PCCs) can heel noticeably in turns; wide-beam ships gain more draught per degree of heel
Wave response Company or port allowance Swell in approaches can exceed the squat effect
Survey uncertainty CATZOC on ENC, source diagram on paper charts Old or poor surveys in areas with mobile seabeds
Meteorological effect Barometer, forecasts, port surge warnings High pressure and offshore winds lower the water level

Water density. If you load in dock or fresh water and then transit in salt water, the ship rises. The reverse is also true.

  • Fresh water allowance (FWA) in mm = displacement ÷ (4 × TPC)
  • Dock water allowance = FWA × (1025 − dock water density) ÷ 25

Use the draught for the water you will actually be in at the critical point.

Hog and sag. A sagging ship draws more amidships than the mean of the forward and aft marks suggests. Read the midship marks on both sides.

Allowances: squat, heel, waves and uncertainty

Squat is the bodily sinkage plus trim change of a moving hull in shallow water. It is caused by the increased water velocity under and around the hull.

Squat increases:

  • roughly with the square of speed through the water
  • with block coefficient
  • with confinement of the channel

Full-form ships with a high block coefficient tend to squat more at the bow. Fine-form ships tend to squat more at the stern.

A widely taught approximation from Barrass gives maximum squat in metres. V is speed through the water in knots.

  • Open water: Cb × V² ÷ 100
  • Confined channel: Cb × V² ÷ 50

These are estimates for planning and exams. Use the ship's own data or port guidance where available. A conservative approach adds the maximum squat to the deepest static draught.

Heel. A simple, conservative approximation for the increase in draught is (Beam ÷ 2) × sin(heel angle). Allow for:

  • heel in turns
  • heel from wind on a high-sided ship
  • any existing list

Wave and swell response. Pitch, roll and heave can bring the keel well below its static position. Many companies and ports set an allowance or speed restriction for exposed approaches. Follow it. Do not use the open-water squat figure as a proxy for swell.

Survey uncertainty. On ENCs, the Category of Zone of Confidence (CATZOC) gives depth accuracy:

CATZOC Depth accuracy (approx.)
A1 ±(0.5 m + 1% of depth)
A2 and B ±(1.0 m + 2% of depth)
C ±(2.0 m + 5% of depth)
D Worse than C
U Unassessed

Companies differ on whether to deduct the full CATZOC uncertainty from UKC. Many do in confined or shallow passages. Check your SMS.

Meteorological effect. Predicted tides assume average conditions. As a rough guide, sea level falls about 1 cm for each hectopascal of pressure above average. Strong offshore winds and negative surges can lower it further, sometimes by much more.

Step-by-step method

  1. Identify critical points. Mark every shoal patch, bar, channel and berth where depth is limited on the planned track. Use the largest-scale chart available.
  2. Note the least charted depth at each point. Include any NM or temporary/preliminary corrections.
  3. Work out the height of tide at your ETA for each critical point. Use secondary port corrections where they apply. Calculate for a window, not a single time, so you know the earliest and latest safe transit times.
  4. Establish the draught expected at that point. Include water density, consumption, trim, and hog or sag.
  5. Calculate static UKC.
  6. Deduct squat for your planned speed through the water. Then deduct heel and wave allowances.
  7. Deduct survey uncertainty and weather effects as your SMS requires.
  8. Compare with the required minimum. Use the strictest of the company and port requirements.
  9. If it fails, change something you control: speed, tide window, draught (ballast, cargo, trim), or route. Then recalculate.
  10. Record it in the passage plan. Brief it to the bridge team. Set the ECDIS safety contour and safety depth to match. Agree the figures with the pilot.

Worked example

Ship and passage data

  • Least charted depth in a dredged channel: 12.40 m, CATZOC B
  • Sheltered dredged channel; company wave allowance here = 0 m (assumed for this example)
  • Predicted height of tide at planned ETA: 2.10 m
  • Deepest static draught in sea water: 11.20 m (aft)
  • Beam 32.2 m, Cb 0.80, planned speed through the water 8 kn, channel treated as confined
  • Expected heel in the channel turn: 1°
  • Barometer forecast 1028 hPa (about 15 hPa above average)
  • Assumed for this example only: company minimum net UKC of 10% of static draught = 1.12 m

First attempt

Item Value (m)
Depth of water (12.40 + 2.10) 14.50
Static UKC (14.50 − 11.20) 3.30
Squat: 0.80 × 8² ÷ 50 −1.02
Heel: 16.1 × sin 1° −0.28
Dynamic UKC 2.00
CATZOC B: 1.0 + 2% of 12.40 −1.25
Pressure: about 15 hPa × 1 cm −0.15
Net UKC 0.60

At 0.60 m against a required 1.12 m, the transit fails.

Revised plan: reduce speed to 6 kn and time the passage for a predicted height of tide of 2.60 m.

Item Value (m)
Depth of water (12.40 + 2.60) 15.00
Static UKC 3.80
Squat: 0.80 × 6² ÷ 50 −0.58
Heel −0.28
Dynamic UKC 2.94
CATZOC B −1.25
Pressure −0.15
Net UKC 1.54

This passes, with 0.42 m in hand.

Cutting speed by 2 knots recovered 0.44 m. That is why speed is the first lever. Then confirm that 6 kn still gives adequate steerage in the expected wind and current, and agree it with the pilot.

Common mistakes

  • Using mean draught instead of the deepest draught, or ignoring sag.
  • Forgetting the density change between the loading port and the transit area.
  • Taking squat at speed over the ground. With a head current, speed through the water is higher, and so is squat.
  • Calculating for one time only. If you run late, the tide may have dropped below your margin. Work out the window.
  • Ignoring secondary port corrections or the time zone of the tide tables.
  • Leaving ECDIS on default settings. The safety contour and safety depth must reflect the calculated draught plus squat and margin.
  • Treating charted depth as exact, especially over old surveys or mobile sandbanks.
  • Not revisiting the calculation when the ETA, draught, weather or pilot's intended speed changes.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

Examiners usually start simple and then push on allowances and judgement. Typical lines of questioning:

  • "Define UKC. What is the difference between static and dynamic?" Give the formula and list the dynamic factors.
  • "What is squat and what affects it?" Speed (roughly squared), block coefficient, depth/draught ratio and channel confinement. Mention bow versus stern squat by hull form.
  • "How does barometric pressure affect your UKC?" High pressure lowers sea level by roughly 1 cm per hPa, so actual depth may be less than predicted.
  • "Your UKC is below the company minimum. What do you do?" Reduce speed, wait for tide, adjust draught or trim, or reroute. Inform the master. Never simply proceed and hope.
  • "What is CATZOC and how do you use it?" Explain the categories and apply the depth uncertainty to the charted depth.
  • "Where in the passage plan does UKC appear?" At appraisal (draught and limitations) and in the detailed plan. It also appears in the critical points, in the safety contour and safety depth settings, and in the master-pilot exchange.
  • "You loaded in a river port. What happens to your draught at sea?" The ship rises. Explain FWA and DWA.

Always answer with the principle first. Then say that the minimum figure comes from the company SMS and the port authority, not from a single international number.

Key points
  • Depth of water = charted depth + height of tide; static UKC = depth of water − deepest draught.
  • Squat increases roughly with the square of speed through the water, so slowing down is the quickest way to gain clearance.
  • Deduct heel, wave response, survey uncertainty (CATZOC) and weather effects to get a realistic net UKC.
  • High barometric pressure and offshore winds can make actual water levels lower than predicted.
  • Minimum UKC is set by your company SMS and the port authority; use the strictest that applies.
  • Calculate a tidal window, not a single time, and recalculate when ETA, draught or weather change.
  • Set the ECDIS safety contour and safety depth to match your UKC calculation.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum under keel clearance for ships?

There is no single international figure. Minimum UKC is set in your company SMS and usually differs for open sea, approaches, channels and berths. Port and pilotage authorities may set their own requirements. Always apply the strictest requirement that covers your situation.

How do I calculate squat quickly?

A common approximation gives maximum squat in metres as Cb × V² ÷ 100 in open water and Cb × V² ÷ 50 in a confined channel. V is speed through the water in knots. Treat the result as an estimate, and use the ship's own manoeuvring data or port guidance where available.

Do I use mean draught or deepest draught for UKC?

Use the deepest draught. This is normally the deepest of forward, aft or midships, allowing for trim and hog or sag. Correct it for the density of the water at the critical point, because using mean draught understates the risk.

Does CATZOC need to be deducted from UKC?

CATZOC shows how accurate the charted depth is likely to be, so a prudent calculation allows for it. Whether you must deduct the full uncertainty depends on your company's navigation procedures. Many companies require it in shallow or confined waters.

How does atmospheric pressure affect the tide?

Predicted tides assume average pressure. As a rough guide, each hectopascal above average lowers the sea level by about 1 cm, and low pressure raises it. Strong winds and storm surges can have a much larger effect than pressure alone.

Test yourself

Charted depth is 9.0 m, height of tide 1.5 m and deepest static draught 8.6 m. What is the static UKC?

Depth of water is 9.0 + 1.5 = 10.5 m, and 10.5 − 8.6 = 1.9 m; forgetting the tide gives 0.4 m. Reference: voyage planning requirement in SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 34, and IMO Res. A.893(21); the arithmetic itself is standard practice.

Which change has the greatest effect on squat for a given ship and channel?

Squat increases roughly with the square of speed through the water, so speed dominates. Trim and density alter draught, but they are not squat drivers in the same way.

Barometric pressure is well above average. What effect does this have on the actual depth compared with the tide prediction?

High pressure depresses the sea surface by roughly 1 cm per hPa above average. Tide predictions assume average conditions, so actual depth is likely to be less than predicted.

Which ENC CATZOC category indicates the highest depth accuracy?

A1 has the tightest depth accuracy, ±(0.5 m + 1% of depth); U means unassessed, not highly accurate. Reference: IHO S-57 CATZOC definitions.

A ship loads in dock water of density 1010 kg/m³ and then proceeds to sea. What happens to her draught?

In denser sea water the ship needs less volume to support the same displacement, so she rises. The change is found using the dock water allowance, FWA × (1025 − 1010) ÷ 25.
