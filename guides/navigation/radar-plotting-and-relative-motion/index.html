Radar Plotting and Relative Motion: The OAW Triangle, CPA, TCPA and Avoiding Action

Quick answer

Radar plotting uses the OAW triangle to turn successive radar observations of a target into its relative track, CPA, TCPA, true course, true speed and aspect. Own ship's course and speed (WO, constructed by laying W off from O in the reverse direction) combined with the target's relative motion (OA) gives the target's true motion (WA). Examiners expect you to do this by hand, explain what ARPA is doing, and plan a substantial alteration that achieves a safe CPA.

Relative motion: what the radar is actually showing you

On a relative motion (RM) display own ship stays fixed at the centre. Every echo moves according to the difference between its motion and yours. The track an echo traces across the screen is its relative track. It does not show where the other ship is going. It shows how that ship moves relative to you.

The relative track gives you two pieces of information directly:

  • CPA (closest point of approach): the perpendicular distance from own ship (the centre) to the extended relative track.
  • TCPA (time to CPA): the distance remaining along the relative track to the CPA point, divided by the relative speed.

The RM display does not show the target's true course, true speed or aspect directly. You need those to decide which COLREG rule applies (overtaking, head-on or crossing) and to predict how the target will respond. To get them you remove your own motion from the picture. That is what the plotting triangle does.

On a true motion (TM) display own ship moves across the screen. With sea stabilisation, the echoes move along their true tracks through the water. Stationary objects stay still only if the display is ground stabilised. Both modes have their uses. Hand plotting is normally taught on a stabilised relative motion picture, either north-up or course-up.

The OAW triangle: what each side means

Most nautical colleges teach this labelling:

Point / side Meaning
O Target's first plotted position (the origin of the plot)
A Target's position after the plotting interval
W Plotted from O, in the direction opposite to own ship's course, at a distance equal to own ship's speed × plotting interval
WO Own ship's course and speed (direction W→O)
WA Target's true course and speed (direction W→A)
OA Target's relative motion (direction O→A)

The logic is simple vector addition. The target's true motion equals own ship's motion plus the relative motion. Written as a triangle, WA = WO + OA.

A stationary target gives a useful check on W. A buoy or an anchored ship would appear to move exactly opposite to your course at your speed, so its echo would travel from O to W. If A lands on W, the target is stopped in the water.

Practical points:

  • Use a plotting interval that is easy to scale, such as 6 or 12 minutes. Six minutes is one tenth of an hour, so distances convert to knots straight away.
  • Take at least three observations at equal intervals. If the plots do not lie on a straight line, the target is manoeuvring and your triangle is invalid.
  • Hold own course and speed steady throughout the plotting interval. Otherwise WO is wrong.

Step-by-step method

  1. Observe and plot the target's bearing and range at regular intervals, for example every 6 minutes. Label the first position O and note the time.
  2. Check the bearing trend. A steady or nearly steady compass bearing with closing range indicates risk of collision. Bearing change alone can mislead with large ships or at close range.
  3. Plot A at the end of your chosen interval. Draw OA and extend it past the centre. This line is the relative track.
  4. Read CPA: the perpendicular distance from the centre to the extended OA.
  5. Find TCPA: measure the distance from A to the CPA point and divide it by the relative speed (length OA ÷ interval).
  6. Construct W: from O, lay off own ship's reversed course for a distance of own speed × interval.
  7. Join WA. Its direction is the target's true course. Its length ÷ interval is the target's true speed.
  8. Work out aspect: the relative bearing of own ship as seen from the target, expressed as Red or Green 0–180°.
  9. Decide the rule situation and your action. Rules 13–15 apply when vessels are in sight of one another. Rule 19 applies in restricted visibility.
  10. Plan the action. Choose a time of action and mark the target's expected position at that time on the relative track. Draw the new required relative track, tangent to your CPA circle. Then find the own ship course or speed that produces it.
  11. Keep plotting after the action. Confirm that the CPA is opening as planned and that the target has not altered.

Worked example with numbers

Own ship: course 000° T, speed 12 knots, stabilised north-up relative motion display.

Time Bearing Range
1000 045° T 10.0 nm (O)
1006 045° T 9.0 nm
1012 045° T 8.0 nm (A)

Relative motion and CPA/TCPA

  • The bearing is steady and the range is closing, so the relative track runs straight down the bearing line through the centre. CPA = 0 nm. Risk of collision exists.
  • Relative distance OA = 2.0 nm in 12 minutes, so the relative speed is 10 knots.
  • TCPA from A = 8.0 ÷ 10 = 0.8 h = 48 minutes, so CPA is at 1100.

True course and speed

  • Own ship covers 12 × 0.2 = 2.4 nm in 12 minutes. From O, lay off W 2.4 nm in direction 180°.
  • WA measures about 1.72 nm in a direction of about 305° T, which is ≈ 8.6 knots.
  • Aspect: the bearing of own ship from the target is 225°. Relative to the target's heading of 305°, that is Red 80°. You would expect to see its port sidelight.

Situation: in clear visibility this is a crossing situation with the target on own ship's starboard side. Own ship is the give-way vessel under Rule 15. It must take early and substantial action under Rules 8 and 16 and avoid crossing ahead.

Planning a starboard alteration at 1018 for a 2 nm CPA

  • At 1018 the target will be at 045°, 7.0 nm, on the original relative track.
  • From that point, draw the tangent to the 2 nm circle that makes the target pass ahead of you. It runs in a direction of about 242°, because sin⁻¹(2/7) ≈ 16.6° either side of the 225° line to the centre.
  • Redraw the triangle, keeping WA (the target's true vector) fixed. Swing an arc of own ship's 12-knot vector until the new relative direction is 242°. This gives a minimum new course of about 022° T.
  • The new relative speed is about 13.1 knots and the distance to the new CPA is about 6.7 nm. The target therefore passes about 2 nm ahead at roughly 1049.

Examiner's point: 22° is the minimum alteration that achieves the 2 nm figure. It may not be readily apparent to the other vessel, especially one observing by radar alone. A bolder alteration usually produces a wider CPA and a more obvious change of aspect. Your company or the master's standing orders may also specify a minimum CPA. Exam plots usually assume the turn is instantaneous. At sea, allow for advance and transfer, and start the turn early.

If you plot this on paper with a parallel rule and dividers, your answers should fall within a degree or two and a tenth of a knot of these figures.

ARPA, stabilisation and the traps behind the numbers

ARPA does the same vector arithmetic continuously, using its tracked history of the target. The arithmetic is not the weak point. The inputs are.

  • Sea stabilisation vs ground stabilisation. With heading and speed through the water as input (sea stabilised), true vectors show the target's heading and speed through the water, so aspect is correct. With COG/SOG as input (ground stabilised), true vectors show the track over the ground. In a strong tidal stream, current or leeway, the apparent aspect can be badly wrong. Sea-stabilised data is generally preferred for collision avoidance assessment. Ground stabilisation is useful for navigation and pilotage.
  • Wrong own ship data. A faulty log or a gyro error corrupts every true vector, because the W point is wrong. CPA and TCPA from the relative track are less affected, because they come from the target's movement on the screen.
  • Lag after manoeuvres. ARPA needs time to settle after either vessel alters. Treat vectors with suspicion for the first few minutes after any change, whether yours or the target's.
  • Target swap. In clutter, or with targets close together, the tracker can jump to another echo.
  • Trial manoeuvre. This shows the predicted result of a planned alteration, including any delay you enter. It assumes the target holds its course and speed.

Rule 7 warns against assumptions based on scanty information, especially scanty radar information. One pair of plots, or a vector that has not settled, counts as scanty information.

Common mistakes in exams and on the bridge

  • Laying off WO the wrong way. W is plotted from O in the reverse of own course. Draw it the other way and every true vector comes out wrong.
  • Confusing the relative track with the target's heading. A target closing straight down the bearing line is not necessarily heading at you. In the example it is heading 305°.
  • Unequal intervals, or own ship altering during the plotting period.
  • Relying on two plots. Two points cannot tell you whether a target is steady or manoeuvring.
  • Reading CPA off the wrong side of the track, or measuring TCPA from O instead of from the latest position.
  • Small successive alterations. Rule 8 calls for alterations large enough to be readily apparent to another vessel observing visually or by radar.
  • Ignoring Rule 19 in fog. When the other vessel is not in sight, the crossing rules do not apply. Rule 19(d) says to avoid, as far as possible, two actions:
    • altering to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken
    • altering towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam
  • Forgetting speed reduction. Reducing speed shortens WO and changes the relative track. It can be very effective for targets ahead, and sometimes taking all way off is the best answer.
  • Not re-plotting after action. The plan only works if the target keeps its course and speed.

Oral exam questions you should be ready for

  • Draw the OAW triangle and explain each side. Name all three vectors and say which way each arrow points.
  • A target's echo moves exactly opposite to your course at your speed. What is it? It is stationary in the water, because A falls on W.
  • Bearing steady, range closing, target on the port bow in fog. What do you do? Explain Rule 19. An alteration to starboard is consistent with 19(d)(i). Under 19(e), reduce speed or stop if necessary once you hear a fog signal apparently forward of the beam or cannot avoid a close-quarters situation. Keep plotting.
  • Why might ARPA aspect be wrong in a 3-knot tidal stream? If the ARPA is ground stabilised (COG/SOG input), the true vectors show track over the ground, not heading, so the apparent aspect can be wrong.
  • How does an alteration of course affect CPA compared with a speed change? A course change rotates WO. A speed change alters its length. Show the effect on OA for each.
  • What is the significance of a target whose bearing changes slowly at close range? Risk of collision can still exist despite an appreciable bearing change, particularly with large ships or at close range (Rule 7(d)(ii)).
  • When do you start plotting? Early enough to assess the risk before a close-quarters situation develops. Long-range scanning is part of Rule 7(b).

Oral exam practice varies by administration. Some examiners hand you a plotting sheet and some sketch on paper. Practise both freehand and with instruments.

Key points
  • The relative track (OA) gives CPA and TCPA; the triangle WA = WO + OA gives the target's true course and speed.
  • W is plotted from O in the reverse of own ship's course, at own speed × plotting interval.
  • Use at least three equally spaced plots and hold own course and speed steady.
  • Aspect, and therefore the rule situation, comes from the true vector, not the relative track.
  • Use sea-stabilised data for collision avoidance; ground-stabilised vectors can give a false aspect in current.
  • Plan action as a new relative track tangent to the required CPA circle, check that the action is substantial, and keep plotting.
  • In or near restricted visibility, Rule 19 governs vessels not in sight of one another; once in sight, Rules 11–18 apply.

Frequently asked questions

What is the OAW triangle in radar plotting?

It is the vector triangle used to find a target's true motion. O is the first plot and A is the plot after a set interval. W is laid off from O in the reverse of own ship's course, at own speed × interval. WA then gives the target's true course and speed, and OA gives its relative motion.

How do I calculate CPA and TCPA by hand?

Extend the relative track OA past own ship. CPA is the perpendicular distance from own ship to that line. TCPA is the distance from the latest plotted position to the CPA point, divided by the relative speed (length of OA divided by the plotting interval).

Should ARPA be sea or ground stabilised for collision avoidance?

Sea stabilisation, using heading and speed through the water, is generally preferred because it shows target headings and therefore the correct aspect. Ground stabilisation shows tracks over the ground. That suits navigation but can mislead on aspect in tidal streams or current. Follow your company procedures and the master's standing orders.

Why can't I rely on ARPA vectors immediately after an alteration?

ARPA derives vectors from the target's tracked history, so it needs time to settle after either vessel manoeuvres. Until then the displayed values lag behind reality. Treat them as scanty information under Rule 7 and keep observing.

Is reducing speed a valid avoiding action on a radar plot?

Yes. Reducing speed shortens the WO vector and changes the relative track, often widening CPA for targets ahead. It also gives you more time to assess the situation. Rule 8 lists slackening speed, stopping or reversing as recognised actions. In certain restricted-visibility situations, Rule 19(e) requires reducing speed to the minimum at which you can keep your course.

Test yourself

In the OAW triangle, which side represents the target's true course and speed?

WA is the target's true motion, the sum of own ship's motion (WO) and the relative motion (OA).

Own ship steers 090° at 10 knots. A target's echo moves on the screen exactly towards 270° at 10 knots relative. What is the target doing?

If the relative motion equals own ship's motion reversed, A falls on W. The target's true vector is then zero, so it is stopped in the water.

A target's bearing is steady and its range has closed from 9 nm to 7.5 nm in 9 minutes. What is the TCPA from the latest observation?

The relative speed is 1.5 nm in 9 minutes, which is 10 knots. CPA is 0 on a steady bearing, so TCPA = 7.5 ÷ 10 = 0.75 h, or 45 minutes. A steady bearing with closing range indicates risk of collision (COLREG Rule 7(d)(i)).

In restricted visibility, a radar-only target is forward of the beam with CPA 0 and is not overtaking you. Which action does Rule 19 say to avoid as far as possible?

COLREG Rule 19(d)(i) says to avoid, so far as possible, an alteration to port for a vessel forward of the beam, other than one being overtaken.

In a strong tidal stream, why might ARPA show a misleading aspect for a target?

With COG/SOG input, the true vectors represent ground tracks. These differ from headings when there is current, so the apparent aspect is wrong. IMO radar performance standards (MSC.192(79)) provide for both sea- and ground-stabilised modes; sea stabilisation is generally preferred for collision avoidance assessment.
