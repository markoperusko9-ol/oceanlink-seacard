Celestial Navigation Basics: The Noon Sight

Quick answer

A noon sight measures the sun's altitude at meridian passage, when it bears due north or south. Corrected to true altitude and subtracted from 90°, that altitude gives the zenith distance, and latitude follows by combining zenith distance with the sun's declination. You need a sextant, the Nautical Almanac, the time of meridian passage, and the correct naming rules.

What the noon sight actually gives you

At meridian passage the sun crosses your meridian and bears due north or due south. At that moment its true altitude, its declination and your latitude are linked by simple arithmetic. No sight reduction tables and no spherical trigonometry are needed.

The result is a latitude, which is a position line running east–west. It is not a full fix. To get a noon position you either:

  • run up a morning sun position line (a sun-run-sun), or
  • cross the latitude with another position line or fix, such as GNSS, a radar range or bearing of a charted object, or (at twilight) a star sight run up/back to noon.

There are good reasons to keep this skill alive even with GNSS on the bridge:

  • It is a standalone check of GNSS that needs no electronics.
  • Some companies' SMS still require observed positions (e.g. a noon latitude) to be taken and logged.
  • Competence in using celestial bodies to fix the ship's position is part of the STCW standard for officers in charge of a navigational watch.
  • Examiners expect you to do it cleanly and explain every step.

The principle in one diagram's worth of words

  • Zenith distance (ZD) = 90° − true altitude. It is the arc from your zenith to the sun along your meridian.
  • Declination (Dec) is the sun's "latitude" on the celestial sphere. Take it from the Nautical Almanac daily pages.
  • Your zenith sits at your latitude on the celestial sphere. So your latitude is the sun's declination plus or minus the zenith distance.

Naming rule. Name the ZD opposite to the sun's bearing:

  • If the sun bears south, you are north of it, so ZD is named N.
  • If the sun bears north, ZD is named S.

Combining:

ZD and Dec Latitude
Same name Lat = ZD + Dec, same name
Different names Lat = difference, named after the greater

Check case. You are at about 5°N and the declination is N 15°. The sun bears north, so ZD is named S and is about 10°. The names differ, so Lat = 15° − 10° = 5°, named N after the greater (Dec). The rule works even when the sun passes on the "wrong" side of you.

Step-by-step: from almanac to latitude

  1. Find LMT of meridian passage. Read the sun's "Mer. Pass." time from the Nautical Almanac daily page. It is tabulated as local mean time and is essentially the same at any longitude for practical purposes.
  2. Convert to UTC. Turn DR longitude into time at 4 minutes per degree. Longitude west, UTC is later, so add. Longitude east, UTC is earlier, so subtract.
  3. Convert to ship's time. Apply the zone or ship's time difference.
    • Check what time the clocks are actually keeping. Some ships keep a non-standard time by company or port practice.
    • If you are making good east or west at speed, recalculate using the DR longitude for the estimated time of noon, not your 0800 longitude.
  4. Take the sight.
    • Start a few minutes early and keep bringing the lower limb down to the horizon while swinging the sextant.
    • The altitude rises, "hangs", then starts to dip. Record the maximum.
    • Note the bearing, north or south.
  5. Correct the altitude. Apply index error, then dip for height of eye, then the sun's altitude correction from the almanac (lower or upper limb, correct seasonal column). The table below shows the order.
  6. Get the declination for the UTC of meridian passage. Interpolate using the d value at the bottom of the daily page.
  7. Calculate. ZD = 90° − true altitude, named opposite to the bearing. Then combine with Dec using the rule above.
  8. Plot and compare with DR and GNSS. Investigate any large difference and log the result as your SMS requires.
Stage Correction Sign
Sextant altitude → observed Index error On the arc: subtract. Off the arc: add
Observed → apparent Dip (height of eye) Always subtract
Apparent → true Sun altitude correction (refraction, semi-diameter, parallax) Lower limb normally positive; upper limb normally negative

Terminology varies between textbooks and countries. Some call the IE-corrected reading "observed altitude". In US and Nautical Almanac usage, "apparent altitude" means after IE and dip, and "Ho" is the final corrected value. Use the terms your examiner's syllabus uses, but the order of corrections never changes.

Worked example (illustrative figures)

The figures below are internally consistent but illustrative. They are not taken from a particular year's almanac, so use the current almanac for real work.

Situation:

  • Mid-October, Arabian Sea.
  • DR 12°40'N 072°15'E.
  • Ship's clocks on UTC +5.
  • Height of eye 16.0 m.
  • Index error 1.5' on the arc.

Time of meridian passage

  • Almanac Mer. Pass. (LMT): 11h 46m
  • Longitude in time: 72° = 4h 48m, 15' = 1m, total 4h 49m E
  • UTC = 11:46 − 4:49 = 06:57 UTC (east, so subtract)
  • Ship's time = 06:57 + 5h = 11:57

Altitude

Item Value
Sextant altitude, sun lower limb (bearing S) 68°49.6'
Index error (on the arc) −1.5'
Observed 68°48.1'
Dip, 16.0 m −7.0'
Apparent 68°41.1'
Sun LL correction (Oct–Mar column, illustrative) +15.7'
True altitude 68°56.8'

Latitude

  • ZD = 90°00.0' − 68°56.8' = 21°03.2' N. The sun bears S, so ZD is named N.
  • Dec at 06:57 UTC (after d correction) = S 08°21.3'
  • The names differ, so take the difference: 21°03.2' − 08°21.3' = 12°41.9' N, named after the greater (ZD).

Result: Observed latitude 12°41.9'N against DR 12°40.0'N. The ship is 1.9' north of DR. That is plausible for current set. Plot it, cross it with a run-up morning position line or another position line, and record it.

Common mistakes that cost marks (and position)

  • Wrong IE sign. On the arc is subtracted and off the arc is added. Check IE before the sight, not after.
  • Declination for the wrong time. Declination must be for UTC of the sight, not LMT or ship's time. Near the equinoxes the sun's declination changes by roughly a minute of arc per hour. Using the wrong hour or skipping the d correction gives a wrong latitude directly.
  • Naming the ZD after the bearing instead of opposite to it. This is the classic error.
  • Wrong limb or wrong season column.
    • A lower limb sight needs the lower limb correction.
    • Upper limb corrections are negative.
    • The Nautical Almanac splits the sun table into Oct–Mar and Apr–Sep columns because the sun's semi-diameter changes.
  • Height of eye guessed. Use the actual bridge wing height for the current draught. A large draught change shifts dip.
  • Using the clock instead of the sun. Observe until the altitude stops rising and starts falling. On a fast ship with a north or south component, or when declination is changing quickly, the maximum altitude can occur slightly before or after true meridian passage. At normal merchant speeds the error is small, but very high altitudes make it worse.
  • Very high altitudes. When the sun is near the zenith it swings through the meridian quickly and its bearing is hard to judge. Be careful naming the ZD and swinging the arc.
  • Missing noon because of cloud. If you miss the meridian passage, an ex-meridian sight reduced to the meridian, or an ordinary intercept sight, can still give a position line. Know that these methods exist and when your textbook allows them.

Oral exam: questions examiners like

Expect to be walked through the method and asked why at each stage. Typical lines of questioning:

  • What is meridian passage, and why does the sun bear due north or south then? Its hour angle is zero, so it lies on your meridian.
  • Why does a noon sight give latitude but not longitude? The altitude is near its maximum and changes very slowly around noon. Timing is therefore insensitive, which suits latitude but makes longitude from the same sight unreliable.
  • Explain dip, refraction, semi-diameter and parallax, and say which way each acts.
  • What is index error and how do you find it? Use the horizon or the sun. Also be ready to explain how side error and perpendicularity are checked and adjusted.
  • How do you find ship's time of noon if steaming east at speed? Iterate using the DR longitude at the estimated time.
  • What if the sun bears north when you are in north latitude? Declination is north and greater than your latitude. Name the ZD south and apply the rule.
  • What is a sun-run-sun and what limits its accuracy? Errors in the run between sights, such as course, speed and current.
  • How would you use the noon latitude to check GNSS, and what would you do with a large discrepancy?

Exam formats, written versus oral and the weight given to celestial work, differ by administration. Check your own administration's current syllabus and notices.

Key points
  • A noon sight gives latitude only; it is a position line, not a fix.
  • ZD = 90° − true altitude, named opposite to the sun's bearing.
  • Same names: add ZD and Dec. Different names: take the difference, named after the greater.
  • Correct in order: index error, dip, then the sun's altitude correction for the right limb and season.
  • Take declination for the UTC of the sight and apply the d correction.
  • Convert LMT of Mer. Pass. to UTC with longitude in time: west add, east subtract.
  • Observe the maximum altitude rather than relying only on the predicted clock time.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the noon sight still taught when ships have GNSS?

STCW requires watchkeeping officers to be able to fix position by celestial bodies, and GNSS can fail, be jammed or be spoofed. A noon latitude is an independent check that needs only a sextant, an almanac and a chronometer. Some companies' SMS still require observed positions (e.g. a noon latitude) to be taken and logged.

How do I find the ship's time of local noon?

Take LMT of meridian passage from the Nautical Almanac daily page. Convert it to UTC by applying longitude in time at 4 minutes per degree, adding for west and subtracting for east. Then apply the ship's time difference. If the ship is making good significant easting or westing, recompute with the DR longitude for the estimated time of noon.

Is index error added or subtracted?

If the error is on the arc, the sextant reads too high, so subtract it. If it is off the arc, add it. Determine it before every set of sights, because it can change with handling and temperature.

What if cloud covers the sun at noon?

You can take an ex-meridian sight shortly before or after meridian passage and reduce it to the meridian, within the limits your textbook or tables allow. Alternatively, take an ordinary sun sight later and work it as an intercept position line. Record that the noon latitude was not obtained.

Can I get latitude from the Pole Star instead?

Yes, in northern latitudes Polaris gives latitude at twilight using the Polaris tables in the Nautical Almanac. It cannot be used in the southern hemisphere, and it becomes difficult close to the equator where it sits very low.

Test yourself

Index error is found to be 2.0' on the arc. How is it applied to the sextant altitude?

On the arc means the sextant over-reads, so the error is subtracted. Off the arc is added (standard sextant practice; Nautical Almanac altitude correction explanation).

At meridian passage the sun bears south. How is the zenith distance named?

The observer is north of the sun, so ZD is named north, opposite to the bearing (meridian altitude principle; standard nautical astronomy texts).

For which time should the sun's declination be extracted from the almanac?

Almanac daily pages are tabulated against UT, so declination must be taken for UTC with the d correction applied (Nautical Almanac, explanation of daily pages).

ZD is 20°00' N and declination is N 05°00'. What is the latitude?

Same names, so add: 20° + 5° = 25°N. Subtracting is only correct when the names differ (meridian altitude rule).

LMT of meridian passage is 12h05m and the DR longitude is 060°W. What is the UTC of meridian passage?

60° is 4 hours. West longitude means UTC is later, so 12:05 + 4h = 16:05 UTC (longitude in time conversion, Nautical Almanac arc-to-time table).
