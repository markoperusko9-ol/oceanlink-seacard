Ship Routeing and Reporting Systems: A Practical Guide for Deck Officers

Quick answer

Ships' routeing systems such as traffic separation schemes, deep-water routes and areas to be avoided are adopted by IMO under SOLAS Chapter V Regulation 10. Ship reporting systems, which tell a shore authority who you are, where you are and what you carry, fall under Regulation 11. Conduct inside an IMO-adopted TSS is governed by COLREG Rule 10, and the detailed requirements for each system are found in charts, Sailing Directions and the Admiralty List of Radio Signals.

Where the law sits

Three instruments do most of the work. Keep them separate in your head, because examiners often test whether you can.

  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 10 (Ships' routeing): IMO is recognised as the only international body for developing routeing systems at international level. A routeing system adopted by IMO can be made mandatory for all ships, for certain categories of ship, or for ships carrying certain cargoes. Ships must use a mandatory system that applies to them unless there are compelling reasons not to.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 11 (Ship reporting systems): reporting systems adopted by IMO can likewise be mandatory. The master must comply with the system and report all the information it requires.
  • COLREG Rule 10: this rule governs how you behave inside a traffic separation scheme adopted by IMO. Rule 1(d) is the hook that allows IMO to adopt routeing schemes for the purposes of the Rules.

The detailed design rules are in the IMO General Provisions on Ships' Routeing (Resolution A.572(14) as amended). For reporting systems, the guidance is in Resolution MSC.43(64) as amended, and the general principles and standard reporting format are in Resolution A.851(20).

Vessel traffic services (VTS) are a related but separate subject under SOLAS V/12. A VTS often runs the reporting system in its area, so on the bridge you will usually meet both together.

Routeing measures you will meet on the chart

The General Provisions define a family of measures. Know each one in a single sentence:

Measure What it means in practice
Traffic separation scheme (TSS) Opposing streams are separated by a separation zone or line, and each stream uses its own traffic lane
Traffic lane An area within defined limits where one-way traffic is established
Separation zone / line Separates lanes running in opposite or nearly opposite directions, or separates a lane from an adjacent sea area
Roundabout A separation point or circular zone with a circular lane around it, where traffic moves anticlockwise
Inshore traffic zone (ITZ) The area between the landward boundary of a TSS and the coast
Two-way route A route with two-way traffic, designed for safe passage through difficult waters
Recommended track A route which has been specially examined to ensure so far as possible that it is free of dangers and along which ships are advised to navigate
Deep-water route A route surveyed for clearance of sea bottom and submerged obstacles, mainly for deep-draught ships
Precautionary area An area where ships must navigate with particular caution, and where a direction of traffic flow may be recommended
Area to be avoided An area where navigation is particularly hazardous or that needs protection, to be avoided by all ships or by certain classes
No anchoring area An area where anchoring is hazardous or could damage the marine environment

Two terms are often confused:

  • Established direction of traffic flow is mandatory, as in the lanes of a TSS.
  • Recommended direction of traffic flow is advisory. It is used in a precautionary area, deep-water route or recommended route, where it is impractical or unnecessary to establish a direction.

IMO publishes the adopted measures in its Ships' Routeing publication. On board, your working sources are the charts, the Sailing Directions, the Notices to Mariners and the ALRS. In some busy areas, such as the English Channel and Dover Strait, an Admiralty Mariners' Routeing Guide is also available. Use the current edition and keep it corrected. Schemes do get amended and new ones are adopted.

Rule 10 in practice, with a worked crossing

Inside an IMO-adopted TSS, Rule 10 requires the following. All points are paraphrased.

  • Following a lane: proceed in the appropriate lane in the general direction of traffic flow, and keep clear of the separation line or zone as far as practicable.
  • Joining or leaving: normally do this at the termination. If you join or leave from the side, do so at as small an angle to the general direction of flow as practicable.
  • Crossing: avoid crossing lanes as far as practicable. If you must cross, do so on a heading as nearly as practicable at right angles to the general direction of traffic flow.
  • Inshore traffic zone: through traffic that can safely use the lane shall not use the ITZ. The ITZ may be used by vessels under 20 m, sailing vessels and vessels engaged in fishing. Any vessel may also use it when going to or from a port, offshore installation, pilot station or other place within the zone, or to avoid immediate danger.
  • Separation zone: do not normally enter it, except to cross, join or leave, in an emergency to avoid immediate danger, or to fish within the zone.
  • Anchoring: avoid anchoring in a TSS or near its terminations.
  • Not using the scheme: keep clear of it by as wide a margin as practicable.
  • Not impeding: fishing vessels must not impede vessels following a lane. Vessels under 20 m and sailing vessels must not impede the safe passage of power-driven vessels following a lane.
  • Exemptions: a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre is exempt to the extent necessary when maintaining safety of navigation in the TSS, or when laying, servicing or picking up a submarine cable.

Rule 10 does not relieve any vessel of her obligations under the other Rules. The steering and sailing rules still apply between ships inside the scheme.

Worked example: crossing a lane in a tidal stream

The set-up:

  • The TSS lanes run 045°/225°.
  • Each lane is 4.0 nm wide and the separation zone is 1.0 nm wide.

  • Your destination lies across the scheme to the south-east, and you are making 12 knots through the water.

  • Heading. At right angles to the traffic flow, the crossing heading is 135° (045° + 90°).

  • Distance and time. You cross 4.0 + 1.0 + 4.0 = 9.0 nm. At 12 knots that takes 45 minutes.
  • Tidal effect. A stream is setting 045° at 2 knots. In 45 minutes it carries you 1.5 nm along the direction of flow. Your ground track is therefore not at right angles to the lanes.
  • The correct action. Keep the heading at 135°. Do not alter into the stream to make the track look square on the chart. The rule refers to heading so that other ships see your aspect presenting clearly as a crosser. Allow for the 1.5 nm of drift when you plan the entry and exit points.
  • Collision avoidance. If you meet a ship following the lane, Rule 15 and the other steering rules decide who gives way. The TSS does not.

For joining from the side in the same scheme, a heading of about 060° is a small angle to the 045° flow. A heading of 090° is not.

Ship reporting systems and the standard format

Ship reporting systems exist for several reasons: safety of navigation, search and rescue, traffic management and pollution prevention.

Some are mandatory and IMO-adopted under SOLAS V/11. Examples include CALDOVREP (Dover Strait), OUESSREP (off Ushant), FINREP (off Finisterre), GIBREP (Strait of Gibraltar), STRAITREP (Straits of Malacca and Singapore) and REEFREP (Torres Strait and inner route of the Great Barrier Reef).

Others are voluntary. AMVER, the search and rescue system operated by the US Coast Guard, is one. There are also national systems, and these vary by administration. Whether such a system is mandatory for your ship often depends on your flag, your size or your cargo. Read the ALRS entry rather than assuming.

Resolution A.851(20) defines the report types:

  • Sailing plan (SP)
  • Position report (PR)
  • Deviation report (DR)
  • Final report (FR)
  • Dangerous goods (DG), harmful substances (HS) and marine pollutants (MP) reports
  • Any other report

The resolution also defines the standard format, which is a series of lettered lines. Each system specifies which letters it wants. Common ones include:

  • A: ship's name, call sign or ship station identity, and flag (many systems also ask for IMO number/MMSI)
  • B: date-time group
  • C: position by latitude and longitude
  • E: true course
  • F: speed
  • I: destination and ETA
  • O: present draught
  • P: cargo, including dangerous goods
  • Q: defects or deficiencies
  • W: persons on board

Worked example: timing a mandatory report

You are northbound at 13.5 knots. The ALRS says the report must be made by VHF on entering the system at a charted reporting line.

  1. The reporting line is 18 nm ahead. 18 / 13.5 = 1.33 h, which is 1 h 20 min. If it is 1340 UTC now, you will cross the line at about 1500 UTC.
  2. Prepare the report beforehand, using only the letters that system asks for. For example: A (name, call sign and flag, plus IMO number/MMSI if the system asks for them), B (date and 1500 UTC), C (position), E (course), F (13.5 kn), I (destination and ETA), O (maximum present draught), P (cargo and any DG) and W (persons on board).
  3. Monitor the listed channel well before the line. Call at the line, and log the time, the station and the information passed.
  4. If the system requires a deviation or final report, put a reminder on the ECDIS or in the passage plan.

AIS is required by SOLAS for most seagoing merchant ships (V/19.2.4), but it does not on its own satisfy a voice or written report unless the system's published requirements say it does.

Step-by-step: building it into the passage plan

Routeing and reporting belong in the appraisal stage, not in the moment you see the VTS boundary on the screen.

  1. Identify every routeing measure and reporting system on the route. Use the corrected charts, Sailing Directions, ALRS, Notices to Mariners and any Mariners' Routeing Guide.
  2. Check applicability. Does the system apply to your ship type, size, draught or cargo? Is it mandatory or recommended? Is it IMO-adopted, or a local scheme that may be subject to local rules made under Rule 1(b)? Check the chart notes and Sailing Directions.
  3. Plan the track. Use the correct lane, join or leave at terminations where possible, plan crossings on right-angle headings, and keep clear of areas to be avoided and no anchoring areas. Use a deep-water route only if your draught needs it.
  4. Mark reporting points on the chart or ECDIS. Note the VHF channels, the report format and the times required.
  5. Brief the bridge team, covering when and how each report is to be made and who makes it.
  6. Execute and monitor. Make the reports on time and record them. Report deviations as the system requires.
  7. If a compelling reason prevents use of a mandatory system, such as an emergency or immediate danger, act safely first. Then record the reasons and inform as required by the system and your company SMS.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a ship in the lane has right of way. It does not. The steering and sailing rules still apply, apart from the specific not-impede duties in Rule 10.
  • Crossing with a square track instead of a square heading. In a cross-tide this produces an oblique aspect that confuses the ships in the lane.
  • Using the ITZ as a short cut when through traffic could safely use the lane.
  • Assuming Rule 10 applies to every scheme on the chart. It applies to IMO-adopted schemes. Non-IMO schemes may be subject to local rules made under Rule 1(b). Check the chart notes and Sailing Directions.
  • Treating AIS as the report.
  • Working from an uncorrected ALRS or an old routeing guide, which leads to wrong channels and wrong reporting lines.
  • Anchoring near a TSS termination to wait for a berth.
  • Missing the final report, which leaves the shore authority with an open record.

Oral exam questions

Q: Who adopts routeing systems, and under what instrument? IMO, as the only international body for this purpose under SOLAS V/10, following the General Provisions on Ships' Routeing.

Q: How do you cross a TSS? Avoid crossing if practicable. If you must cross, do so on a heading as nearly as practicable at right angles to the general direction of traffic flow, and keep a full lookout because the steering rules still apply.

Q: Who may use an inshore traffic zone? Vessels under 20 m, sailing vessels and vessels engaged in fishing. Any vessel may use it when proceeding to or from a port, offshore installation, pilot station or place within the zone, or to avoid immediate danger.

Q: What is the difference between a recommended track and a recommended route? A recommended track is a route which has been specially examined to ensure so far as possible that it is free of dangers and along which ships are advised to navigate. A recommended route is a route of undefined width, often marked by centreline buoys, provided for the convenience of ships in transit.

Q: Where do you find the details of a reporting system? In the Admiralty List of Radio Signals, supported by chart notes and the Sailing Directions.

Q: Name the report types in the standard format. Sailing plan, position report, deviation report, final report, the dangerous goods, harmful substances and marine pollutants reports, and any other report.

Q: Does Rule 10 change Rule 15 inside a TSS? No. Rule 10 does not relieve any vessel of her obligations under any other rule.

Key points
  • SOLAS V/10 covers ships' routeing, V/11 covers ship reporting and V/12 covers VTS, and IMO is the only international body adopting routeing systems.
  • COLREG Rule 10 applies to IMO-adopted traffic separation schemes and does not replace the steering and sailing rules.
  • Cross a lane on a heading as nearly as practicable at right angles to the traffic flow, not on a square ground track.
  • Join or leave at terminations where possible, and at as small an angle as practicable when joining or leaving from the side.
  • Mandatory reporting systems must be complied with in full, and AIS alone does not count unless the system says so.
  • The ALRS, corrected charts and Sailing Directions are the on-board sources for routeing and reporting details.
  • Plan reporting points, channels and report content at the appraisal stage of the passage plan.

Frequently asked questions

Is it compulsory to use a traffic separation scheme?

COLREG Rule 10 does not force every ship into a TSS, but a ship using one must follow it, and a ship not using one must keep clear by as wide a margin as practicable. Some routeing systems are made mandatory under SOLAS V/10 for certain ships or cargoes. Check the chart notes and Sailing Directions for each scheme.

Does a ship following a traffic lane have right of way over a crossing ship?

No. Rule 10 does not relieve any vessel of her obligations under the other Rules, so crossing, head-on and overtaking situations are resolved by the steering and sailing rules. The exceptions are the duties of fishing vessels, sailing vessels and vessels under 20 m not to impede ships following a lane.

Where can I find the reporting requirements for a particular area?

The Admiralty List of Radio Signals gives the reporting systems, VTS details, channels and report formats. Chart notes and the Sailing Directions support it, and some busy areas also have Mariners' Routeing Guides. Always use the current, corrected edition.

Is AMVER mandatory?

AMVER is a voluntary search and rescue ship reporting system operated by the US Coast Guard. Some flag states and companies encourage or require participation through their own instructions. Check your flag administration's requirements and your company SMS.

Does AIS replace the reports required by a ship reporting system?

Not unless the published requirements of that system say so. Most mandatory systems still expect a voice or written report containing specific information, such as draught, cargo and persons on board. Read the ALRS entry for each system.

Test yourself

A power-driven vessel is obliged to cross a traffic lane in an IMO-adopted TSS. How should she cross?

Rule 10 specifies the heading, not the track, for crossing. A small angle applies to joining and leaving from the side (COLREG Rule 10(c) and 10(b)).

Which body is recognised as the only international body for developing ships' routeing systems?

SOLAS V/10 recognises IMO as the only international body for developing guidelines, criteria and regulations on ships' routeing at international level (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 10).

A 180 m container ship that could safely use the adjacent traffic lane may still use the inshore traffic zone when:

Rule 10(d) permits any vessel to use the ITZ when proceeding to or from a port, offshore installation, pilot station or other place within the zone, or to avoid immediate danger (COLREG Rule 10(d)).

Which instrument places the obligation on the master to comply with an IMO-adopted ship reporting system?

Ship reporting systems are covered by SOLAS V/11. Rule 10 governs conduct in a TSS, and V/19 covers carriage of navigational equipment (SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 11).

COLREG Rule 10 applies to which traffic separation schemes?

Rule 10 applies to traffic separation schemes adopted by IMO. Non-IMO schemes may be subject to local rules made under Rule 1(b), so check the chart notes and Sailing Directions (COLREG Rule 10(a) and Rule 1).
