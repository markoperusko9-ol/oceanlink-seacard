Dead reckoning and estimated position: method, worked example and oral exam questions

Quick answer

A dead reckoning (DR) position comes from the last known position, the true course steered and the distance run through the water. An estimated position (EP) adds the expected effect of leeway and current or tidal stream. Neither is a fix. The difference between your DR and your next fix tells you the set and drift you actually experienced.

What DR and EP actually mean

Examiners like this topic because candidates who navigate only by GNSS often blur the terms. Keep three ideas separate.

  • Fix: a position found from observation, such as cross bearings, radar ranges, celestial sights or GNSS. It is independent of where you think you are.
  • DR position: worked forward from a known position using only the true course steered and the distance run through the water, from the log or from engine revolutions and slip.
  • EP: the DR position corrected for the things you expect to push the ship off that line. These are leeway from wind and set and drift from current or tidal stream.

Textbooks and schools disagree on one point. Some apply leeway in the DR, giving a 'water track'. Most treat the DR as course steered only and put leeway and current into the EP. In the oral exam, say which convention you are using and stay consistent.

The point of both is the same. They tell you where the ship probably is, and how much to trust that, when you cannot get a fix or between fixes.

The inputs, and where the errors come from

A DR or EP is only as good as its inputs. Know each one and its weakness.

Input Source Typical weakness
True course steered Gyro or magnetic compass, corrected for error Uncorrected gyro error, or variation and deviation applied the wrong way
Distance through water Speed log, or revs and slip Log calibration, fouling, and logs that measure the wrong thing
Leeway Estimated from wind, draught, freeboard and speed It is a judgement, so it varies with loading condition
Current or tidal stream Tidal stream atlases, chart diamonds, Sailing Directions, routeing charts Predictions are averages, and actual stream varies with weather
Time Bridge clock Wrong interval, or a missed alteration time

Check which speed your log is giving you:

  • An electromagnetic log measures speed through the water.
  • A Doppler log may be in water-tracking or bottom-tracking mode. Bottom-tracking gives speed over the ground.
  • GNSS gives speed over the ground (SOG) and course over the ground (COG).

If you use a ground-referenced speed for a DR and then also apply the current, you count the current twice.

Apply leeway away from the wind. With the wind on the port side the ship is pushed to starboard, so the water track is greater than the course steered. With the wind on the starboard side the water track is less than the course steered.

Step-by-step: laying off DR and EP on a paper chart

  1. Start from a reliable position. This is normally your last fix, noted with its time.
  2. Convert the course to true. For a gyro, take off a high error and add a low error. For a magnetic compass, apply deviation and variation to get true.
  3. Work out the distance run. Use the log difference over the interval, or speed through the water multiplied by time.
  4. Lay off the course steered from the fix and mark the distance along it. That point is the DR. Label it with the time.
  5. Apply leeway if your convention puts it here. Re-lay the line along the water track.
  6. From the end of the water track, lay off the expected current or tidal stream. Use its set (the direction it flows towards) and its drift (the distance over the same interval). The end of that line is the EP.
  7. Join the start point to the EP. That line is the expected ground track. Its direction is the course made good and its length is the distance made good over the ground.
  8. When the next fix comes in, compare it with the DR. The vector from the DR to the fix is the set and drift experienced over the interval.

A commonly taught arrow convention is:

  • one arrowhead for the water track
  • two arrowheads for the ground track
  • three arrowheads for the tidal stream or current

Fixes are usually drawn as a circle and EPs as a triangle. Follow your company or school convention for symbols, and keep it consistent on the chart.

Worked example

Given:

  • At 0800 the fix is 10°00.0'N 070°00.0'E.
  • Gyro course 091°, gyro error 1° high, so the true course is 090°T.
  • Log speed through the water is 12.0 kn. Leeway is negligible.
  • The predicted current is 180°T at 2.0 kn.

DR at 1000:

  • Distance run is 12 × 2 = 24.0 nm due east. That is a departure of 24.0 nm.
  • d.long = departure ÷ cos lat = 24.0 ÷ cos 10° = 24.0 ÷ 0.9848 ≈ 24.4'.
  • DR = 10°00.0'N 070°24.4'E.

EP at 1000:

  • Current drift is 2 × 2 = 4.0 nm towards 180°, which is a d.lat of 4.0'S.
  • A current flowing due south adds no d.long, so the longitude is unchanged.
  • EP = 09°56.0'N 070°24.4'E.
  • Expected ground track: tan⁻¹(4 ÷ 24) ≈ 9.5°, so the course made good is about 099.5°T.
  • Distance made good is √(24² + 4²) ≈ 24.3 nm, so the speed made good is about 12.2 kn.

Fix at 1000: radar ranges put the ship at 09°55.0'N 070°23.0'E.

Set and drift experienced (DR to fix):

  • d.lat = 5.0'S.
  • d.long = 1.4'W, which gives a departure of 1.4 × cos 9.96° ≈ 1.38 nm W.
  • Set = S 15.4° W, or about 195°T.
  • Drift = √(5.0² + 1.38²) ≈ 5.2 nm in 2 hours, or about 2.6 kn.

What this tells you:

  • The real current was stronger and further west than predicted.
  • Use 195°T at 2.6 kn for the next EP until a new fix says otherwise.
  • Remember that 'set and drift' found this way includes every unaccounted error, not only current. Leeway, log error, steering error and any residual gyro error all end up in that vector.

DR and EP in the age of GNSS and ECDIS

GNSS has not made DR obsolete. It has made it a cross-check and a fallback.

  • Cross-checking GNSS. A GNSS position that suddenly jumps away from a sensible EP is a warning. The cause may be spoofing, jamming, a datum problem or a receiver fault. Compare COG and SOG with your heading and log speed too. A large unexplained difference needs investigating.
  • GNSS loss. Many ECDIS units fall back to a DR or EP mode using gyro and log inputs when the position source fails. How this works and what the display shows depend on the manufacturer. Know your equipment from the type-specific familiarisation.
  • Running fixes. Transferring an earlier position line uses exactly the same DR/EP reasoning. A running fix inherits every error in the course, distance and current you applied.
  • Passage planning. Good plans note predicted currents, set and drift, and areas where you would depend on DR, such as long open-sea legs or poor radar coast. Plans also note a fix interval suited to the proximity of danger.

In practice:

  • Plot an EP at regular intervals on open-ocean legs.
  • Plot one immediately when GNSS degrades.
  • Note in the log when you are navigating on DR or EP, so the next watch knows how much to trust the position.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the gyro error the wrong way. If the gyro reads high, the true course is less than the gyro reading.
  • Mixing speed references. Using GNSS SOG or a bottom-tracking Doppler log, then adding current on top.
  • Applying leeway towards the wind instead of away from it.
  • Reversing set. Set is the direction the current flows towards. Wind is named for the direction it comes from. Confusing the two puts the EP on the wrong side of the track.
  • Wrong time interval. Using the rate of drift without multiplying by elapsed time, or forgetting an alteration of course part-way through the interval.
  • Measuring set and drift from the EP to the fix. That only gives the error in your prediction. Measure from the DR to the fix to get the total effect actually experienced.
  • Treating an EP as a fix. An EP has an uncertainty that grows with time. Fix as soon as you can, especially near danger.
  • Ignoring departure versus d.long. On a paper chart the scale handles this. In a sailing calculation, forgetting to divide departure by cos lat gives a wrong longitude.

Oral exam questions and model answers

Q: Define a DR position and an EP. A: DR is the position from the last known position using the true course steered and the distance run through the water. EP is the DR corrected for estimated leeway and current or tidal stream.

Q: How do you find the current experienced on a passage? A: Compare the DR position for the time of the fix with the fix. The vector from DR to fix, divided by the elapsed time, gives the set and rate. It includes all unaccounted errors, not only current.

Q: Your GNSS fails mid-ocean. What do you do? A:

  • Note the time and last good position.
  • Inform the Master as per standing orders.
  • Switch to plotting EPs at regular intervals using gyro, log and the best available current information.
  • Use celestial or radar fixes when available, and log the situation.
  • Check whether the ECDIS has changed position source and what it is now displaying.

Q: Wind is on the starboard beam. Which way do you apply leeway? A: Away from the wind, so the water track is to port of the course steered. The water track angle is the course steered minus the leeway.

Q: Why might your EP be badly wrong even with good instruments? A:

  • The current predictions are averages.
  • Leeway is a judgement.
  • Log calibration may be off.
  • Steering yaw in a seaway shifts the average heading.

The uncertainty grows with time since the last fix.

Q: What is a running fix, and what is its weakness? A: A position line taken earlier is transferred along the course and distance run (DR or EP) to cross a later position line. Any error in course, distance or current applied goes straight into the running fix.

Key points
  • DR uses the true course steered and the distance through the water from the last known position, and nothing else.
  • EP is the DR corrected for estimated leeway and current or tidal stream; it is never a fix.
  • Set is the direction a current flows towards; apply leeway away from the wind.
  • Measure the vector from the DR to the fix to find the set and drift actually experienced.
  • Never combine a ground-referenced speed (GNSS SOG, bottom-tracking Doppler log) with an applied current.
  • Keep plotting EPs on long legs so a GNSS fault or spoofing stands out immediately.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a DR position and an estimated position?

A DR position uses only the true course steered and the distance run through the water from the last known position. An EP adds the expected effect of leeway and current or tidal stream. Some textbooks include leeway in the DR, so state your convention in the exam.

How do you calculate set and drift from a fix?

Work out the DR position for the time of the fix. Then measure the direction and distance from the DR to the fix. The direction is the set and the distance is the drift; divide the drift by the elapsed time for the rate in knots.

Is dead reckoning still needed with GPS and ECDIS?

Yes. DR and EP are your independent check on GNSS and your fallback if it is lost, jammed or spoofed. Many ECDIS units revert to a DR or EP mode on loss of position input, but you must understand what the system is doing and confirm it with your own plotting and fixes.

Which speed should I use for a DR, log speed or GPS speed?

Use speed through the water from the log, or revs and slip. GNSS SOG, and a Doppler log in bottom-tracking mode, give speed over the ground, which already includes the current. Using either for a DR and then adding current counts the current twice.

What symbols are used for DR, EP and fix on a chart?

A common convention is a circle for a fix and a triangle for an EP, with the DR marked on the course line and timed. Arrowheads are often one for the water track, two for the ground track and three for the current. Conventions vary between schools and companies, so follow your company's chartwork instructions.
