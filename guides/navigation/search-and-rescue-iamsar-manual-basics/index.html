Search and Rescue: IAMSAR Manual Basics

Quick answer

The IAMSAR Manual is the joint IMO/ICAO guidance on search and rescue. Volume III (Mobile Facilities) must be carried on board and tells the ship what to do on receiving a distress alert, how to work with the RCC, how to act as On-Scene Co-ordinator, and how to run standard search patterns. For your orals, you must know the SAR organisation, your SOLAS duty to assist, how datum is derived from drift, and when to use each search pattern.

What the IAMSAR Manual is and which volume you carry

The International Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue (IAMSAR) Manual is published jointly by IMO and ICAO. Ships and aircraft therefore work to the same terms, phases and procedures. It comes in three volumes:

Volume Title Main users
I Organization and Management Governments, SAR system managers
II Mission Co-ordination RCC staff and SAR mission planners
III Mobile Facilities Ships, aircraft, rescue units

SOLAS Chapter V requires ships to carry an up-to-date copy of Volume III. The manual is amended periodically. Check that the edition on the bridge is current and that any amendments are inserted. PSC inspectors and auditors do check this.

The legal framework underneath it is short:

  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 33 sets out the master's duty to respond to persons in distress.
  • SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 7 requires governments to provide SAR services.
  • The SAR Convention 1979 divides the oceans into search and rescue regions.
  • UNCLOS Article 98 contains the general duty to render assistance.

The SAR organisation: who's who on the radio

Learn these terms cold. Examiners use them, and so do RCCs on the radio.

  • SRR (Search and Rescue Region): an area of defined size for which one RCC is responsible.
  • RCC / MRCC / JRCC: the Rescue Co-ordination Centre (maritime, or joint aeronautical and maritime) that runs SAR in its SRR.
  • RSC (Rescue Sub-Centre): a subordinate unit under an RCC.
  • SMC (SAR Mission Co-ordinator): the person at the RCC who runs a particular case. The SMC is ashore.
  • OSC (On-Scene Co-ordinator): the unit designated to co-ordinate the units physically at the scene. This is often a merchant ship when no naval or coastguard unit is available.
  • ACO (Aircraft Co-ordinator): co-ordinates multiple aircraft over the scene.

The SMC normally designates the OSC. If no OSC has been designated, the first unit to arrive should assume the role until the SMC appoints someone. In practice that often means the master of the first merchant ship on scene.

RCCs also classify incidents into three emergency phases:

  • Uncertainty (INCERFA): doubt exists about a vessel's safety, for example an overdue report.
  • Alert (ALERFA): there is apprehension for safety.
  • Distress (DETRESFA): there is reasonable certainty of grave and imminent danger requiring immediate assistance.

Your duty and first actions on receiving a distress alert

Under SOLAS V/33, a master who receives information that persons are in distress at sea must proceed with all speed to assist them. The master should inform the persons in distress or the SAR services, if possible, that the ship is doing so.

If the ship is unable to go, or it would be unreasonable or unnecessary in the circumstances, the master must do two things:

  • enter the reason in the logbook
  • inform the appropriate SAR service.

Silence is never an option.

Typical sequence for the OOW and master, following the Volume III action checklists and your company SMS:

  1. Call the master and note the time. Record everything you receive: position, identity, nature of distress, persons on board, and the type of alert (DSC, EPIRB, voice, flare).
  2. Handle acknowledgement and relay by the GMDSS guidance. Ships should not casually acknowledge or relay a DSC distress alert by DSC. Coast stations and RCCs normally acknowledge first. A ship close to the casualty generally acknowledges by voice on the associated distress frequency. Follow the DSC flow charts on board for VHF, MF and HF.
  3. Contact the RCC responsible for the area. Pass your position, speed, ETA to the scene and your capabilities.
  4. Plot the casualty. Work out your course, ETA and the positions of other ships on AIS and radar. Start a dedicated log of the case.
  5. Prepare the ship on passage. Prepare the following:
    • extra lookouts with binoculars, and searchlights
    • rescue boat crew
    • scrambling nets, pilot ladder, heaving lines and lifebuoys
    • a recovery station on the lee side with the lowest practical freeboard
    • blankets and first aid, and a hospital space
    • a radar watch tuned for small targets, and SART detection.
  6. Keep the RCC updated with situation reports (SITREPs). Volume III gives a standard SITREP format.

Datum, drift and the search area

The datum is the most probable location of the search object at a given time, after correcting for drift. Survivors move away from the last known position (LKP), so you must not simply steam to the LKP.

Total drift is made up of two components:

  • Total water current: the combined effect of all currents acting on the search object. This means sea current and wind current, plus tidal current and any other local current in coastal waters. This moves everything in the water.
  • Leeway: the movement of the object through the water caused by wind acting on its exposed parts. A liferaft with a canopy has much more leeway than a person in the water. Leeway is not always exactly downwind; it can diverge either side.

Usually the SMC calculates the datum and search area and passes them to the OSC. If you are OSC and nothing has come from the RCC, you work out a datum yourself using the best current and wind information you have.

The commence search point (CSP) is where the pattern begins. For expanding square and sector searches, the CSP is the datum itself.

Search patterns in Volume III and when to use them

Pattern Use when Key features
Expanding square (SS) Location known within close limits; small area; single ship Starts at datum. All turns are 90° in the same direction. Legs grow as S, S, 2S, 2S, 3S, 3S..., where S is the track spacing. Needs accurate navigation, especially on the short early legs.
Sector search (VS) Position known accurately, small area, e.g. MOB seen to go over Starts at datum. All turns are 120° to starboard, so each leg equals the radius and the ship passes back through datum repeatedly. A marker (smoke float or lifebuoy light) is often dropped at datum.
Parallel track (PS) Large area; location uncertain; one or more ships Straight parallel legs at spacing S. Several ships can search in line abreast at the assigned spacing.
Track line (TS) Craft missing along a known route Search along and beside the intended track of the missing craft, often as a first quick look.

Track spacing (S) depends on the search object, the visibility, sea state and the height of eye. Volume III contains recommended track spacing tables for merchant ships. Use the tables in your copy, or the spacing the SMC or OSC assigns, rather than a remembered figure. Volume III also covers co-ordinated searches by ships and aircraft together.

Worked example: datum and an expanding square search

Situation. A yacht's crew abandoned to a liferaft. Their last known position (LKP) was reported at 0600. Your ship can reach the area at 1000, so the drift time is 4 hours. The RCC has not yet issued a datum and you are designated OSC. Your best information is:

  • total water current: 090° (T) at 1.0 kn
  • liferaft leeway: taken as 000° (T) at 0.5 kn for this illustration.

Step 1: drift distances over 4 h.

  • Current: 4 × 1.0 = 4.0 NM east.
  • Leeway: 4 × 0.5 = 2.0 NM north.

Step 2: resultant drift.

  • Distance = √(4² + 2²) = √20 ≈ 4.5 NM.
  • Direction = tan⁻¹(4 ÷ 2) ≈ 063° (T).
  • So the datum bears 063° (T), 4.5 NM from the LKP, for 1000.

Step 3: choose the pattern. The area is small and the position is reasonably well known, so use an expanding square from datum. Suppose the track spacing from the Volume III table for your conditions is 1.0 NM. This is an assumed figure for the example only.

Step 4: the legs. The legs run 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4 NM, a total of 20 NM. At a search speed of 10 kn, those eight legs take 2 hours.

Step 5: report. Send a SITREP to the RCC giving:

  • the datum used
  • the pattern, track spacing and CSP
  • the time you commenced
  • the weather on scene.

If the search drags on, remember that the datum keeps moving. Update it, or ask the SMC for a revised datum.

Common mistakes and practical lessons

  • Steaming to the LKP and searching there. Always correct for drift and keep updating it.
  • Relaying or acknowledging DSC alerts incorrectly. Unnecessary DSC relays and acknowledgements cause confusion and false alerts. Follow the on-board GMDSS guidance.
  • Not logging the decision. If you do not proceed, the log entry and the message to the RCC are mandatory, not optional.
  • Poor navigation on expanding square legs. The short early legs are exactly where an error of a cable or two leaves gaps in coverage.
  • Unplanned recovery. A big ship is a hard platform to recover people onto. Plan to make a lee, use nets or the rescue boat, and keep survivors horizontal where possible, because hypothermia victims can collapse when lifted vertically.
  • Not debriefing survivors. Survivors may tell you that others are still missing, or describe the other craft involved.

Oral exam questions you should expect

  • Which IAMSAR volume is carried on board, and why? Volume III, Mobile Facilities. SOLAS Chapter V requires it, and it is the volume written for ships and aircraft.
  • Difference between SMC and OSC? The SMC co-ordinates the whole case from the RCC ashore. The OSC co-ordinates the units actually on scene, normally as designated by the SMC.
  • What are the emergency phases? Uncertainty, Alert and Distress.
  • What is datum and how do you find it? Datum is the most probable position of the search object, corrected for drift. You apply total water current and leeway over the drift time to the LKP.
  • Expanding square vs sector search? Both start at datum and suit a small, well-defined area. The expanding square uses 90° turns with legs growing in multiples of S. The sector search uses 120° turns to starboard, with every leg equal to the radius, and is good when the position is known accurately.
  • You cannot proceed to a distress. What do you do? Inform the SAR services and enter the reasons in the logbook, as SOLAS V/33 requires.
  • Which manoeuvres does Volume III cover for your own man overboard? The Williamson, Anderson and Scharnow turns. Be ready to explain which suits an immediate action and which suits a delayed action.
Key points
  • IAMSAR has three volumes; ships must carry an up-to-date Volume III (Mobile Facilities) under SOLAS Chapter V.
  • SOLAS V/33 obliges the master to proceed with all speed to persons in distress, or log the reason and inform SAR services if not.
  • The SMC runs the case from the RCC; the OSC co-ordinates units on scene; the first ship on scene may have to act as OSC until designated.
  • Datum is the most probable position corrected for drift (total water current plus leeway), not the last known position.
  • Expanding square uses 90° turns with legs S, S, 2S, 2S...; sector search uses 120° turns to starboard with legs equal to the radius.
  • Take track spacing from the Volume III tables or as assigned by the SMC/OSC, not from memory.
  • Follow GMDSS guidance on DSC acknowledgement and relay, and keep the RCC updated with SITREPs.

Frequently asked questions

Is it compulsory to carry the IAMSAR Manual on board?

Yes. SOLAS Chapter V requires ships to carry an up-to-date copy of IAMSAR Volume III. Check your flag administration's requirements on the accepted format, such as paper or electronic. Also check that the edition and amendments are current.

What is the difference between an RCC, MRCC and JRCC?

All are rescue co-ordination centres responsible for SAR in a search and rescue region. An MRCC handles maritime SAR. A JRCC handles both aeronautical and maritime SAR. Which type exists, and where, depends on how each coastal state has organised its SAR services.

Does a merchant ship have to divert for every distress alert?

Under SOLAS V/33 the master must proceed with all speed once he receives information that persons are in distress. The exception is where he is unable to, or it is unreasonable or unnecessary in the circumstances. In that case the reason must be entered in the logbook and the SAR services informed. The RCC may also release a ship once enough units are assisting.

When should I use a sector search instead of an expanding square?

Use a sector search when the position of the object is known accurately and the area is small. A man overboard seen to go over the side is the classic case. Use the expanding square when the position is known within close limits but less precisely. Both patterns start from datum.

Who decides the search pattern and track spacing?

Normally the SMC at the RCC plans the search and passes it to the OSC. If you are OSC and no plan has arrived, you choose a suitable pattern using Volume III. You take the track spacing from its tables for your search object and conditions, and you report your plan to the RCC.

Test yourself

Which volume of the IAMSAR Manual must be carried on board ships?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 21 requires ships to carry an up-to-date copy of Volume III. Volumes I and II are aimed at SAR administrators and RCC staff.

How are the turns made in an expanding square search?

The expanding square uses 90° turns in one direction, with legs increasing as S, S, 2S, 2S and so on. 120° turns belong to the sector search (IAMSAR Manual Vol. III).

In IAMSAR terms, what is 'datum'?

Datum is the LKP corrected for total water current and leeway over the drift time. It moves as time passes (IAMSAR Manual Vol. III).

A master receives a distress alert but decides the ship cannot reasonably proceed. What must he do?

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 33 requires the reason for not proceeding to be logged and the SAR services informed. Unnecessary DSC relays are discouraged by GMDSS guidance.

Who normally designates the On-Scene Co-ordinator (OSC)?

The SMC designates the OSC. Until that happens, the first suitable unit on scene should assume the role (IAMSAR Manual Vol. III).
