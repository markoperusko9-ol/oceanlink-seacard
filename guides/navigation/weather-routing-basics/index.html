Weather Routing Basics: Principles, Method and Oral Exam Questions

Quick answer

Weather routing means choosing and adjusting an ocean route using weather and sea forecasts, current data and your own ship's performance, so the passage is as safe and efficient as possible. It can be done on board or advised by a shore routing service, but the master always keeps the final decision. For the exam, you need to understand the principles, the isochrone method and when to trust or question the routing advice.

What weather routing is, and what it is not

IMO's recommendation on weather routeing (Resolution A.528(13)) describes it as working out an optimum route for a particular passage. The route is based on weather and sea forecasts, the ship's characteristics and any special requirements of the voyage. IMO documents spell it "routeing", and you will see both spellings on board.

"Optimum" does not always mean shortest. Depending on the objective, the best route might be:

  • Least time: arrive as early as possible.
  • Least fuel / fixed ETA: arrive on a required date using the least fuel.
  • Minimum risk / minimum damage: reduce motions, slamming, green water and cargo shifting. This matters for deck cargo, livestock, heavy lifts or a ship with known structural concerns.
  • A combination: for example, least fuel while keeping significant wave height below a stated limit.

Weather routing is not a substitute for passage planning or for good seamanship. It is one input into the plan required by SOLAS Chapter V. A routed track still has to be checked like any other track for:

  • navigational dangers
  • load line zones
  • emission control areas
  • ice limits
  • piracy high-risk areas
  • traffic separation schemes

Climatological versus forecast-based routeing

There are two layers, and examiners like candidates who can separate them.

Climatological (planning) routeing uses long-term average conditions. Typical sources are:

  • Admiralty Routeing Charts or Pilot Charts
  • Ocean Passages for the World
  • Sailing Directions

These show the following, by month:

  • prevailing winds and currents
  • frequency of gales
  • tropical storm tracks and seasons
  • ice limits
  • load line zones

You use them at the appraisal stage to choose a sensible basic route for the season.

Forecast-based (synoptic or tactical) routeing uses the actual forecast for the days ahead. The sources are:

  • official forecasts and warnings, broadcast through the GMDSS (SafetyNET and NAVTEX)
  • weather fax
  • GRIB model output
  • the routing service's own advice

The route is then modified as the forecast changes.

The key limitation is forecast reliability. Forecast skill falls off with lead time. Experienced masters therefore treat the first two or three days as fairly firm and the later days as a trend only. The route should be re-evaluated whenever a new forecast arrives. Routing for a 12-day ocean crossing is never a one-off decision.

Ship performance: the part cadets forget

A forecast is useless until you know what it does to your ship. Routing depends on performance data, usually in the form of curves or tables. These show how speed changes with:

  • Wave height and direction relative to the ship: head, bow, beam, quartering and following seas. Head seas usually cost the most speed. Following seas usually cost little speed, but bring their own dangers.
  • Loading condition: loaded or ballast, draught and trim. A ballast ship with shallow forward draught slams earlier.
  • Hull and propeller condition: fouling adds resistance.
  • Swell versus wind waves: a long swell from ahead can cause heavy pitching even in light winds.

Two kinds of speed loss matter:

  • Involuntary speed loss comes from added resistance at constant engine power.
  • Voluntary speed loss happens when you reduce power yourself to stop slamming, propeller racing, green water or violent rolling.

A good routing decision accounts for both.

Stability and motions also count. IMO's revised guidance to the master on adverse weather (MSC.1/Circ.1228) explains the main dangers in following and quartering seas:

  • surf-riding
  • broaching
  • parametric rolling
  • synchronous rolling

A route that saves six hours but puts a large container ship in conditions prone to parametric rolling is not optimum.

The isochrone method, step by step

Routing software does this automatically. You should still be able to explain the logic, because examiners ask for it and because it helps you sanity-check the software.

  1. Set the objective and limits. Decide on least time, least fuel or a fixed ETA. Set the maximum acceptable wave height and any no-go areas, such as land, shoals, ice, ECA boundaries, piracy areas or load line zone limits.
  2. Take the departure position and the time step. The step is commonly 6, 12 or 24 hours.
  3. Fan out headings. From departure, draw a spread of tracks either side of the great circle.
  4. Find each track's distance for the step. For each track, read the forecast wind, waves and current for that time and area. Apply the performance curve to get speed made good, then speed × time = distance.
  5. Join the end points. The outer envelope of these points is the first isochrone: the line of positions the ship can reach in the same time.
  6. Repeat. Treat points along each isochrone as new start points and use the forecast valid for the next time step.
  7. Trace the least-time track. When an isochrone reaches the destination, trace back to departure through the points that produced it. That track is the least-time track.
  8. Check the route. Test it against the passage plan, stability limits, ECAs and load line zones. Re-run it with every new forecast.

For least-fuel or fixed-ETA routing, the same idea applies with power or speed as a variable. The question becomes which route and speed profile reach the destination on time for the least fuel.

Worked example: direct track or diversion?

The figures below are illustrative and kept simple so the arithmetic checks.

Situation. A loaded bulk carrier makes 14 kn in calm water at service power. Main engine consumption at that power is 30 t/day, assumed constant because the engine is kept at constant power. Two options exist:

Route A (direct) Route B (diversion south)
Distance 1,680 nm 1,820 nm
Weather 48 h of 5–6 m head seas, then fair Moderate seas throughout
Speed (from performance curves) 7 kn in heavy weather, 14 kn after 13 kn average

Route A - Heavy-weather segment: 48 h × 7 kn = 336 nm - Remaining distance: 1,680 − 336 = 1,344 nm at 14 kn = 96 h - Total time: 48 + 96 = 144 h (6.0 days) - Fuel: 6.0 × 30 = 180 t

Route B - Time: 1,820 ÷ 13 = 140 h (about 5.83 days) - Fuel: 5.83 × 30 ≈ 175 t

Conclusion. Route B is 140 nm longer, yet it arrives about 4 h earlier, burns about 5 t less fuel and avoids two days of slamming and possible heavy-weather damage.

What a good officer then asks:

  • How firm is the forecast? The heavy weather on Route A falls in the first 48 hours (days 1 and 2), which is fairly reliable. If it were forecast for the last days of the passage, the comparison would rest on a much less reliable forecast and the saving would be speculative.
  • Is the 7 kn figure realistic? Would the master reduce speed further to stop slamming? If so, Route A gets worse.
  • Does Route B cross anything new? Check for a different load line zone, an ECA (requiring fuel changeover), shoals or heavier traffic.
  • What is the escape option? If the low tracks south faster than forecast, what can you do on Route B?

Shore routing services and the master's authority

Many owners and charterers contract a commercial routing service. Typically the ship sends its intended route, loading condition and requirements before departure. The service then:

  • recommends an initial route
  • sends updates as forecasts change
  • may produce a post-voyage performance analysis

Two things to understand clearly:

  • The master decides. SOLAS Chapter V (Regulation 34-1) protects the master's professional judgement on safe navigation from interference by the owner, charterer or anyone else. Routing advice is advice. If what you see on board, such as a sea state worse than forecast or a falling barometer, contradicts it, act on safety and tell the service.
  • Records matter commercially. Charter parties often contain speed and consumption warranties measured in "good weather" conditions, and the definition varies from one charter party to another. Routing services may analyse performance using their own weather data. Accurate deck log entries are your evidence. Record wind force and direction, sea and swell, speed reductions and the reasons for them.

Whatever a company's routing procedure says, it sits inside the ship's SMS. Follow your company's instructions on reporting, deviation and communication.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Mistakes seen at sea and in the exam room

  • Treating GRIB files as forecasts. GRIB data is raw model output with no forecaster's judgement. Coarse grids may underestimate tropical cyclone intensity and small, intense lows. Official warnings broadcast for the METAREA take precedence.
  • Plotting the routed track without checking it. Shoals, ECAs, ice limits and load line seasonal zones still apply. A northern diversion in winter can put a ship in a zone where her draught is non-compliant.
  • Looking only at wind. Swell direction and period often decide ship motion.
  • Using wrong performance data. Ballast curves applied to a loaded ship, or no allowance for fouling, give the wrong answer.
  • Never updating the route. A route fixed at departure on a long crossing is not weather routing.
  • Getting too close to tropical cyclones. Trying to slip past is dangerous. Allow for forecast track error and follow company TRS guidance.

Typical oral questions

  1. What is weather routing and who is responsible for the final route? Answer: optimising the route using forecasts and ship performance. The master is responsible.
  2. What is the difference between climatological and forecast-based routing? Answer: long-term averages for planning, against actual forecasts for the voyage.
  3. Explain an isochrone. Answer: a line joining all positions reachable in the same time from departure. Then describe the step-by-step build-up.
  4. Where do you get weather information at sea? Answer: GMDSS SafetyNET and NAVTEX, weather fax, routing service, GRIB, and your own observations.
  5. You disagree with the routing service's recommendation. What do you do? Answer: assess, act for the safety of the ship, inform the service and the company, and log your reasons.
  6. What dangers exist in following seas? Answer: surf-riding and broaching-to, reduction of intact stability when riding a wave crest, synchronous rolling and parametric rolling, as described in MSC.1/Circ.1228.
Key points
  • Weather routing optimises the route for a stated objective (time, fuel, safety or a fixed ETA), not simply the shortest distance.
  • Climatological routeing sets the seasonal plan; forecast-based routing adjusts it as forecasts update.
  • Ship performance curves turn a forecast into speed made good, so wrong curves mean a wrong route.
  • An isochrone joins all positions reachable in the same time; the least-time track is traced back from the destination.
  • Every routed track must still be checked for dangers, ECAs, ice and load line zones.
  • Routing services advise; the master decides and records the reasons in the log.
  • GRIB data is raw model output, so official GMDSS warnings take precedence.

Frequently asked questions

Is weather routing mandatory for merchant ships?

SOLAS Chapter V requires a passage plan that takes weather into account, but there is no general international requirement to use a commercial routing service. Whether one is used depends on the company SMS and the charter party. Many owners and charterers contract one as standard on ocean passages.

Can I follow GRIB files instead of official forecasts?

GRIB files are useful for visualising model output, but they are raw computer data without a forecaster's review and can underestimate intense systems. Official forecasts and warnings broadcast for your METAREA via SafetyNET or NAVTEX should take precedence. Use GRIB as a supplement, not a replacement.

What should I do if the routing service advice looks unsafe?

Assess the conditions on board, act in the interest of safety and inform the service and your company. SOLAS Chapter V protects the master's discretion on safe navigation. Record your observations and reasons in the deck log.

How far ahead can a weather route be trusted?

Forecast accuracy falls with lead time. The first few days are usually reasonably reliable, while the later days should be treated as a trend. That is why the route must be re-evaluated whenever a new forecast arrives.

Why does a longer route sometimes arrive earlier?

Head seas cause large speed loss, both from added resistance and from voluntary speed reduction to stop slamming. A diversion of extra miles in moderate weather can therefore give a higher average speed and an earlier arrival. It often uses less fuel and causes less damage as well.
