Compass error: deviation and variation

Quick answer

Compass error is the angle between true north and the direction your magnetic compass points. It is the algebraic sum of variation, which comes from the Earth's field and is read from the chart, and deviation, which comes from the ship's own magnetism and changes with the ship's head. Apply easterly error by adding when converting compass to true and westerly error by subtracting, and check the error regularly (typically each watch and after major course alterations, per your SMS) by transit, azimuth, amplitude or gyro comparison.

The three norths and why the compass is never quite right

A magnetic compass does not point to true north. Two separate effects pull it off, and you need to keep them apart in your head.

  • True north (T) is the direction of the geographic pole. Charts, GNSS positions and the gyro (once corrected) work in true.
  • Magnetic north (M) is the direction a freely suspended needle would point at your position under the Earth's field alone.
  • Compass north (C) is where your ship's compass actually points. The Earth's field and the ship's own magnetism together set that direction.

The angles between them have names:

Angle Between Caused by Depends on
Variation True and Magnetic Earth's magnetic field Position and year
Deviation Magnetic and Compass Ship's own magnetism Ship's head, plus other factors
Compass error True and Compass Both combined All of the above

Each angle is named East if the north it points to lies east of the north before it, and West if it lies to the west. The order is True to Magnetic for variation, and Magnetic to Compass for deviation.

Variation: reading it off the chart and bringing it up to date

Variation comes from the Earth, so the ship cannot change it. It changes slowly over the years, and it changes with your position.

Where to find it - On paper charts, look at the compass rose. It gives the variation for a stated year and the annual change, East or West. - Small-scale charts and some publications show isogonic lines, which are lines of equal variation. - ECDIS and many GNSS receivers compute variation from a magnetic model. Know which source your bridge equipment is using. - Charts and Sailing Directions note local magnetic anomalies. In those areas the magnetic compass can be badly disturbed regardless of what the rose says.

Updating the rose value 1. Note the variation and its base year from the rose. 2. Multiply the annual change by the number of years since that base year. 3. Apply the result in the direction stated. Watch for the annual change being opposite in name to the variation, because then the variation is decreasing. 4. Use the rose nearest your position.

Example: the rose reads 4°20'W (2020), annual change 8'E. For 2025, the change is 5 × 8' = 40'E. The variation is now 4°20'W − 40' = 3°40'W.

Deviation: the ship's own magnetism

Deviation is caused by the ship's steel and electrical fields acting on the compass. Because that magnetism is fixed to the ship, its effect changes as the ship swings. Deviation depends on the ship's head, not on the direction of the object you are bearing.

The magnetism comes from two sources:

  • Permanent magnetism (hard iron) is built into the hull during construction.
  • Induced magnetism (soft iron) is induced in the steel by the Earth's field. It changes with heading and with magnetic latitude.

A compass adjuster reduces deviation with correctors, such as fore-and-aft and athwartships magnets, quadrantal spheres, the Flinders bar and the heeling magnet. The adjuster then swings the ship and records the residual deviation on a deviation card (table or curve). SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires a properly adjusted standard magnetic compass (or other means independent of any power supply) and a means of correcting heading and bearings to true at all times. How often the compass must be adjusted, and what triggers a re-swing, depends on the flag administration and the company SMS. Check yours rather than assuming.

Deviation can change from what the card shows when: - the ship makes a large change in magnetic latitude - steel cargo is loaded or discharged, or containers are stowed near the binnacle - hot work, structural repairs or new equipment are fitted near the compass - the ship has steamed on one heading for a long time (retained magnetism) - the ship heels or rolls (heeling error) - portable electrical gear or speakers are left close to the binnacle

The standard compass and the steering compass sit in different places, so each has its own deviation. Do not use one card for both.

Combining and applying the errors: the step-by-step method

Combining variation and deviation - If they have the same name, add them. The error takes that name. - If they have different names, subtract the smaller from the larger. The error takes the name of the larger.

Applying the error - Compass to True: add Easterly, subtract Westerly error. - True to Compass: do the reverse. - Memory aids: "Error East, compass least; error West, compass best" and CADET (Compass to true, ADd East). The full sequence is True – Variation – Magnetic – Deviation – Compass.

Converting compass to true 1. Note the compass heading. If you are converting a bearing, note the compass heading at the moment the bearing was taken. 2. Enter the deviation card with the ship's head. Check whether the card is tabulated by compass or by magnetic heading, and interpolate. 3. Get the variation from the chart, updated for the year. 4. Combine them to get the compass error. 5. Apply it: add East, subtract West.

Converting true to compass (course to steer) 1. Apply variation to the true course to get the magnetic course. 2. Find the deviation for that heading. If the card is by compass heading, take a first estimate, then re-enter with the resulting compass course. Repeat until the value stops changing. 3. Apply deviation in reverse: subtract East, add West.

Worked examples

Example 1: Compass course to true

Variation is 3°40'W (from the updated rose above). The ship's head is 095°(C) by standard compass. The deviation card, by compass heading, gives 2°E on 090°(C) and 3°E on 100°(C). Interpolating gives 2°30'E.

  • Compass error = 3°40'W and 2°30'E, which are different names. Subtract to get 1°10'W.
  • The error is West, so subtract: 095°00' − 1°10' = 093°50'(T).

Check: with a westerly error the compass should read more than true. 095° is more than 093°50'. Correct.

Example 2: Finding deviation from a transit

The ship's head is 180°(C). Two charted marks come into line, and the chart gives the transit bearing as 212°00'(T). The standard compass bearing of the transit at that moment is 214°30'(C). Variation is 3°40'W.

  • Compass error = 214°30' − 212°00' = 2°30'. The compass reads high, so the error is West (compass best).
  • Deviation = compass error minus variation, algebraically: 2°30'W − 3°40'W = 1°10'E.

Check: 3°40'W combined with 1°10'E gives 2°30'W. Correct.

Record this as the deviation for ship's head 180°(C). It is not the deviation for 214°.

Example 3: Gyro comparison

The gyro reads 092°, and the gyro error found from an azimuth is 1° Low, so the true heading is 093°. The standard compass reads 095°(C), so the compass error is 2°W. With variation 3°40'W, the deviation on that heading is 1°40'E. This is the routine way to check the magnetic compass on a modern bridge. Compare the deviation you get with the card, and investigate any significant difference.

Ways to find compass error at sea and on the coast

The STCW Code (Table A-II/1) expects an OOW to determine the errors of the magnetic and gyro compasses by both celestial and terrestrial means, and to allow for them. Company SMS procedures typically require errors to be checked each watch and after major alterations of course. Follow your own SMS for the exact frequency and how to log it.

  • Transit bearings use two charted objects in line. This is the most reliable coastal method and needs no time or almanac.
  • Bearing of a distant charted object from a known position. Only use this when the position is accurate and the object is far enough away that small position errors do not matter.
  • Azimuth of a celestial body, by sun, star or planet, using tables or a calculator.
  • Amplitude of the sun at theoretical rising or setting. Note when the observation is made at visible rather than theoretical rising.
  • Comparison with the gyro, once the gyro error has been found.

Whichever method you use, log the ship's head, observed bearing, true bearing, compass error, variation and resulting deviation in the compass observation book or equivalent record. A sudden jump in deviation tells you something has changed near the compass.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Mistakes examiners see over and over - Entering the deviation card with the bearing of the object instead of the ship's head. - Using the rose variation without applying the annual change. - Applying the error the wrong way. Always sense-check: with a West error the compass reads higher than true. - Mixing up gyro error (High/Low) with magnetic compass error (East/West). - Using the standard compass card for the steering compass. - Forgetting that the card may be by magnetic heading rather than compass heading. - Trusting the compass inside a charted local magnetic anomaly.

Typical oral questions and the core of a good answer - Define variation and deviation. Variation is the angle between true and magnetic meridians, caused by the Earth's field. Deviation is the angle between the magnetic meridian and the compass needle, caused by the ship's magnetism. - Why does deviation change with heading but variation does not? The ship's magnetism moves with the ship. The Earth's field at a position does not. - You take a compass bearing of a light. Which deviation do you apply? The deviation for the ship's head at that moment. - What can change deviation after the card was made? Change of magnetic latitude, cargo, repairs or hot work, retained magnetism, heel, and electrical equipment. - How would you find compass error with no land in sight? Use a celestial azimuth or amplitude, or compare with the gyro once gyro error is known. - Why carry a magnetic compass at all when we have a gyro and GNSS? It needs no electrical power and works independently, so it stays available when everything else fails.

Practise saying each answer in one or two clear sentences. Then be ready to work a numerical example on paper in front of the examiner.

Key points
  • Compass error is the algebraic sum of variation and deviation: add same names, subtract different names.
  • Variation comes from the chart compass rose and must be updated using the annual change.
  • Deviation comes from the ship's magnetism and depends on the ship's head, not the bearing of the object.
  • Compass to true: add Easterly, subtract Westerly error. Error East, compass least; error West, compass best.
  • Check compass error regularly by transit, azimuth, amplitude or gyro comparison, and log the result.
  • Standard and steering compasses have different deviations and need their own cards.
  • Re-check deviation after changes of magnetic latitude, steel cargo, repairs or long periods on one heading.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between deviation and variation?

Variation is the angle between true north and magnetic north. It is caused by the Earth's magnetic field and depends on your position and the year. Deviation is the angle between magnetic north and compass north. It is caused by the ship's own magnetism and depends mainly on the ship's head.

How do I calculate compass error from variation and deviation?

If both have the same name, add them and keep that name. If one is East and the other West, subtract the smaller from the larger and give the result the name of the larger. For example, 5°W variation and 2°E deviation give a compass error of 3°W.

Do I add or subtract an easterly error when converting compass to true?

Add it. When converting compass to true, add easterly error and subtract westerly error. Going from true to compass, do the reverse. A quick check: with westerly error the compass reading should be higher than the true value.

Why does the deviation card use the ship's heading?

The magnetism that causes deviation is fixed in the ship's structure. As the ship turns, that magnetism lines up differently with the Earth's field, so deviation changes with heading. Every bearing taken on a given heading carries the same deviation, whatever direction the object lies in.

How often should the magnetic compass be adjusted and swung?

This is set by the flag administration and the company SMS, so check your own requirements rather than relying on a general rule. As a matter of practice, it should also be re-adjusted when observed deviations differ significantly from the card, for example after structural work, major repairs or new equipment fitted near the compass.

Test yourself

The compass course is 120°(C) and the compass error is 3°E. What is the true course?

Compass to true, add East: 120° + 3° = 123°(T). This is the competence in STCW Code Table A-II/1 to allow for compass errors.

Deviation of a ship's magnetic compass depends mainly on which of the following?

Deviation comes from the ship's own magnetism, which moves with the ship, so it changes with heading. That is why the deviation card is tabulated against ship's head.

Variation is 5°W and deviation is 2°E. What is the compass error?

The names are different, so subtract: 5° − 2° = 3°, named after the larger, giving 3°W.

You take a compass bearing of a lighthouse. Which deviation should you apply to correct it?

Every bearing taken on a given heading carries the deviation for that ship's head. This is the standard terrestrial correction expected under STCW Code Table A-II/1.

Where does the OOW normally obtain the value of variation for the ship's position?

Variation is a property of the Earth's field at a position. Charts give it with a base year and annual change, and the OOW applies that change to bring it up to date.
