Chartwork basics: position fixing methods

Quick answer

A fix is a position found from two or more simultaneous position lines that cross at a good angle. The main methods are cross bearings, bearing and range, radar ranges, transits, sextant angles and the running fix, and examiners expect you to choose the right one, plot it correctly and know its weaknesses. GNSS should be cross-checked against an independent method whenever one is available.

What a fix is, and what it is not

A position line is any line, straight or curved, on which the ship is known to lie at a given moment. Examples are a bearing of a lighthouse, a range circle from a headland, or a transit of two leading marks. A fix is where two or more position lines taken at the same time intersect.

Keep these three terms separate on the chart and in your answers:

  • DR (dead reckoning) position: course steered and speed through the water from the last fix, with no allowance for set, drift or leeway.
  • EP (estimated position): the DR adjusted for estimated tidal stream, current and leeway.
  • Fix: a position obtained from external references, such as terrestrial marks, radar, celestial bodies or GNSS.

Common chart conventions are:

  • a circle with a dot for a fix;
  • a triangle for an EP;
  • a short line across the track for a DR.

Your company SMS or bridge procedures manual may set its own symbols and labelling, so follow those on board. Always mark the time against every fix. If the fix was not visual, note the method too.

The watchkeeping principles in the STCW Code require the officer of the watch to check the course, position and speed at sufficiently frequent intervals, using any available navigational aids necessary, to ensure the ship follows the planned course. GNSS is therefore not a fix you simply accept. It is one input, and you cross-check it with an independent method whenever one is available.

Choosing marks. Use conspicuous, charted, fixed objects, such as lighthouses, beacons, sharp headlands and charted towers.

  • Avoid buoys for fixing. They can drag or be off station.
  • Prefer nearer objects. The same angular error gives a smaller linear error when the object is close.

Step-by-step: a three-bearing visual fix

  1. Identify at least three charted objects. If you are unsure, check the light characteristics against the chart and the list of lights.
  2. Pick objects that give a good angle of cut. With two bearings, aim for about 90° between them. With three, aim for about 60°. Treat cuts below about 30° with suspicion.
  3. Take bearings in the right order. Take objects near the bow or stern first, because their bearings change slowly. Take objects near the beam last, because their bearings change fastest. The time of the last bearing is the time of the fix, so note it.
  4. Apply compass error to get true bearings. For gyro bearings, apply gyro error. For a magnetic or standard compass, apply variation and deviation.
  5. Plot each true bearing as a line drawn from the object, on the reciprocal of what you observed, towards the ship. Only draw the part near your expected position.
  6. Assess the result. Three lines rarely meet at a point. They usually form a small triangle, the "cocked hat".
  7. Compare with the EP. If the fix is well away from the EP, find out why before you trust either.

Worked example: applying gyro error. The gyro error found from the last azimuth is 2° High. The observed gyro bearings are:

  • Lighthouse A: 047°(G)
  • Tower B: 318°(G)
  • Headland C: 182°(G)

The gyro reads high, so subtract 2° from each reading. The true bearings are 045°T, 316°T and 180°T.

The angle of cut is the acute angle between the lines. Here the cuts are:

  • about 89° between A and B;
  • 44° between B and C;
  • 45° between C and A.

All are above 30°, so the fix is acceptable, though not the ideal 60° spread. Plot the three lines and you have a reliable fix, provided the triangle is small.

Large cocked hat? Look for these causes:

  • wrong compass error, or error applied the wrong way;
  • misidentified object;
  • too long an interval between bearings;
  • plotting error.

A consistent compass error tends to produce a triangle of similar size each time. If you cannot resolve it, treat the corner nearest danger as your position until you get a better fix.

Running fix and the four-point bearing

When only one object is available, use a running fix:

  • Take a bearing.
  • Run on for a known time.
  • Take a second bearing of the same object or another one.
  • Transfer the first position line along the course and distance made good between the two bearings.

Where the transferred line crosses the second line is the running fix, at the time of the second bearing.

Worked example (no current). The ship steers 090°T at 12 knots.

  • At 0900 a lighthouse bears 040°T.
  • At 0930 it bears 340°T.
  • Run between bearings: 0.5 h × 12 kn = 6.0 nm along 090°.

To plot it:

  1. Lay off the 0930 position line.
  2. Take any point on the 0900 line and move it 6.0 nm in direction 090°.
  3. Through that new point, draw a line parallel to the 0900 line. This is the transferred position line.
  4. Where the transferred line crosses the 0930 line is the 0930 running fix.

On these figures the 0930 position is the lighthouse bearing 340°T, distance 5.3 nm.

With tidal stream. Transfer the first line along the course and distance made good, allowing for set and drift, rather than along the course steered. If your stream estimate is wrong, the running fix is wrong, and that is its main weakness. Label it clearly as a running fix and confirm it with a true fix as soon as you can.

Doubling the angle on the bow. If the relative bearing of an object doubles, for example from 30° to 60° on the bow, the distance run between the two bearings equals the distance off at the second bearing. This assumes no current or leeway.

The four-point bearing is the special case of 45° to 90°. The distance run between the object being 45° on the bow and abeam equals the distance off when abeam.

  • Example: steering 000°T at 10 kn, a light bears 45° on the starboard bow at 1000 and is abeam at 1030. The run is 5.0 nm, so the light was 5.0 nm off when abeam.

Radar, sextant and transit methods

Radar ranges. Radar range is generally more accurate than radar bearing. Horizontal beamwidth smears the bearing, and any heading error feeds straight into it.

  • Two or three ranges of well-defined, steep-to objects make an excellent fix.
  • Make sure the echo really is the charted feature. It could be a low point behind it, or a vessel close inshore.
  • A visual bearing combined with a radar range of the same object is also a strong fix.

Transits. When two charted objects are in line, you are somewhere on the line through them. This is the most accurate position line there is, and it needs no compass error. Cross it with a bearing or range of another object for a fix. Comparing the charted transit bearing with your gyro reading also gives a quick gyro error check.

Vertical sextant angle (VSA). Measure the angle between the top of an object of known elevation and the waterline beneath it, after correcting for index error.

  • Distance off = height ÷ tan(angle), with height and distance in the same units.
  • Correct the charted elevation for the height of tide, using the vertical datum stated on the chart.
  • Nautical tables also tabulate this calculation.

Example: corrected height 60 m, observed angle 0°55.7'. tan(0°55.7') ≈ 0.0162, so distance ≈ 60 ÷ 0.0162 ≈ 3,700 m ≈ 2.0 nm.

The method only works if the base of the object is visible, so the object must be within your horizon.

Horizontal sextant angles (HSA). Measure the two angles between three charted objects. Plot them with a station pointer or tracing paper. No compass error is involved.

The method fails when the ship lies on, or close to, the circle passing through all three objects. The position is then indeterminate, so check for this before relying on the result.

GNSS, ECDIS and the habit of cross-checking

On modern ships most positions come from GNSS, displayed on ECDIS. The skill examiners and auditors look for is independent verification:

  • Cross-check GNSS at intervals set by the passage plan and company procedures. Use radar ranges, visual bearings, parallel indexing or transits.
  • Check the datum on paper charts. GNSS positions are referred to WGS 84. Read the chart's satellite-derived positions note and apply any stated shift before plotting.
  • Watch for degraded or spoofed signals. Warning signs include position jumps, speed spikes, steady offsets from the radar overlay, and HDOP or integrity warnings.
  • Know your ECDIS position monitoring. Most ECDIS can take a manual or radar-derived fix as a secondary position. Learn how your type-specific system does it.

How often to fix. Fixing frequency is not a single regulatory number. It depends on:

  • distance from danger;
  • speed;
  • traffic;
  • reliability of the available methods;
  • your company's passage planning instructions, which often specify intervals for coastal and pilotage waters.

As a rule of thumb, between fixes the ship should never be able to run, undetected, further towards the nearest danger than is safe.

Common mistakes and oral exam questions

Mistakes examiners see repeatedly:

  • Applying gyro error the wrong way. High means subtract, low means add.
  • Plotting the observed bearing from the ship instead of laying the reciprocal from the object.
  • Taking beam bearings first, so the fix is already stale by the time the slow-changing bearings are taken.
  • Fixing on buoys.
  • Accepting a fix with a poor angle of cut.
  • Transferring a running fix line along course steered when a tidal stream is running.
  • Failing to correct a VSA for height of tide.
  • Plotting GNSS on a paper chart without the datum shift.

Typical oral questions and model points:

  • Why take bow and stern bearings first? Their bearings change slowly, so the delay matters less. Beam bearings change fastest. Take them last, closest to the time of the fix.
  • You get a large cocked hat. What do you do? Check identification, compass error and plotting, then re-take the bearings. If doubt remains, assume the position most dangerous to the ship and act on it.
  • Which is more accurate on radar, range or bearing, and why? Range. Bearing accuracy is limited by beamwidth and heading input.
  • When does a horizontal sextant angle fix fail? When the ship is on or near the circle through the three objects.
  • GNSS and radar disagree by 0.4 nm near the coast. Your actions? Treat the position as doubtful until resolved, and do not assume either source is correct. Fix by independent means and inform the Master as required by standing orders. Check for datum or equipment faults, and record it in the log.
  • What are the limitations of a running fix? It depends on the accuracy of the course and distance made good between bearings. Unknown current or leeway corrupts it.
Key points
  • A fix needs two or more simultaneous position lines from external references crossing at a good angle.
  • Aim for about 90° cut with two lines and about 60° with three; the angle of cut is the acute angle between lines, and cuts below about 30° are suspect.
  • Take bearings near the bow and stern first and near the beam last.
  • Radar ranges are more reliable than radar bearings; transits are the most accurate position line.
  • A running fix is only as good as the course and distance made good between bearings.
  • Verify GNSS with an independent method whenever one is available, and check the chart datum note.
  • If a fix is doubtful, assume the position nearest danger and act on it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most accurate method of position fixing?

A transit crossed with a radar range or a good visual bearing is generally the most accurate terrestrial fix, because a transit has no compass error. Three radar ranges of well-defined, steep-to objects are also very reliable. Which is best on the day depends on the marks you actually have.

What is a cocked hat in chartwork?

It is the small triangle formed when three position lines do not meet at a single point. A small one is normal. A large one points to errors in compass error, identification, timing or plotting. If you cannot resolve it, take the corner nearest danger as your position.

What is the difference between a DR, an EP and a fix?

A DR uses only course steered and speed through the water. An EP adds estimated set, drift and leeway. A fix is a position obtained from external references such as bearings, ranges, celestial sights or GNSS.

How often should the officer of the watch fix the ship's position?

There is no single international interval. The STCW Code requires the officer of the watch to check the course, position and speed at sufficiently frequent intervals, using any available navigational aids necessary, to ensure the ship follows the planned course. Actual intervals come from the passage plan, the Master's standing orders and company procedures, and they become much shorter close to danger.

Why can I not just rely on GPS for position?

GNSS can fail, degrade, be jammed or be spoofed, and it may be referred to a different datum from a paper chart. A single unverified source gives no warning when it is wrong. Independent cross-checks such as radar ranges or parallel indexing catch the error before it puts the ship in danger.

Test yourself

When taking a three-bearing visual fix, which object's bearing should normally be taken last?

Beam bearings change fastest, so they are taken last to keep them closest to the time of the fix. This is part of the position-determination competence in STCW Table A-II/1.

The gyro error is 3° Low and a lighthouse bears 122°(G). What is the true bearing?

Low error is added, so 122° + 3° = 125°T. This is part of the position-determination competence in STCW Table A-II/1.

Using the four-point bearing, a light is 45° on the bow at 1400 and abeam at 1424, with the ship making 15 knots and no current. How far off was the light when abeam?

The distance run from 45° on the bow to abeam equals the distance off when abeam: 0.4 h × 15 kn = 6.0 nm. This is part of the position-determination competence in STCW Table A-II/1.

Why is a radar range usually preferred to a radar bearing when fixing?

Beamwidth smears the bearing and any heading error feeds directly into it, whereas range is measured from echo timing. This is part of the radar navigation competence in STCW Table A-II/1.

A GNSS position is to be plotted on a paper chart. What must the officer check first?

GNSS positions are referred to WGS 84, and a chart on another datum carries a note giving the shift to apply. Correct use of navigational equipment is required under SOLAS Chapter V and the STCW Code, Table A-II/1.
